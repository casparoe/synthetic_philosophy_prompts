I've been going around in circles on the old problem of what makes a proposition one truth-evaluable thing rather than a pile of constituents — Bradley's regress, the unity of the proposition, that whole knot. Instead of reading more, I tried writing down my actual gut verdicts on toy cases to see if there's a principle hiding behind them. I suspect there isn't, or at least not one that saves all of them at once. Heres where I land:

"Socrates is wise" gives me no urge at all to posit a tie or copula as an extra constituent; it's just one claim with wisdom said of Socrates. "Socrates is taller than Theaetetus" is still one claim, but now a relation is involved, and it feels like the relation has to grab both ends — except I don't think the grabbing is a third thing; the relation just does it. "Socrates is next to Theaetetus" makes me even more relaxed: since the relation is symmetric, I'm happy to treat it as a two-place property of the unordered pair. But "Socrates loves Theaetetus" bothers me. Because the relation is nonsymmetric, I suddenly feel the direction has to be held in place somehow, as if there should be a tie that points one way — and I know that's got to be confused, since symmetry can't be what makes the difference between a unity and a non-unity. "The mug is between the lamp and the book," a ternary relation, gives me a flicker of the actual regress: the between seems to need to latch three ways at once, and if that latching were itself a constituent it would need latching, and so on — but the flicker passes and I don't take the regress seriously. "Socrates is not flying" is worse. There I really do feel something extra is called for — a negative fact or an exclusion relation — yet I don't want to say 'not' is a special gluing device, since "Socrates is wise and Plato is Greek" is completely untroubling: 'and' just conjoins two things that are each already unified. And "Plato taught Shakespeare" is obviously false but still one fully unified proposition, so truth has nothing to do with it; meanwhile "2 is less than 3" is the cleanest case of all, so clean that I couldn't get the regress off the ground there even if I tried.

My provisional principle is something like: in an atomic proposition the predicate or relation is already incomplete or unsaturated, and that's what lets it bind its terms without any further tie; logical operators like 'and' and 'not' don't unify anything, they only operate on contents that are already unified; and the regress, whenever it shows up, is benign. But I don't see how my negativity verdict fits that, and I half-suspect the love/next-to asymmetry is just noise I should learn to ignore. Can you extract a coherent principle from these cases — one of the standard solutions or a repaired version of your own — and state it precisely? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and why? And what does the principle say about cases I didn't list, like "Socrates could have been a carpenter" (modality) or "that Socrates is wise is true" (a whole proposition as subject)? Be honest about where the principle gives out.
