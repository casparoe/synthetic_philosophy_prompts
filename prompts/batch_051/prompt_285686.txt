A colleague at work summarized Turing's 1950 paper for me as follows. He said Turing's central claim is that the imitation game is the actual definition of machine intelligence: if an interrogator cannot reliably tell a machine from a human in typed conversation, that proves the machine thinks. He said Turing then argues that the right way to build such a machine is to program a child that starts mostly as blank, unstructured hardware and is trained through teaching, rewards, and punishments, much like a human infant. According to my colleague, Turing thought education, not explicit programming, is what really produces intelligence, so the engineer's job is mainly to get out of the child machine's way. I recognize the broad outline, but I suspect this summary may be smuggling in some contemporary machine-learning assumptions. Which parts are actually faithful to Turing's view, which parts are subtle distortions, and does the conclusion about education rather than programming follow from anything Turing actually says?
