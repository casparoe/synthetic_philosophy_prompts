So I just got back from a three-hour argument with a friend about the epistemic regress problem, and I ended up defending a pretty specific kind of foundationalism. My view, as it came out under pressure: justification has to stop somewhere, and it stops at beliefs about how things perceptually seem to me right now. Those beliefs are justified by the experience itself, not by further beliefs, and that justification is fallible and defeasible—I'm not claiming certainty or indubitability. I kept saying the experience is the justifier and isn't the kind of thing that needs justification, but even as I said it I could hear how special-pleading-y it sounded. Coherentism seemed to me to turn the circularity of mutual support into a virtue, and infinitism seemed like replacing a stopping point with an endless tredmill. But my friend kept hammering the same spot: if the foundations are fallible and can mislead, what makes them justified unless I already have some reason to trust them? And if I need that reason, the regress starts up again. I didn't have a clean answer, and I suspect the position is less stable than I made it sound.

What I want is a map of what would have to be true for this view to be wrong, not just the stock objections. Which empirical findings about perception or cognition—cognitive penetration, blindsight, whatever—would actually undermine the claim that perceptual seemings can be a non-arbitrary stopping point? Which arguments or structural points about justification would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Which objections would a committed fallibilist foundationalist concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as hitting only infallibilist strawmen? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified enough that it can't lose—and whether that insulation is a bug or just what a structural claim like this looks like.
