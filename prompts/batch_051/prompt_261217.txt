I help code a long-running database of political violence for a comparative project, and we've got a case we keep going back and forth on. Our coding manual defines terrorism, roughly, as politically motivated violence directed at civilians with the aim of intimidating a wider audience, but the wording is vague enough that we've mostly fell back on our own earlier coding decisions. So far we've coded: a bomb in a market that killed twenty shoppers as terrorism; a guerrilla ambush on a military convoy in peacetime that killed six soldiers as armed action rather than terrorism; the shelling of a residential neighbourhood by state forces that killed about forty residents as state terrorism; the torching of an empty luxury housing estate by eco-activists, with a warning sent beforehand and nobody hurt, as sabotage rather than terrorism; and the car-bomb assassination of a finance minister, which also killed their driver, as terrorism.

The case we're stuck on now is this: an armed separatist group attacked a rural police post at night, killing three uniformed officers, with no bystanders involved. In a statement afterwards they said police are legitimate targets because they enforce the government's laws, and that the attacks would continue until police left the district. I can see our past decisions being run by at least two different rules: one that treats uniformed police as civilians for coding purposes, and one that treats any armed agent of the state as a non-civilian. How should we code the new incident if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our earlier decisions is the closest analogue, and if our precedents genuinely leave it open, what principle are we adopting with each option? What further case would settle which rule we've actually been using, and should we just fix the wording in the manual while we're at it? Answer in British English.
