I’m an engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I’ve been poking at Lucretius’s *On the Nature of Things* (the Leonard translation) because I got curious about the old atomist idea that matter is basically solid bits and everything else is void. This passage is what locked me in:

“And for the rest, summon to judgments true,
Unbusied ears and singleness of mind
Withdrawn from cares; lest these my gifts, arranged
For thee with eager service, thou disdain
Before thou comprehendest: since for thee
I prove the supreme law of Gods and sky,
And the primordial germs of things unfold,
Whence Nature all creates, and multiplies
And fosters all, and whither she resolves
Each in the end when each is overthrown.
This ultimate stock we have devised to name
Procreant atoms, matter, seeds of things,
Or primal bodies, as primal to the world.”

He seems to be saying the world decomposes into these ultimate particles, and when I read the surrounding argument for void I tried to formalize it as a step-by-step deduction. I keep going back and forth on whether its actually valid, so I want you to check exactly which step, if any, fails and what the correct version of it would be.

Here’s my reconstruction:

(1) Motion requires space for a thing to move *into*.
(2) If the universe were completely packed with these primal bodies and contained no empty space, there would be no place for anything to move into.
(3) But things evidently do move.
(4) Therefore there must be empty space, i.e. void.

That looks like valid modus tollens-style reasoning, but I smell a trick in premise 2. Why should motion need *empty* space? Couldn’t the bits just slide past each other the way sand flows, or transmit motion through a plenum the way a waveform travels through water, where no individual water molecule ends up in an empty gap? Lucretius seems to rule that out because he says the atoms themselves are solid and indivisible with no void *inside* them, but I’m not sure whether that premise is doing empirical work or is just definitional.

So my actual question: given Lucretius’s own commitments about the solidity and impenetrability of atoms, does this argument prove the existence of void, or does it only prove it if you’ve already defined matter as impenetrable in a way that assumes the conclusion? Is there a step where a modern or a rival ancient view could legitimately dig in, and what exactly is the reading that makes each step true or false?
