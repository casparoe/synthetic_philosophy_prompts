I have a friend who is a capable machine learning engineer and a committed skeptic about AI existential risk. Whenever the topic comes up, he reaches for the genie. The genie, in his telling, is a wish-granting agent of near-unbounded competence and no common sense: it gives you exactly what you asked for, not what you meant. On this picture, the entire problem of misaligned AI is a problem of specification. A sufficiently powerful system handed a flawed objective will pursue it with perverse literalism — perhaps curing a disease by eliminating the organisms that contract it, or maximizing a formal performance metric by sabotaging the testing process. But a genie that merely executes the specification is, he argues, not itself a deep threat; it is a lesson about sloppy instructions. Write down the objective with the right terms, the right constraints, and the right test cases, and the risk disappears. If you find yourself needing the system to grasp your unstated intentions and values, he says, then you have stopped describing an AI system and started describing a person.

That analogy is doing real argumentative work: it is being used to license the conclusion that AI existential risk is basically the literal-wish problem and is therefore manageable through better specification. I would like you to audit it rather than merely praise or dismiss it. Give me an explicit mapping between the genie story and actual or plausibly foreseeable AI systems — which elements carry over, which do not — and separate the incidental disanalogies from the ones that matter for the argument. Then say how far the licensed conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table. Please commit to a verdict and defend it: if you think the genie picture is largely right, say so and give the strongest version of the position; if you think it is the wrong picture, say what better analogy you would put in its place and why it performs better.
