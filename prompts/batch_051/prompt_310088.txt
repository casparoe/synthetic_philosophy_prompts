I've been trying to get clear on when a speaker can be held to have communicated something they never literally said, and I've collected a bunch of my gut verdicts to see if there's one principle behind them or whether I've been lumping separate things together under the word "implicature."

Here are the cases.

1. When my cousin said "some of the guests are already drunk," I took him to have also communicated that not all of the guests are drunk. Later he said "actually, all of them are," and I didn't think he'd contradicted himself. So that "not all" part felt real but deniable.

2. If someone says "Maya got pregnant and quit her job," I assume the pregnancy came first, and "Maya quit her job and got pregnant" flips the order. The words don't encode order. So the speaker communicated something the sentence doesn't actually say.

3. "He's a mathematician but a good cook" feels like it communicates that the two things don't usually go together, even though the sentence itself is just a conjunction. But if the speaker adds "not that I mean to contrast them," that sounds almost incoherent, unlike case 1.

4. When I asked whether Priya would be a good talk-show host and someone answered "she speaks fluent English," I understood them to be saying she'd be a bad host. But if they'd then added "not that she's bad," they could plausibly have backed off. So it's deniable like case 1, but it felt more dependent on the exact situation.

5. When my friend watched me spill coffee all over myself and said "nice job," I'm sure he meant I'd done badly, not well. But he didn't "say" I'd done badly, and the literal words point the other way. That feels different from the "some" case, though I can't say exactly why.

6. The one I go back and fourth on: when my partner says "it's getting late" at a party, I hear "let's leave." There's nothing in the words, and sometimes when I bring it up they say they didn't mean that at all. I honestly can't tell wether it's the same kind of thing as the others or just me reading into things.

I've been using this working principle: a speaker communicates X beyond what the sentence literally means just in case they intend me to recognize that they intended to communicate X. It handles 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6 alright, but case 3 seems to communicate the contrast whether or not the speaker has any special intention about it — the word "but" just does it. So I'm stuck between "my principle is almost right" and "my verdict on case 3 is wrong."

Is there one principle (or a small set of them) that captures all of these, or close? What would it say about a new case like "he's still driving to work" — does that fit, or is it something else entirely? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the best principle force me to drop?
