I just got put in charge of adding a "minimize side effects" penalty to the reward function for a delivery robot we're piloting in a hospital, and the more I read about low-impact AI the more I feel stuck between two things I can't both keep. On one side, the elegant approach really appeals to me: don't tell the robot what's valuable; just penalize it for making the world different from how it would have been if the robot had done nothing, or keep it from destroying its ability to achieve a wide range of possible goals. That looks like the only way to define "side effect" without a full specification of value, which nobody on my team could write down anyway—it's simple, quantitative, and gives a decision procedure: do the task but keep the world close to the no-intervention baseline. But I also can't shake the intuition that "close" or "different" is doing unacknowledged value work. A robot that bumps a patient's IV stand and a robot that shifts a waiting-room chair are both changing the world, but only the first is a side effect in the sense we care about, and no physics-level similarity metric I can imagine says why: either it smuggles in a judgment about which features matter or it ends up missing exactly the differences we'd call harmful. So maybe "avoid side effects" is only well-defined relative to some specifcation of what matters, and the whole low-impact project either collapses into just choosing values in disguise or hides the value judgment in the baseline and metric. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would cost to give up each: does the theory lose its only real advantage once "difference" has to be value-laden, and should my intuition be debunked as a confusion about what the formalism was ever trying to do? Or is there a reading where both survive, with the theory as a practical engineering tool for bounded cases and the intuition right about the general case? Walk me through the strongest version of both.
