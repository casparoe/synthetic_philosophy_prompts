I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and the vocabulary I actually think with when I draft is the working vocabulary of narrative craft: point of view and its shifts, free indirect discourse, focalization, interiority, voice, unreliable narration, and the distinction between scene and summary. I use those concepts fluently and daily, but I have no formal training in philosophy, so I need the translation to meet me where I already work. Lately I keep meeting a position in essays about minds and machines that is sometimes called illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. As I understand it, the view holds that introspection appears to reveal private, felt, qualitative properties—the ache of a particular headache, the redness of a red apple, what it is like to be me rather than you—but that introspection is systematically mistaken. There are no such properties, the view continues; there are only functional, representational, and dispositional states, and the sense of a rich inner qualitative life is an introspective misdescription, a kind of user illusion. The realism it opposes insists that these felt qualities are real and that they are exactly what a novelist is tracking when a passage of interiority works.

I would like you to restate this position, and where it helps the realism that opposes it, in the vocabulary of narrative craft I listed above, as faithfully and systematically as you can. Take each craft term and say what an illusionist would translate it as; take each central illusionist claim and say which craft device, if any, corresponds to it. Be scrupulous about keeping the translation honest: do not smuggle in my craft's presuppositions and call them translation, and do not pretend a loose analogy is a systematic mapping. Then give me the residue report. Tell me which parts of the illusionist position translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, and also which of my craft concepts have no counterpart in the illusionist picture. Finally, tell me what the residue shows. Is my craft vocabulary silently committed to phenomenal realism, such that illusionism would make the very idea of interiority incoherent? Or does illusionism survive translation and merely expose interiority as a technique rather than a metaphysical claim? I am less interested in which side wins than in learning exactly where the translation breaks, and whether a working novelist should take illusionism seriously or treat it as irrelevant to what happens on the page.
