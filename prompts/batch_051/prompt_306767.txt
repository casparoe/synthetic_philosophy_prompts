I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the badness of death, and I've hit a variation on the Epicurean argument that I'm not sure how to analise. The classic line, as I understand it, is that death can't be bad for the person who dies because while you exist death isn't present, and once death is present you no longer exist—so there is no subject left to be harmed. The standard reply is the deprivation account: death isn't bad because of anything you undergo; it's bad because it deprives you of the goods your life would otherwise have contained.

Here's the variation. Suppose there is an oracle that can tell you, with perfect reliability, the exact date of your death; the date is fixed and knowing it doesn't change it. Every newborn gets a sealed envelope, free to open whenever they like. Now take three people, Ada, Bea, and Cy, all of whom in fact die at 45, and all of whom would have lived to 80 (with the same goods) had they not died at 45. Ada opens the envelope at 20; Bea opens it at 44; Cy never opens it. Their outward lives and actual lifespans are the same; only when (if ever) the deadline becomes known differs. If the deprivation account is right, the three deaths look equally bad, since each person loses the same 35 years of goods. But that seems off: Ada spends twenty-five years with her plans and emotional life shaped by a known deadline, Bea has a year of it, and Cy has none. I want to know whether that extra harm in Ada's case is a harm of death at all, or just a separate harm of receiving bad information; and whether the original Epicurean argument handles the foreknowledge case better or worse than the deprivation account does. Run both positions through the variation properly and tell me which one survives it, or if they end up agreeing in this case. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
