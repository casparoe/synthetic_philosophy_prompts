I spent my career in solid-state physics, and now that I'm retired I've been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group. One debate that keeps going around in circles is whether Noether's theorem actually explains conservation laws or just repackages them. Everyone in the room knows the mathematics cold: a continuous symmetry of the action yields a conserved current. Nobody disputes the proof. But the argument over whether translational symmetry explains momentum conservation, or whether that's only a compact way of restating the Euler-Lagrange equations, has stayed exactly where it was decades ago, as far as I can tell.

The symmetry side says the theorem tells you why momentum is conserved the way a mechanism tells you why a clock runs: the laws don't care where you are, and momentum conservation falls out of that indifference. The bookkeeping side says an equivalence is not an explanation. You can run the derivation the other way and recover the symmetry from the conservation law, so the mathematics doesn't pick out which side explains which. On their view, conservation laws didn't get explained; they got translated into a different vocabulary.

My working hypothesis is that the actual disagreement is about what a mathematical explanation has to do in physics: whether an explanation needs a causal or mechanistic story, or whether constraint by symmetry counts as explanation in its own right. But I'm not confident. There might be a hidden formal question underneath it—e.g., whether the relevant symmetries are features of the world or artifacts of how we chose the Lagrangian, and what happens in non-Lagrangian systems or with gauge symmetries. I also wonder wether the two sides mean different things by "why."

What I'd like is a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this really: substantive, verbal, two camps answering different questions under one heading, or a fight about standards of explanation? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a sharper account of explanation, a theorem, an instructive case, or a reason to think the question was ill-posed all along?
