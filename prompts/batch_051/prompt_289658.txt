Most discussion of the platforms' January 2021 bans of Donald Trump treats the case as a fight about free speech, platform power, or content moderation. I want to set those framings aside and analyze the bans as an episode of social sanction functioning as punishment, using the standard theories of legal punishment to see how well they travel to a case where the sanctioning agents are private firms rather than the state.

The facts I have in mind are these. After the January 6 attack on the U.S. Capitol, Twitter permanently suspended the president's main account, Facebook and YouTube suspended his accounts (with later revisits to whether the suspensions would become permanent), and Amazon, Apple, and Google removed or moved to remove services used by platforms associated with the riot. These decisions happened within days, were made by independent private actors rather than by any court or legislature, and were driven by some mix of employee pressure, advertiser pressure, and public demand. There were no formal findings of fact and no sentence fixed in advance.

Treat those decisions as a decentralized form of punishment. Work through the main families of punishment theory—retribution, specific and general deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, and expressive or communicative theories—and for each, say who the punisher is, who the punished is, what conduct is being sanctioned, what the theory would require or permit, and what would count as success or failure. I want the mapping to stay precise: distinguish, for instance, the platform as punisher from the platform as the site where advertisers, employees, or the public do the punishing.

Then run the analysis in the other direction. Which features of this episode do punishment theories explain well, and which do they strain to handle? The candidate trouble spots I notice are: there was no single punisher with standing and no institutional obligation to act proportionately; the sanction came as a bundle of overlapping bans and service removals rather than as one measured sentence; the practical severity was indefinite exclusion from major infrastructure rather than a duration chosen in advance; and the platforms mostly framed their actions as safety or integrity measures rather than as deliberate punishment. Are those minor deviations that punishment theory already has resources for, or do they expose a real limit in applying the framework to private deplatforming?

End with a bottom line: was this a legitimate exercise of social sanction, a failure of social sanction, or an event that punishment theory is simply not built to evaluate? If the last, say what kind of theory—or what institutional design change—would be needed before distributed private sanctions could be evaluated as punishment rather than as something else.
