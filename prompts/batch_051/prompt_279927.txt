I have been working out a position I call graded modal realism, and I would like an honest, committed assessment of it rather than a survey of alternatives. I will state it as plainly as I can.

I accept the core of Lewisian modal realism: possible worlds are concrete, maximal, and causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another, and they are not merely abstract descriptions or ersatz representations. What I reject is the thesis that all worlds are equally real. My central claim is that worlds differ in a primitive property I call ontological weight. The actual world has weight 1 and is the unique maximum; every other world has some weight strictly between 0 and 1. Weight is objective and non-epistemic: it is not a measure of how easily we can imagine a world, how often we discuss it, or how probable we judge it to be. It lines up with our comparative similarity judgments — the world in which I finish this sentence one second earlier has a weight very close to 1, while a world with different fundamental constants has a weight near 0 — but I take weight to be the deeper fact, and I regard the similarity ordering as a shadow cast by ontology rather than the other way around.

I accept a consequence worth flagging: this makes actuality non-indexical. Exactly one world has maximal weight, and it is the world I and my readers inhabit. I am not sure whether to treat that as a cost, a feature, or merely a consequence I must live with.

I use ontological weight to do two jobs. First, for the modal operators themselves: a proposition is possible iff it is true at some world with positive weight, and necessary iff it is true at every world with positive weight; the high and low ends of the scale make no difference to the operators themselves. Second, for counterfactuals: instead of taking Lewis's comparative similarity relation as unexplained, I say the closest world in which an antecedent holds is simply the antecedent-world with the greatest ontological weight.

I think this buys two things. It blunts the incredulous stare, because one is no longer paying full ontological commitment for a vast plenum of worlds all as real as this one; one pays full price only for actuality and a sliding fee for the rest. And it explains the graded character of much modal thought: we speak as though some possibilities are nearer than others, and on this view that talk is not merely psychological or metaphorical.

Assess this charitably but candidly. Is existing to a lesser degree a coherent primitive, or does it collapse into similarity under another name, into probability, or into a merely psychological sense of closeness? Does the view still deliver what a realist wants from worlds — truthmakers for possibility and necessity — once every non-actual world has only a fraction of being? What are the three strongest objections to it, and is any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? I suspect something like this has been tried before near talk of degrees of being or graded modal notions; identify the nearest views from what you know, but do not give me a bibliography. End by taking a definite position: adopt the view, repair it in a specified way, or abandon it — and if you repair it, describe the smallest change that makes it viable. Do not lay out interpretations and leave the choice to me.
