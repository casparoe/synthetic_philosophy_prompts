I run a twice-monthly reading group at a community center; it began as a library "big ideas" series, and the regulars are mostly working adults with no academic training. We have spent the last two meetings on play and leisure, working mostly through Huizinga's Homo Ludens and Pieper's short book Leisure: The Basis of Culture, and one frustration keeps resurfacing. Both authors insist that play and leisure have their point inside themselves rather than in some further payoff, and that modern life relentlessly turns them into instruments: recreation becomes work-recovery, games become measurable fitness, hobbies become self-improvement. The group can repeat that slogan back to me, but I can tell nobody has yet felt how the same activity changes its character depending on what the player is aiming at, and they keep sliding back into treating leisure as mere time off or a reward for being productive.

I would like you to design a ninety-minute facilitated activity for roughly ten adults in that room, which has tables and chairs and no special equipment beyond what I can buy cheaply at a supermarket. Its purpose would be to make participants experience the difference between doing something for its own sake and doing the same thing for a score, a prize, or a deadline, and then to go one step further: to distinguish genuine leisure from idleness and recuperation in Pieper's sense, and to feel what Huizinga means when he says real play is voluntary, absorbing, bounded by rules, and productive of its own order. Please give me the complete run of the session rather than a discussion guide: exact rules and roles, materials, a timeline, the instructions I should give and the things I should deliberately not explain until afterwards, and a thorough debrief that maps specific moments of the exercise onto those ideas. I would also like an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce, which features of these views I would still have to supply by talking afterward, and what will predictably go wrong when I run it, with suggestions for handling that. The point is for the idea to emerge from what happens in the room, not from a lecture I give at the start.
