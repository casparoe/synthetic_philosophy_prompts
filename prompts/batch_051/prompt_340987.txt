In the classic Ellsberg two-urn choice, Urn A contains 50 red and 50 black balls, while Urn B contains 100 balls, each either red or black, in a proportion unknown to the chooser. A chooser who wins a fixed prize by correctly naming the color of a ball drawn from the selected urn typically prefers to bet on a color from Urn A rather than on the same color from Urn B, for both red and black—a pattern no subjective expected utility maximizer can exhibit. Now alter the case as follows. Urn B is no longer arbitrary: before the chooser entered, a fair coin was flipped in secret. On heads, Urn B was filled with 80 red and 20 black balls; on tails, with 20 red and 80 black. The chooser is told all of this but not the coin's outcome. Betting on red from Urn B is thus a one-half chance of facing an 80 percent chance and a one-half chance of facing a 20 percent chance, which under the usual reduction of compound lotteries is exactly a 50 percent chance—the same as betting on red from Urn A. Does the Ellsberg pattern survive this modification? If a chooser still strictly prefers Urn A, is that preference still a violation of subjective expected utility in the modified setting, or has it become something else? In your answer, distinguish what a multiple-prior/maxmin representation and a second-order-probability representation would each need to assume about the coin in order to predict or rule out the Urn A preference, and say whether their divergence here illuminates or changes the standard interpretation of the original Ellsberg results.
