George Orwell argued that a central disease of modern political prose is its use of language that distances readers from reality: stale and mechanically reused figures of speech, inflated or Latinate diction, passive constructions, and euphemisms that allow indefensible actions to be discussed without being seen. He also maintained that the decay of language and the decay of political thought reinforce one another, and he proposed remedies aimed at clarity, concreteness, and the active voice.

Now consider a practice that emerged long after his death. A democratic government deploys a large language model to rewrite all official communications—welfare notices, police statements, regulatory decisions, ministerial speeches—through a set of filters that automatically substitute plainer vocabulary, shorten sentences, remove the passive voice, and purge emotionally charged or offensive phrasing. The system is publicly justified as realizing Orwell's own linguistic preferences.

What, in your assessment, would Orwell's position be on such a system? In answering, be precise about which features of the technology he would likely approve as genuinely clarifying and which features he would diagnose as a new mechanism of the very disease he described. Pay particular attention to the difference between a writer voluntarily choosing plain language and a machine imposing a standardized surface style, the role of the training data in recycling stale figures of speech rather than eliminating them, and the possibility that stripping offensive vocabulary out of official documents could become a new way of concealing state violence rather than disclosing it. Conclude with what you take to be the single deepest divergence, if any, between Orwell's ideal of plain political speech and the automated version of that ideal.
