For an advanced undergraduate seminar on philosophy of social science, I am designing a sorting exercise to help students locate themselves in the debate about whether national character is a coherent notion and whether generalizations about a people are ever acceptable. I want the exercise built around a taxonomy of positions rather than a quiz with a scoring key. It should take the form of a decision tree with eight leaves, each leaf named for the position it sorts into, and each branching question phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing the labels.

The passage I want the whole exercise to hang on is from David Hume\'s essay "Of National Characters." Here is the passage:

> We are apt to imagine, that if we were transported into a distant kingdom, we should there find a very different set of people from those with whom we are conversant at home. But if we travel into a foreign country, we find, that the people are not so different from ourselves, as we had expected. The greater part of mankind may be resolved into a few characters; and the difference of countries is not so great, but that a man may soon be at home anywhere.

I would like the tree to begin from Hume\'s observation that the folk notion of distinct national characters seems to overstate the actual differences among peoples. The first split should be whether the student takes this observation to show that national-character talk is simply a confused way of talking about something else. Each later branch should be labeled with the specific claim that separates it from its siblings, such as whether national character is reducible to aggregated individual traits, whether it is an emergent property of institutions, whether it is a mere statistical generalization about a population, or whether it is an ideological construction.

One leaf must be a view that accepts that many national-character generalizations are statistically true but holds that they are never acceptable as reasons in deliberation because they fail to treat persons as individuals; another leaf must be a view that accepts such generalizations as epistemically respectable but morally corrosive in a way that still leaves them sometimes permissible. Please flag any pair of positions the tree cannot really separate without adding a controversial assumption. The handout should be in American English and should include, for each leaf, the name of the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
