Had a long argument with a friend yesterday about envy and resentment. He kept insisting Rawls actually gives envy a kind of diagnostic role. In A Theory of Justice, in the bit on the problem of envy, Rawls says envy isn't a moral feeling the way resentment is — it doesn't appeal to any principle of justice — and he calls it a vice. But he also says it can be excusable when the gap is so big that it undermines the worse-off person's self-respect. My friend ended up saying social media is basically a factory for excusable envy. So I wrote this argument and now it seems to prove too much.

(1) If Rawls is right, then situations designed to reliably produce excusable envy are morally objectionable. (2) Social media feeds are designed to reliably produce excusable envy, because they constantly show you flattering slices of everyone else's life. (3) But by the same reasoning, any public display of good fortune — a wedding album, a promotion announcement, just wearing nicer clothes — is objectionable if it reliably produces excusable envy. (4) That's absurd. (5) So Rawls's criterion for excusable envy must be doing too much work somewhere. I'd rather keep (1) than give up (4). Where does this go wrong, and what would I be paying if I reject one of the first three premises instead?
