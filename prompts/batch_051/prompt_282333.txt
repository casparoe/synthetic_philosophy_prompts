I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a piece about why we pour money into preserving beautiful things that almost no one will ever see—sealed-off cave paintings, reefs too fragile for divers, paintings that live in climate-controlled storage. While rereading G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica I hit a pair of passages that seem to be pulling in different directions, and I can't tell if Moore is contradicting himself or if I'm flattening a distinction he's actually making. In Chapter III, section 50, pushing back on Sidgwick's claim that no one could rationally aim at beauty apart from any possible contemplation of it, Moore writes: "Let us imagine one world exceedingly beautiful. Imagine it as beautiful as you can; put into it whatever on this earth you most admire—mountains, rivers, the sea; trees, and sunsets, stars and moon. Imagine these all combined in the most exquisite proportions, so that no one thing jars against another, but each contributes to the beauty of the whole. And then imagine the ugliest world you can possibly conceive. Imagine it simply one heap of filth, containing everything that is most disgusting to us, for whatever reason, and the whole, as far as may be, without one redeeming feature." He then asks us to suppose that no human being can ever see either world, and he treats it as rational to hold that the beautiful world is still the better one. So unperceived beauty carries some intrinsic value.

But later, in Chapter VI, section 113, after saying that by far the most valuable things we can imagine are certain states of consciousness—roughly, the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects—he adds: "I have myself urged in Chap. III. (§ 50) that the mere existence of what is beautiful does appear to have some intrinsic value; but I regard it as indubitable that Prof. Sidgwick was so far right, in the view there discussed, that such mere existence of what is beautiful has value, so small as to be negligible, in comparison with that which attaches to the consciousness of beauty." This second passage feels like he's walking the first one back to almost nothing. If the intrinsic value of unperceived beauty is negligible, what work is the two-worlds argument really doing?

So here's what I'm after. Can these passages be reconciled without special pleading—is he using "value" in two different senses, comparing a whole world in one case and a part of a world in the other, silently changing his mind, or something else? Which reading is the most honest to the text and which ones are just retrofitting? And more practically for my article: can a Moorean still say those sealed-off paintings and unvisited reefs matter in themselves, or does his own later passage make their value negligble?
