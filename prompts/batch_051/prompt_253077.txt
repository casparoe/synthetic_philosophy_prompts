Next month I'm giving a seminar to a mixed social science audience — mostly cross-cultural psychologists and comparative political scientists, plus a couple of sociologists — defending the claim that national character isn't a coherent notion and that we should mostly stop generalizing about what a people is like. My sketch of the argument: the trait ascriptions are too vague to test, within-country variation is huge, apparent cross-national differences in survey data mostly reflect response styles and institutions rather than durable dispositions, and the few stable differences left are better explained by situations, selection, and history than by shared character. The audience isn't hostile, but they work with cross-national data all day — tightness-looseness, individualism-collectivism, personality geography, trust and state capacity — and they'll think I've built a strawman. The two I'm most worried about are a cross-cultural psychologist who does personality geography and a comparativist who studies how institutions shape social trust. Prep me for Q&A: what objections will I actually get from this crowd, roughly in order, phrased the way those two would put them? For each, what should I concede, what should I hold, and where is my reply softest? And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
