In a reading group on Frege's "On Sense and Reference," I got stuck on a short passage I had not paid much attention to before. Frege writes:

Yet the sentence 'Odysseus was set ashore at Ithaca while sound asleep' obviously has a sense, even though 'Odysseus' has no reference. The thought remains the same whether or not 'Odysseus' has a reference.

The remark seems harmless, but every time I try to extend it to fiction I arrive at a conclusion I cannot live with. Here is the argument I keep building, with the steps laid out as clearly as I can manage:

(1) The thought expressed by a sentence containing an empty proper name is the same whether or not the name has a reference.
(2) So the thought itself is existentially neutral: no part of the thought one grasps consists in the name's actually referring to something.
(3) Frege also holds that, because the name has no reference, the sentence as a whole has no truth value; it is neither true nor false.
(4) But we ordinarily accept as true such claims as "In the Sherlock Holmes stories, Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" and "In the Odyssey, Odysseus was set ashore at Ithaca while sound asleep."
(5) By (3), the embedded sentences "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" and "Odysseus was set ashore at Ithaca while sound asleep" have no truth value.
(6) Therefore, the larger claims in (4) cannot be true either, since they attribute truth to something that is not truth-evaluable.
(7) Hence all ordinary claims of the form "It is true in the story that p," where p contains a fictional name, are either false or not truth-evaluable.
(8) But that is absurd: "In the Sherlock Holmes stories, Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" is one of the most certain things I know, and it is certain precisely as a truth.

I would rather keep the ordinary practice of saying that sentences about Holmes are true-in-the-stories than accept Frege's inference from the emptiness of the name to the lack of truth value. But I also do not want to simply walk away from Frege's point that the thought remains the same whether or not the name refers, because that point is doing real work in his semantics. Which step or premise should go, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is the mistake in the move from "the thought is the same whether or not the name refers" to "the thought is existentially neutral," or is it in the move from "the sentence has no truth value" to "the larger story-qualified sentence cannot be true," or somewhere else entirely? I want the strongest diagnosis you can give, with attention to the main options: Frege's own view, Russell's analysis of ordinary names as descriptions, pretense or make-believe theories of fiction, and views that treat fictional characters as abstract objects.
