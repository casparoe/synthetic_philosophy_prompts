I convene a standing reading group in value theory, and for the past year two of its members have been deadlocked over the question whether maximal moral goodness — what the literature now calls moral sainthood — is an ideal we should want ourselves and others to approximate. They have agreed to stop trading argument sketches and to produce an adversarial-collaboration statement instead. Draft the document they should have written.

One party, call it the Primacy party, holds that moral goodness is not one value among several but the governing standard for how a life should be lived. On its view, the familiar objection that sainthood crowds out humor, aesthetic refinement, physical skill, and deep personal attachments is either moralized self-indulgence or a confusion about what morality actually demands. The other party, call it the Pluralist party, holds that a good human life includes non-moral excellences — wit, beauty, craft, sport, erotic and parental love — whose value is not merely instrumental to moral goodness, and that a life optimized for moral achievement at their expense is not the best life for most people, however admirable it is in one respect.

The statement should contain four sections.

First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign, written in neutral terms and specific enough to be informative. The list should say what they jointly mean by moral goodness, what the possibility of a saintly life does and does not show, and which simpler positions both reject (for example, that self-interest is the only standard of a good life, or that moral considerations have no bearing on it).

Second, the exact residual disagreement, phrased so that each party would accept the wording of its own side. Then say plainly whether any part of their disagreement is merely verbal — a divergence in how they use terms such as 'moral', 'good life', or 'ideal' — and, if so, give the reformulation that removes the verbal element and leaves the substantive remainder visible.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one party. Include at least these three: whether non-moral value has non-instrumental weight in an all-things-considered judgment of a life; whether the losses the saint seems to incur are genuine losses or merely the disappearance of temptations; and whether lives can be ranked on one scale of overall goodness at all, or whether the standards are several and incommensurable. Add further cruxes if the dialectic demands them.

Fourth, for each crux, at least one concrete case or test that both parties would agree bears on it, with each party's prediction or reading recorded in advance. The cases may be invented vignettes, but they must discriminate: they should be ones where the two parties give different verdicts, not merely ones that illustrate positions they already hold.

Finish with a stability note of the sort a careful drafter would append for readers: identify the most fragile load-bearing claim in the joint statement, say which single argument, case, or finding would most change the overall balance of the draft, and explain where your own drafting is most likely to have been pulled by one party's idiom rather than by genuinely common ground.
