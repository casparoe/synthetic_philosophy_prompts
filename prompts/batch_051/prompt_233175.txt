I'm not a student of philosophy or anything, but I've been trying to pin down what I think about frugality after a few arguments with my family, and my gut verdicts on specific situations are more consistent than my ability to explain them. The real puzzle, I think, is whether the point of frugality is something about my own choices or something about what actually ends up happening in the world, and I want to know what principle, if any, is doing the work underneath my gut. Here are the cases as I honestly feel them.

(1) My phone screen is cracked and the battery is weak, but it still works. I can easily afford a new one. I've been carrying a power bank and plan to keep the phone another year. I think this is the right thing to do, evem though I know my one phone is a drop in the bucket for e-waste either way.

(2) My coworker Sam buys a $9 lunch out every workday; it's his one treat, his budget is tight but he's not in debt. Another coworker told him to pack a sandwich and called him wasteful. I thought the lecture was preachy and out of line, and I didn't side with the one giving it — even though Sam's takeout does make a lot of packaging trash. I don't think I should push Sam to be frugal.

(3) My city added a fee on plastic bags and put up posters about mending clothes instead of replacing them. I support both. The city is nudging thousands of strangers, but it doesn't feel preachy to me the way the coworker did, even though each individual's behavior only changes a little.

(4) My cousin has a good salary and replaces his whole wardrobe every fall and spring, donating the old stuff. I judge him for it. When I try to say what's actually wrong, I can't point to a harm; the clothes get donated and reused. It feels like the thoughtless replacing is itself the wrong thing, not its effects. But if the act itself is what matters, why was it wrong for our coworker to push an act of frugality on Sam?

(5) There's an old man in my building who lives on plain oatmeal, keeps the heat at 58 in the winter, and washes and reuses plastic wrap. He can afford better. I don't admire him; he seems a little sick. But I can't explain why his frugality looks excessive while my cracked phone feels like something a decent person would just do.

The nearest thing I have to a principle is this: it's good for me to make the frugal choice myself, and it's okay for a big institution to nudge everyone toward frugality, but it's not okay for one person to push it on another face-to-face. That sounds incoherent — if it's fine to say it to thousands of strangers through a poster, shouldn't it be fine to say it to one coworker? — but it fits my gut. Is there a cleaner principle that captures these verdicts, and wich of them does it force me to drop? And what does the principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: my friend Nia works a minimum-wage job but saves up for months to buy a $300 perfume. Do I admire the patient saving, judge the purchase, or just shut up, and why?

You're a professional philosopher who's unusually good at digging principles out of messy ordinary moral intuitions. Please think carefully — maybe start by brainstorming a few candidate principles, test them against my cases, then give me the best one, state it precisely, and tell me honestly where it breaks with my gut.
