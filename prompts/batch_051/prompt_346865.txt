I keep going back and forth on this and would appreciate a take that traces through the structure of the argument instead of just summarizing the two camps. I've been reading through the metaphor debate, and the positions feel maddeningly hard to reconcile. Davidson's line, as I understand it, is that there is no such thing as metaphorical meaning: a metaphocial sentence means exactly what its words literally mean, and everything else—the resonance, the surprise, the way it reorganizes your thinking—is just a causal effect the sentence has on a listener, no more part of what the sentence means than being startled by a loud bang is part of what the bang says. Lakoff and Johnson, by contrast, hold that metaphor is mostly a matter of thought rather than language: stable conceptual mappings like ARGUMENT IS WAR or TIME IS MONEY structure how people reason, and the words on the page just trigger those mappings.

Here's the development I can't stop thinking about. Suppose there were a large, well-controlled body of experiments showing that swapping a single metaphor in an otherwise identical text changes people's judgments in systematic, predictable ways. For instance, people who read about crime described as a virus afflicting a city tend to recommend diagnosis, inoculation, and treating underlying conditions, while people who read the same article with crime described as a beast preying on the city tend to recommend catching, caging, and harsher punishment—and the differences track the entailments of the virus and beast source domains rather than anything in the literal wording. Stipulate the effects are real and robust. Does Davidson's argument survive this, does it need repair, or is it simply dead? I want to know which specific premise is under pressure, and whether there is a weaker Davidsonian view that can grant that metaphors have systematic, predictable cognitive effects while still denying that they carry a special metaphorical meaning in the linguistic sense.
