After a reading group on the Five Ways, I have come to accept Aquinas’s Third Way as the strongest version of the contingency argument. The part I keep returning to is his opening move in the *Summa Theologiae*: many things are generated and corrupted, so if everything were the sort of thing that can fail to exist, there would have been some time at which nothing existed, and from this he concludes that there must be something whose existence is necessary. I know the familiar objections—that it commits a fallacy of composition, and that even if a necessary being exists it need not be the God of classical theism. What I want is a finer-grained map of the position’s exposure. If I hold the Third Way, what exactly would have to be true for it to fail? Which logical results, changes in general ontology, or empirical discoveries would count decisively against it, and which would be merely awkward? Which of these would a careful defender of the argument concede as a genuine cost, and which would they reject as question-begging or irrelevant? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the position is perhaps too well insulated.
