I have been working through a debate in the philosophy of photography about whether looking at a photograph is ever, in a strict sense, a way of seeing the photographed thing itself — as a mirror, a telescope, or a live video feed is ordinarily taken to let one see something — or whether a photograph is always only a picture that one sees, with the thing depicted but not seen. The usual considerations pull in both directions: on one side, a photograph is produced by light from the scene registering on a light-sensitive surface, so the image stands in a causal, informational relation to the scene that a drawing does not; on the other side, a still photograph does not update as the scene moves, and a photograph of something no longer there does not seem to put one in the presence of the thing. I want the full specification before I take a position.

Write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of photographic transparency must satisfy: the cases it has to handle (surveillance footage, photographs of staged or fictional scenes, long-exposure and composite images, heavily retouched images, film versus digital capture, and borderline cases such as X-rays or thermal images); the distinctions it cannot collapse — in particular, the difference between seeing through a picture and merely receiving reliable information from it, and between transparency and realism or evidentiary value; and the constraints that come from the concept of seeing itself rather than from one side of the debate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one camp. If some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say which ones conflict and why the conflict is structural rather than a gap in the evidence. Finally, pick the single requirement that, enforced strictly, would eliminate the most popular positions, and say what survives it.
