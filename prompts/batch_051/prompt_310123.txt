Two colleagues of mine argued over lunch about whether the machine-learning systems now being deployed should already count as transformative. I reconstruct their argument as four moves:

(1) The first said no: transformative must mean something on the scale of the Industrial Revolution — a sustained, society-wide reorganization of work, institutions, and daily life, showing up in long-run productivity growth. The current wave of models and the tools built on them has been widely used for only a few years, and the broad productivity statistics do not yet show a durable jump, let alone institutional reorganization. Calling today's AI transformative, he said, drains the word of content and leaves it meaning only "impressive."

(2) The second said this standard cannot be applied from the inside. If a technology becomes transformative only once its downstream reorganization is complete, then nobody in 1830 could say the Industrial Revolution was underway — yet it evidently was. She also offered another sense of transformative: a discontinuous expansion in what can be automated. Software that can read, write, code, and analyze on request is such a discontinuity, whatever the productivity numbers have shown so far.

(3) The first replied that a discontinuous capability is not the same as a transformation. Electricity was a discontinuous capability, and we call it transformative only because it eventually reorganized manufacturing, the household, and the city. The power loom was a discontinuity too, but it transformed one industry rather than a society. The capability sense of "transformative," he argued, licenses predicting and then backdating: one calls the technology transformative now because one assumes the reorganization will come.

(4) The second answered that he was smuggling in a completeness test. If people in 1895 could not yet call electricity transformative because the factories, suburbs, and household appliances had not yet been rebuilt around it, that is a reason to regard the concept as hindsight-bound, not a reason to deny that a transformation was underway. By his test, she said, every transformation passes through a period in which it is already happening but not yet confirmable, and that is plausibly where we are now; waiting for the lagging statistics is not neutrality, it is a bias toward the past.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say whether the disagreement is merely verbal (two senses of "transformative") or substantive, which definition is doing the main work, what, if anything, both parties are missing, and what evidence could settle the question. Please write the answer in American English.
