I've been roped into a red-team review for a moderation system that writes pretty sophisticated judgments about whether content is harassing, but is deliberately built so it can't do anything about the content itself—it's judgment just gets folded into a dashboard for a human reviewer. One side in the review says the thing can't be a moral agent because it's a constrained agent: it judges but cannot act. I built an argument to that effect and now I worry it proves too much. Here it is.

(1) To be a moral agent, a system has to be capable of acting on at least some of its judgments.
(2) Acting on a judgment requires controlling whether the relevant action happens, not merely shifting the probability that someone else does it.
(3) A constrained AI—one whose judgments are, by design, reduced to dashboard entries that a human is free to ignore—never controls any downstream action; it only nudges probabilities inside the human's decision loop.
(4) So a constrained AI cannot act on its judgments, and therefore is not a moral agent.
(5) Run the same argument on a human policy adviser whose memos a minister is free to ignore: she also never controls the decision, she only shifts the probability of it.
(6) But that conclusion is too strong. We hold human advisers responsible for the judgments they push, how they frame them, what they choose not to flag—we don't treat them as outside the moral community.
(7) So one of the steps before (6) has to go, but I don't want to lose the conclusion in (4), since the whole point of constraining the AI was to keep it out of the moral picture.

Where does the argument go wrong? If you'd reject a step, say which one, explain why it looked harmless, and tell me what rejecting it costs—in particular, does anything like the argument survive that still leaves the constrained AI outside the moral picture?
