I have drafted the following notes for a short essay, working title 'The corporate-person analogy cuts the other way,' and I would like detailed comments of a particular kind: first, whether the central argument actually goes through; second, which specific claims below would need to be weakened or discarded; and third, where a sophisticated defender of legal personhood for AI would push back hardest. Please treat the notes as a working sketch rather than a finished paper, and do not suggest further reading; I want an assessment of the argument on its own terms.

The essay begins from a familiar observation: proponents of legal personhood for AI often argue that since corporations are legal persons although they are not natural persons, legal personhood has already been detached from biological humanity, so extending it to an AI system is not a radical conceptual movement. My aim is to argue that, read carefully, the corporate-person analogy supports the opposite presumption.

The notes, in rough order:

(1) Legal person is a term of art for a bundle of legal capacities, such as the capacity to hold property, make contracts, sue, and be sued. To call a corporation a person is not to assert a deep similarity between corporations and human beings; it is to organize legal relations.

(2) The paradigm function of corporate personality is aggregation of the legal activity of underlying natural persons. The corporation is a device for enabling many human beings — shareholders, directors, employees — to act, and to be acted upon, as a unit. Its legal capacities are exercised by natural persons and are ultimately answerable to their interests.

(3) If that is the paradigm, then the corporate analogy should prompt a specific question when AI personhood is proposed: which natural persons stand behind the proposed AI person, and whose legal capacity would AI personhood aggregate, represent, or hold answerable? For a corporation the question has an answer in principle; for an AI system operating without a determinate principal, it is unclear that it has one at all.

(4) The analogy therefore reveals a missing element rather than normalizing the extension. A legal person in this paradigm is a vehicle for interests and a locus of accountability that traces back to natural persons. Where there is no underlying principal whose agency is being bundled, the analogy furnishes no reason to confer the bundle — and some reason to hesitate.

(5) Consequently, the positions that survive the corporate analogy are narrower than the usual debate assumes. AI personhood is plausible either as an administrative device that routes liability to identifiable humans (in which case it is not the kind of personhood at issue), or as a genuine status claim about AI interests or moral patiency — a claim that would require an argument of a different sort, which the corporate analogy does not by itself supply.

I would especially like comments on the following. Does the argument equivocate anywhere between person as a bundle of legal capacities and person as a status term? Would the fact that the corporate form is often used to limit and fragment responsibility, not only to aggregate it, weaken premise (2) or strengthen the case for the analogy? What is the strongest counterexample to the principle that there is no legal person without an underlying human principal — for instance, a charitable trust, or an action against a vessel in rem, that is, a proceeding against the vessel itself — and how much damage does it do? Finally, is (5) stated too strongly, and what is the most defensible version of the conclusion?
