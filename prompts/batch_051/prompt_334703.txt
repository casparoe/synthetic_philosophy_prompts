I am currently taking a political philosophy course on the concept of liberty, and our work so far has distinguished three traditions: negative liberty, understood as freedom from interference by others; positive liberty, understood as the capacity to direct one's own life and pursue one's own goals; and republican liberty, understood as freedom from arbitrary or uncontrolled power. I have been thinking about what would happen if someone tried to build these distinctions into an actual administrative decision procedure, and I would like you to work through a concrete proposal with me. I am not asking you to defend or reject it in the abstract, but to trace how it would behave in practice, where it would break down, and what that reveals about the three concepts.

Here is the proposal. A university creates a Liberty Impact Assessment. Before any new campus policy can take effect, whether it concerns housing, speech events, academic-integrity procedures, dining services, or club recognition, the proposing office must score the policy on three separate axes. The negative-liberty axis measures the number and severity of new enforceable constraints the policy imposes, such as fines, bans, mandatory trainings, curfews, ID checks, or limits on where and when certain activities may occur. The positive-liberty axis measures the policy's expected effect on students' capacity to formulate and pursue their own goals, including whether it supplies skills, resources, information, or real options, and whether it removes internal or environmental obstacles such as financial barriers, inaccessibility, or lack of counseling support. The republican-liberty axis measures the policy's effect on subjection to arbitrary power, operationalized through questions such as who may grant exceptions or reinterpret the rule without student input, how much discretion the text leaves to administrators, and whether affected students can contest a decision through a process that can actually reverse it.

A policy may proceed only if it does not push a weighted aggregate score below a fixed threshold. If it does, the Vice Provost may still certify in writing that no less-restrictive alternative exists, and that certification must be published. Walk through how this procedure would actually operate if it were implemented. What would it incentivize among the administrators who draft the policies, the committee members who assign the scores, and the students and student organizations who learn to use the process? Where would the system be gamed first? Consider, for example, how constraints might be reclassified from one axis to another, how exceptions and discretion might be written into rules to evade the republican-liberty measure, how the composition of the scoring committee could be manipulated, and how the no-less-restrictive-alternative certification might be used as a pressure valve. Where do the three axes pull against one another, and could a single policy score well on all three for opposing reasons? Could the failure modes be fixed by adjusting the weights, the threshold, the appeal process, or the committee's composition, or would such adjustments merely relocate the gaming? Finally, what would the routine operation of this procedure teach its users about the differences among negative, positive, and republican liberty? Please answer in American English.
