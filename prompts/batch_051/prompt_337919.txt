I'm a senior distributed-systems engineer with only a casual background in philosophy of mind. I'd like you to restate a debate about the timing of conscious experience in the vocabulary I actually use day to day—event ordering, happens-before, vector clocks, causal versus wall-clock order, linearizability, serializability, write buffering, deferred commits, read repair, reconciliation and compaction, consistency levels, commit points, and the distinction between a system's own log and the history a client observes—as faithfully as you can. Then I want the residue report: what translated cleanly, what had to be bent to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary.

Here is the material. A subject sees a red dot flashed briefly at one location, then a green dot flashed briefly a short distance away, with suitable timing. The subject reports seeing a single dot move from the first location to the second and change from red to green at the midpoint, as though the green were experienced at the midpoint before the green flash itself was physically presented. Two accounts of this are standard. On the Orwellian account, the system first experiences red and then green, and afterward retroactively edits the record of what was experienced so that the green is recalled at the midpoint; the revision happens after the fact, at the level of memory. On the Stalinesque account, the system buffers the first input, waits for the second, constructs a single coherent sequence, and only then presents it; the revision happens before experience, at the level of perception. Daniel Dennett's familiar deflationary move is that there may be no fact of the matter between these two. Because there is no privileged line separating experience itself from the memory of experience, the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories may be two descriptions of the same underlying process, and the appearance of a dispute comes from insisting that the editing must occur at some definite point in the stream.

Treat the two flashes as events in a distributed system, and treat the subject's report as the result of a read. For each of the three positions—Orwellian, Stalinesque, and Dennett's no-fact-of-the-matter view—give the best engineering analogue you can, precise enough that a colleague could reconstruct it in terms of write ordering, visibility, and commit decisions. If I have mischaracterized any of the philosophical positions, correct me while translating; I care about the ideas, not my wording. Then report what does not survive the translation: which parts of the philosophical debate map onto real engineering choices, and which choices; which parts you could only force across by quietly adding assumptions; and which parts have no counterpart because distributed systems and the first-person character of experience are answering different questions.

Finally, tell me what the residue means. I have a standing intuition that the whole dispute is just a choice of consistency model—buffered writes versus lazy read repair—plus a linearizability story retrofitted onto the read path, and that once it is stated that way the philosophical problem dissolves into an engineering tradeoff. Tell me honestly whether that intuition is right or is missing something, and if it is missing something, what exactly the distributed-systems vocabulary cannot express.
