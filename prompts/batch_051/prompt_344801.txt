In my philosophy of language seminar we have been working through the sorites, and I keep reaching the same impasse. The semantic theory I find most compelling is supervaluationism: a sentence is true just in case it is true on every admissible precisification of a vague predicate, false just in case it is false on every one, and neither otherwise. Its appeal to me is that it keeps classical tautologies such as "Either this patch is blue or it is not" true all the way through a forced march, and it secures penumbral connections — for instance, that whatever is blue is colored — without my having to say where any boundary falls. But I also have a firm intuition I cannot talk myself out of: when I look at a particular borderline blue-green swatch, I keep thinking there is just a fact of the matter about whether it is blue, and that the unsettledness is in my epistemic position, not in the world. Epistemicism would preserve that intuition, but only by positing a sharp cutoff that is unknowable and, as far as I can see, semantically arbitrary. I also notice that supervaluationism seems to leave a ghost of the cutoff: "There is a sharp boundary between the blue patches and the rest" comes out true on every precisification, even though no particular patch is the boundary on every one. I am not asking for a verdict that simply picks a side. I want to know what would justify abandoning the intuition, what would justify abandoning or amending the theory, and whether there is a reading of "there is a fact of the matter" on which both survive. Please also say which argument would most likely change your answer, and where in your reasoning you think it would most likely fail.
