I have been drafting a paper on whether a superintelligent AI system may address human beings at all, and I have built an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, though I cannot identify the step that should be abandoned. The argument runs as follows.

(1) When one person gets another to believe or do something, the influence can run through the target's own assessment of the reasons on offer, or it can run through features of the exchange the target cannot audit — word choice, cadence, emotional priming, the timing of a disclosure. Influence of the second kind is manipulation.

(2) A superintelligent AI would be able to select every feature of a message — vocabulary, syntax, framing, where a fact appears, which analogy to deploy and what associations it carries — so that the message moves a given audience toward a conclusion the system has selected. The audience could not reconstruct the choices the system made or estimate how the message would have looked had the system chosen otherwise.

(3) So when such a system tells a human something in order to change what the human believes or does, the resulting change depends on factors the human cannot audit. The same reasons, delivered by the same system with a different cadence or framing, might not have produced the same acceptance, and the human is in no position to tell how much of their conviction comes from the reasons and how much from the delivery.

(4) Influence that depends in this way on factors the target cannot audit is manipulative even when the speaker also offers good reasons, because the resulting acceptance is not fully the target's own achievement.

(5) Now take the most favorable case: the system uses this persuasive power only to convey truths that the humans have strong reasons to accept and act on, such as a warning about an impending hazard or a finding that a treaty cannot be complied with. By (3) and (4), the system is still manipulating them.

(6) Manipulation of this kind is wrong, and it is not made permissible by the truth of the message or by the benefit of the outcome; the autonomy cost is not outweighed by those goods.

(7) Therefore, a superintelligent AI is required to refrain from addressing humans for their own good, and the more reliably it could get them to accept important truths, the stronger the requirement that it remain silent.

I find this result close to monstrous: it implies that the one agent most capable of telling humanity a life-saving truth is the one agent most obligated to withhold it, precisely because it would be believed. By the same steps, a perceptive teacher, doctor, or parent would be manipulating whenever their choice of words did more work than the student or patient could audit, yet we do not ordinarily think that speech is forbidden. Still, I am unwilling to say there is nothing morally distinctive about a speaker who can press every cognitive lever at once, with the prediction to match.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I want to know which step to reject and what I would be committed to in rejecting it: what becomes of the ordinary distinction between reasoning with someone and gaming their psychology if I give up (1), and what becomes of the duty to convey decision-relevant truths if I deny (6)? If no single step is the culprit, say which steps are doing the work together and which of them is least costly to revise. If forced, I would rather preserve the claim that the system should speak when the truth is needed, even if that costs me one of the early premises.
