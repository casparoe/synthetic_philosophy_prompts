Two people on my team got into a proper row at lunch about something another colleague did, and I couldn't referee it. Dan had paid £45 for a year of theft-and-accidental-damage cover on a £350 phone. The policy's small print put the chance of a total-loss claim in a year at about one in fifty, so the expected payout is around seven quid. Callum said it was plainly irrational: the insurer has to price in profit and running costs, so the cover has negative expected value, and paying a negative-EV premium is just throwing money away. Priya said by that logic all insurance is irrational, since insurers never sell policies at a loss, and pointed out that Callum had renewed the contents insurance on his own flat last month. Callum said those weren't the same thing. Contents insurance is there for a loss big enough to genuinely hurt, where a sensible utility function is curved. But the range from £45 to £350 is pocket money, and over that range utility is basically linear, so diminishing marginal utility can't explain Dan's purchase. What's really doing the work, he said, is that Dan is weighting a 2% chance much more heavily than 2%, and that's exactly the kind of move risk-weighted expected utility makes with its risk function — but that's no longer standard expected utility. Priya said so what, expected utility isn't a neutral test of rationality. Risk-weighted expected utility has a separate parameter for how much a person minds risk precisely because that's a real attitude, not a bug, and as far as she could see Callum was just declaring Dan irrational because the classical theory has nowhere to file his preference.

Could you act as referee? Go through what each of them said in the order they said it and say, for each move, whether it's clearly mistaken, halfway right, or basically right and why. Then give an overall verdict: which of them is closer to being right, and what are they both missing?
