I'm putting together a small philosophy-of-science podcast, and next week's guest is a historian of economic thought who thinks Malthus gets a bad rap. Her line, as she's described it to me, is that Malthus's core claim was right — population tends to outrun the food supply unless something checks it — and that the reason his grim predictions didn't come true on schedule is just that he was early: he couldn't have foreseen fossil-fuel agriculture, synthetic fertilizer, the demographic transition, or the Green Revolution. She likes to say 'right but early.' I want maybe ten interview questions that don't let her rest with that slogan, and each big question should arrive with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

In particular I want the questions to press on: what could count against a theory that doesn't set a deadline for its own predictions; whether piling on post hoc mechanisms like cheap energy and falling birth rates is a genuine rescue of the original theory or just a new theory that keeps the name; whether 'the long run' is doing so much work that the view becomes unfalsifiable; and whether the same defense could be used to keep any failed forecast alive forever. If she says she's predicting constraints rather than events, the follow-up should ask her what evidence would show the constraint was never there. If she says the arithmetic was right but the mechanism changed, ask whether the arithmetic matters once institutions and technology are allowed to respond to scarcity. End with a question about what would make her say, on some concrete horizon, that Malthus was just wrong rather than early again. And I'd like one question that asks whether she'd accept the same 'right but early' defense when it's used for other historically failed predictions, and if not, what the difference is.
