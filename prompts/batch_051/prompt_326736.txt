I'm a journalist who covers climate science and policy, so I sit through a lot of debates about discount rates and intergenerational equity. Lately I keep noticing a move that I don't have a word for, and its driving me slightly crazy. Here's the pattern. Someone starts by saying we shouldn't sacrifice real present benefits for speculative future benefits, because future people will almost certainly be richer than us, so a dollar to them matters less than a dollar to someone now. Fine. Then someone points out that climate damages aren't just about money – they're about lives, ecosystems, dislocation. The first person then shifts and says, look, the particular future people whose lives would be affected wouldn't exist at all if we chose different policies, so no determinate person can claim to be harmed. No one is being wronged; it's just that different policies create different people. And then, once that point has landed, they go back to talking as if future costs and benefits should simply be discounted on a standard economic schedule. The two moves do different work, they rest on different premises, but they get merged into one vague "we can't be expected to weight future people equally" stance.

I was reading Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics the other day, and he says something near where he argues that the mere date at which someone exists is no reason to care less about their happiness, but then he immediately pulls back and says something like: from a practical point of view, the future is different because future people are not here to press their claims, and their very existence may depend on what we do now. I'm paraphrasing, not quoting, because I don't have the book in front of me. But what struck me is that Sidgwick seems to hold both thoughts at once without marking them as two separate arguments, and modern climate debate does the same thing.

So my question: is there an established name for this specific double move in intergenerational ethics – first treating future people as if they're just like us but poorer or richer, then treating them as if they're not determinate individuals at all, and switching between those frames when it's convenient? I know about the non-identity problem, and I know about pure time preference and discounting. But I'm after the exact phenomenon of toggling between those two justifications while talking as if it's a single consistent position about the future. If it has a name, what are its edges? If it doesn't, could you give it a careful definition and say how it differs from neighboring concepts? I don't want just a label – I want to know what conditions make it a fallacy, whether it's always a fallacy, and whether there are versions where the two frames are legitimately integrated rather than just swapped.
