For the past several years I have covered the neuroscience of volition for a general readership, and there is an interpretive move in that literature that I have never been able to name to my own satisfaction. In the classic experiment, a participant is asked to flex a wrist whenever the urge arises and to note the moment of that urge. Scalp electrodes record a slow electrical buildup that begins a few hundred milliseconds before the reported urge, and commentators routinely describe this as the brain's having decided before the person did. Yet the participant had already formed the only intention that mattered when they agreed to follow the instructions and prepared to wait for the impulse; the later choice is a staged, low-stakes release of an existing plan. The same slide appears when a model can decode, above chance but imperfectly, which of two buttons a subject will press a second or two before the subject reports making the choice: the early neural signal is treated as the real decision, and the conscious report becomes an after-the-fact story.

I am not looking for the name of the general position that such results debunk free will. I want to identify a narrower inferential step: taking evidence of nonconscious preparation for an action and reading it as evidence that the conscious decision was causally idle, as if an early component of the action, or the timing of a report, amounted to the whole act of deciding. Does this step have a settled name in the philosophical or neuroscientific literature? If it does, which of the two cases above does it genuinely cover, and which does it merely resemble? If it does not, I would like the carving more than the coinage: what exactly the phenomenon is, where its boundary lies, and how it differs from the related charge that such experiments mistake a part of the brain's activity for the whole person's acting. I would rather leave it unnamed than get a label without an account of its edges.
