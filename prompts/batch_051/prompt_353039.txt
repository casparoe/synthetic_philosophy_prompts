I spent my professional life as a microbiologist, working chiefly on how infectious agents pass through populations, and in retirement I have begun reading philosophy in earnest. Lately I have been working through Tolstoy's What Is Art?, and I keep noticing how much weight his account places on a single analogy: that art infects. His central claim, as I understand it, is that art is a transaction of feeling. The artist has genuinely lived through a feeling and deliberately externalizes it in a set of signs—words, sound, paint, gesture. The audience, encountering those signs, takes on that same feeling; Tolstoy calls this process infection and makes it the criterion of art as such. A work is art in the strict sense only insofar as it infects. Its degree of infectiousness depends, on his telling, on the individuality of the feeling, the clarity with which it is expressed, and above all the sincerity of the artist. He then introduces a second distinction: among works that are art in this strict sense, good art transmits feelings that draw human beings together—in his late view, feelings in keeping with a sincere religious understanding of what human life is for—while bad art transmits feelings that divide or degrade. Works that merely imitate, aim at effect, or excite interest he sets aside as counterfeits of art rather than bad art, because they are composed without a feeling the artist has actually lived and are not built to transmit one.

The infection language is not ornamental here; it is carrying conclusions. It is what allows Tolstoy to draw the boundary between art and counterfeit art, and it underwrites his claim that the moral worth of a work is a matter of what it passes from one person to another. I would like you to audit this analogy the way one would audit a model in my old field. Please set out: (1) a correspondence table, as explicit as you can make it—what plays the role of the pathogen, the route of transmission, the vector or fomite, the susceptible host, the infectious dose, and any further biological features that deserve counterparts; (2) the points where the mapping breaks, attending both to what the biological picture quietly imports into the aesthetic case that may not belong there and to what the aesthetic case has that the biological picture cannot represent; (3) a judgment as to which disanalogies matter for the specific work Tolstoy wants the analogy to do—the art/counterfeit boundary, and the further claim that the moral value of art is tied to what it infects us with—and which are merely incidental. If the analogy fails precisely where it is carrying weight, I want to know whether Tolstoy's conclusions can be restated in a non-biological vocabulary without loss, or whether they were being supported by the metaphor all along. If a better comparison suggests itself once this one has been audited, say what it is and indicate both what it can carry and what it cannot.
