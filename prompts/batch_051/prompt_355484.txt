I am a novelist rather than an academic philosopher, and I have been invited to give a paper at a university philosophy department where several of the faculty already know my fiction. The paper defends a modest reading of the Sartrean claim that existence precedes essence. My thesis is that, properly understood, the claim is not that human beings have no nature in any biological or descriptive sense, but that for a self-interpreting agent, the person one becomes is explained by what one chooses and commits to, rather than being discovered in advance as a fixed particular nature. I intend to argue that the standard charge of incoherence, that a thing cannot exist before it is something in particular, depends on conflating two senses of essence: a bare descriptive sense concerning the properties an entity has, and a practical sense concerning the normative identity an agent takes up. I also want to show that this reading leaves the thesis plausible without turning it into a trivial point about human variability.

The audience is fairly mixed, and I expect the difficulty to come from four corners. One faculty member works on Aristotelian essentialism and takes kind-membership to be metaphysically fundamental. Another is a Kantian who suspects the paper confuses autonomy with the absence of a rational nature. A third works in the philosophy of action and will press on the relationship between reasons, character, and choice. And the chair is the sort of reader who thinks a Continental slogan earns its keep only if it can be paraphrased without loss. My argument, in brief, is this: the slogan should be read as a claim about the direction of explanation within practical identity; the natural facts about a person are the raw material a fundamental project has to take up, not the finished essence that settles in advance what she is; and the appearance of contradiction comes from treating practical identity as if it were an item in the inventory of descriptive facts. Write me a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper. Give me the objections I will actually get from this group, arranged by roughly how likely and how damaging each will be, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them. For each, tell me what to concede, what to hold, and a two-minute reply, and note where the reply is weakest. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks.
