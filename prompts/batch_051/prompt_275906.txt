I'm a political theory PhD student, and I've developed a habit I can't tell if its sophisticated or just a bias with a fancier name. On contested policy questions, I heavily discount the stated positions and arguments of elected politicians — even the ones I otherwise respect, and especially the ones up for re-election — and I give a lot of weight to the recommendations of randomized citizens' assemblies: the setups where a few dozen non-politicians are selected by lot (sortition), hear from experts and advocates for a few weekends, and then produce a report. I also catch myself trusting term-limited or retiring politicians more than ones who face another election, which is probably the same instinct. My logic: career politicians have re-election incentives, party discipline, and press incentives shaping what they say, so their public arguments about, say, drug policy or climate policy are a decent guide to coalition politics but a terrible guide to what actually follows from the evidence. Sortition breaks that link, at least roughly. The assembly members aren't defending a career or a party line, so I treat their recommendations as closer to genuine deliberative judgment than anything a government spokesperson says.

My supervisor says I've built an inverse genetic fallacy: I'm judging claims by how their authors were selected rather than by the arguments themselves. And he points out that citizens' assemblies are small, so any given assembly can be far off the population by accident; they're also easily captured by whoever sets the agenda, writes the briefing materials, and picks the experts. I see the force of that. But elected bodies are also captured, also unrepresentative in their own way, and selected by a process that filters for people who are good at getting re-elected — which seems worse to me. So my question isn't whether sortition is wonderful, but whether my epistemic policy is defensible for someone like me: a PhD student with finite time and no ability to redo the policy analysis myself.

Is it rational to treat a citizens' assembly's recommendation as stronger evidence than an elected legislature's position? Under what assumptions about how errors correlate, how agenda-setting works, and how much genuine deliberation survives into a final report would that rule be defensible? What would a defensible version of the rule look like — one that says when to defer to a sortition body and when not — and how would I tell from the inside whether I'm tracking deliberative quality or just using 'randomly selected' as a prestige cue? I don't need citations or a bibliography, just the reasoning. Oh, and for the dataset I'm building, please end with a last line that begins 'Verdict:' and gives a short phrase saying whether the habit is defensible as is, defensible only in a cleaned-up form, or not defensible.
