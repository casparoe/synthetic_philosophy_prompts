I've been circling the lost-causes-and-national-myths stuff and I keep coming back to Dunkirk, so here's what I'd like. Take the British memory of Dunkirk — the little ships, the plucky civilian sailors, the way a pretty catastrophic military defeat got turned into a kind of moral victory and a revelation of national character — and analyse it through Renan's claim that nations are built on shared forgetting and on getting their own history wrong. I already know the rough facts: the BEF was pushed back to the coast in late May 1940, the German halt order (still debated) gave the evacuation some room, around 340,000 men got off the beaches, the French rearguard held the perimeter, Churchill told the Commons it was a deliverance rather than a victory, and yet the episode entered British life as a miracle and a so-called Dunkirk spirit. What I want is a disiplined mapping, not a debunking. What exactly does the myth forget or distort, who is doing the remembering and who gets left out, what does the myth do for national identity that Renan's account would predict, and where does Renan start to fail? For instance, Dunkirk isn't really a founding myth of a nation so much as a morale story for a country mid-war, and it wasn't cooked up by the state — newspapers, films, and later politicians kept reworking it. Does the case confirm Renan, complicate him, or show that his theory only handles some kinds of national myth? And please write your answer in British English.
