I am a novelist, and I have been drafting a novel whose central figure is a former biblical scholar who ruined himself by uncovering ever more elaborate numerical patterns in scripture. In learning to write him without simply condescending to him, I developed a criterion I would like examined on its merits.

I call it the closed-count criterion. A number in a scriptural text does genuine interpretive work, rather than being an artifact of pattern-hunting, only when three conditions hold together. First, the number belongs to a count the text itself performs or marks off — days, years, entries in a list, a repeated gesture — rather than being assembled by a reader from occurrences scattered across unrelated passages. Second, its significance is generated by the structure surrounding it, as the seventh day takes its meaning from the six days of work that precede it, rather than being imported from a general table of number meanings. Third, the numerical reading must resolve something already puzzling in the passage — an awkward detail, a repetition, a missing explanation — so that the number explains the text instead of merely decorating it.

The criterion is meant to separate the number-symbolism a careful reader should respect from long-range pattern-hunting: the practice of collecting every occurrence of, say, forty across unrelated books, computing sums and ratios, and treating joint frequency as if frequency were meaning. I suspect the latter is an artifact of the counting itself, not a property of the text.

I would like a candid assessment. Is the criterion coherent, or does it crack under pressure? Has something close to it already been worked out under an established name? What does it correctly exclude, and what does it wrongly exclude? Please give me the strongest scriptural counterexample you can produce, and tell me whether the criterion can absorb it or must be abandoned.
