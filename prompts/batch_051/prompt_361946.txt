You are a philosopher of science who works on the epistemology of climate model ensembles and on decision-making under deep uncertainty. Think through the question carefully, and before you evaluate my policy, brainstorm the conditions under which a multi-model mean would be a principled summary at all.

I work as an analyst in a regional water authority, where I help decide how much reservoir storage and flood-protection capacity to build. We commission downscaled projections from a multi-model climate ensemble: about twenty model runs from several modeling groups. The models come from different institutions, but they are not fully independent—many share components, parameterization schemes, and historical calibration targets, and the ensemble was not designed as a random sample of possible models. My standing policy has been to take the multi-model mean as my best estimate of the future climate, to treat the spread around that mean as roughly a confidence interval, and to set aside the extreme high-end projections as outliers that would only distort planning. My rationale is that averaging many imperfect estimates cancels errors better than trusting any single model would, and that I cannot evaluate the merits of individual models myself.

A colleague who works on climate risk tells me I have turned a convenient summary statistic into an epistemic rule. The ensemble mean, she says, can inherit correlated errors from shared model code and shared calibration targets; the spread is not a probability distribution over future outcomes; and the tail members I set aside may be exactly the information that matters for an authority planning against severe droughts and floods. I do not want to overbuild, but I also do not want to hide behind spurious precision. Is my shortcut defensible? Under what conditions—independence, unbiasedness, exchangeability, or others—would the ensemble mean actually be the right summary? When those assumptions fail, is there a restated policy that keeps the benefits of the shortcut without ignoring tail risk? And should robustness—agreement across structurally different models—be doing work for me that the mean and spread cannot do? Please frame the issue as one about the epistemology of a heuristic, and say clearly whether I am using a defensible rule or dressing up an aversion to uncertainty as a rule about evidence.
