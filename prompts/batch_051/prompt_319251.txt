I've been trying to sort out whether every statement has to be true or false, and my gut gives me a bunch of verdicts that I can't seem to fit under one rule. Going case by case:

- "France is a monarchy" is just false. France is a republic, so there's a fact and the sentence gets it wrong.
- "The present king of France is bald" is neither true nor false. It's not false the way "France is a monarchy" is false; the whole thing misfires because the subject doesn't exist.
- "Sam is bald," said about a man who's right at the hairy edge of baldness so the word could go either way, is neither true nor false. There's just no fact about where the cutoff falls exactly.
- "There will be a sea battle tomorrow," said tonight when neither outcome is settled by how things currently are, is neither true nor false. The future hasn't picked one yet.
- But "either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't be," said the same night, seems true — maybe even necessarily true. I know that sits uneasily with the previous bullet.
- "Goldbach's conjecture is true" is already either true or false, even if nobody ever proves it. My not knowing doesn't make a gap.
- "The number seven is green" is neither true nor false; it's not even the right kind of thing to be false.
- The liar sentence, "this sentence is false," isn't true, but I can't call it false either, since calling it false makes it true. It just doesn't get a normal truth value.

What I want to know is whether there's one principle — or maybe a small set of candidate principles — that captures all of this. It has to say what the gap cases have in common, and what seperates them from the just-false case and the unproven-but-determined Goldbach case. It also has to explain how the sea-battle disjunction can be true while neither disjunct is true. If no single principle gets all the verdicts, I want to know which ones it forces me to abandon, and why those are the least defensible ones. Then apply the principle to two more cases I'm genuinely undecided on: (1) "There is intelligent life elsewhere in the universe" — should I treat that as already true-or-false, or as gappy until we find out? And (2) inside a novel, a claim about a minor character's favorite color when the text never says anything about it — true, false, or neither within the fiction? I'd like a precisly stated principle, with the case or clause that motivates each part, rather than just a vibe.
