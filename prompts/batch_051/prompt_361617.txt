I've been trying to pin down what it means to understand a target system, as distinct from reliably getting the right answers by deferring to a tool or another person. The criteria I've converged on: (1) you can give a mechanistic explanation of why the system produces its outputs, not merely predict those outputs; (2) you can say in advance how a novel change or failure would propagate, before running the experiment; (3) you can recombine pieces of your account to answer unfamiliar what-if questions, rather than only recognizing previously encountered cases; (4) your performance survives interference — remove the tool's scaffolding, change notation or surface features, add time pressure — and you can still recover the key moves. Now the borderline case. A developer maintains a medium-sized service largely through an LLM assistant. Unaided, they cannot write the service from scratch, but they can give an accurate overview of each module, read a stack trace once the assistant points at it, routinely catch the assistant's suggestions when those suggestions are semantically or syntactically wrong for this codebase, and have fixed many small bugs by asking targeted follow-up questions. When a novel failure crosses module boundaries, though, they have no prior sense of where to look and simply iterate by prompting. Does this developer understand the service? Work through the criteria one by one, flag where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether the case is really testing whether understanding belongs to the person, the person-plus-tool system, or is a matter of degree. Finally, say whether criterion (4) is redundant — because surviving interference may just be the operational test of actually possessing the mechanisms described in (1)–(3) — or whether it captures something the others miss.
