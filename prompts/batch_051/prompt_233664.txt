I am a novelist working on a scene in which two characters try to settle whether their own senses can be trusted by appealing to each other's agreement. I want the epistemology in the scene to be exact rather than impressionistic, so I am working through a variant of a standard problem instead of relying on the vague intuition that two witnesses are better than one.

Take the standard easy-knowledge and bootstrapping problem in its perceptual form. A subject looks at a red table in ordinary light, acquires the belief that the table is red through vision, and then—on the basis of that belief alone, or a short track record of such beliefs—concludes that her color vision is reliable. The standard objection is that the inference is viciously circular: the deliverances of a faculty are being used to certify the very faculty that produced them, with no independent check.

Now vary one feature of the case: add a second observer and move the moment of the reliability inference. Let A and B stand before the same red table. Each first forms the belief that the table is red from her own visual experience alone. Only afterward do they compare notes, each taking the other's report as sincere. After A learns that B saw the table as red too, A infers that her own color vision is reliable; B does the same. A's evidence for her own reliability now includes something she did not produce with the faculty under test—B's report—yet B's report itself rests on B's equally uncertified vision.

Analyze the two-observer variant. Does the added report break the bootstrapping circle, or merely widen it? Take into account that each receives the other's report through further perceptual and linguistic faculties that have likewise not been independently certified. Work through how the main competing diagnoses of the original case would handle this variant: (i) the view that the problem is generated by closure of knowledge under known entailment; (ii) the view that perceptual justification is immediate but cannot by itself underwrite a conclusion about the reliability of the faculty; (iii) a reliabilist view on which a process earns its credentials through a track record of true outputs. For each, say whether it condemns the inference, permits it, or requires modification. Then consider whether adding a third, fourth, or nth observer would eventually change the answer, and if not, explain why sheer numbers of agreeing observers do not reach the structure of the problem. Conclude by classifying the two-observer case: is it bootstrapping, a legitimate use of consensus as evidence, or a third kind of case?
