I'm in a political theory class this semester, and we've been doing a unit on the Simone Weil argument that political parties are basically machines for replacing judgment with loyalty, so they should be done away with entirely. Next week I'm the one moderating the Q&A for a visiting lecturer who defends a modernized version of this, and I don't want to just ask soft setup questions.

Her version, from the talk abstract and an earlier class visit: a party isn't just any association; it's an organization whose first commitment is to its own survival and growth, and it trains members to let a collective "we" do their thinking. Once you're inside one, she says, the test for whether an idea is good becomes "does it serve the party," which is a stand-in for asking whether it's true or just. Even radical parties become conservative in this very specific way, because the machine wants to persist no matter what its platform says. She then extends this to party-like things on campus—student political orgs with a name and a platform, movements that behave like brands—and says they should be replaced by temporary single-issue coalitions that disband once the issue is settled.

She's annoyingly good at deflecting structural objections. Her usual move is something like "that's just a nicer way to run the machine, not a reason to keep it" or "the fact that you're trying to fix the party shows it's already thinking for you."

Write me a sequence of six or seven questions, in increasing order of how hard they make that deflection to use, that would actually corner her on the mechanisms she's invoking, not just on the examples she happens to dislike. Each question should press on the real content of the position: for each one, anticipate the reply she'd likely give and give me the follow-up question that blocks that reply. The final one should definetely force her to either name a precise limiting condition or say what would have to be true, in observable terms, for her to revise the view.

For the format: give me a JSON object with two parts. First, a "questions" array, where each element has these fields: "question" (the actual question I can read aloud), "target" (one sentence on what mechanism or premise this is meant to expose), "likely_reply" (her probable move), "follow_up" (the prepared follow-up after that reply), and "risk" (a way the question could let her off the hook, and how I avoid it). Second, a "reading_notes" array, where each element has "point", "status", and "why": for anything the questions lean on that's an interpretation of Weil rather than the lecturer's own extension, flag whether it's settled in the text or genuinely contested among people who read Weil carefully, and then say why. And please don't present a contested reading like it's just what Weil obviously thought.
