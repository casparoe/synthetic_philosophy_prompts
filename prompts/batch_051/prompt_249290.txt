I'm the research director at a small political-risk shop that sells quarterly regime-durability notes to institutional investors. Each quarter our analysts have to assign each of about forty countries a probability that the current government or ruling coalition survives the next five years. If we're wrong in either direction clients loose money and cancel; if we put out 'no number' too often they unsubscribe too. The analysts are smart but not trained in statistics, and they keep slipping back into writing country narratives instead of forecasts. I've read a bit about the outside view and reference class forecasting—the idea that you should start from the base rate of how long comparable regimes or empires actually lasted before adjusting for case specifics—but I can't figure out how to turn that into something my people can follow without it becoming either mechanical nonsense or a blank check for their pet analogies.

Give me a step-by-step procedure, in the form of a checklist or tests to run in order, for taking a country and producing a five-year survival probability from historical base rates of comparable regimes or empires, then adjusting for case-specific factors. I especially need the procedure to handle the reference-class problem: how to pick the right comparison class, what to do when plausible classes give very different base rates, and how to know when the historical sample is too small to justify a number at all. Also say what counts as 'survival' and 'collapse' for our purposes, and what the procedure cannot do. Work it through for three cases: a Gulf monarchy, a post-Soviet personalist regime, and one where the procedure pushes out an answer our analysts won't like but the method says is right. Flag the places where judgment is unavoidable and how an analyst can deviate if they get sign-off from me.
