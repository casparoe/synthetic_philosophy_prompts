I have been trying to adjudicate a disagreement between Schopenhauer and Nietzsche about the moral status of compassion, and I keep getting stuck because neither philosopher could have considered a body of evidence that now seems directly relevant. Schopenhauer held that compassion is the one genuinely moral motive: in compassion, another person's suffering becomes the immediate and disinterested object of my will, and all actions of genuine moral worth ultimately arise from compassion rather than from abstract duty or self-interest. Morality, he thought, can therefore be grounded in something human beings actually feel. Nietzsche regarded this as a dangerous mistake. On his account, pity looks generous but is mostly a disguised pathology: it adds the spectator's own suffering to the sufferer's, quietly feeds the pitying person's sense of superiority or virtue, and teaches people to value relief from pain above strength and self-overcoming.

Now consider a development that postdates both philosophers. Moral psychology has accumulated evidence of an identifiable victim effect and of psychic numbing: people respond far more intensely to a single named or pictured victim than to knowledge that many people are suffering, and compassion tracks salience and proximity rather than the moral weight of the need. It is also well documented that repeated exposure to others' pain often produces numbness, avoidance, or compassion fatigue rather than durable moral motivation. These results look like empirical confirmation of the kind of pathology Nietzsche described, though they might equally be read as damaging only a stronger version of Schopenhauer's view than the one he actually held.

Does Schopenhauer's argument that compassion is the basis of morality survive this development? Identify the particular premise of his argument that the findings most directly bear on, and say whether the argument is dead, can be repaired, or was never committed to the premise in question. If a repair is possible, state the strongest version of it, for example by distinguishing compassion as an immediate affect from compassion as a commitment that can be trained and deliberately extended, or by treating compassion as a valuable but corrigible heuristic that must be checked against impartial principles. If you think the findings vindicate Nietzsche instead, explain what, if anything, should replace compassion in ethics.
