I've been reading around in Kant and I'm kind of sold on the machinery: moral standing comes from rational nature, so you can have direct duties only to persons, and whatever duties we have about animals are really duties to ourselves — cruelty is wrong because it coarsens your sympathy and makes you more likely to mistreat people, not because it wrongs the animal. The clean criterion appeals to me. But I also have a completely firm intuition that if someone were systematically cruel to a dog in total isolation, and we somehow knew it would never coarsen him or spill over into how he treats any person, he'd still be doing something wrong — and wrong because of what he's doing to the dog, not just because of the effect on himself. I dont want to abandon the Kantian view over one case, but I can't shake the intuition either. Walk me through what a Kantian should say here. Is there a reading of the view that puts the wrongness in the right place without giving up the rational-nature criterion, or does the view really force me to say that the dog is owed nothing and only the abuser's own character matters? And whichever way it goes, what should a clean resolution look like — should the intuition be debunked, the theory amended, or is there a third reading?
