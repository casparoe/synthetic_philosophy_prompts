I have been teaching an undergraduate seminar on historical analogy as a form of political argument, and the example students keep reaching for is the fall of the Roman Republic as a lens on the present. The problem is less that nothing interesting gets said than that the same broad gestures recur — a Gracchi moment here, a Sulla there, a vague warning about Caesar — with no settled standard for what would make any of it good rather than merely evocative. Before I assign the final round of readings, I want the specification of what an adequate analogy actually has to do.

Write the desiderata: what any intellectually respectable account of a modern democracy's fragility that uses the collapse of the Roman Republic as its governing analogy must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Cover, without turning the answer into a history essay, the historical sequences any user of the analogy must be able to confront rather than cherry-pick around (the Gracchan tribunates, the Social War and the extension of citizenship, Sulla's march on Rome, the crossing of the Rubicon, the consolidation of one-man rule under Augustus); the disanalogies it may not silently pass over (such as Rome's restricted and stratified citizen body, its slave economy, and its situation of near-continuous Mediterranean warfare); and the distinctions it cannot collapse — between diagnosis, prediction, and prophecy; between formal institutional change and the erosion of norms; between causes modern democracies might genuinely share with Rome and causes local to the Roman case. I also want included a requirement that the analogy state in advance which modern developments would count as breaking the parallel, so it cannot be protected from failure in hindsight. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular interpretive camp. Then identify which single requirement, if enforced strictly, would disqualify most popular Rome analogies, and say whether any two requirements cannot be jointly satisfied. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases motivating them rather than on scoring particular authors; the list is what I want to teach from.
