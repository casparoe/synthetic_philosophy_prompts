In my decision-theory course, we have been working through coherence arguments for expected-utility maximization. The readings run roughly as follows: an agent whose degrees of belief or preferences violate the probability axioms or the expected-utility axioms can be offered a set of bets or a sequence of trades, each acceptable in its own right, that jointly leave the agent with an outcome they themselves rank lower than an available alternative — a Dutch book or money pump. One camp in the seminar takes these results to show that expected-utility maximization is a constitutive requirement of rationality. The other camp says this overstates the result: the arguments show only that certain preference profiles are exploitable under idealized, possibly question-begging conditions (a bookie with no superior information, utility linear enough in money, no re-evaluation of earlier trades, and a sequence arranged so that the independence axiom does the work). They also insist that the representation theorem — every agent who satisfies the axioms can be represented as maximizing expected utility — does not by itself show that anyone rationally ought to satisfy the axioms. Write a dialogue between two equally competent characters, one defending each position, in which they respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another and work out as precisely as possible what the coherence arguments can and cannot establish. Keep the dialogue under 800 words.
