I keep encountering the same fork in conversations about love, and I would like to know whether the fork itself has a name in philosophy, along with a clean way of drawing it. Here is the pattern. One person, asked why she loves her partner, answers by citing his qualities: he is patient, he is funny, he is steady in a crisis. She seems to treat those qualities as what justifies the love, roughly the way evidence justifies a belief; but if you press her and ask whether she would have reason to switch to someone with more of the same qualities, she resists without being able to say why. A second person says this gets love backwards: the loving is not a response to value already there in the beloved; it is what makes those qualities matter to you in the first place, and asking what there is to love about him before you love him is like asking for the grounds of an affection you have not yet formed. A third person rejects both. She does not think love is justified by a person's transferable good qualities, but she also denies that it is groundless or self-created; for her the reasons are relational and historical, attached to the particular fact that this is the person with whom you have built something over time, reasons that would not apply to a stranger with the identical qualities. So I seem to have three positions: love grounded in the beloved's properties, love as conferring importance on the beloved, and love grounded in the relationship's history. Does the first contrast have an established name, and where does the third view sit in relation to it? I am less interested in a label than in the carving: which of these positions are genuinely distinct, whether the second is really a thesis about justification at all, and what test would sort an unfamiliar account into one box rather than another.
