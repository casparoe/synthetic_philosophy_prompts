Consider a proposed internal rule for a state intermediate appellate court with at least a dozen judges. In any case whose outcome turns on the meaning of a statute, the following procedure applies. After the three-judge panel has circulated a tentative majority draft, the clerk's office randomly selects one judge of the court who did not sit on the panel to write a "counter-method opinion." If the tentative draft reasons chiefly from statutory text, the assigned judge must write the strongest purposivist opinion the same record can support; if the tentative draft reasons chiefly from legislative purpose or history, the assigned judge must write the strongest textualist opinion. The counter-method opinion is capped at half the length of the tentative majority draft, is published directly after the final majority opinion, and has no precedential force. The panel's final opinion, however, must include a short response section explaining why the counter-method opinion does not change the result. Later courts may read counter-method opinions, but litigants may cite them only as persuasive discussion, not as authority.

Analyze how this rule would actually behave if adopted, rather than whether it is a good idea in the abstract. In particular, address what it would incentivize for panel judges, for the judge assigned to write the counter-method opinion, for litigants, and for later courts; how each group would game, soften, or neutralize the rule; where the rule would most likely fail; and what second-order effects it would have on opinion-writing, doctrine, the court's internal politics, and the public's understanding of statutory decisions. Conclude by considering whether the main failure modes could be fixed without abandoning the mechanism, and which defects are probably irreparable.

Please structure the answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. Operating logic (about 150 words): the basic change the rule makes to the workflow and incentives of the court.
2. Strategic behavior and gaming (about 250 words): how panel judges, counter-method judges, litigants, and later courts would adapt.
3. Failure modes (about 250 words): the most serious ways the rule would misfire or be neutralized.
4. Second-order effects (about 200 words): consequences for doctrine, opinion-writing culture, internal court politics, and public perception.
5. Repairability (about 150 words): which defects can be fixed within the mechanism and which cannot.

Write the answer in American English.
