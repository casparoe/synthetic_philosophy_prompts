I have recently taken over the restoration of a neglected teaching garden, and the planning disputes have forced me back onto distinctions I had not thought about since first reading Aristotle: should we restore the original materials, the original layout, or the original purpose? These are plainly different questions, and I have come to suspect that they matter far beyond gardening, because they track something deep about what explanation requires. I would like you to argue for a definite position in this vicinity. In particular, argue that Aristotle's four causes are best understood not as four competing kinds of cause, from which a modern scientist must choose, but as four different explanatory factors answering four different questions: what a thing is made of, how it is organized, what produced it, and what it is for. So understood, the final cause was not eliminated by the rise of mechanism or by natural selection; it was transformed into the idea of a function that arises within a self-maintaining population. Argue, further, that final-causal statements in biology—for example, the claim that the heart is for pumping blood—cannot be reduced without remainder to efficient-causal statements about selection, development, or present causal capacities. Defend this against at least one strong objection, such as the objection that natural selection is blind and mechanical and therefore can yield no genuine talk of what something is for.
