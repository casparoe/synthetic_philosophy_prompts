I'm a graduate student leading a fifty-minute discussion section for a second-year epistemology course. The students have just spent two weeks on Descartes's First Meditation, and they have absorbed the idea that a serious answer to external-world skepticism must either establish that the senses are reliable or justify our belief in the external world by an inference from sense-experiences that somehow survives skeptical doubt. Next week I have to teach Moore's proof of an external world: Moore holds up each hand in turn, points out that here is one hand and here is another, and draws the conclusion that he has proved the existence of at least two external things — and he insists that the premises are more certain than anything the skeptic could cite against them and the inference is fully rigorous. I don't want to explain the argument at them; I want to plan a session that makes them see why this is a serious philosophical move rather than a joke, a refusal to engage, or a changed subject. Plan my session. What has to be in place before Moore's proof can even register as a move in the dialectic rather than as failing to answer the problem as they have been taught to understand it? What single in-class gesture or example should carry the whole explanation, and exactly what must it have and what must it lack? Which misconceptions will the students predictably bring or form — for instance, that Moore is simply assuming the external world, that he is using 'know' in a weaker or merely practical sense, or that he is begging the question by treating his hands as evidence — and at which point in the sequence should I head each one off? What should I openly leave out at this level? And what question can I ask in the final five minutes to check that they have understood what Moore is doing rather than merely been entertained by it?
