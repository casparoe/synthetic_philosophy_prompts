I'm writing a short piece on the ethics of sweatshops and I keep going back and forth on whether my own commitments actually fit together. Before I show it to anyone I want an audit of the set. Here's what I find myself wanting to say, more or less all at once:

(a) I basically accept the non-worseness idea: if a transaction is consensual and leaves both parties better off than their next-best option, then it isn't morally worse than not transacting at all, and that counts strongly in favour of its being permissable.

(b) Sweatshop labour is very often wrong even when workers choose it and are better off than they would be outside the factory.

(c) Exploitation isn't really about paying below some objective just price or fair wage; it's about taking unfair advantage of someone's vulnerability.

(d) A choice isn't coerced merely because the alternatives are terrible. So the fact that a worker's fallback is extreme poverty doesn't by itself make their consent invalid.

(e) A company acts wrongly when it deliberately sites production in countries with weak labour laws precisely because workers there are desperate enough to accept very low pay.

(f) But I don't think an individual consumer does anything wrong by buying a sweatshirt made in one of those factories. One purchase makes no difference to anything, and anyway the worker would be worse off without the sale.

Where am I actually inconsistent, where do I only appear inconsistent, and what is the least costly revision? Please order the tensions by how serious they are rather than treating them as all equally bad.
