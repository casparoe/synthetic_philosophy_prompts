I am a PhD student in bioethics working with a university-affiliated fertility clinic on a revision of its genetic counseling protocols. The clinic currently offers preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy and for a panel of serious monogenic conditions such as cystic fibrosis, and it is deciding whether to extend counseling to polygenic risk scores for conditions like type 2 diabetes or major depression and to other predicted characteristics such as height or educational attainment. The medical director wants the new protocol organized around the principle of procreative beneficence: when prospective parents are already selecting among possible future children, they have a moral reason, other things being equal, to select the child who can be expected to have the best life, or at least not to select one expected to have a worse life. At the same time, the clinic is committed to a liberal eugenics framework: no coercion of patients, no single substantive conception of the good, genuine deference to parental autonomy, and explicit acknowledgment of the eugenic history of reproductive genetics.

Please translate procreative beneficence into a reusable procedure—an ordered checklist or a flowchart in words—that a genetic counselor can actually run. The counselor conducts six to eight of these consultations a week; each pre-test consultation lasts roughly twenty-five minutes; the counselor has no formal philosophical training; and any procedure must be defensible to patients who push back and must be approved by the clinic's ethics board before implementation. The stakes are high: the transfer decision determines which possible child comes to exist, and the clinic cannot afford to be seen as drifting toward coercive eugenics. The procedure should specify, in order, what the counselor asks; which information rises to the level of a recommendation, a neutral mention, or something the counselor should not raise unless the patient does; when the principle's conditions are met and when they are not; and which steps are judgment calls that the procedure cannot eliminate.

I want the procedure to face a specific tension squarely. Procreative beneficence appears to require at least a partial shared standard of a better or worse life, while liberal eugenics is supposed to remain neutral among rival conceptions of the good. The procedure should therefore say whether it can rank embryos by expected welfare without importing a substantive view of what makes a life worth living. It should also mark the difference between selecting against a serious disease and selecting for a probabilistic enhancement, since the clinic's lawyers worry about treating polygenic scores as if they were diagnostic tests. Finally, the protocol must not quietly rely on the claim that selecting an embryo with lower expected welfare harms the resulting child, because a different selection would have created a different child altogether; if the procedure uses the language of harm or of doing what is best for your future child, it should explain how that language survives this point.

Work the procedure through three cases: a couple using PGT because they carry a cystic fibrosis variant who asks whether they should also use polygenic scores to choose the embryo with the lowest predicted risk of major depression; a Deaf couple who requests selection of an embryo carrying a variant associated with congenital deafness; and a couple who asks to rank their embryos by a polygenic score for educational attainment. Include at least one case in which the procedure yields an answer that I or the clinic will be reluctant to accept, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do—what it cannot settle about the value of a disabled life, the limits of parental discretion, or the difference between beneficence and eugenics.
