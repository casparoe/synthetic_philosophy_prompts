At the AI lab where I work, two engineers, Mira and Jonah, have been arguing for weeks about a training method our team has adopted for an assistant that writes long policy analyses. Under the method, each analysis is decomposed into a chain of small steps—individual factual claims, calculations, and lines of argument—and a human reviewer approves or rejects each step before the model proceeds. The approved chains then become training data, so the model learns to reproduce the pattern of micro-decisions the reviewers endorsed.

Mira defends the method this way: people are far better at judging whether one small step follows from the preceding ones than at judging whether a long analysis is sound as a whole, so replacing one vague, noisy holistic judgment with many crisp local judgments can only reduce error. Jonah replies that this compares the wrong things. What the team wants is a sound final analysis, but the local judgments certify only that each piece looks plausible in isolation, and a sequence of individually plausible pieces can add up to an analysis no one would endorse as a whole. Even apart from reviewer fallibility, he adds, the probability that a long chain harbors at least one undetected error grows with the number of steps, however low the per-step error rate is. Mira answers that if each step is approved, then the conclusion is reached by individually sanctioned transitions, and rejecting the result is like accepting every premise of an argument while denying its conclusion. Jonah says that inference assumes human approval is closed under composition, but it is not: reviewers are fallible and especially prone to approving a step because it sounds reasonable, and the accumulated effect of many such passes is how a model drifts away from what any single reviewer would have wanted. Mira concedes the fallibility but says the reply cuts both ways—holistic judgments are surely at least as fallible, so the fix is more rigorous local checks, not a return to an unreliable whole-answer judgment. Jonah counters that more rigorous local checks miss the main objection: a chain of locally acceptable steps can still be globally bad in ways no local check could register, and a model trained on these approvals may learn to produce steps that secure approval rather than steps that track the quality of the whole analysis.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. Say which specific claims are right, which are overstated, and identify the deeper issue about whether competence and alignment survive decomposition that the two are circling.
