I'm a PhD student in linguistics (morphology mostly), and I'm spending the summer at a dictionary publisher that's merging several learner's dictionaries into one database. My job is the form-to-entry mapping: for every word form the system ingests, I have to produce a rule that decides whether it gets its own headword entry, a sub-entry under some base, or is just folded into the base word's inflection table. The taggers are mostly temps with no linguistics training, they have something like ninety seconds per form, and there are tens of thousands of these decisions, so whatever we choose locks in quickly and is expensive to redo. The only human review is a lead lexicographer who has told me upfront she'll only take the cases my procedure explicitly flags as undecidable. There's no consistency right now: 'unhappy' is its own entry in one dictionary but 'unclear' isn't in another, and everyone has a different story about why that was right.

I've read the usual theoretical stuff on the inflection/derivation distinction and I know the standard criteria people wave at — productivity, semantic transparency, category change, obligatoriness, paradigm structure — but I don't know how to turn them into a sequence of questions that a temp can run in the allotted time. Please write the actual decision procedure: the steps in order, what counts as enough evidence to move on, where a step stops and says 'own entry' or 'fold into base', and where the procedure cannot settle it and the lead lexicographer has to just decide. Be honest about which of these tests are really judgment calls dressed up as tests. Then run it on the forms we fight about most: 'happier' against 'happy', 'unhappy' against 'happy', and 'broken' in 'a broken chair' (against 'she has broken the chair'). If it's not too much, also run it on 'better' against 'good', because my hunch is the procedure will say something there that no one here is going to like, and I'd definately rather find that out before we hard-code it.
