My reading group has spent a year going back and forth between a reductionist about testimonial justification and an anti-reductionist, and we've finally got them to agree to do an adversarial-collaboration writeup. Could you draft the document they would produce? The reductionist is the kind who thinks a hearer's justification for believing what someone says has to come from her other evidence: background evidence that this speaker, or speakers like this one in this kind of situation, are competent and sincere. The anti-reductionist is the kind who thinks testimony gives you a default entitlement to believe absent defeaters, more the way perception might, so you don't need positive reasons about each speaker before you're entitled to trust them.

Structure it as: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign about what testimony is, about what positive evidence of reliability does, about defeaters, and about the difference between epistemic and practical trust. Make this section substantive, not a pile of trivialities, but it also can't smuggle in assumptions one side would reject. (2) Then a disagreements table with the residual differences stated precisely, in wording each camp would accept for its own position and not feel caricatured by. (3) Then cruxes: the sub-questions where settling them would actually move one of them. For each crux give at least one concrete case or thought experiment both agree bears on it, and record what each predicts about it.

Some things I want the draft to face head-on rather than dodge. First, the difference between a local reductionist, who says you just need background evidence about this particular speaker, and a global reductionist, who says you need prior evidence that testimony as a class is reliable—and whether the global version is circular because you'd have to rely on testimony to gather that evidence. Second, the mutual-worry pair: the anti-reductionist seems to liscense gullibility (the stranger at the bus stop who tells me my train leaves from platform nine, and I have nothing either way about him), while the reductionist seems to rule out justified belief in children and in one-off encounters where you have no track record. Third, I suspect part of their fight is about the word justification—whether it means having reasons available or being within your epistemic rights—so try to catch anything merely verbal and mark it as such before they both sign wording they don't actually disagree about.

Finally, separate what is settled among people working on testimony from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus. If you find yourself writing something like "it is widely accepted that...," check whether it actually is.
