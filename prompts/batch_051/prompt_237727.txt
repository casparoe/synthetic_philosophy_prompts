I am a retired atmospheric chemist, and since leaving my laboratory I have been spending my time on the moral and political philosophy I never had room for during my career. This year I joined the advisory board of a small family foundation with a broad mandate — to improve human welfare and to safeguard the conditions under which future generations can flourish — and an annual grant-making budget of roughly two million dollars. We have no program staff, so the board members themselves must decide how the money is allocated.

We are about to set our priorities for the next five years, and three proposals have survived our initial screening. The first would support the distribution of an inexpensive, well-tested drug against a parasitic infection that debilitates several hundred million people in the tropics. The treatment is cheap, its benefits are documented, and surprisingly few other donors work on the disease, although it kills relatively few people and instead causes blindness and stunted growth. The second would fund a longitudinal study of early midlife biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease. The problem is vast and growing, and the surrounding science is advancing quickly, but the field is already crowded with far larger public and commercial funders, and our annual contribution would be a rounding error. The third would support a small team of climate scientists and mathematicians developing early-warning indicators for abrupt shifts in ocean circulation. The stakes could be enormous and almost no one funds this kind of cross-disciplinary work, but it may well produce elegant mathematics that never reaches a single policy decision.

I find that I reach for a different principle depending on which proposal I am defending. For the first, I argue that a small foundation should simply purchase the largest credible improvement in human welfare per dollar. For the second, I argue that the sheer size of the problem ought to dominate, and that our being a marginal contributor is not by itself a decisive objection. For the third, I argue that funders with modest overheads and a tolerance for failure are exactly the ones who should be taking on work whose payoff cannot be estimated in advance. I suspect these impulses are not independent considerations, but I cannot yet articulate the deeper structure that would let me weigh them against one another.

I would be grateful for your help laying out that structure. I am not asking for a ranked list of the three proposals. I want a principled account of how a small generalist foundation ought to reason across a choice with this shape: a cheap and proven opportunity in a neglected area, a marginal contribution to an enormous and already well-funded problem, and an uncertain but potentially transformative line of research that sits between disciplines.
