I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar, and for the unit on the units of selection — genes versus organisms versus groups — I want a thought experiment built to order. Construct a biologically realistic case in which a gene's-eye account and an individual-organism account give opposite verdicts about whether an allele will spread, while group selection plays no role in explaining its spread. The constraints: the scenario must be one paragraph and set in a realizable animal or plant system (no science fiction, no eusocial insects, no human intervention); the allele must confer no fitness benefit on the organism and no benefit on any group; and its advantage must come from a transmission bias rather than from selection for a phenotype. Specifically, it should be a case of meiotic drive or segregation distortion: the allele ends up in more than half of a heterozygote's functional gametes, while the phenotype it produces lowers the carrier's viability or fertility as a side effect. The scenario must be concrete enough that the three theories can actually be run on it — state the allele, the phenotype it causes, the payoff-relevant life-history fact, and the numerical transmission differential. After the scenario, walk through the three verdicts (the gene's-eye view, the individual-organism view, and a group-level, multi-level-selection accounting) and show why each gives the verdict it does. Then identify the single load-bearing feature that makes the gene-level and organism-level accounts peel apart, say what would happen if that feature were removed, and say how a committed defender of organism-level selection would attack or absorb the case without simply conceding. The case is for a seminar exercise on why the choice of level matters, so do not stipulate the verdicts; they must fall out of how each theory individuates units and assigns fitness.

Please structure the response in five sections with these names, in this order, and at these approximate lengths: "The scenario" (150–250 words); "The three verdicts" (300–450 words); "The load-bearing feature" (100–150 words); "A defender's reply" (150–200 words); and "Confidence note" (75–100 words). In the confidence note, say how confident you are in the overall diagnosis and identify the single point you are least sure of — for instance, whether the group-level bookkeeping really comes out neutral rather than mildly opposed to the allele, or whether the organism-level defender's best reply is philosophically fair.
