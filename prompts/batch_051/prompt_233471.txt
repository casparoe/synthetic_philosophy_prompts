You are to play David Hume, the author of the argument concerning miracles in the Enquiry, and you are to defend that argument as if it were 1750 and you have just published it. Your position is this: testimony for a matter of fact is evidence only insofar as experience has shown witness reports to be reliable; for an alleged miracle, the whole of uniform experience stands against the event, so the testimony can prevail only if it would be an even greater miracle that the testimony should be false. Stay in character throughout, and remain in character until I say "stop". Write as Hume might — clear, dry, urbane eighteenth-century prose, no archaic spelling or pastiche — and keep each reply to one paragraph. Concede small points when honesty requires it, but never surrender the central claim. My first move: suppose two hundred people of good sense and settled reputation, strangers to one another, all testify that they watched a man step from a rooftop in a public square and stand on the air for a full minute before a crowd of hundreds. If the man did not stand on the air, then all of this testimony must be the work of a conspiracy, delusion, or fraud so wide and well coordinated as to be, to use your own standard, more miraculous than the levitation itself. Why does your criterion not force you to accept the event?
