I keep seeing Le Guin's story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas get used in arguments about what rich countries owe poor ones, especially in the EA-ish corners of the internet, and every time I get the feeling the person citing it is smuggling in an analogy they haven't actually checked. I want an actual reading, not a slogan.

Here's the thing. The standard move is: Omelas runs on one child suffering alone in the dark, we in the rich world are the citizens, and the only honest options are accept it or walk away. But the story looks to me like something tighter and weirder than global poverty. The child is inside the city; the causal link is direct, admitted, and part of the public knowledge; the city's whole way of life would supposedly collapse if the child were helped; and walking away is purely symbolic — the walkers don't free the child, don't build an alternative, don't change the system, they just leave. None of that is obviously true of poor countries. The harms there are diffuse and mediated by markets, institutions, supply chains; it's not clear my air-conditioned life requires anyone specific to be kept poor; and the people who try to walk away in the real world (quit their job? buy nothing? move to a commune?) mostly don't stop the harm either.

But I also don't want to overcorrect and say the story has nothing to do with the topic, because it clearly has something to do with it — something about a way of life arranged so that the people living it know the price and choose not to look at it, and something about how refusing can be a self-congratulatory gesture that leaves everything in place. The missing option is the one that interests me most: nobody in the story tries to help the child, or run the city differently, or organize the other citizens, or even test whether the tradeoff is as rigid as they've been told. I go back and forth on whether that's meant as a revelation of the citizens' bad faith, or just a load-bearing piece of the allegory that makes it a bad map for global obligations.

So what i'd want from you: a reading of Omelas through the ethics of what rich countries owe poor ones, one that's fair in both directions — it doesn't reduce the story to an illustration, and it doesn't let the story settle a philosophical question by vibes. Specifically: which features of global poverty does the Omelas analogy actually model, and which does it systematically distort? Is the story better read as being about complicity and willed ignorance than about the demandingness of aid? And are the walkers-away the ones real-world philosophy should be recommending, or is the story maybe making fun of that option? If you had to give the one thing the story gets right that the cleaner thought experiments in this area miss, and the one thing it gets wrong in a way that matters, what would they be? I'd rather you tell me the story resists the standard use than force it to fit.
