For several decades the question of how deeply entrenched practices like slavery and dueling actually came to an end has had three live answers, and none of them has managed to drive the others from the field. The first is an argument-driven account: moral criticism accumulated until enough people were persuaded, and changes in law and conduct followed changes in conviction. The second is an interest-driven account, in the tradition of Eric Williams's thesis about abolition: the practices ended when they stopped serving the interests of the classes that maintained them — slave labor lost its economic rationale at the relevant margins, and dueling became a liability rather than a credential for men whose standing came to depend on commerce and law. The third is an honor-driven account, associated with Kwame Anthony Appiah: the practices ended when their social meaning shifted, so that what had once been honorable became contemptible or ridiculous, and the same desire for esteem that had sustained the practice was redirected toward abandoning it. Each camp can handle its paradigm case convincingly and then struggles with the others' cases, and the impasse has persisted even where the parties agree on the main historical record. I have begun to suspect the disagreement is not really, or not mainly, an empirical one about causes, but a disagreement about what is being explained and what an explanation must do: one camp explains institutional change, another explains changes in incentives, another explains changes in social meaning; and the phrase "why did the practice end?" is too under-specified to force a choice. But I can also see respects in which it might be a genuine causal dispute that better comparative evidence could settle. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on the history: is it substantive, verbal, or several questions sharing a name; is there a hidden factual issue doing the work; is it ill-posed; and what, given the diagnosis, would actually count as progress?
