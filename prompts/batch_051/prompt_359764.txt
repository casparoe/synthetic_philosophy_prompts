In our American history class we have been reading Frederick Douglass on the Constitution, and I keep getting stuck on what look like two flatly incompatible positions from the same person. In an abolitionist editorial from the 1840s, when he was closely allied with William Lloyd Garrison, Douglass treated the Constitution as a pro-slavery document: whatever the private feelings of the men at the Philadelphia convention, the bargain they produced gave legal protection to slavery, and honest abolitionists should repudiate it rather than try to use it. By the early 1850s, after he had broken with Garrison's wing of the movement and was editing his own paper in Rochester, he defended what seems like the exact reverse, arguing that the Constitution, read literally and in light of its own stated purposes, nowhere recognizes human beings as property and is in fact an instrument of freedom that abolitionists ought to claim.

What I cannot figure out is whether these two positions can be reconciled without making one of them a pretense. I can imagine several strategies: the word Constitution might mean the bare text in the later period but the text as it was actually administered in the earlier one; Douglass's deeper commitments might have stayed fixed while only his factual beliefs about the document changed; the later view might have been mainly a strategic move for the antislavery cause rather than a sincere legal conviction; or the change might be genuine and driven by letting the preamble and general purposes control the disputed clauses. I would like an account of which of these readings, if any, is the most honest reconciliation of the two passages, and which ones are just special pleading designed to avoid admitting that Douglass contradicted himself.
