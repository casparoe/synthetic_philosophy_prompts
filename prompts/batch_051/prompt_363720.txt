Here's a counterfactual I'd like you to work through. Maimonides held that animal sacrifice wasn't God's first choice—it was a kind of pedagogical concession. The ancient Israelites couldn't have handled a purely verbal form of worship after living among idolaters, so the Torah gave them sacrifice but restrained it: one place, one altar, a single priesthood, no animal blood outside that frame. Now suppose in, say, 1200, a Jewish community in Fustat not only accepts this account but makes it the official, publicly taught doctrine—kids learn it in school, laypeople hear it in sermons, everybody knows that sacrifice is a divine accommodation to weakness, not something God wants for itself. Then, in the same generation, political conditions allow them to rebuild a temple and restart animal sacrifice, and they do it. Predict, with reasoning, what happens to the practice over the next three generations. Does it stay central? Become a kind of self-conscious historical reconstruction? Or does the official explanation undermine the thing it explains? I want the chain of mechanisms—psychological, institutional, halakhic, whatever you think matters. Mark which predictions are robust and which depend on assumptions about how ordinary laypeople actually internalize the idea, as opposed to the scholars.
