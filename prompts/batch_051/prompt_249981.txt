I'm presenting a paper to a mixed workshop of constitutional lawyers and democratic theorists, defending the claim that the practice of keeping a designated survivor—a cabinet member held away from the State of the Union address—is best justified not as continuity of government but as a fair-division mechanism: it allocates the burden of being the one who may have to govern without having been present for the shared information and deliberation, and it should be judged by criteria of fairness among the eligible officers rather than by maximal readiness. My further claim is that this reframing changes who should be chosen and what they should be told in advance. Prepare me for the question period. The audience splits roughly between public-law formalists who regard the practice as extraconstitutional theater with no real legal status, and democratic theorists who think the survivor inherits office without having shared in the risk, information, or deliberation that could have killed everyone else. I want the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as those two groups would phrase them, with which objections have real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the one objection I should be most worried about.
