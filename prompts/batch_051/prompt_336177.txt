I am a PhD student working on the semantics and metaphysics of future-tense discourse. The underlying issue is the one raised by the classical sea-battle problem, but I want to work it through a wider set of cases rather than the stock example. I need a working definition of the word settled as applied to future facts — what it takes for it to be settled now that a future-tensed proposition is true, or settled now that it is false. Please provide the definition and then apply it to each case below: state the verdict your definition gives, flag any case where it gives no answer or an answer you think is wrong, and either revise the definition or defend the particular verdict.

1. A sealed bottle of water is left on a desk in a closed, quiet room; consider the proposition that it will still be on the desk at noon tomorrow. (I would call this clearly settled.)
2. A colleague has not yet decided whether to accept a job offer, though she must decide by Friday; consider the proposition that she will accept it. (I would call this clearly not settled.)
3. A die is about to be tossed in a cup in the ordinary way; consider the proposition that it will show six on the first toss.
4. A simple deterministic mechanism has already fixed which side a hidden coin will show when it is uncovered, but no one knows the mechanism's initial state; consider the proposition that the coin will show heads.
5. The same coin is uncovered and shows heads; consider the claim, made then, that a minute before it was uncovered the outcome was already settled.
6. I say now, while fully intending to do it, that I will finish a referee report by Tuesday; consider the proposition my utterance expresses.

I especially want your definition to separate two things: being fixed by the past and present together with the laws, and being knowable in principle from the past and present. If you conclude that a single definition cannot do justice to both, say what has to be given up. Please answer in American English.
