I've recently been put in charge of the corrections and retractions policy at a newspaper, and a pair of decisions last week has forced me to confront a question I can no longer answer from instinct.

In the first case, an article reported that the city council had approved a rezoning measure by a vote of five to four, when the measure had in fact failed by that same margin. The reporter had done everything we ask: she checked the council's published minutes and confirmed the outcome with two city staffers, all of whom said the measure had passed. The error came from a corrupted file in the city's own system. We corrected the article, added a notice at the top, and left it in our archive.

In the second case, an article about a local business made central claims that later turned out to be true, but we discovered that the reporter had invented a customer and quoted him praising the owner. We retracted the article and removed it from our archive, even though its main factual assertions were accurate.

I keep failing to state the principle behind the difference. In the first case, we treated the article's defect as a failure of truth. In the second, we treated its defect as a failure of the reporter's warrant or method, even though the first-order claims were true. In short, we corrected a false article that had been reported responsibly, and we retracted a true article that had been reported irresponsibly.

Please reconstruct, as rigorously as you can, the implicit theory of assertion — or of the norm governing published factual claims — that this pair of practices presupposes. Does it make sense only if a published news item asserts at least two things at once: a first-order claim about the world, and a second-order claim about the reporter's epistemic position with respect to that claim? If so, what norm governs each, and how do they interact? Or is there a single norm of assertion that yields the different remedies for the two different failures? Be careful to distinguish what the correction/retraction policy strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit, and say which familiar views — roughly, that assertion is acceptable when it is true, when it is believed, when it is justified, or when it is known — are quietly ruled out by each half of the practice. Finally, tell me something practical: if the presupposed theory were replaced with a pure truth norm, or with a pure knowledge norm, what exactly would have to change in a corrections and retractions manual, and what would the paper have to give up in either direction?
