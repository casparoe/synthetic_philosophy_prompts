I'm giving a colloquium in a philosophy of science department next month. The talk defends this thesis: computational irreducibility — the fact that for some simple-rule systems the only way to learn their long-run behavior is to run them — sets no special limit on scientific explanation, only on exact prediction; a process is not unexplained merely because it cannot be shortcut. My argument sketch: explanation need not be compression; identifying the higher-level regularities a computation realizes, and knowing which features of the underlying rule are responsible for them, is already explanatory even when the regularity can be seen only by simulating the system; and demanding that a good explanation let us derive or shortcut the dynamics in closed form conflates prediction with understanding. The audience: senior faculty who work on mechanistic explanation and think an explanation should decompose a system into organized components and show how they produce the phenomenon; two faculty who take the standard cellular-automaton results to show that some real systems are opaque in principle, and read this as a discovery about the limits of explanation rather than merely of prediction; and one complex-systems researcher who will insist that practicing scientists already accept running the model as an answer and that philosophy should catch up. Nobody in the room is a specialist in the automata literature, but everyone knows the canonical examples.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered from most to least dangerous, where danger is a matter of both how likely the objection is in this room and how much damage it does if pushed. Phrase each objection the way this audience would put it. For each, give a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Mark clearly which objections I should concede in part up front and which I should hold, and explain why each concession does not sink the thesis. Include the question I should be most afraid of but hope no one asks, and say whether it is a fair statement of the deepest weakness in my argument. Commit: do not present a menu of possible responses — choose one ordering, one reply per objection, and one concession/hold line, and give the reasons for those choices. Begin with the prep sheet itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no restating of the setup. The first sentence of the sheet should be a one-sentence bottom line: the most dangerous objection I face and the defensive line the whole talk hinges on. Then go straight into the ordered objections.
