Over the past term I have been sitting in on a seminary seminar on the old question of how the letter of James and the letters of Paul fit together about the place of works in justification. My professor has been strikingly consistent in her rulings on the usual test cases, but I still cannot tell exactly which account she is running. I have written down her past verdicts, paraphrased from discussion, and I would like you to predict how she will rule on a new case I intend to raise next week.

Her rulings so far are these. She holds that God treated Abraham as righteous on the basis of his trusting the promise, and that his later willingness to offer Isaac was the act that made that trust visible rather than anything that added to his standing. She holds that Rahab was justified by what she did in hiding the messengers, but that the deed counted as an embodied act of allegiance, not as a merit that purchased favor. She treats the criminal crucified beside Jesus who asked to be remembered as a genuine limit case: saved by bare trust with no opportunity for any subsequent deed, showing that where no work is possible none is required. She says the rich man who kept the commandments but would not surrender his wealth was not justified, because the refusal exposed where his ultimate loyalty lay and his law-keeping counted for nothing. On Cornelius, the God-fearing centurion who gave alms and prayed before he ever heard the apostolic message, she says his charities showed a real Godward disposition and were noticed by God, but that the decisive thing was his responsiveness when the message arrived rather than the deeds themselves. On the passage in Matthew where the nations are separated at the last judgment by how they treated the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the naked, the sick, and the imprisoned, she insists the judgment really does proceed by deeds of mercy toward the vulnerable, and she resists any reading that treats those deeds as dispensable noise. And on Paul's contrast between faith and law-observance, she insists Paul is targeting the distinctive boundary-marking practices of the Mosaic covenant, such as circumcision, food regulations, and the calendar, rather than good deeds in general; on her account James and Paul are using the same word for different things and are not actually in conflict.

The new case is this. A woman converts in middle age, sincerely trusts Christ, and is baptized. She then lives an outwardly honorable but unremarkable life: conventional morality, donations that cost her little, no visiting of the sick or imprisoned, little of the costly and risk-taking love that the letter of James and the Matthean judgment scene seem to make central. Everyone who knows her regards her faith as genuine, but its expression is thin. I would like to know whether my professor will say that this woman is finally justified, and why.

Please write your answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email or a letter. I would like four things from you. First, lay out the candidate accounts that could underlie her past rulings, including at least a forensic faith-alone account on which works are evidence but never the basis, and an operative-faith or final-deeds account on which a working faith is itself the condition of final justification. Second, say which account you think is doing the real work and what it predicts about the woman. Third, identify which earlier case is the closest analog to the woman and which earlier case puts the most pressure on her position. Fourth, specify a further case that would best decide between the competing accounts, and say what each account would require as its verdict on that further case.
