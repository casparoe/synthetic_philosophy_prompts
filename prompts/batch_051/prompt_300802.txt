In a seminar on Kantian ethics and its afterlives, we have been comparing Kant's Formula of Universal Law, Hare's universal prescriptivism (roughly: moral judgments are universalizable prescriptions one must be willing to apply from every role a situation contains), and what some call the generalization argument — the thought that "what if everyone did that?" can condemn an act even when the individual act causes no direct harm. A classmate offered the following reconstruction. Each premise seemed unobjectionable when I first heard it, but the conclusion cannot be right, and I want to know which step gives way and at what cost.

(1) An action is permissible only if the agent could rationally will that the maxim of the action become a universal law. (2) To will a maxim as a universal law is, at minimum, to be able to will the state of affairs that would result if everyone acted on that maxim, without thereby defeating ends the agent in fact holds. (3) Consider Rina's maxim: "I will pursue the career I personally find most fulfilling, even where another career open to me would better meet urgent social needs, such as nursing or elder care." (4) If everyone acted on this maxim, the resulting state of affairs would be one in which each person's own sense of fulfillment, rather than social need, governs occupational choice; and in a world where many essential forms of care are less fulfilling than the alternatives, the result would be a chronic underprovision of exactly the care Rina herself expects to receive when ill or old. (5) Rina cannot rationally will that state of affairs, because it would defeat ends she holds — receiving care when vulnerable, and having those she loves receive care. (6) Therefore, by (1) and (2), Rina's career choice is morally impermissible. (7) But this conclusion is unacceptable: a principle with that result would condemn nearly any career pursued for fulfillment rather than maximal social usefulness, and no plausible moral theory requires everyone to enter whatever profession currently has the greatest unmet need. (8) So at least one of the premises must be rejected.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If you had to reject exactly one step, which would it be, and what would rejecting it cost for the universalizability program? I would most like to keep (1), because it seems to convict free-riders and queue-jumpers, and (7), because any account of permissible life choices must leave room for vocational choice; but I cannot see how to hold both without making something else give way. A diagnosis is more useful to me than a hunch: I want to know whether the fault is local to the way Rina's maxim is described, or structural, lying in what the argument assumes about willing a universal law or about how universalization relates to consequences. What could a repair look like, and what would it cost?
