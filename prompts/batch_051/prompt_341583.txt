I'm a novelist — I write historical fiction, and the new one is set in secenteenth-century France, mostly around the magistrates and the courts, so I've been living in Pascal's Pensées lately, especially the fragments on justice and force. A philosopher friend asked me to write down the half-formed views I've ended up with so she can poke at them before the novel's legal philosophy turns incoherent. Here's what I seem to believe:

(1) I'm a modest realist about justice: some legal arrangements are really better than others, not just better relative to a culture, and at least sometimes we can tell the difference.
(2) Justice can't do anything in the world without force behind it. A court whose judgments can't be enforced is just a debating club, so a just order has to coerce at some point.
(3) Descriptively, what gets treated as justice in a society is mostly settled by custom, habit, and the interests of whoever holds power. Most people obey the law because it's the law, not because they've checked whether it's just.
(4) So the idea of justice that actually gets institutionalized is usually the powerful people's idea of justice with a nicer name. Power doesn't make something just, but it does decide which conception of justice wins.
(5) Still, I refuse to treat justice as a sham: you can live inside a system like that and still point to genuinely unjust laws and work to change them.
(6) And I think Pascal was onto something when he said justice needs force to be worth anything and force needs justice to be legitimate, which is why I'm fine with courts and police as such: coercion is what makes justice actual rather than merely aspirational.

So: do these actually hang together? Please don't just tell me they're all compatible or all hopeless. I want to know which pairs are in real tension, which only seem to be, how bad each tension is, and what the cheapest fix is. If there's one change that would make the whole set cohere best, id want that spelled out too.
