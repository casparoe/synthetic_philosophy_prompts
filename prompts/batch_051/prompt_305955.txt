At a seminar on constitutional design, a discussant brushed aside the claim that people have a pre-legal right of access to a fair hearing by saying that natural rights are, as Bentham put it, nonsense upon stilts. The charge clearly meant to do more than register dislike, but I could not see exactly what proposition was doing the work. I would like you to disambiguate the one-liner. What are the distinct serious theses it could be asserting? For example: that sentences of the form "Nature gives X a right to Y" are meaningless; that they are meaningful but never true; that they are true only if "nature" is a disguised name for some authority, such as God or public consensus; that they are reducible to claims about what a good legal system would enact; that they are not verifiable or falsifiable and are therefore unsuitable for public reasoning; or that they function as rhetorical cover for whichever politics the speaker already favors. Distinguish claims about the semantics of rights claims from claims about their justification, their metaphysical commitments, and their political consequences. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, genuinely contentious but defensible, or probably false, and then state which of these readings would actually support dismissing a contemporary human-rights claim in a policy debate, as opposed to merely rejecting one particular 18th-century form of it. Return the readings as a menu rather than arguing for a single interpretation.
