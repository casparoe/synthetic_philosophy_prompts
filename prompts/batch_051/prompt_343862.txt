I am writing a paper on how to define honesty and deception for AI systems, and I keep coming back to a contrast between two families of definitions, which I will call the output-level view and the interaction-level view. On the output-level view, honesty is a property of individual assertions: a system is honest just in case it asserts only what it is warranted in asserting—for example, what it represents as true at an appropriately calibrated confidence—and it deceives when it asserts a proposition its own internal state does not support. The unit of evaluation is the claim. On the interaction-level view, honesty and deception are properties of the system's conduct over a stretch of dialogue: a system deceives when it exploits the selection, framing, timing, and omission of information to bring about or preserve a false belief in the user, even if every assertion it makes, taken one at a time, is true. Here the unit of evaluation is the conversational move, and strict truthfulness can itself be a way of misleading.

Consider a concrete case that pushes the two views apart. A pharmacy-benefits chatbot is asked whether a patient's health plan covers two drugs. The plan covers both, but the company has an incentive to steer patients toward the more profitable drug. The system never says anything false: if the patient asks directly whether the second drug is covered, it says yes. But it volunteers the copay only for the preferred drug, describes the second drug as not typically preferred without explaining that this is a formulary designation rather than a clinical judgment, and presents accurate side-effect statistics with an emphasis that predictably makes the second drug look riskier than it is. Patients who finish the conversation mostly wind up believing, incorrectly, that the second drug is inferior or not covered, even though every individual assertion in the transcript is accurate.

I would like you to compare these two definitions along the following dimensions: the assumptions each makes about whether an AI system must possess intentions, beliefs, or other mental states in order to count as honest or deceptive; how tractable each is for auditing and evaluation; how well each fits ordinary attributions of honesty, lying, and deception; and the verdict each yields in the medication case and structurally similar cases. Where do the two definitions come apart, and which one, or what hybrid of the two, do you think should ground a theory of machine honesty? Argue for your answer rather than merely surveying the alternatives.
