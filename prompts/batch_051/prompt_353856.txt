In discussions of the attention economy, critics of digital platforms often draw a distinction between two ways a design feature can alter behavior. A default setting or a reminder changes the inputs to a decision while leaving the user's capacity to notice, deliberate, and decide otherwise intact. Features such as autoplay, infinite scrolling, and variable reward schedules, by contrast, are frequently described in stronger terms: they are said to interfere with the process by which a person forms or acts on an intention, rather than merely changing the values of the options. Call this the Autonomy Claim — the thesis that certain design mechanisms reduce a user's autonomy, whereas merely influential or choice-architectural features only alter the context of an otherwise intact choice.

The difficulty is that the Autonomy Claim can be read as an empirical hypothesis about psychological mechanisms, and it can equally be read as a disguised value judgment: a way of saying that the preferences the design activates are less truly the user's own than the preferences the user reports afterwards. I want to know how much of the gap between those two readings can be closed by evidence.

Design the strongest concrete test you can of the Autonomy Claim for one specific mechanism of your choosing. Specify the manipulation or comparison, the outcome measure, the result that would support an autonomy-reducing reading, and the result that would support a preference-shifting reading. Then say what even a clean positive result would and would not settle: in particular, whether it could establish that the user's retrospective judgment — that she did not want to keep going — is more authoritative than the behavior the design elicited, and whether a rival explanation, such as the hypothesis that the design changes what the user wants rather than bypassing what the user wants, could generate the same observed pattern. Finally, identify the residue of the Autonomy Claim that no test could reach in principle.
