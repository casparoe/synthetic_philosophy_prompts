A colleague has been circulating an argument against higher-order thought theories of consciousness, and I cannot find the step at which it fails. It runs as follows.

(1) According to the higher-order thought theory, a first-order state — a red sensation, say — is conscious just in case it is the object of a higher-order thought to the effect that the subject is in that state.
(2) Higher-order thoughts are representational states, and representational states can misrepresent their objects or lack them altogether: a thought can represent a unicorn though no unicorn exists, and can represent a thing as F when it is not F.
(3) Since, on the theory, it is the higher-order thought that makes the first-order state conscious, the conscious character of the subject's experience must be fixed by how the higher-order thought represents the first-order state, not by how the first-order state actually is. If it were otherwise, a misrepresenting higher-order thought would simply be a counterexample to the theory, whereas the theory must allow misrepresentation as a routine possibility for any representational state.
(4) Therefore, if the higher-order thought represents the subject as having a red sensation while the subject's actual first-order state is a green sensation, the subject is conscious of a red sensation.
(5) The same principle extends to the case in which there is no relevant first-order state at all: a higher-order thought could represent the subject as having a red sensation though the subject is in no sensory state of that kind. By (3), the subject would then be conscious of a red sensation.
(6) But being conscious of a red sensation is a conscious state of the subject. In the targetless case, the only state available to serve as that conscious state is the higher-order thought itself; yet the theory insists that higher-order thoughts are themselves non-conscious, on pain of an infinite regress of ever higher-order thoughts. So the theory is left with either a conscious state that no actual state of the subject realizes — an experience with no bearer — or a higher-order thought that is conscious after all, which restarts the regress.
(7) Hence the theory must choose between admitting bearerless experiences and imposing an unexplained ban on targetless higher-order thoughts. Both look untenable, so the theory fails.

I find (3) the most plausible step and would prefer to keep it, but I cannot see how the argument's conclusion is avoided. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
