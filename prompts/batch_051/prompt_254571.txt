I've been going back and forth with a friend about Goodman's new riddle of induction, and I hit a wall I can't get past. The setup is the usual one: pick some future time—say 2100—and define a contrived predicate 'grue' that applies to an object just in case it is examined before then and is green, or it is not examined before then and is blue (or some near variant of that; the details don't matter for what's bugging me). Before 2100, every examined emerald that looks green also satisfies grue, so the same observations that seem to confirm the generalization that all emeralds are green seem to confirm the generalization that all emeralds are grue—and those two generalizations predict different colors after 2100. My friend's view is that there is something genuinely better about 'green' here: that green is a projectible predicate and grue is a broken one, and that this is a real fact about which sorts of concepts fit the world (or at least fit rational inductive practice), not just a boring fact about which ones our language happens to have a short word for. We have gone around in circles, and I cannot tell whether he is saying something testable or just dressing up a convention in the word 'genuinely'.

So here is what I want to know. Suppose someone claims that some predicates are more projectible than others in a non-relative sense—that induction over green is objectively better supported than induction over grue, and not merely better relative to our choice of vocabulary. Is there any observation or experiment that could bear on that? What would the strongest concrete test look like—something like taking successive time-slices of old data and checking which style of generalization would have paid off, or giving people or neural networks an artificial language in which a grue-like predicate is just as short and easy as a green-like one and seeing which one they learn and project, or something else entirely? Set it up concretely: say exactly what result would count for the claim and what result would count against it. Then be honest about what even a favorable result would leave untouched—where the non-empirical residue is, if there is one.

I would like a commited answer, not a menu of options. Tell me which test you think is strongest and whether you think it could actually settle my friend's dispute, and defend that verdict.
