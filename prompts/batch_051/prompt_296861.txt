For the past three years I have served on the governance committee of a cooperative society whose constitution was drafted by founders convinced that decisions should be made by prediction markets rather than votes. Article VII of that constitution reads: "At each convocation the Registrar shall adopt whichever duly sponsored proposal the standing market assigns, conditional on its adoption, the greatest expected increase in the Index of Civic Welfare over the subsequent three years." The clause has worked tolerably well for ordinary spending and policy proposals, and it was plainly written to stop the membership from making self-serving decisions by simple majority.

At next month's convocation, a bloc of members has duly sponsored one proposal whose operative text reads, in full: "Article VII is repealed. All future proposals shall be selected by majority vote of the members present at the convocation." The standing market currently ranks this proposal highest by its conditional welfare score. Traders' public commentary offers two reasons for that ranking: first, repeal is expected to restore member goodwill and participation, which the Index would register; second, the conditioning event would itself dissolve the standing market, so the prediction could not be settled in the ordinary way — and some traders claim it could therefore never be falsified by any subsequent Index reading. Some committee members conclude the Registrar is mechanically required to adopt the proposal; others reply that the clause cannot have been meant to reach a proposal that abolishes the very mechanism on which the clause depends, and that a conditional estimate whose conditioning event destroys the estimator is not an estimate at all.

I would like a careful opinion on what Article VII actually requires here. Please work through the textual reading, the purpose of the clause, what its drafters would most plausibly have said if asked about self-abolishing proposals, and what a reasonable member reading the constitution would take the rule to mean. Then say whether the market's conditional score is admissible evidence in this case, and whether the market can coherently price a rule change that would put an end to its own pricing. If the Registrar adopts the proposal, does the adoption itself tend to refute the prediction that welfare would improve, or does it merely end the procedure for checking that prediction? Finally, what does your treatment imply for the more general question of whether prediction-market governance can handle the case of its own amendment?
