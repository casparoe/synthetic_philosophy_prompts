I teach an upper-level undergraduate political theory seminar, and next week we begin a unit on Machiavelli's Prince. I would like to run a low-tech, 75-minute game or simulation for my twenty students that makes them feel the relation between fortuna and virtù before we discuss the text, so that the concepts later attach to decisions the students actually made rather than to my gloss on Machiavelli.

Here is the specific reading I want the activity to press on them. I understand virtù as a cluster of practical dispositions rather than moral virtue: reading a situation correctly, acting at the right moment, adjusting one's conduct to circumstances, and being willing to break faith or act ruthlessly when events demand it. Fortuna is the domain of what cannot be planned away: foreign invasions, floods, shifts of alliance, plagues, accidental timing. Two nuances matter to me. First, Machiavelli's claim that fortune governs perhaps half of what we do while our own virtù governs the rest: virtù does not eliminate fortune, and even the best-prepared ruler can be ruined by events. Preparation, he suggests with the image of a river that can be banked and channeled but will sometimes flood anyway, changes exposure rather than removing it. Second, the concept of occasione: the opening or occasion that fortune supplies, and in which virtù must act. Virtù without an occasion is wasted; an occasion without virtù is wasted. I want students to feel the difference between controlling outcomes and preparing for variance, and the difference between mere caution and the active, sometimes morally costly, readiness to seize a moment.

Design the activity. It should be an abstract classroom game rather than a historical reenactment, with rules, roles, materials a teacher could print in an afternoon, and a payoff scheme that uses points or classroom currency rather than real money. The conceptual distinction should emerge from the incentives and the sequence of events, not from a framing mini-lecture: students should run into the problem themselves before I name virtù or fortuna.

Give me the setup and rules in enough detail that another instructor could run it; a schedule for the 75 minutes; the payoff structure and how it creates the intended pressures; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game (a flood that breaks a cautious player's defenses, a player who wins by breaking a treaty at exactly the right moment, an opening missed through hesitation) onto the concepts above. Also tell me three or four ways the activity predictably goes wrong and how to handle each, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular I am thinking of the theological and providential background of fortuna, the real stakes of political life, and the question whether virtù has any normative content beyond effectiveness; say what I should supply in discussion to cover those gaps.
