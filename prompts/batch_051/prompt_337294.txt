So I'm taking a course on the ethics of lying and deception and I have to write an essay on whether there's a morally significant difference between lying and merely misleading someone. I don't want the essay to just do the standard "lying is worse because it violates a rule against false assertion" move, because the seminar got past that pretty fast. The essay should start by nailing the distinction: in both cases the speaker knows the audience will come away with a false belief, but in the lying case the false content is asserted, while in the misleading case the false content is implied, presupposed, or left for the audience to infer. It should then weigh the two main positions. One side says lying is worse: when you assert something false you stake yourself on it, you can't retreat, and you breach a trust people place specifically in what's said flat-out. The other side says misleading is at least as bad, maybe worse: it deliberately exploits the audience's normal inference patterns and then hands the speaker deniability — they get to say "I never said that" while having aimed the false belief all along. I've been playing with a distinction between being responsible for the wrong message (what you asserted) and being responsible for the wrong inference (what you knew they'd take away), and I think it helps but I'm not sure it settles things. Could you use something like that distinction to work toward a verdict? Also the essay needs to handle the hard case where someone says only truths but arranges them so the other person draws a false conclusion — is that closer to lying, or closer to manipulation, or does it show the whole lying/misleading contrast is the wrong way to cut the territory? Don't stay on the fence; pick a verdict, even if it's something like "the difference is real but smaller than the everyday distinction assumes" and defend it.
