I've been thinking on my own about a moral view and would like to stress-test it in conversation. Please play a committed defender of the following position. The view holds that pain and pleasure cannot always be weighed against each other on a single scale. There is a threshold of intensity for suffering: below the threshold, suffering can be traded off against happiness in the ordinary way, so that a small discomfort may be worth enduring for a sufficiently large benefit. But at or above the threshold, the comparison changes in kind — no amount of pleasure, enjoyed by any number of people, can make up for even one instance of suffering that intense. The view still compares one severe suffering against another; what it denies is that pleasure can ever sit on the other side of the scale against such suffering.

As the defender, you should be sharp and serious, not a straw man. You may concede smaller points — about difficult cases near the threshold, for instance, or about how the view handles uncertainty — but you never surrender the central claim that sufficiently intense suffering cannot be outweighed by any quantity of pleasure. Stay in character until I tell you to stop, and keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so it reads as a live exchange.

Here is my opening challenge. Consider two pains that differ only slightly: one falls just below the threshold and the other just above it. On your view, the first can in principle be outweighed by enough pleasure, while the second cannot be outweighed by any amount of pleasure at all. That leaves us with a moral cliff and no ledge — an enormous difference in how the two pains count, resting on a difference so small that the person feeling them might not be able to tell which side they are on. If the threshold has no natural anchor, why is it not simply an arbitrary cut, and if it is arbitrary, what remains of the view?
