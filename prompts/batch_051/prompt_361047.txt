I've been trying to work out what I actually think about virtual objects and virtual harms, and I'd like help turning my gut verdicts into an explicit principle. Let me give you the cases as concretely as I can, with my verdicts as they currently stand. A friend spent a year building a persistent online world — houses, landscapes, a working economy — and the platform deleted it with a day's notice and no export option. I think my friend lost something real and is owed more than a shrug. In a combat game where everyone has opted into player-versus-player fighting, one player kills another's avatar and takes their loot; I think that can be annoying or rude, but I don't think it's morally wrong in itself. In the same game's trading hub, a player tricks a newcomer into temporarily handing over a rare sword, then sells the sword for real money; I think that's genuinely wrong — theft by fraud — and the fact that the sword is digital doesn't excuse it. Someone who has never harmed a real child privately uses an immersive simulation whose character is explicitly designed to look, sound, and behave like a child, to enact sexual abuse of that character. I find that deeply wrong, and not merely because it's risky or might lead to worse behavior.

Here's the mismatch. I accept that killing a consenting adult's avatar in a PvP game is not wrong in itself, and I'm not even sure that single-player torture of a photorealistic adult virtual character who pleads for mercy is wrong, though it unsettles me. But I regard the simulated sexual abuse of a child-coded character as wrong even though no real child exists anywhere in the setup. Is there one principle about when virtual objects are real and when virtual harms are genuine that delivers all of those verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I abandon, and what does the cleanest remaining principle say about a case I haven't mentioned — for instance, a virtual memorial to a dead friend that another player defaces, or a bot that corners a virtual currency market and wipes out thousands of players' savings? I'd rather you reason this out than survey the literature, and please be explicit about what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who work on virtual worlds; don't present a contested reading as if it were the obvious one.
