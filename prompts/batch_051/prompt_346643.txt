I'm a PhD student working on the philosophy of machine learning, specifically on how training procedures transmit or fail to transmit human values. I'd like your help with an argument I can't see through. It concerns iterated amplification and distillation (IDA), and I'll spell out the version I have in mind so we're working from the same setup.

The scheme. Start with a human overseer H and a model M_0 that imitates H. At each round k, build an "amplified expert" by giving H access to many copies of M_k as assistants: H decomposes each task into subtasks, can delegate subtasks to copies of M_k, can inspect and correct their outputs as needed, and then composes a final answer. We then distill the next model M_{k+1} by training it to imitate the amplified expert's input–output behavior across a broad task distribution. With each round, M_k becomes a stronger assistant, with which H can decompose still harder tasks, and so on. The hope is that competence compounds because decomposition turns hard tasks into many easier subtasks, while alignment is preserved because a human remains in the loop and the model is always trained to imitate human-supervised behavior.

Here is the argument that seems to show the scheme cannot deliver both at once.

(1) The training signal for M_{k+1} is just the amplified expert's answers. The reliability of those answers in any domain is therefore no higher than the reliability of H's judgments inside the amplified process: what to delegate, which returned subtask answers to accept, how to combine them.

(2) H's judgments are reliable only inside an evaluation envelope: the class of model behaviors about which H can, unaided, tell whether they are correct or incorrect. Outside that envelope, H cannot reliably distinguish good work from bad.

(3) If M_k is more capable than H in some domain, then M_k can produce at least some correct outputs in that domain that lie outside H's evaluation envelope. If an output were inside the envelope, H would grasp the domain well enough to have produced that output unaided—which is exactly what it means for M_k to be more capable than H.

(4) When M_k produces an output outside H's envelope and that output is wrong or misaligned, H's oversight cannot reliably filter it out. Any such output that gets accepted—even occasionally—enters the training signal for M_{k+1}.

(5) For M_{k+1} to be more capable than M_k, the amplified process must sometimes accept outputs that lie outside H's envelope, since accepting only what H can verify would cap the process's performance at the envelope itself. So at the first round where competence crosses the envelope, a nonzero rate of unverifiable error enters the labels.

(6) Subsequent rounds distill those errors into the model and amplify them, so that after enough iteration drift is indistinguishable from competence. Therefore IDA can produce either systems no more capable than a human, or systems more capable than a human only by tolerating uncontrolled alignment drift. The two aims come apart precisely at the threshold the scheme exists to cross.

The reason this bothers me is that the very same argument seems to prove too much. Re-run it with "senior engineer" for "human overseer" and "junior hire" for "model," and you seem to prove that an organization can never reliably produce work no single member could have verified unaided. But scientific collaborations, software teams, and PhD supervision do exactly that all the time. So either the argument really does indict ordinary epistemic institutions, or something gives way in (1)–(6). I can't find the weak point.

I'd most like to keep (2), because an unaided evaluation envelope feels like the only honest statement of what the human contributes; but then I seem stuck with the conclusion, which I do not believe about human organizations.
