At a cognitive science lab meeting, a discussion after a talk on mental imagery turned into a long argument about the format of the mental representations underlying imagery. Mara defended a depictive account in the spirit of Kosslyn: mental images are carried by a quasi-pictorial, spatial medium that preserves metric relations. Eli defended a propositional account in the spirit of Pylyshyn: imagery is real as an experience, but the underlying representation is a structured description, and its spatial appearance is explained by what people know and expect, not by the code itself. I tried to record the exchange accurately, and I could not decide who was winning. I would like you to adjudicate it point by point.

Mara began with the image-scanning results. In one classic paradigm, people memorize a map of an island and then form an image of it; when they are asked to scan from one landmark to another, their response time increases linearly with the distance on the map. The same pattern appears in mental rotation: the time to judge whether two shapes are the same increases almost linearly with the angular difference between them. She said this is exactly what one would expect if imagery is carried by a spatial medium: scanning traverses intermediate locations, and rotation traverses intermediate orientations. A description of the map would list landmarks and relations, but there is no obvious reason why retrieval time from a list should track metric distance so precisely.

Eli replied that the linear functions do not force that conclusion. Participants know what it is like to scan a real map or rotate a real object, and they know that farther distances and larger angles take longer. They bring this tacit knowledge into the experiment and produce response times that match it, especially when the task makes distance or angle relevant. Demand characteristics need not be deliberate faking; people are very good at picking up what an experiment is about. And the picture metaphor faces a deeper problem: a picture in the head would still need an interpreter to inspect it, so saying the image is a picture postpones the explanatory work rather than doing it.

Mara answered that the tacit-knowledge explanation is too flexible. If participants were merely using rough expectations about how long things take, it would be surprising that the data are linear across many distances and angles rather than step-like or noisy, and that the effect survives tasks where no one instructs people to scan or rotate. She added that a functional spatial medium need not be a literal photograph examined by an inner eye; it is a representation whose format preserves spatial relations and lets processes access them directly.

Eli said that preserving spatial relations is not yet a depictive format. A list of coordinates preserves spatial relations, but it is not picture-like; it is a description. The real question is whether imagery uses an analog spatial code or whether a structured description plus spatial predicates can explain the same results. He also noted that if imagery were genuinely pictorial, people should be able to reinterpret their own mental images the way they reinterpret an ambiguous drawing. But when people are asked to form an image of an ambiguous figure and then find the other interpretation, they often fail unless they are given a cue. That, he said, is easier to explain if the image encodes the interpretation the person had, not the raw geometry.

Mara replied that failures of reinterpretation reflect limitations of memory and attention in the visual buffer, not the absence of a spatial format. She then pressed the reverse challenge: the propositional account can be made consistent with any outcome. If scanning time tracks distance, the description includes metric distances; if it did not, the description would omit them. What result would show that a propositional code is wrong? The depictive account at least makes risky predictions about linearity, about intermediate positions being traversed, and about small features becoming available when an image is expanded.

Eli answered that a propositional system can make risky predictions too, once its processing assumptions are specified; the bare picture-versus-language contrast is too coarse. He also pointed to neuroimaging: imagery does activate retinotopic visual cortex, but that shows only that some spatially organized brain tissue is involved. It does not show that the representational format used by cognition is depictive, because the same regions could be active during conscious imagery without being the computational medium in which the relevant information is encoded. The question is about the architecture of the code, not about where the activity happens.

They went around once more on that point, with Mara insisting that the simplest explanation of the full pattern—linear scanning and rotation, inspection and re-inspection, expansion revealing detail—is a format that itself has spatial structure, and Eli insisting that every one of those phenomena can be generated by operations on descriptions once we factor in the participants' knowledge and the task. At that point the meeting ended, and I was left unsure whether either side had really landed a decisive blow.

Please adjudicate the argument as I have reported it. For each main point in the exchange, say who is right and why, and say what, if anything, that point does or does not establish about the format of imagery. Then say who has the better of the argument overall, and what both parties are missing or conflating. I am not asking for references or a survey of the literature; I want a careful analysis of these specific arguments.
