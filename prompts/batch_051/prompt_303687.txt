I am a novelist working on a character whose professional sense of duty slowly becomes indistinguishable from a refusal to meet other people's faces. I would like a careful reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's novel The Remains of the Day through Levinas's claim that ethics is first philosophy: before any principle, judgement, or calculation there is the face of the other, exposed and destitute, already demanding a response, and a responsibility I have not chosen. Where does Stevens actually stand before such a face — when Miss Kenton comes to tell him that his father has died, when he carries out the dismissal of the two Jewish maids — and how does his first-person narration register or deflect the demand? Does the novel vindicate Levinas's insistence that the face makes its demand without the help of any social institution, or does it suggest something Levinas underplays, namely that a household and a life of professional training can render that demand genuinely illegible to the person who looks? I am less interested in whether Ishiguro knew Levinas than in where the novel forces the philosophy to be revised, and what the form of the novel can show about unresponsiveness that an essay in ethics cannot. Please answer in British English.
