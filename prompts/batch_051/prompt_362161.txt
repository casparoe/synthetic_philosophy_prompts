I am preparing a unit for a course in the philosophy of language, and I want to open it with a definition of language that does not simply take spoken or written language as its paradigm. In a recent seminar a colleague claimed that sign languages are not real languages because they lack a written form and are produced with the hands, and I found myself unable to state, quickly and precisely, what principle of the concept of language that claim offends against. I suspect the everyday concept has been built around speech and then patched to include sign languages as a special case, and I would like to test whether a cleaner definition is available.

Please propose a working definition of a human language that counts American Sign Language and British Sign Language as full languages in the same sense in which Tamil or Vietnamese is a language, and that counts the honeybee waggle dance as not a language. Then run the definition against these harder cases, giving a definite verdict on each and saying why: (1) homesign, the gesture system an isolated deaf child builds without exposure to a signed language; (2) the early, less grammatically regular stages of Nicaraguan Sign Language, before it became the full language it later became; (3) a programming language such as Python, which has syntactic rules, recursion, and a community of users; (4) whistled Turkish, which encodes Turkish speech in whistles; and (5) the two-word utterances of a toddler acquiring a first language. Where the definition yields no answer or an implausible answer, say so rather than smoothing it over, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. I care less about where the boundary falls than about the principle that draws it, and I especially want a definition that does not penalize signed languages for not being spoken or written.
