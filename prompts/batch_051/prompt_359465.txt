Reading Benacerraf has been giving me a feeling of déjà vu from my years in systematics, and I can't tell whether its deep or just pattern-matching. His dilemma, as I've absorbed it, is this. If numbers are mind-independent objects that never enter causal relations, then on a causal theory of knowledge we could never get into epistemic contact with them; but if we deny that picture, we owe an alternative story about what mathematical truth consists in. Now the parallel. On the 'species are individuals' view I was trained in, a species is a single spatiotemporally bounded historical entity, not a kind or class. You never observe that entity itself. You handle a few museum skins, tissue samples, or DNA aliquots, which are parts of it, and you reconstruct the whole individual from those parts. So the species seems to sit exactly where Benacerraf's numbers sit: the object of the knowledge claim isn't what you're causally connected to — only its parts are. I want to know whether a specimen is a causal link to the species in a way a numeral or a proof is not a link to a number, and if so what's doing the work: parthood, spatiotemporal continuity, something else? And if specimens do solve the access problem for species, does that point to a way out of the dilemma for numbers, or does the analogy break down somewhere that makes it superficial? Also, when you take a side, tell me which consideration would most likely flip your verdict, or where your reasoning is least secure.
