I am on my high school's ethics bowl team, and my teammate, whom I will call S, has a habit that makes her a useful sparring partner and a maddening one. Whenever we work through a case, she writes down a one-line ruling for herself and refuses to revise it, no matter how hard the rest of us push. We have spent the last few weeks on advance directives, and her ledger now runs as follows. A woman had signed a written directive refusing a ventilator if she ever became permanently unconscious; after a car accident left her in exactly that state, S ruled that the directive should be honored. A man with schizophrenia, while fully lucid, had signed an agreement to accept antipsychotic medication even if he later refused it; in a later psychotic episode he refused and called the agreement part of some plot, and S ruled that he should be treated over his current objection. A woman in the early stages of dementia had repeatedly told her daughter, in passing, that she never wanted to end up on machines, but she never wrote anything down or discussed the details; after a stroke left her unable to communicate, S ruled that the remark was not binding and that a ventilator should be used according to her best interests. And a man had written a directive refusing a feeding tube if he reached the point of no longer recognizing his family and no longer eating; he now recognizes no one, cries frequently, has stopped eating, and each time a tube is placed he pulls it out and becomes more agitated, and S ruled that the refusal should be honored.

The new case is one we invented, and it is not in the packet. A woman in the early stages of Alzheimer's disease writes a directive stating that if her dementia advances to the point where she no longer recognizes her children, she refuses antibiotics for any infection, even an ordinary pneumonia, and she adds explicitly that the refusal is to be honored even if she seems happy and content at that time. She now has advanced dementia; she does not recognize her children, but she laughs throughout their visits, enjoys meals and music, and the staff describe her as one of the most content residents in the unit. She develops a pneumonia that antibiotics would almost certainly cure. If S is to remain consistent with her own ledger, what should she rule? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the principle or principles she seems to be applying, whether her four prior rulings genuinely pin down a single rule or leave several distinct rules that fit them equally well, which of the earlier cases is the closest analog, and which further case would best tell the remaining rules apart.
