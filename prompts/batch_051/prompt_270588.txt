Consider the following case, of the sort I use to open a seminar on causal inference and the epistemology of evidence-based policy. An employment agency runs two training programs for unemployed workers, P and Q, and follows one thousand participants for a year. Participants are classified at intake by work history as either long-term unemployed or recently unemployed. Assignment to P or Q was made through the agency's ordinary intake procedures, not by randomization. The re-employment outcomes are as follows:

| Work-history group | Program P | Program Q |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term unemployed | 90 of 300 re-employed (30%) | 20 of 100 re-employed (20%) |
| Recently unemployed | 160 of 200 re-employed (80%) | 300 of 400 re-employed (75%) |
| All participants | 250 of 500 re-employed (50%) | 320 of 500 re-employed (64%) |

The agency reads the aggregate row as evidence that Q is the more effective program. A critic objects that the table exhibits Simpson's paradox: within each work-history group, P's re-employment rate is higher than Q's; the aggregate reversal arises because Q served a far larger share of recently unemployed participants, who tend to find work at higher rates under either program.

My question concerns your credence in the critic's causal claim. State a single number for your credence in the following proposition: on average, within each of the two work-history groups, assignment to P rather than Q makes re-employment within a year more likely. In other words, how likely is it that P is the genuinely more effective program once work history is held fixed, and the pooled comparison is a compositional artifact rather than evidence about program effectiveness? I do not want only a number or only a verdict; I want the number together with the derivation that produces it.

In deriving the number, identify the main sub-questions on which it depends. These might include, among others: how likely it is that assignment to P versus Q was effectively unconfounded within each work-history group; how confident you are that the two work-history categories form the right partition and capture the relevant confounders; how much of the within-group advantage could plausibly be due to sampling error; and whether one-year re-employment is the right outcome to give content to the phrase "more effective." Assign a rough probability to each sub-question you invoke, explain how they combine to determine your overall credence, and state which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to. Finally, tell me what single additional piece of evidence would do the most to move your credence.
