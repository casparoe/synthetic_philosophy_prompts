I keep running into the slogan "the mind is not in the head" — or, in its more modern form, "your phone is part of your mind" — and I can't tell how much of it I should actually take literally. I first met it in a cognitive science course, where it did fairly modest work: the lecturer used it to say that much of everyday cognition happens only with tools, notations, and other people in the loop, as when you do long division with a pencil or navigate with a chart rather than with an internal map. Since then I've seen the same one-liner put to much stronger uses. In one online discussion, someone argued that a person with severe memory loss who relies on a well-organized notebook literally remembers her appointments, because the notebook is part of her memory. In another, someone said there is no principled boundary between me and my devices, so that deleting a person's files can be a kind of assault on their mind. A colleague, meanwhile, uses it to end arguments about AI by saying that a language model's training data is part of its "mind."

I gather that some version of this is a real philosophical position — perhaps called the extended mind thesis — but I'm fuzzy on exactly what it says, and it seems to me that "the mind is not in the head" covers several claims that run together. Then tell me which reading was doing the real work in each of the uses I described above — in particular, whether the notebook-as-memory case needs the strong claim that mental states themselves extend, or only some weaker claim about external storage functioning like internal memory.
