Write a dialogue of exactly five exchanges between two advisors to a newly enthroned ruler who wants to know whether to govern through a legalist system of public standards (fa), clear rewards and punishments, and the assumption that people are reliably self-interested — or through the cultivation of virtue: ritual, education, shame, and moral example, from the top down.

Advisor A is the legalist, Han Feizi-ish: he argues that virtue is rare, unverifyable, and unequally distributed, so any system that depends on it will be gamed; only fixed public standards and penalties can make even a mediocre ruler govern well.
Advisor B is the Confucian-style defender of rule by virtue: he argues that a system of incentives silently presupposes some prior stock of honesty and loyalty, otherwise the standards themselves get bent, sold, or enforced corruptly, and the whole thing collapses.

They should focus on one question the whole way through: does a well-designed system of rewards and punishments presuppose at least some virtue it cannot itself produce or detect? Each exchange must begin by referencing something the other just said — restate it, then answer it. Both must be equally sharp; no strawmen, no one wins, and give them a real back-and-forth rather than two parallel speeches.

Format: five numbered exchanges, no introduction or conclusion, each exchange a sentence or two per character.
