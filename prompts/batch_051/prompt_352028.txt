I am a novelist researching a book whose central conflict begins when a pianist, a recording engineer, and a music critic inherit a small archive tied to a single invented piano piece. The characters disagree about what exactly they have inherited: the music itself, evidence of it, a set of instructions, or something else. The dispute turns practical almost immediately, because they must decide what may be performed, what may be reissued, what counts as a restoration, and who gets a say. I want to run a workshop in which a group of non-philosophers runs into this same disagreement and feels its force before anyone hands them the vocabulary for it.

The group is twelve people, a mix of practicing musicians and non-musicians, meeting for a single three-hour evening. There should be no lecture up front, and the phrase "ontology of musical works" should not appear until after the debrief. The centerpiece should be a structured small-group exercise in which participants have to do something rather than discuss abstractions: they should classify, trade, license, restore, perform, or set policy for a small set of candidate objects built around one invented short work for solo piano. The candidate objects should include at least a manuscript score with a real notational ambiguity, a performance from memory, a live broadcast that was never stored, an edited studio recording assembled from many takes, a bit-perfect digital transfer, a historically informed reconstruction, and one deliberately vexing case of your own invention. Do not build it around actual repertoire or real recordings.

Design the activity with roles or teams, clear rules, a concrete objective, and consequences or points that do not use real money. The mechanics should be arranged so that a split emerges among the participants themselves before it is named from the front: for example, two groups beginning with apparently compatible assumptions end up assigning the same object to different categories, or a role is forced to break its own announced rule. The idea should show up in what happens, not in what the facilitator explains afterward.

Write me the full runnable activity: materials, setup, rules, timeline with time per phase, what the facilitator says at the start and at the transition, and a debrief that maps the moments of breakdown onto the relevant distinctions of work, performance, recording, copy, and restoration. Be concrete about what the participants should come away seeing. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce on its own and therefore has to be supplied by the facilitator at the end.
