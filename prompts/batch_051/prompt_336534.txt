Clause 12(b) of a teaching hospital's research-consent code reads: "No subject may be enrolled in a trial unless the subject has signed a statement affirming: 'I know that I have understood the material risks and alternatives to participation.'" The clause was introduced after a scandal in which several subjects signed consent forms while misunderstanding the treatment's most basic risks.

A prospective subject, R, passes every first-order test the clinic can devise: she restates the risks accurately in her own words, catches a deliberate misstatement by a researcher, and answers hypothetical questions about how she would respond if each risk materialised. The hospital's own capacity examiner finds her understanding thorough and genuine. But R refuses to sign the affirmation. She does not call the risks themselves into doubt; she grants that she can state and reason about them perfectly. What she denies is that she knows that she has understood them. Understanding, she says, is not the kind of state one can reliably detect from the inside, so signing the form would assert a second-order claim she is not entitled to make. The drafters anticipated subjects who overestimate their understanding; they did not anticipate a demonstrably competent subject who refuses the higher-order certification.

Does the clause bar R's enrolment? Analyse this from the text alone, from the clause's purpose, from what its drafters would most plausibly have said if asked, and from what a reasonable clinician applying the code would require; say which approach should govern and why. Then address the epistemic issue underneath: if the KK principle held in this domain—if anyone who knows the risks already knows that they know them—would the affirmation add anything? If instead understanding is not luminous, and first-order competence can outrun second-order assurance, what has the clause actually screened for, and should informed-consent rules impose higher-order conditions at all?
