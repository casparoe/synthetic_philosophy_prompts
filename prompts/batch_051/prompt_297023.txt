I'm a philosophy of mathematics PhD student filling in for a historian colleague in a second-year undergraduate course called "What Counts in Mathematics?" The students have calculus and a little linear algebra but no background in the history of mathematics. I have a single seventy-five-minute session to move them through one arc: how the criterion for a legitimate mathematical object shifted from the Greek demand that objects be constructible with straightedge and compass, through the early modern debate between "geometric" and merely "mechanical" curves (the move on which Descartes admitted algebraic curves like the conics while excluding the spiral and the quadratrix), and eventually to the acceptance of transcendental curves and the modern tendency to let objects enter mathematics through definition and theory rather than through construction by an instrument. Last week they saw the three classical construction problems and the limits of straightedge and compass; next week the course turns to the nineteenth-century rethinking of existence and axiomatics. My job is to make this session do the conceptual work that connects those two sessions.

I do not want a lecture script; I want a teaching plan. Please lay out: the order in which the component ideas must be introduced, with a clear statement of what each step presupposes and what it has to discharge before the next can be understood; the one carrying example that should run through the whole session (I had considered bisection versus trisection of an angle, but I would like to know whether that is the best choice and, in general, what a carrying example here needs to have and not have); the misconceptions students predictably bring or form — such as reading the modern existence question back into antiquity, treating "illegitimate" as "not really existing," taking the straightedge-and-compass restriction as arbitrary conservatism, or supposing that "mechanical" meant merely "imprecise" — and for each one, the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off; a single final exercise or question by which I can tell whether they have understood that the standard of legitimacy is a shifting disciplinary achievement rather than a fixed test for mathematical reality; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.
