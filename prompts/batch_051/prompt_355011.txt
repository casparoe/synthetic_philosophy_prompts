I'm a retired plant ecologist, so before I got into philosophy I spent thirty years thinking about why some things spread and others don't — invasives, pests, fire regimes, that kind of thing. The last year or so I've been reading philosophy of biology, and I keep coming back to memes and the Darwinian theory of culture. I've got rough notes for an essay, and I'd like real comments, not reassurance.

Act as a philosopher of biology who works on cultural evolution and isn't impressed by the word meme. Take the time to think it through. Start by reconstructing my argument in your own words (I want to see whether it actually hangs together), then give me a committed response: say flat out which of my claims you'd cut, which you'd defend, and whether the core objection is fatal or fixable. Don't do the thing where you list six possible objections and leave me to pick.

Here are the notes.

Working title: The habitat problem for Darwinian theories of culture.

Claim 1. The gene analogy quietly assumes culture comes in discrete, countable, faithfully copied units. In genetics the unit is anchored by a material substrate — DNA plus a copying machinery with a measureable error rate. In culture the unit is whatever the investigator decides to count: a word, a melody, a handshake, a recipe, a belief, a whole ritual. So the meme isn't discovered; it's scored.

Claim 2. I don't want to make the usual complaint that memes are vague. My point is different. Even if we grant countable units, the Darwinian triad of variation, replication, and selection only gets you change, not adaptation. Adaptation requires an environment that supplies a consistent selective gradient. Genes mostly face an environment that exists apart from them — drought, predators, temperature. Memes face an environment that is mostly other memes plus the bottlenecks of human cognition. The landscape is constantly re-landscaped by the very traits under selection.

Claim 3. If the fitness landscape is endogenous like that, the usual payoff of Darwinism goes missing. In biology, selection explains apparent design by appealing to an environment that isn't itself designed by the organism. For memes, saying a cultural item spread because it was fit risks circularity: fit means it spread, it spread because it was fit. Unless we can measure meme fitness independently of spread, we have a vocabulary, not an explanation.

Claim 4. The constructive move. Keep the selection machinery but stop treating the gene as the template. Think of cultural evolution as more like ecosystem succession or host–parasite dynamics: the interesting question is not which unit wins but what makes a cultural trait invasive, endemic, or obligately symbiotic. A meme doesn't need sharp boundaries; it needs a niche. That's why some ideas survive only inside institutions (obligate symbionts), some are generalists, and some are weeds that bloom after a disturbance. I'd rather talk about niche-dependence and landscape engineering than about fads and traditions.

Where I'm stuck: I can't tell whether this is a real scientific difference or just a change of metaphor. Does the niche/ecology version actually make different predictions, or does it just rephrase the standard story in fancier language? And does the circularity charge land against Dawkins's original modest claim, or only against the stronger memetics-as-science program?

Also, I have a nagging worry I've accidentaly reinvented some older cultural-epidemiology idea under new labels, but I haven't read enough to know. If that's what I'm doing, say so plainly and tell me what my labels are papering over.

Go hard. I'd rather find out now that claim 3 is broken than after I wright 3,000 words.
