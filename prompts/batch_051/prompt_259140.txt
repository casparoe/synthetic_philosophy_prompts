Last year I got dragged onto my lake association's rules committee — we're a volunteer group that drafts and vets the rules for docks, noise, short-term rentals, boat traffic, all of it. We meet monthly, and anything we pass goes to the full board for a vote with our part-time attorney checking enforceability at the end. We got burned a few months ago by a rule that fined people for 'excessive wake' near the swimming beach; it was so vague we enforced it one way in July and another in August, and now a neighbor is threatening a formal appeal. There's three proposals in front of us next month and I want us to stop winging it and start checking each one against the ideal of the rule of law before it leaves our committee.

You're a philosopher of law who works on the rule of law as an ideal — Fuller, Raz, and any of Waldron's doubts you think are relevant. Start by brainstorming the distinct ways a rule can fail as law, then turn that into a reusable procedure. I need a checklist my committee can actually run on a proposed rule in under an hour. We're not lawyers, and half of us are going to read it in the parking lot before the meeting, so the steps have to be plain English — but I don't want them dumbed down so far they lose the distinctions that matter. I especially want it to be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

The backbone should be whatever those thinkers actually say about rules being general, made public, not retroactive, clear, consistent with each other, possible to comply with, stable over time, and actually administered the way they're written — however you want to translate that family of ideas into questions we can ask in order. But here's the part that keeps me up. A rule could pass all eight and still be a lousy, unfair rule, and I don't want our checklist to give us a clean-conscience stamp when that happens. From what I dimly remember, that's Waldron's kind of point. So build in a step that tells us 'this rule is procedurally lawful but that says nothing about whether it's just' — and tell us what we're supposed to do with that when we still have to vote yes or no.

Then show me the checklist by running it on three real-ish cases: (1) a rule banning 'unreasonably loud watercraft' that we proposed after one person bought a cigarette boat; (2) a rule that retroactively charges last year's dock permit holders for the new seawall; and (3) a rule our attorney says is bulletproof — a flat 9 p.m. quiet time, clearly posted, applied equally — but everyone on the committee knows it's aimed at one family whose kid has autism and plays music in the evening because it helps him settle. I'm fine getting an answer I don't like from the procedure, as long as you say exactly what the procedure can and can't do and where a human has to decide.
