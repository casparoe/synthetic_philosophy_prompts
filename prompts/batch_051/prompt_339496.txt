Charles Goodhart wrote in his 1975 article on UK monetary policy, "Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience":

"Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."

I work in the quality-improvement office of a large public hospital system. My team designs performance dashboards for clinical departments and individual clinicians. Each quarter we propose a metric and a target (for example, average length of stay, patient satisfaction percentile, door-to-antibiotic time, risk-adjusted readmission rate), and the metrics affect public reporting, departmental funding, and individual performance reviews. We have limited analyst time, so we need a reusable procedure for deciding, before a metric goes to the board, whether it is likely to collapse in the way Goodhart describes—through specification gaming or other pressure—or whether it can withstand being used for control purposes.

Please respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Item 1 should state the first screening question we should ask about any proposed metric, and what counts as a sufficient answer to pass to item 2. Item 2 should state the second question, focused on whether the metric is an observed statistical regularity in Goodhart's sense and whether the proposed target places pressure on it. Item 3 should state the stop rule: the conditions under which the procedure returns a no, and the conditions under which it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Item 4 should apply the procedure to these two proposed metrics: (a) a target that each department's patient satisfaction scores fall in the top quartile nationally, and (b) a target that risk-adjusted 30-day readmission rates fall below 12 percent. For each, it should state what the procedure recommends, even if that recommendation is one a hospital board would not want to hear. Item 5 should state the main limitation of the procedure: the point at which Goodhart's law cannot tell us what to do and a values judgment is unavoidable.
