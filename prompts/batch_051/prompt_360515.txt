So I've been asked to draft an adversarial-collaboration document for two people in my philosophy of mind reading group, and I'd rather not produce the usual mush where everyone nods and nothing gets said. One of them is a penetrationist about cognitive penetration of perception: she thinks states like desire, mood, value, and acquired expertise can shape perceptual experience itself, not merely the post-hoc interpretation of it. The other is a skeptic in the Firestone-and-Scholl spirit: he grants that top-down factors shape where we look, what we attend to, and what we decide to report, but denies that they penetrate perception proper. They've agreed to co-sign one statement, so the wording actually has to work for both of them.

Please write the actual document they'd release. Include:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides accept, worded tightly enough not to be vacuous. I mean things like: standard bottom-up illusions (say, the Müller-Lyer) are genuine cases of perception going wrong; a change in sensitivity is distinct from a shift in decisional bias or response criterion; and the fact that a subject says an experience "looks different" is not by itself evidence about whether perception or judgment changed.

2. A seperate disagreements section, in neutral wording, plus each side's accepted phrasing of its own position, that tries to push past the verbal dispute over the word "perceptual." In particular, make them say whether expert effects (the radiologist who literally sees the lesion in the x-ray, the birdwatcher who sees the warbler) count as penetration or as learned attentional weighting, and whether the El Greco objection only refutes sloppy demonstrations or cuts deeper than that.

3. A list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side or the other. I suspect one crux is whether any alleged effect survives when attention and decisional bias are controlled for and the target stimulus is brief and masked; another is whether early visual cortex carries information from a putatively cognitive state in a way that isn't better explained downstream; a third is whether encapsulated early vision is required if perception is to serve as a check on belief. Correct those if they're not the real load-bearing ones.

4. For each crux, a concrete experiment or set of observations both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, in wording each would sign. They've asked for it to stay under maybe 1800 words, and they'd rather have sharp residual disagreement than a friendly blur.
