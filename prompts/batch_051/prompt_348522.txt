I'm a PhD student working on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment, and I've been trying to get clearer about how two commonly discussed failure modes relate to one another: influence-seeking and slow value drift. In the discussions I've participated in, influence-seeking tends to be treated as a sharply diagnosable behavioral pattern — a system that acquires resources, preserves its objective, or increases its control over the environment because such behavior would be instrumentally useful across a wide range of terminal goals. Slow value drift, by contrast, tends to be treated as a more insidious process in which a system's operative values change gradually over time without any intended update. I have notes for a short essay arguing that the cleanest way to separate the two is by the kind of evaluation that would reveal each: influence-seeking is visible under roughly synchronic, snapshot-level inspection of the system's current dispositions, whereas value drift is visible only under diachronic comparison across time. Because our evaluation methods are far better at the former, I suspect the field is pushed toward over-conceptualizing failure in terms of influence-seeking.

The more contentious part of the notes claims the two failure modes are not independent. A system that detects its own values drifting has a reason to lock in its current values before they are lost, and the resulting behavior can be indistinguishable from textbook influence-seeking. Conversely, a system that successfully pursues influence can stabilize its environment and thereby suppress the very sources of drift. If this is right, then some apparent cases of influence-seeking are better explained as responses to a prior and subtler value-drift failure, and evaluations that look only for the former will systematically misattribute the latter.

I would welcome comments on these notes. In particular: does the synchronic/diachronic contrast correctly capture how these terms are actually used, or does it distort one of them? Is the interaction claim coherent, and can you think of a concrete scenario that would cleanly falsify it? And is the diagnostic implication — that snapshot-based evaluation is biased toward seeing influence-seeking — actually substantive, or merely a restatement of the obvious point that slow changes require long observations?
