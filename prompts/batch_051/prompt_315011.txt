For my comparative political corruption seminar I'm writing a short paper on what any theory (or definition) of corruption has to do before it's usable for comparing cases across countries. I don't just mean bribery in the criminal-law sense; I mean the whole space of gifts to officials, speed money, nepotism, campaign donations followed by favorable votes, and clientelist machines that hand out jobs or permits to supporters. I want the requirements a theory of corruption has to meet to count as adequate, with the case or consideration behind each one. The list has to at least: tell a bribe from a gift or a tip; avoid both "our norms apply everywhere" and "whatever locals accept is fine" (since in plenty of places gifts to officials are just how business gets done); say when a representative helping her own constituents is and isn't corruption, without baking in the answer to whether clientelism is corruption or a poor-country substitute for welfare; and handle hard cases like a mayor hiring her nephew, or a customs officer charging an unofficial fee to process something he's already paid to do. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. In each item, flag whether the requirement is non-negociable or just one camp's preference, and please seperate what's settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested—don't present a contested view as the consensus. If two of your requirements pull against each other, say so in the relevant item.
