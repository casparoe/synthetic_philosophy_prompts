Next month I'm doing a best man speech, and because I'm me I've gone down a rabbit hole trying to work out why people laugh before I write a single joke. I've been reading Herbert Spencer's essay _On the Physiology of Laughter_. The passage that's lodged in my brain is where he describes laughter as what happens when nervous energy, already summoned up for some expected feeling or action, is suddenly blocked, and then takes the nearest available exit through the muscles of breathing, voice, and face. I find that picture oddly convincing, and I don't want to just abandon it.

But I have a bunch of other commitments about humour and I need a consistency check before I write the speech. I hold:

(a) Spencer's relief story is right as the physiological basis of laughter.

(b) Incongruity is the cognitive heart of humour: the laugh happens when an expectation is violated, especially when the violation is then resolved.

(c) Benign violation is the best explanation of why only some incongruities are funny: the thing has to be seen as a violation but also as safe or not serious.

(d) Superiority is not a myth. Some put-down humour and a fair bit of Schadenfreude is best explained by the pleasure of feeling above someone, without any real incongruity or resolution.

(e) A good theory of humour should not be a toolbox. If we need four independent mechanisms, we have got the wrong level of description.

Where am I actually in trouble? Go through the pairs, say which are real tensions and which only look like tensions, rank the real ones by how much they cost, and tell me the cheapest single revision that would make the package hang together. Please write the answer in British English.
