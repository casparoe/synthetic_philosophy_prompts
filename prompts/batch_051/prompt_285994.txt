For some years I have watched a recurring argument between American and European colleagues that never seems to advance. When an American says that liberals support an expanded welfare state, higher taxes, and government regulation, a European replies that this is a misuse of the term: properly understood, liberalism is the creed of free markets, limited government, and individual liberty against the state, and many policies the American calls liberal are, on the European usage, closer to illiberal social democracy. Each side then accuses the other of having a debased or provincial vocabulary, and the conversation ends there. The same impasse appears in print: writers on either side of the Atlantic use the word with evidently different extensions and different evaluative colouring, and no amount of pointing to dictionaries or national histories moves anyone.

I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a survey of the word's history. Is it merely verbal — two concepts sharing a label — such that the sensible move is to stipulate definitions and carry on? Or is there a substantive dispute underneath: about which political tradition is the legitimate heir of the liberal inheritance; about whether liberal principles really commit one to a positive or a negative conception of liberty; about how much weight to give to the historical self-understanding of a movement as against the implications of its founding commitments? My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not answering the same question. One is using liberal as a party-political label anchored in twentieth-century American coalitions; the other is using it as the name of an intellectual tradition anchored in nineteenth-century European thought; and the residual heat is a dispute over legitimacy and self-definition rather than over the definition of a term. Is that right, or is there a genuine normative disagreement that survives disambiguation?

Please diagnose the disagreement concretely, drawing on what each side actually says, and then say what would count as progress given the diagnosis — a distinction, a reformulated question, a historical or empirical finding — or, if no progress should be expected, why not. I am less interested in a verdict on who uses the word correctly than in understanding why the debate stalls and what its persistence reveals about the two sides.
