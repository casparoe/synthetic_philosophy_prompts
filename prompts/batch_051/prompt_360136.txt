I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been trying to get clear on a debate in philosophy of mind. For concreteness, I have been thinking of perception as a pipeline with an early feature-extraction stage and a later interpretation stage. Cognitive penetration of perception, as I am using the term, is the claim that standing cognitive states such as beliefs, desires, and expectations can directly alter the outputs of the early perceptual stage — that is, alter perceptual experience itself — rather than merely alter how those outputs get interpreted downstream.

I am compiling objections to that claim. So far I have collected these five:

1. The response criterion objection: in many of the classic demonstrations, what the belief or expectation changes is the participant's judgment, report, or response threshold, not the perceptual experience itself. The supposed top-down effect is on post-perceptual processing.
2. The attention objection: many apparent cases are really cases where a belief or desire redirects attention, and attention then changes what is seen. Because everyone agrees attention can shape perception, these cases do not show a direct channel from belief to experience.
3. The perceptual learning objection: some cases reflect long-term recalibration or learned associations built up before the episode of seeing. Those are changes to the system's parameters over time, not an online causal influence of a currently held belief or desire.
4. The modularity objection: early perceptual processing may be informationally encapsulated, meaning its computations take only sensory input as data. If the architecture has no input channel from belief or desire into that stage, direct cognitive penetration is impossible, not merely poorly evidenced.
5. The stubborn illusion objection: in displays such as the Müller-Lyer illusion, the illusion persists even when the viewer knows the true lengths. If beliefs could penetrate visual experience, knowing the truth should at least weaken the illusion; it does not.

I want a sixth objection that is not on this list and is not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of these five. Ideally it should have a different structure from all of them rather than being a variation on one mechanism. Then I want you to be honest about whether the sixth objection is any good.

1. The missing objection (150–250 words): State the additional objection precisely enough that it could sit on a list with my five.
2. How it differs (100–180 words): Explain why this is not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of the five. If it superficially resembles one of them, say exactly where the resemblance ends.
3. A defender's reply (100–180 words): Give the strongest response someone who defends cognitive penetration could make to your new objection.
4. My assessment (100–150 words): Say whether you think the objection is a genuine addition, and whether it is ultimately compelling.
