Write a rigorous, evenly matched dialogue between two cognitive scientists who disagree about the nature of mental imagery. The depictivist holds that mental images are representations in a genuinely picture-like, depictive format: they preserve metric spatial structure, and the mind processes them by exploiting that structure. The descriptivist holds that imagery is propositional thought in a symbolic code, and that the experience of "picturing" is an epiphenomenal accompaniment rather than a format that does real explanatory work.

The dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth in which each character responds to the other's strongest points rather than talking past them. It should work through at least the following: (i) the classic mental rotation results, where response time increases with angular disparity, and what they do and do not establish about representational format; (ii) image scanning results, where time to "move" across an imaged scene increases with distance, and the descriptivist's reply that subjects may be simulating what they tacitly know about seeing; (iii) the homunculus worry — if images are pictures, who or what reads the picture — and how a depictivist might answer it without introducing an inner viewer; and (iv) the fact that people are strikingly poor at reinterpreting their own mental images, for example finding the alternative construal of an ambiguous figure they have already encoded, and what this asymmetry between public pictures and private images shows.

Neither side should be a strawman. Each should concede the points where their view is genuinely vulnerable, then show what their view would have to look like in order to absorb the concession. End at a point of clarified disagreement, with each side stating the single empirical or conceptual development that would most move them.
