On a forum I follow, someone posted a summary of the information-cascade result, and it is now being used to support a strong deference policy in everyday judgment. I want to know how accurate the summary is before I import it into my epistemic practice. The post ran roughly as follows:

Economists have shown that a group of fully rational, Bayesian agents can end up unanimously choosing an option even when most of them privately believe it is wrong. Each agent observes a private signal about which option is better, and also observes the choices of everyone before her. Because those earlier choices count as evidence, once a few early movers happen to pick A, later agents rationally discount their own contrary signals and follow A. The cascade is individually rational, yet the group can lock onto a false consensus. Moreover, once a cascade has started it cannot be broken by further private information; only new public information or an external shock can dislodge it. The practical lesson is that when many independent, informed people have made the same choice, their pooled choices contain more information than your own judgment, so the rational response is to defer to the crowd, unless you have reason to think the early movers were not independent.

I am inclined to accept most of this, because it matches the standard rational-herding picture and seems to license a clean policy: discount my own disagreement whenever the crowd is large and apparently independent. But two parts make me uneasy: the claim that a wrong cascade cannot be broken by private information, and the leap from "each individual in the sequence is rational" to "I, as an observer, should defer to the crowd." I would like an accuracy audit. Which parts of the summary faithfully capture the result, which parts conflate distinct claims, and does the deference-to-the-crowd conclusion survive once those distinctions are in place? If not, what is the more careful practical takeaway?
