Two people I know got into it about counterfactual mugging and I honestly can't tell who's right. The setup they agreed on was the standard one: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a coin. If tails, Omega asks you for $100. If heads, Omega gives you $10,000 if it predicted that you would have paid in the tails case. The actual flip was tails, and Omega asked for the $100.

My roommate says you shouldn't pay. His argument is that the coin already landed tails, so the heads branch is dead. There is no future in which your hundred dollars turns into ten thousand. He says this is just causal decision theory being right: your action now can't cause a prediction Omega made before the flip. He also said the only way paying makes sense is if you had precommitted before the coin flip, and since you didn't, that ship has sailed.

My friend from work says you should pay. Her line is that paying isn't about causing the prediction, it's about selecting a policy. If you're the kind of agent who pays when asked, then Omega's prediction in the heads branch is favorable, so you end up with $10,000 in those cases. She said if you refuse, you're optimizing the branch you happen to find yourself in and getting looted by Omega. She says you don't get to condition on the coin after the fact; you should act on the policy that was best before the flip. She called his refusal "just losing by insisting on local causation."

Then my roommate came back and said that's insane because Omega's prediction was already made, and your brain state on tails can't reach back and edit the past. He said this is just Newcomb's problem with extra steps, except in Newcomb the money is at least in the same branch. He said your essentially paying for a lottery ticket that was already drawn.

She said no, it's not Newcomb's problem, because in Newcomb the reward is in the same branch, while here the payoff is in the other possible branch, so the disagreement is exactly about whether your decision procedure should treat other branches as part of the outcome. She said he's refusing to count the heads branch even though the whole point of the scenario is that Omega's prediction ties the branches together.

I want a point-by-point verdict. Who is right about which specific claim? Is this a real disagreement about decision theory, or are they just using "prediction" differently? And does the answer depend on which decision theory you already accept, or is one of them making a mistake even by their own lights?
