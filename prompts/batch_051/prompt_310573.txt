I have been trying to get my own views about deference into order — when it is a virtue and when it is a failure — because I find I have quite stable verdicts on particular cases but no clear statement of the principle behind them. The cases below are ones I collected from conversations and from a course on the ethics of authority; my verdicts are mostly firm, though there are two that bother me because they seem to point in different directions. (1) During a mass-casualty exercise, a combat medic is told by a senior medical officer to set up triage in a way that departs slightly from written protocol in order to save time; the medic obeys. I call that fine. (2) A nurse sees that a physician has prescribed a dose above the printed range for the patient's condition; the physician says the usual range does not fit this patient's weight and history. The nurse administers it. I call that fine, though it gave me pause. (3) A soldier is ordered to fire on a building where civilians are likely present; the action is legal under the rules of engagement, but the soldier judges it unnecessary and morally wrong. I call it not fine to obey without pushing back — perhaps not fine to obey at all. (4) A junior analyst at a bank is told by a manager to sign a report describing a trading position's risk in a way that is technically within the letter of the rules but will mislead the recipient; the analyst signs. I call that not fine. (5) A student writes a paper using the professor's interpretation of a passage even though the student privately finds another reading more plausible. I call that fine. (6) A driver at a traffic stop is told to step out of the car and does so, though the driver believes there is no good reason. I call that fine. (7) A juror believes the judge is instructing the jury to apply a legal rule in a way the legislature did not intend; the juror follows the instruction. I call that fine — taking the law from the judge is part of the jury's role. (8) A civil servant faithfully implements a democratically enacted policy the civil servant believes is unjust but not atrocious. I call that fine, though I would want the objection registered internally. (9) A doctoral student defers to a supervisor's insistence and omits statistically real results that contradict the supervisor's published theory. I call that not fine.

The principle I keep flirting with is something like this: deference is a virtue when the other party is substantially better placed than I am to judge the matter, and is acting within the bounds of a role I have reason to respect. But I can already see the trouble. The nurse case and the analyst case look structurally similar on that principle — a junior person deferring to a senior who is better placed and acting within a recognized role — yet the first feels fine and the second does not. The soldier case is more troubling: the officer plainly has role-based authority, and the soldier's on-the-ground judgment is exactly the sort of thing one might distrust, but my verdict still comes out against deference. So either the principle is missing something, or some of my verdicts have to go.

Can you help me extract the principle? Propose a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, and indicate which clause of the principle each case is doing work for. Run it through all nine cases, say what it implies about a case I keep going back and forth on — an employee asked to brief the press with a statement that is technically true but deliberately misleading, on behalf of a supervisor — and be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate.
