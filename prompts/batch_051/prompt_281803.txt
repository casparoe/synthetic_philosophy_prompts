Fix the purpose as follows: a theory of mathematical proof or justification is adequate only if it explains when a mathematical community may rationally treat a claimed theorem as proved and known, without idealizing away the fact that its members are finite, fallible, and heavily dependent on one another. I am interested specifically in computer-assisted and unsurveyable proofs — the kind introduced by Appel and Haken's proof of the four-colour theorem and continued through Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture and its later Flyspeck formal verification. Rather than a survey of positions, I want the specification first: the full list of requirements such a theory must meet, with the consideration or case behind each. Cover at least these phenomena and distinctions, but do not stop at them: the difference between proving and checking, between surveyability and validity, between understanding and knowledge, and between a proof as an object and a proof as a social process of certification; the fact that the human-readable part of such a proof can be modest while the computational part is too large for any one mathematician to go through; the fact that traditional surveyable proofs are also error-prone; and the settled phenomenon that working mathematicians treat these theorems as proved. For each requirement, say what makes it a requirement and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp — surveyability essentialism, the view that only machine-checkable proof counts, a prioricity, epistemic individualism, and so on. Then flag any subsets of the list you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable. I specifically want to know whether the tension is real between wanting mathematical knowledge to be a priori and wanting results that rest on the actual behaviour of physical machines, and between wanting formal verification to be authoritative and wanting the formalized statement to be exactly the informal theorem people cared about. If other pairs collide, name them. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding or embarrassing the standard positions. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring named contenders, though a brief scorecard is acceptable; a table for the requirements is fine. End by saying how confident you are in this specification as a whole and which single requirement or alleged tension you are least sure about.
