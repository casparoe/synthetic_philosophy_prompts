Essay sketch: 'The Two Economies of Time.' Ordinary punctuality obligations seem to sit uneasily between promises and mere conventions. If I agree to meet someone at three o'clock, my lateness can wrong them even if they have not altered their plans in reliance on my arriving at three. But if I am habitually late to a recurring meeting whose time was fixed by group practice rather than by any explicit undertaking on my part, the wrongness of my lateness seems to depend on the fact that others organize their time around the schedule. The essay labels these two models the promise economy and the convention economy. In the promise economy, temporal obligations are created by discrete undertakings, and their content is fixed by what was undertaken. In the convention economy, temporal obligations are created and sustained by a shared practice of mutual accommodation, and their content is partly fixed by the pattern of reliance the practice makes reasonable.

The essay's central claim is that neither model, taken alone, captures ordinary small social commitments. Most punctuality obligations are hybrids: they begin as loose expectations, harden into quasi-promises through repeated accommodation, and can lapse back into convention when the group stops relying on them. The essay argues that neither economy reduces to the other. Convention cannot be reduced to promise, because lateness can be wrong even when nobody undertook anything; promise cannot be reduced to convention, because an explicitly agreed meeting time binds even when the other party would not have acted differently. A middle section considers the objection that the distinction is merely verbal, since both models ultimately concern reliance. The essay replies that the two models locate reliance differently: in the promise economy, the promisee's reliance is normatively downstream of the promise, whereas in the convention economy, shared reliance is itself the ground of the obligation.

The essay closes with a puzzle. If convention-based punctuality is grounded in actual patterns of reliance, then arriving late to an event where one's absence is entirely unnoticed should be only trivially wrong. Yet many people report that it is still wrong. The essay proposes that this residual wrongness is best explained by fairness: the latecomer treats the shared schedule as optional for himself while benefiting from the fact that others keep it, even if on this occasion no one was harmed or inconvenienced.

Please comment on this sketch. In particular: Is the asymmetry between promise-based and convention-based lateness characterized correctly, or does it rest on an equivocation in the notion of reliance? Does the closing fairness explanation succeed, or does it collapse back into the convention model it was meant to supplement? And what is the strongest objection to the central distinction that the sketch has not anticipated?
