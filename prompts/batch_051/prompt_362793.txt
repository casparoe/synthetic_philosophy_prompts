Proponents of active METI (messaging extraterrestrial intelligence) — deliberately transmitting powerful, targeted messages to nearby stars — hold that remaining silent imposes certain and accumulating costs: the loss of whatever a successful exchange would bring, and a standing policy of fear that forecloses a choice later generations might not get. Dark-forest quietists hold the opposite policy: minimize our electromagnetic signature and refuse beacon transmissions, because first contact is a one-shot, anonymous, irreversible gamble against actors about whom we know almost nothing, and the downside is plausibly existential. The two camps have argued for years and have agreed to produce a joint text.

Draft the joint statement that this adversarial collaboration should end with. It should contain four parts. First, a numbered section of claims both camps can sign without gutting hedges — covering what they agree on about the evidence, our signaling capability, the game-theoretic structure of contact, and the precise content of each side's recommendation. Second, a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement appears as a single neutral description with two columns, each column worded so that its camp would sign it as an accurate statement of its own view. Third, a list of cruxes: the factual or normative sub-questions that, if settled, would materially move at least one camp, stated so the other camp regards them as legitimate. Fourth, for each crux, the concrete observation, model, or historical case both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Where a disagreement is merely about words or about risk tolerance rather than about the world, say so explicitly; the document should not present a difference in utility function as if it were a disagreement about evidence.
