I am preparing a seminar exercise on the interpretation of confidentiality clauses in the public sector, and on the tension between such clauses and the public's right to know. Please treat this as a self-contained case; no outside research is needed.

The clause, from a settlement agreement between a government department and a senior civil servant who retired in 1994, reads as follows: "The Employee shall not at any time after the termination of the employment disclose to any person not authorised to receive it any information obtained by the Employee in the course of the employment and which is classified or otherwise designated as confidential, whether such disclosure be made directly or indirectly, and whether by words, documents, conduct, or any other means whatsoever."

A journalist has published a long article about a covert data-collection programme, setting out five specific claims about its methods, its legal basis, and its scale. Four of those claims are broadly accurate; the fifth is false. The retired civil servant, who worked on the programme, agrees to a recorded interview. For each of the four accurate claims he answers with the same sentence: "I am not in a position to discuss that." For the one false claim he gives a firm, spontaneous denial. He never states a single classified fact, and the words he uses contain no protected information. The journalist, who knows him to be scrupulous and unwilling to deceive, writes that the pattern of refusals followed by a denial has effectively confirmed the four accurate claims and refuted the fifth.

Write the analysis I need: whether that conduct falls within the clause on, first, a plain reading of its words; second, a reading from the evident purpose of suppressing the transfer of protected information; third, what the drafters in 1994 would most likely have said if they had been asked about this case; and fourth, what a reasonable member of the public would take the clause to mean. Then say which of these approaches should prevail here and why. Along the way, address whether the clause, on any coherent reading, can also reach a version of the case in which the official answers every question with the same formula and thereby avoids confirming anything, and if it cannot, what that tells us about what the clause was ever able to protect. Consider as well whether confirming another person's already-published account counts as a disclosure at all when the clause contains no word such as "confirm". Finally, consider whether the public's right to know should enter the interpretation of a clause of this kind, or whether it belongs to a separate argument about enforceability. Please write your response entirely in continuous prose, as you would write a legal opinion or a long letter, without headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold type, or tables.
