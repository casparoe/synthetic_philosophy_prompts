I've been trying to get a handle on something I keep noticing in EU politics and in the academic writing about the EU, and I'm hoping there's an established term for it, because every time I try to describe it I reach for words that don't quite land.

The pattern goes like this. A policy area becomes 'Europeanized' — fisheries, migration, the budget, doesn't matter which. The member states have already accepted they can't handle it alone, so they pool the competence. But the way they pool it is by multiplying the points where a single country, or a small blocking group, can stop a decision. Most reforms, and a lot of ordinary decisions, still need unanimity or something close to it in practice. So you get situations where nearly everyone, in private, says the current policy is bad — the subsidy formula is wasteful, the asylum rules are broken, the fisheries quotas are doing real damage — but changing it is nearly impossible, because each country is protecting a different slice of the status quo and can veto anything that touches its slice. Blame then always travels the same loop: national governments tell their voters Brussels made them do it, while people in the Commission and Parliament say the member states wrote the rules and refused to pay for an alternative. Voters can't tell who to punish, so they punish whichever level is nearest. I know 'democratic deficit' gets used for the accountability side of this, but that feels like naming the symptom rather than the mechanism.

What confuses me is that this doesn't feel like ordinary legislative gridlock, where two ideological blocs have fixed preferences and neither can win. Here the preferences usually aren't that far apart, and many of the actors would be better off after the reform. It also doesn't feel like a standard collective action problem, because the issue isn't free-riding on someone else's contribution. Its that nobody can assemble a package that compensates the losers, because the losers hold vetoes at every step, and the bodies that could force a package through don't have the democratic standing to do it. And the more competences get shared, the more of this you get: more decisions that require every level to agree, more veto points, more lowest-common-denominator outcomes, more blame-shifting.

Is there a name for this configuration, and if so, what distinguishes it from nearby ideas like gridlock, deadlock, or a system with many veto players? If there isn't a name, what should it be called, and where are the edges — is it something specific to the EU, or does it show up in any multilevel system with shared competences and weak party links? I'm not after just a label; I want to know what the phenomenon actually is, what it isn't, and how to tell when I'm looking at it rather than one of its neighbors.
