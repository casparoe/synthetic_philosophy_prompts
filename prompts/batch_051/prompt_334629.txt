I run a one-hour seminar for American law students who have completed constitutional law and can work comfortably with enumerated powers, preemption, and the anti-commandeering doctrine. They currently treat subsidiarity as a vague preference for keeping decisions at the lowest level of government, and they assume it does the same work as the US enumerated-powers inquiry. I want the session to equip them to see subsidiarity as EU law uses it: a constraint on the exercise of a competence once the competence exists, not primarily a rule about whether the competence exists. Plan the teaching sequence rather than the explanation itself: the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what each depends on, the single concrete case or example that carries the distinction and what it must include and must avoid, the misconceptions I should expect and where in the sequence to head each off, the check for understanding that would show the distinction has landed, and what I should openly set aside as too technical for one hour. Give the plan as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Within the plan, identify the one assumption about my students or about the law that, if false, would most change the sequence you propose.
