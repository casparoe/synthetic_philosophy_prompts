I am a historical novelist drafting a story set in the twelfth century, in which two monks argue about Peter Abelard's doctrine of intention. I want their dispute to have real philosophical texture rather than serve as mere background. Please act as an expert on Abelard's ethics, specifically his treatise Ethics, or Scito te ipsum.

In the text, Abelard argues that the morally decisive element of a sin is consent, not the outward act. One of his well-known moves is to say that a man who consents to adultery has already committed the sin in God's eyes even if he is prevented from carrying it out, whereas someone who performs the same outward act without such consent—for example, through force or ignorance—has not committed the sin of adultery, though he may be guilty of some other negligence.

Now consider this variation, which I intend to put into the monks' debate. A man knowingly consents to an act he believes to be gravely sinful—adultery, for instance—and his consent is real and complete. But then, before the act can occur, he deliberately maneuvers to make the act impossible: he does not withdraw his consent; he still wishes the act would happen, but he arranges circumstances so that the opportunity will not come. Perhaps he warns the woman's husband, or arranges a forced absence for himself. His aim is to have the sin of consent without the external act as a separate willed choice, because he fears the act's consequences but still desires the sin.

Does this break Abelard's analysis, or is it simply one more instance of it? Concretely: on Abelard's view, does the second act—arranging the prevention—change the moral assessment of the first consent, and if so, in what exact respect? Does the man sin once or twice? Is the later will to avoid the outward act itself a sin, a mitigation, or morally inert? Work out the answer step-by-step from Abelard's principles about consent, intention, and the relation of internal act to external act. Be careful not to import a modern Kantian or consequentialist framework; think inside Abelard's distinctions. End with a short dialogue exchange between the two monks that captures the strongest point each side would make.
