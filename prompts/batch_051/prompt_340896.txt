In his 1912 essay On the Notion of Cause, Bertrand Russell argued that the concept of causation is not required for the most advanced sciences. His argument, in outline, was that physics had already replaced causal laws with differential equations describing functional relations among variables; that the word cause carries pre-scientific associations of pushing, producing, or compelling; and that philosophy would do better to eliminate the notion entirely than to keep using a concept that is both unnecessary in mature science and misleading in thought. He compared the law of causality to a relic of an earlier age that survives only because it is presumed to do no harm.

Since the early 1990s, however, a substantial technical literature has developed around formal causal models: structural equation models, the do-calculus, and interventionist accounts of causation. These frameworks treat causal claims as precise enough to be encoded, manipulated, and tested. They are now standard tools in epidemiology, econometrics, and machine learning. This looks like a genuine change in the situation Russell was describing: causal talk is no longer merely the loose idiom of pre-scientific common sense.

Does Russell's eliminativist argument survive this development? I want you to say, precisely, which premise in Russell's argument is most under pressure, and whether the argument can be repaired in a weaker form. In particular: (1) Does the new formal work recover the notion of cause that Russell wanted to eliminate, or does it define a different notion and simply keep the word? (2) Would Russell's point about mature physics still hold for fundamental physics, even if the special sciences find causal models indispensable? (3) Is there a version of his eliminativism that remains plausible today, perhaps restricted to fundamental physics or to a distinctively metaphysical kind of causation? Give a verdict on the argument, mark the spot where it is weakest, and say what evidence would most shift your assessment.
