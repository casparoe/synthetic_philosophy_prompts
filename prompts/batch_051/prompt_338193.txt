I've been trying to work out whether there is a single principle behind the way I talk about seeing things through images and instruments, and I'd like you to test whether one can be found. My verdicts, as they come: In a bathroom mirror, I see my own face — not merely a representation of it. Looking through a telescope at Saturn, I see Saturn, even though I'm looking into an eyepiece and the light is old. Watching a shop's live CCTV monitor, I see a shoplifter put something in his coat; a week later, watching the same footage, I still say I saw him on the tape. Looking at an old film photograph of my grandmother from the 1970s, I'm inclined to say I see her — her, as she was then — whereas a skilled painted portrait from the same year shows me a picture of her, not her. A photorealist painting copied from that photograph also falls on the picture side: I don't see her in it. Mild processing doesn't bother me: if the photograph is cropped or color-corrected, I still see her. But if someone has edited her into a scene she never occupied, I don't see her in the resulting image — I see an edit. So far it feels like a line between mechanically recorded traces and handmade pictures. But the line blurs. I don't think I see a real person in a photorealistic digital reconstruction built from motion-capture data, even though the movements are genuinely that person's. And I waver on a telescopic image that has been heavily processed by stacking exposures and applying false color to bring out structure: I lean toward saying I see the nebula, but uneasily.

Can you extract a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them, and state it precisely enough that it can be applied to new cases? Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether by your lights those verdicts should be overturned rather than saved by adding epicycles. Then apply it to cases I haven't listed: a live video call on a phone; a Mars rover image that arrives twenty minutes after the light hit the camera; an artificial-intelligence upscaling of a blurry license plate in a security photo; and a coaching video in which a deepfake likeness of an actor, built from real photographs, is made to deliver a talk the actor never gave. I want the resulting view to be as simple as it can be while still matching what I'd defend under pressure, not just a label for the list.
