A foundation is funding a one-volume global history of the last six hundred years, written as a single continuous narrative, with an explicit anti-Eurocentric charter. Governance: the world is split into twelve macro-regions, each with a board of historians from that region that drafts the passages about it's own region. Any board can veto any passage anywhere in the book that makes substantive claims about its region, or about processes substantially involving its region, on grounds of misrepresentation. A vetoed passage goes to a central committee for renegotiation; after three failed rounds it is cut, and the cut plus the vetoing board's stated reason gets printed in an appendix. The central committee, appointed by the funder, controls periodization, chapter order, what counts as an event or process, and all connective tissue between regional sections, but cannot make substantive claims about any region without that board's sign-off. I keep going back and forth on whether this is clever institutional design or just a vetocracy where the most intransigent board writes the book by deletion.

Treat it as a mechanism, not as a moral question about the project. Walk through how the finished book actually comes out: what the central committee learns to do under constant veto threat, how boards use the veto strategically, which passages survive and which get cut, and whether the result is genuinely less Eurocentric or just differently distorted. Then say where it breaks first, and whether any fixes (pre-registered periodization, weighted votes, a separate synthesis board whose connective claims need no regional sign-off, etc.) would repair it without smuggling one perspective back in as the default.

Give me the response in these sections, in this order: The incentives (~150 words), How it actually plays out (~250 words), Failure modes (~200 words), Second-order effects (~100 words), Can it be fixed (~150 words).
