I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether the endowment effect and loss aversion deserve to be called biases at all. I'd like your help with a question that straddles the empirical and the conceptual: how would you test the claim that loss aversion, as it appears both in risky choice and in the riskless endowment-effect paradigm, is a bias rather than a legitimate preference?

Some background from what I've been reading. The standard laboratory result is a wide gap between willingness to accept and willingness to pay for a randomly assigned good: the famous mug experiments. In risky choice, people often require a prospective gain to be roughly twice the size of an equally probable loss before they will accept the bet. Both patterns are routinely described as instances of loss aversion, and both are routinely called biases. But that classification carries a lot of theoretical weight, and I can see at least three reasons to hesitate.

First, the relevant reference point is not directly observed. If the reference point is set by expectations about the transaction rather than by current ownership, then an asymmetry around that point may be internally coherent rather than an error. Second, the exchange gap could be rational for unfamiliar goods: owning a good may be informative about its value, or distrust of hidden defects and transaction costs may depress what buyers offer. This predicts that the gap should shrink as people gain trading experience, and some field studies with experienced traders report that it largely disappears; what remains contested is whether that pattern reflects adaptation, selection, or learning. Third, calling any pattern a bias requires a normative benchmark. If a person's stable, informed, self-reflective preference genuinely weighs losses more heavily than gains, then the charge of bias may be smuggling in a welfare criterion that one is not obliged to accept.

I would like you to design the strongest test you can of the claim that loss aversion is a bias rather than a legitimate preference. Be concrete about the populations, materials, incentives, comparison arms, and measurements. Then tell me which pattern of results would most push you toward the "bias" interpretation, which would most push you toward the "legitimate preference" interpretation, and which would leave you genuinely uncertain even if the experiment ran flawlessly. Finally, tell me what no empirical test could settle here even in principle: the residual conceptual or normative question that survives all the data.

One request about epistemic hygiene: in your answer, please separate what is empirically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested reading, such as the claim that the endowment effect disappears with experience or that loss aversion is an evolved irrationality, as the consensus view.
