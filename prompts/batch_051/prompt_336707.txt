I run a small reading group for rationalist-adjacent friends with a decent amateur economics background: they can manipulate utility functions, they understand Pareto dominance, and they have spent real time arguing about whether "Pareto improvement" is even a useful standard for collective choice. Next month I want to give the session on safe Pareto improvements. As I understand them, the central idea is this: when individuals' preferences are incomplete, an outcome should not count as a genuine Pareto improvement over the status quo merely because it is Pareto-superior under one permissible completion of everyone's preferences; it earns the label only if it is Pareto-superior under every such completion. The group has never handled incomplete preferences formally, and my experience is that at least some of them will immediately assimilate preference gaps either to indifference, or to information the analyst is entitled to fill in, or to caution that can be captured by ordinary risk aversion.

I am not asking you to write the explanation itself. I am asking you to design the explanatory sequence for a forty-minute whiteboard session. I need to know: what has to be in place before the definition can even land, and what depends on what; the one concrete case that should carry the idea, and what it must have and what it must lack (I am not married to an Edgeworth-box exchange and suspect it will smuggle in too much); the misconceptions this specific audience will form, in the order they will form them, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a final check that would reveal whether someone has understood the notion rather than merely learned the phrase "robust across completions"; and what I should explicitly decline to cover at this level, so the session does not dissolve into formalism about preference representation.
