Pascal's wager, except the payoffs are finite and your credence is only pinned to a range. Suppose theres a god who gives believers a finite utility H and nonbelievers a finite disutility D; with probability 1 − p there's no god and everyone gets 0. Living as a believer costs c. In the original the infinity does all the work: you never have to say what p actually is, because any nonzero p times an infinite payoff swamps the finite cost. But now H and D are finite, you don't know p except that its somewhere between 10^-9 and 0.9, and you don't know H and D precisely either, just rough ranges. Does any version of the dominance reasoning survive this change, or does the whole wager just collapse into an ordinary expected-utility calculation whose answer depends on numbers you don't have? If it collapses, what decision rule should you fall back on (expected value with your best guesses, worst-case tilting, something else) and why?
