Draft the joint statement that a committed advocate of the Past Hypothesis — the view that the thermodynamic arrow of time is to be explained by an objective, extremely low-entropy boundary condition on the early universe, one not derivable from the reversible microdynamics but nomologically significant — and a committed advocate of the interventionist/coarse-graining account — the view that the arrow is grounded in our standpoint as agents who coarse-grain, intervene, and keep records, and that the low-entropy "boundary condition" is really a perspective-dependent posit rather than a fundamental feature of the world — could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should not read as a compromise in which either side silently concedes its position; it should do the harder work of finding formulations that are non-vacuous without being slanted, and of separating genuine residual disagreements from disagreements that were only verbal.

Produce the statement in the following form. Begin with a numbered agreed-claims section: the empirical asymmetries, distinctions, and constraints that both camps accept, stated precisely enough to block the usual talking-past moves — for example, exactly what is time-symmetric in the microdynamics, exactly what is asymmetric at the macroscopic and epistemic levels, and what minimal role a low-entropy condition must play even on the interventionist view. Then give a disagreements table whose rows cover at least these residual points: (i) whether the low-entropy early condition is an objective fact about the universe or a perspective-relative posit; (ii) whether the direction of explanation runs from the thermodynamic arrow to the asymmetries of records and interventions, or from those agent-relative asymmetries to the arrow; and (iii) whether the Past Hypothesis does explanatory work beyond the coarse-graining, intervention, and record-keeping machinery once that machinery is admitted. Each side's formulation in the table should be one its own camp would accept as an exact statement of its view. Finally, identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and for each crux describe a case, explicit construction, or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction for that case written down in advance. A good answer will make the residual disagreements narrow and concrete rather than sweeping, and will note where a would-be disagreement dissolves once the terms are fixed.
