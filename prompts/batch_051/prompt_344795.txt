Here's an asymmetry in how people judge sweatshops and I genuinely can't tell if it's defensible or just a moral inconsistency we've learned to live with.

Take a clothing company that opens a factory in Bangladesh and pays workers the local going rate for garment work, call it $2 a day. Standard reaction: the company is exploiting those workers. Now suppose the job really is better than thier alternatives — nobody's misinformed, they took it freely, and the fallback is subsistence farming or whatever, which is worse. Even granting all that, we still call it exploitative and wrong, or at least morally criticizable.

Now take a second company with the same resources, that could open the exact same factory and hire the exact same people on the exact same terms, but doesn't. The workers keep their worse fallback. Nobody pickets that company. Nobody says it wronged them by failing to offer the job. But if the first company's low-wage offer is wrong, how is the second company's non-offer not worse? And if the second company does nothing wrong, what exactly is the first company doing wrong by giving people an option that's better than what they'd otherwise have?

So: is the asymmetry justified? What principle draws the line — using another person's desperation, causing vs. allowing harm, paying less than the value the worker creates, complicity in an unjust global system, something else? And test whatever principle you pick on a couple of nearby cases: the rescue team that charges a drowning person his life savings for a rescue, the farmer who hires hungry workers at just-barely-above-starvation wages, the company that declines to open a factory because it refuses to pay under $2 a day and so hires nobody. Do we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up?
