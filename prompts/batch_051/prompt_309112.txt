I'm giving a seminar paper next month defending a two-part line on the Enlightenment. Part one: what deserves credit for the usual story of modern progress — the escape from Malthusian poverty, mass literacy, the long decline in infectious-disease mortality — is a cluster of practices rather than a canon of texts: organized criticism, fallibilism, and the norm that factual claims must answer to public evidence. Part two: the familiar charge that the Enlightenment was an engine of domination (colonialism, scientific racism, subjugation of nature) attaches mostly to the content of what particular Enlightenment figures believed and to how the toolkit got deployed, not to those practices themselves, so you can accept the domination critique and keep part one. The room: two postcolonial theorists, three intellectual historians of the long eighteenth century, two moral philosophers broadly sympathetic to Enlightenment liberalism but on the fence about exactly this, and a Kant specialist who thinks the critical philosophy is doing the real work. There's also a moderate chance a political scientist who works on state capacity shows up.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet, not a defense speech. Structure it like this:

- Where the thesis is brittle (about 150 words): what I should quietly fix before the talk so the obvious objections don't land harder than they need to.
- Objections, in the order I'll actually hear them (about 120 words each): eight objections, each tagged with who is likely to ask it, phrased in their own voice, followed by a two-minute reply and one sentence on where that reply is softest.
- What to concede up front (about 100 words).
- The one reply that has to land (about 150 words).
- The question I'm praying nobody asks (about 100 words), including why it would hurt.
