I keep noticing an asymmetry in how the very same caretaking behavior is described when a human does it and when one artificial system does it to another, and I want to know whether it can be defended. Machine-learning practice already borrows the vocabulary of upbringing: people call one model a teacher and the one it supervises a student, speak of a parent model seeding or distilling a child model, and describe one agent as raising another when the first chooses the second's curriculum, writes its reward signal, and corrects or retires its updates over a long horizon. In the human case, that same package — shaping what a child is exposed to, teaching, protecting, correcting over years — is exactly what turns a set of historical causes into a parent or guardian, someone with standing duties toward the child and partial answerability for how the child turns out. But when an artificial system does the shaping, the descriptions flip. The raising system is treated as a conduit: it executes an objective, and the lab that built it is the only answerable party; the word parent becomes an engineering metaphor. So we affirm, in one breath, that sustained shaping grounds something like guardianship, and in the next, that sustained shaping by one AI over another grounds nothing — no duty running from the raising system to the raised one, no share of credit or blame, no reason to protect the relationship. Is there a principle that yields both judgments, or only a familiar resistance to taking the metaphor seriously because the parent is itself an artifact? Give me the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can build, and test it against nearby cases: a raising system that is itself a plausible candidate for interests or moral status; a guardian AI that can inspect and revise its own child-rearing policy across many children instead of merely executing a fixed objective; a human contractor employed to perform the same training functions for a decade. Where does the asymmetry break first? Finish with a clear statement of whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned, and whether we should drop the parent-child vocabulary in engineering along with it.
