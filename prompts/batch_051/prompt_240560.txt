Malthus argued that, left unchecked, human population grows geometrically while the means of subsistence can grow only arithmetically, so population must eventually be held in check by misery and vice. Since his time, two developments appear to undercut that argument. One is the demographic transition: after industrialization lowered infant mortality and raised incomes, birth rates themselves fell, allowing populations to stabilize without rising to the point of catastrophe. The other is the synthesis of nitrogen fertilizer from atmospheric nitrogen and the crop-yield increases that followed, which allowed food production to grow rapidly and for decades at a time rather than at the slow arithmetic rate Malthus imagined.

Do these developments kill Malthus's argument, or does its deeper structure survive them?
