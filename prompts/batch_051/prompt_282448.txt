You are a philosopher of science with a side interest in the epistemology of disagreement; think carefully and resist jumping to a verdict until you have considered the structure of the dispute. For years I have watched the debate over Hempel's ravens paradox run in place, and what I want is a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another proposed solution. The familiar moves are all on the table. Hempel's setup yields the result that, given the Nicod criterion and the equivalence condition, a white shoe confirms the generalization that all ravens are black, because the shoe is a non-black non-raven and the generalization is equivalent to its contrapositive. One camp treats this as a reason to distrust pre-theoretic intuitions about confirmation and defends a syntactic, instance-based account. A second camp replies that the Bayesian apparatus dissolves the paradox, since the shoe confirms the hypothesis only infinitesimally and the intuitive surprise tracks the size of the confirmation rather than its sign. A third camp says the whole puzzle is an artifact of ignoring background knowledge, projectibility, and the way the evidence was selected or sampled. Everyone has said this for decades, and nobody moves.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that the debate is not really about ravens or even about the Nicod criterion. It is a collision between at least three things that all go by the name confirmation: confirmation as a formal relation between an observation sentence and a hypothesis, confirmation as an increase in rational credence, and confirmation as evidence that would license an inductive projection for a real inquirer carrying background knowledge. On the first reading the paradox is genuine but harmless; on the second it evaporates into quantitative differences; on the third the original question is under-specified and may need to be reframed. If that is right, the disagreement is largely definitional, and progress would come from a sharper taxonomy rather than a decisive argument. But I can see rivals: the sides might genuinely disagree about whether intuitive judgments about confirmation are data a theory must preserve; about whether a purely formal or syntactic theory of confirmation is even possible; or about which features of an agent's epistemic situation a theory is answerable to. Diagnose the debate. Lay out two or three candidate diagnoses before defending one. Say what each major response has to assume for its dissolution to work and which assumption is doing the real argumentative work. Then give your verdict on the kind of disagreement this is, what would count as progress given that diagnosis, and whether the question as posed is well-posed at all.
