In my modern history class we have been comparing how nations remember military defeats, and our teacher keeps returning to one analogy: a nation's memory works like an individual's memory, repressing shameful details, retelling events around a more flattering core, and replacing painful facts with consoling myths, much as a person might after a trauma. She applies this to four cases: the American South's Lost Cause, where a defeat in defense of slavery became a story of honorable sacrifice; Israel's memory of Masada, where a failed revolt and mass death became a story of heroic self-sacrifice; Serbia's memory of the 1389 Battle of Kosovo, where conquest by the Ottomans became a story of martyrdom and chosenness; and Britain's memory of Dunkirk, where a military collapse and evacuation became a story of national spirit and deliverance. Her conclusion is that these myths are not merely lies or propaganda; they function like psychological defense mechanisms, so we should be cautious about debunking them, just as we would hesitate to attack a person's way of coping with trauma. I suspect the four cases do not all work the same way. Please audit the analogy: give me a table of correspondences (what plays the role of the person, the trauma, the defense mechanism, and the recovery), identify what the analogy quietly imports from individual psychology that a nation may lack, show where the mapping breaks down in each case, and tell me whether her cautious conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there is a better analogy for how national memories of defeat actually operate, I would like to know what it is.
