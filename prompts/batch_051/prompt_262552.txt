Consider the standard argument that a state and a corporation are unlike in kind: the state successfully claims a monopoly of legitimate violence within a territory, while a corporation exercises only such powers as a state delegates to it, on terms the state can revoke. That argument took shape when even the most powerful chartered companies—bodies that once raised armies, collected taxes, and governed subject populations—could ultimately be brought back under the control of a territorial sovereign. Now suppose the largest platform firms set and enforce detailed rules for much of a person's economic and social life, run their own binding dispute systems, maintain private security, control credentials and payment rails, and can exclude an individual from services she depends on across many jurisdictions at once, with no single state able to review or reverse the exclusion effectively. Does the standard argument still distinguish state from corporation in kind, or has it collapsed into a difference of degree? Say which premise the platform case puts under the most pressure, and whether the criterion can be repaired or should be replaced. Also say what would change your verdict: which consideration would most likely drive you to the opposite conclusion, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
