Psychiatric practice appears to treat delusions in three different ways at once. Diagnostic manuals describe a delusion as a false belief held with conviction despite strong evidence against it, and clinicians document and assess it as one of the patient's beliefs. Yet clinical training often instructs staff not to argue with a delusion, not to contradict it directly, and not to try to talk the patient out of it; the recommended stance is to work with the distress surrounding the delusion while avoiding both endorsement and direct challenge. At the same time, cognitive-behavioral therapy for psychosis works on the assumption that the patient can revise delusional content through reasons, using collaborative examination of the evidence for and against it. What theory of whether delusions are beliefs does this pattern of practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, identify where the different parts of the practice presuppose different things, and then commit: state which account best rationalizes the practice as a whole—one on which delusions are genuine beliefs, one on which they are not beliefs but something else such as imaginings or avowals, or an intermediate account such as the two-factor theory, on which an anomalous experience plus a second failure to reject it produces the delusion—and say what clinical practice would have to change if a rival account were correct.
