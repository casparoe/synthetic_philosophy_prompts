I'm writing a proposal for a variant of AI-safety-by-debate that tries to directly target obfuscated arguments. In the 2018 paper "AI Safety via Debate" by Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, and Dario Amodei (arXiv:1805.00899), they write: "Taking a step up in alignment difficulty, some tasks are too difficult for a human to perform, but a human can still judge the quality of behavior or answers once shown to them—for example a robot doing a backflip in an unnatural action space."

My twist is to add a third agent, an "obfuscation auditor," who doesn't argue for either side. It has a small budget of interruptions. At any point it can stop the debate and claim that one side's last statement uses a named rhetorical failure mode: confident false precision, appeal to unverifiable internal state, shifting to a meta-claim about what a reasonable expert would believe, etc. The human judge then has to rule on just that claim: if the challenged speech was obfuscatory, the offending agent loses some of its remaining speaking turns. If the auditor's challenge is itself spurious, the auditor loses budget. Walk through how this would play out if you tried to actually train such a system. What new incentives appear for the debaters? for the auditor? Does the judge now need a skill that is just as hard as judging the original question? Where does this design get gamed first, and are the failure modes patchable without turning the auditor into a truth-certifier?

As you analyze, separate what's fairly settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on debate and alignment; don't present one side as obvious consensus.
