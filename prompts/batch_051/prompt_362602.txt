I write about biotech and transhumanism for a magazine, and I'm trying to write a piece that reads Aldous Huxley's Brave New World against a position I've became a bit obsessed with lately: what gets called the hedonistic imperative, the project David Pearce laid out in his 1995 manifesto of that name. The core idea is that suffering has no intrinsic value, so the right long-run goal of biotechnology isn't merely curing disease but abolishing suffering itself across all sentient life, humans and wild animals included. And this isn't, in Pearce's telling, a plan for drugged stupor: it's about rewiring the vertebrate genome and reward system so that our baseline mood sits at something like alert, well-informed contentment that stays responsive to what's going on, with pain replaced by neutral warning signals that still do the functional work pain does now. Soma, in Huxley's novel, looks like the cruder cousin: a hangover-free escape hatch that trims grief and anxiety but leaves people vacuous and passive. Meanwhile the World State has also conditioned its citizens to be happy with their lot, from the hatcheries and hypnopaedic slogans down to the caste system, so its often read as a nightmare about consumerism and social control rather than as a serious attempt to end suffering. I can't tell how deep the overlap actually goes.

My question: read Brave New World through the lens of the hedonistic imperative. Is the World State actually a version of Pearce's project, or is it doing something else that only resembles it from a distance? Where do the novel's criticisms land: against eliminating suffering as such, or against the specific coercive, flattening way this society does it? The Savage keeps insisting that a life with no pain has no depth, no real virtue, no intensity worth keeping; what should a present-day hedonistic-imperative person say back to him? The long argument between the Savage and Mustapha Mond near the end is where I'd especially want close attention, because the Controller sounds almost utilitarian and the Savage sounds like he's defending the intrinsic value of being miserable. I'd like a reading that doesn't treat the novel as a warmed-over illustration of the theory, and also doesn't pretend Huxley was secretly writing about 1990s transhumanism. Say where the book genuinely anticipates the idea, where it caricatures it, and what the novel adds to the philosophical argument that Pearce's essays by themselves can't.
