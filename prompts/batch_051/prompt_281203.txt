"State the sorites paradox for a vague predicate of your choice, and explain how the epistemicist and the supervaluationist respond. In particular, does either view allow the paradox to be resolved without there being a sharp boundary?"

And here's the student's answer, word for word:

"The sorites paradox comes up for vague words like 'bald.' If someone with 50,000 hairs is bald, removing one hair cannot turn a bald person into a non-bald person, so repeated application says a person with zero hairs is bald, which is absurd. Three claims pull in different directions: clear positive and negative cases, a tolerance principle, and classical logic, and not all three can be kept. Epistemicists keep classical logic and say there is some particular number of hairs that is the cutoff, but we cannot know which one it is. Supervaluationists instead reject the tolerance principle. On their view, each admissible sharpening of 'bald' draws a boundary somewhere, and a sentence is true iff it is true on all sharpenings, false iff false on all, and otherwise neither. In a borderline case, 'this person is bald' is neither true nor false; the tolerance premise itself likewise comes out neither true nor false, so the chain never gets going. Supervaluationism therefore avoids any commitment to a sharp boundary, because no particular cutoff sentence is true, and vague language turns out coherent despite the paradox."
