I'm sitting in on a philosophy of biology seminar, and the instructor has been strikingly consistent all term in how she rules on whether a trait has a biological function, though none of us can yet state her principle. Among the verdicts she has given: the pumping of blood is a function of the human heart; the thumping sound a heartbeat makes is not a function of the heart even though physicians rely on it; supporting a pair of eyeglasses is not a function of the nose; a wing-color pattern that first appeared as a neutral mutation and spread by drift but is now maintained by selection because predators avoid patterned insects is a function; and a display trait that lowers a male's chance of surviving but increases the number of mates he sires is a function.

Next week we will be asked about a new case, and I would like to know what consistency commits her to. A population of island beetles descends from a single pregnant founding female, so the whole population is fixed for an odd little flange on the exoskeleton; the flange was selectively neutral at the time of the founding and rose to fixation by drift alone. Recently an introduced predator reached the island, and flanged beetles—which is all of them—turn out in laboratory trials to evade that predator better than flange-less beetles do. No flange-less beetle has yet occurred on the island, so natural selection has had no variation to act on and has never favored the flange for predator escape. Does the flange have the function of predator escape? I would like the answer to identify the candidate rules that fit her track record, state what each rule predicts about the flange, identify which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, say which rule you would bet captures her actual position and why, and say what further case would most sharply tell the remaining candidates apart. Please write the answer in American English.
