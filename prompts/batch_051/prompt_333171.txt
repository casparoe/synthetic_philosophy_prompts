Public-health agencies, patient-decision aids, and popular discussions of risk now often express hazards in micromorts — a one-in-a-million chance of death — so that a chest X-ray, a day of motorcycling, a general anaesthetic, and a year of living with average urban air pollution can be placed on a single scale. The stated aim is to help individuals make informed choices. What theory of rational decision-making and of the badness of death does this practice presuppose? Treating mortality risks as units with a common value seems to leave no room for the thought that the distribution of risk matters: whether death is voluntary or imposed, delayed or immediate, suffered by the young or the old, concentrated in a few people or spread thinly across many. Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what a risk-communication metric would have to look like if it took those distributional features to carry independent moral weight.
