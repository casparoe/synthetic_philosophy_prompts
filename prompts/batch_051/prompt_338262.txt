I have been tutoring mathematics at a community learning center for a few years now, mostly algebra and precalculus, and I keep circling back to a question I cannot settle on my own. I have one student who can reproduce the standard proof that the square root of two is irrational flawlessly: he assumes it equals a fraction a over b in lowest terms, squares both sides to get a squared equals two b squared, concludes that a is even, writes a as two c, substitutes, concludes that b is even too, and then points to the contradiction. But when I ask why the proof begins with lowest terms, whether the same argument would prove the square root of four irrational, or what the conclusion actually rules out, he cannot say. I have another student who sometimes loses track of the algebra, but who can explain that the proof is a machine for turning the assumption that the square root of two is a ratio into impossible demands on the two integers, and she can see at once where the argument would break for the square root of four.

My question is this: what is your actual credence that the first student does not understand the proof, where "does not understand" means roughly that his gap would not be closed by more drill on the algebra and would show up in failures on nearby variants and why-questions that track the structure of the argument? I would like a number, not the observation that it depends on what one means by understanding. Please decompose the credence: how much rides on whether understanding a proof is fully constituted by the ability to reproduce and verify each step; how much on whether, if understanding requires something beyond that, the behavior I described is strong evidence that he lacks that extra component; how much on whether the second student's kind of grasp is the right benchmark rather than an unrealistically demanding one; and any other term you think belongs. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question with a line of justification, show how they combine into your final number, then tell me which term that number is most sensitive to and what specific observation of the two students would move that term the most.
