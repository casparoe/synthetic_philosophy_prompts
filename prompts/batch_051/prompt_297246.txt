I'm a philosophy major currently taking an upper-division philosophy of language course, and we have spent the past few weeks on vagueness. I have come to find epistemicism persuasive: the view that vagueness is not semantic indecision or a gap in the world, but a kind of ignorance. On this picture, every vague predicate has a sharp, non-arbitrary cutoff — a definite last grain that makes the difference between a heap and a non-heap, a definite last hair that separates bald from not bald — but we are simply not in a position to know where that cutoff lies. Supervaluationism strikes me, by contrast, as treating our inability to settle borderline cases as a fact about language rather than a fact about our epistemic limits. I would like to know how exposed the epistemicist position really is.

What would count against epistemicism? I am familiar with the standard incredulity objection: the intuition that meaning, being fixed by our finite, messy usage, could not have selected a precise cutoff that no competent speaker can articulate even after full reflection. But I want a finer-grained map of the view's exposure. Which intuitions, possible cases, linguistic data, or results in neighboring areas — psycholinguistics, epistemology, formal semantics — should actually move an epistemicist, and which can a committed epistemicist reasonably dismiss as question-begging or as costs already priced in? What does epistemicism predict about borderline cases that supervaluationism does not, and are any of those predictions testable, say by probing speaker judgments about truth and falsity? Finally, I would like an honest assessment of whether epistemicism insulates itself by making the cutoff unknowable in principle, and whether that insulation is a strength of a semantic theory or a way of avoiding the very data it was meant to explain. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated if that is where the arguments lead.
