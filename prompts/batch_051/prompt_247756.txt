I've been arguing with a friend about language death for weeks, and I've sort of backed myself into a position I can't defend cleanly. The position is roughly this: when a language loses its last fluent speakers, something valuable is lost that documentation can't preserve, and the value isn't just instrumental — it's not only about losing medicinal plant knowledge or oral history or whatever. I want to say linguistic diversity is worth having for its own sake, the way biodiversity is, and that a world with thousands of languages is better, other things equal, than a world with a few hundred even if everyone in both worlds is equally happy and equally able to communicate with each other. I also catch myself wanting to say this even when the last speakers themselves are indifferent or actually want the language to die — and that's where my friend says I've gone of the rails.

What I'd like is a map of what would count against this position, not a generic list of 'both sides' points. I mean the specific cases and arguments that should genuinely worry me: a language dying because its speakers are escaping poverty or state persecution; the thought that the value is really in the speakers' capabilities rather than in the language itself; the worry that my view commits me to saying people have a duty to keep speaking a language they'd rather abandon; the 'museum' worry that I'm valuing languages the way people value stuffed animals; the question of whether the biodiversity analogy actually does any work or is just a slogan; maybe the harder case of a language that has exactly one speaker left who wants it to die with them. For each thing you identify, I want to know whether it's fatal or merely awkward, whether a defender of my position would themselves concede that it counts against them and what their best reply is, whether patching the position to survive it makes the position unfalsifiable or vacuous, and — where it applies — what the view predicts that a purely instrumental view of language loss wouldn't predict, and what would have to happen for those predictions to come out wrong. I'd rather keep the core claim — that there's a non-instrumental loss — and drop the biodiversity analogy if I have to, but I want to see what I'm paying for that.

After the discussion, give me a JSON summary with this schema: an array called "defeaters", where each item has fields "defeater" (a short name), "target" (which part of the position it hits), "severity" (one of 'fatal', 'serious', 'awkward', 'weak'), "defender_would_concede" (true/false), "best_reply", and "patch" (what modification of the position, if any, survives it); plus a final field "verdict" saying whether the core claim survives, survives only in a weakened form, or should be abandoned.
