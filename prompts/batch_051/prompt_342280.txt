I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical training. My working vocabulary is built around Turing completeness, interpreters, compilers, transpilers, virtual machines, emulation, language specifications versus implementations, decidability, halting, and oracles, which I encounter in practice as external calls, syscalls, or APIs that supply a function no program written in the language itself can compute.

I keep running into the Church-Turing thesis and its epistemic status, and I want it restated inside that vocabulary rather than left in the philosophical idiom. As I understand it, the thesis says that every effectively calculable function — every function that could in principle be computed by a finite, mechanical procedure of the sort a human being could carry out with unlimited time and paper — is computable by a Turing machine. There is also a separate, provable result that many formalisms (Turing machines, the lambda calculus, general recursive functions, register machines, and so on) all define the same class of computable functions; I gather that this equivalence theorem is usually distinguished from the thesis proper, because the thesis concerns an informal notion that floats above all the formalisms. And there is a further claim, the physical Church-Turing thesis, about what any physical process can compute, with the refinement that quantum computation seems to change the complexity of solving some problems rather than enlarging the class of computable functions. Finally, there is the question that actually interests me: what kind of claim is the thesis in the first place — a definition of computability, an empirical hypothesis, a stipulation, a law of nature, or something else — and why people argue about this when the claim cannot be formally proved because one of its terms is informal.

Please translate this cluster of claims into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, item by item. For each of the following, say what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no counterpart at all: (1) the informal notion of effective calculability; (2) the thesis that this informal notion coincides with Turing computability; (3) the distinction between the equivalence theorem about formalisms and the thesis itself; (4) the physical Church-Turing thesis, including the complexity-versus-computability point; and (5) the debate over whether the thesis is a definition, an empirical claim, or something else.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical discussion survive the translation, and which do not? In particular, I want a verdict on my standing temptation to say that the whole thing is just the fact that all Turing-complete languages can emulate one another. If that is not the whole thing, I want to know whether my vocabulary is missing a real distinction or whether the philosophers' residual dispute is less substantive than it looks once the informal notion is set aside. Where the translation fails, say whether the failure marks a defect in the target framework or a place where the original claim leans on an idea that has not been made precise.

Please write the answer in British English.
