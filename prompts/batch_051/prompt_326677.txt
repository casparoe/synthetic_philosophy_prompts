Imagine that, from 1 January next year, the following comes into force. Whenever an adult levels a public moral criticism, accusation, or demand for accountability against another person—in a speech, an article, a broadcast, a post on a social platform, a workplace complaint, or a filing in court—a verified public dossier appears alongside the accusation. The dossier is generated automatically by a tamper-proof system drawing on existing records: criminal and civil filings, employment records, lawfully obtained correspondence, posts, footage, and testimony. It contains only true instances in which the accuser engaged in morally comparable conduct, meaning conduct of roughly the same kind and seriousness as what they are now condemning. It says nothing about the accused; it concerns the accuser alone. Anyone who receives the original accusation can also access the dossier, without the accuser's consent. The system's errors are confined to the margins—disputed evidence, threshold judgements—never outright fabrication. Nothing else about the law, the technology, or human behaviour changes.

I want a reasoned prediction of how blame, and the practices built on it, change over the following ten years. Trace mechanisms and incentives rather than telling a story. In particular: who stops criticising whom, and on which subjects; what happens to political campaigns and to public pressure for accountability; what happens to institutional blame practices such as investigative journalism, workplace grievance procedures, and adversarial courtroom testimony; whether blame migrates into deniable or indirect forms—irony, hints, proxy accusers, paying or manoeuvring others into speaking—and how durable those substitutes are; and what happens to norms of forgiveness, rehabilitation, and who is entitled to call out whom.

Where the outcome turns on a particular philosophical account of standing to blame—for example, whether hypocrisy's problem is that it undermines the epistemic value of blame, that it denies the target equal moral standing, that the blame is insincere, or that the critic's record is irrelevant in principle—say so explicitly, and indicate how the ten-year trajectory would differ under the competing accounts. Separate what is well established about blame and hypocrisy from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Throughout, mark which links in your prediction are robust, which are fragile, and what assumption, if changed, would reverse each fragile link.
