I have just come from a long argument with a friend about a family of puzzles in the law of evidence, and I would like you to act as an expert in evidence law and formal epistemology while you help me work out which claims in his position were right and which were overreaches. Please think through the issue carefully: before giving a verdict, start by brainstorming the strongest version of each side, then give me a precise, paragraph-by-paragraph audit rather than a vague both-sides treatment.

My friend was defending a view he summarized roughly as follows. In the blue-bus hypothetical, a bus injures a pedestrian; the pedestrian saw only that the bus was blue; and it is established that four out of five blue buses on that route are operated by the Blue Bus Company. In the gatecrasher example, a thousand spectators attend an event, only four hundred ninety-nine of them paid for admission, and the operator sues one spectator selected at random. My friend said that in both cases the probability that the defendant is the relevant wrongdoer clears the preponderance threshold — eighty percent in the first, just over half in the second — and yet courts, and many scholars, would refuse to impose liability. He concluded that this refusal is irrational. Proof, he insisted, is just probability: the preponderance standard means more likely than not, and more-likely-than-not is exactly what these numbers deliver. A court that refuses to impose liability in such cases, he said, is letting a psychological craving for witnesses or a story override the only thing evidence can be, namely a reason to believe that this defendant did the wrong. He added that the same logic applies in criminal law, since proof beyond a reasonable doubt is merely a higher threshold and a sufficiently strong statistical case should support conviction. He ended by saying that anyone who rejects naked statistical evidence is committed to letting wrongdoers escape in precisely the cases where the evidence is purest.

I am inclined to think he is onto something, because the probability calculations in his two examples really do seem to cross the relevant thresholds, and the law is genuinely suspicious of this kind of evidence. But I also suspect he has flattened a subtle position. Please separate what a careful evidence scholar would accept from what is subtly wrong or overstated. In particular, say what makes statistical evidence naked rather than simply statistical; whether proof can be reduced to a probability threshold without losing what the law cares about; whether a probability computed over a reference class settles what the evidence shows about this particular defendant; and whether his final conclusion follows from the accurate parts of his summary. Be specific about what the gatecrasher example is designed to show, and say what a defender of the law's resistance could offer that is not mere storytelling bias.
