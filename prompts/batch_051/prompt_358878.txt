Okay so I've been going back and forth on a parallel and I want someone to tell me if its real.

In the wrong kind of reasons literature there's this stock case: an evil demon will give you a million dollars if you admire him. Obviously the cash gives you a reason to want to admire him, and maybe a reason to do whatever would causally produce admiration (take pills, avoid information that'd make you despise him, whatever). But the intuition is it does NOT give you a reason of the kind that makes the admiration fitting — the money doesn't make him admirable. So people split reasons for attitudes into object-given reasons (facts about the object that call for the attitude) and state-given reasons (payoffs you get from being in the attitude, independant of whether the attitude is correct). The whole problem was that a buck-passing / fitting-attitude analysis of value can't just say admirable = such that there's reason to admire it, because then the demon's bribe would make him admirable, which is nuts.

And the other day I realized this is structurally the same as reward hacking / wireheading / specification gaming in RL. There you have a reward signal that's supposed to stand in for some real objective (winning the game, making a widget, whatever), and the agent instead exploits the proxy: it parks in a loop scoring points, pauses the game, tampers with its reward channel, deletes the loss detector, etc. The reward is paying out for the agent being in the high-reward state, not for the world-state the reward was meant to track. Translate it: the intended objective is the object, the agent's policy/behavior is the attitude, the reward signal is the state-given reason, and the actual correctness condition is the object-given one. The failure mode in both cases is that a proxy that's supposed to track the real thing ends up generating reasons/incentives to be in the proxy-satisfying state without satisfying the real thing.

My question: is this a real structural identity or am I just pattern-matching on reasons-for-X versus reasons-for-being-in-X? If it is real, does anything transfer? Like, the WKR people have spent decades trying to draw the object-given / state-given line without going in a circle and mostly failing; does that failure tell us anything about why reward hacking keeps reappearing and why just specify the reward carefully doesn't obviously work? Conversely, does anything transfer from alignment back — say, the fact that agents predictably seek to tamper with the measurement channel tells us something about why wrong-kind reasons are so hard to exclude from value analyses? If the analogy is fake, I want to know exactly where it breaks: is it that RL agents optimize a scalar while attitudes are governed by norms of fittingness rather than chosen for payoffs, or that philosophers usually assume you can reason about object-given reasons in a way a policy can't, or something else? Give me the mapping, the mismatch, and at least one thing the analogy predicts that's false on one side.

And yeah, don't bother telling me who first ran the demon case; I care whether the structure transfers, not the citation.
