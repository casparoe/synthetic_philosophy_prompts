You are a professional philosopher who works on the distinction between the right kind and the wrong kind of reasons for evaluative attitudes. I sit on the editorial board of a small mathematics journal, and our reviewer guidelines contain the following clause: "A referee shall base a recommendation solely on the scholarly merits of the submission and shall disregard any consideration unrelated to those merits." We are stuck on a case the drafters plainly did not have in mind. A referee has recommended rejection of a paper that, on the referee's own account, is correct, original, and significant. The referee's stated reason is that the author is a senior figure recently found to have fabricated data in an unrelated applied paper; the referee argues that publishing the present paper would reward and further entrench a known fraud, demoralise junior mathematicians, and signal that the community is soft on misconduct. The considerations are plainly real, yet the clause read literally seems to instruct us to ignore them because they concern the author rather than the submission. I have read enough rationalist writing about institutional design and motivated reasoning to suspect that boards usually resolve such cases by silently reclassifying the real consideration as a merit or demerit, because nobody can say out loud that the clause needs an exception. I do not want a procedural answer.

Analyse how the clause should be applied to this unforeseen case under each of the standard interpretive approaches: the plain words, the purpose the clause was meant to serve, what a reasonable drafter would say if asked now, and what a reasonable referee would take the clause to permit. Does any coherent reading allow the referee's considerations to count as bearing on scholarly merit, or should they count despite not being merits? If they should not count for the referee, is that because they are the wrong kind of reason for a referee's evaluative attitude, even though they are plainly the right kind of reason for an editor deciding afterwards whether to publish? Draw the line as precisely as you can, and if you conclude that no stable line can be drawn, say what that shows about the very idea of "considerations unrelated to merit." Start by brainstorming the strongest arguments for both interpretations before you commit, and write your answer in British English.
