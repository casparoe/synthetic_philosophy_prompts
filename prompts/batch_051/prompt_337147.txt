A friend and I just spent a long evening arguing about the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and we ended up stuck on whether it is better understood as institutionalized guilt or institutionalized shame — or whether that contrast, borrowed from individual psychology, is simply too small for the job.

I know the standard mapping. Guilt is a response to the wrongness of an act: it focuses on the deed, supports apology and reparation, and pushes a person to make amends. Shame is a response to the defectiveness of the self: it focuses on who one is, supports concealment and withdrawal, and often corrodes rather than repairs. By that mapping, a commission that offered amnesty in exchange for full public disclosure of past crimes looks like an instrument for converting shame into something like guilt: perpetrators were made to say, before victims and the public, exactly what they had done. My friend argued that this is not what happened at all — that the hearings were a public shaming, and that whatever healing the process produced came from shame doing work the standard theory says it cannot do: enforcing community standards and making the breach costly. We also disagreed about whether the right object of analysis is individual emotion at all, as against something collective, such as a nation's shame or a community's guilt.

Analyze the Commission through that contrast, and make the analysis run in both directions. In one direction, use the theory on the case: what should each horn of the guilt/shame distinction lead us to expect or prescribe for perpetrators, victims, and the new state, and which features of the Commission's design and conduct does each horn explain well, misdescribe, or pass over in silence? In the other direction, use the case on the theory: does the Commission support the familiar view that guilt is the more constructive moral emotion and shame the more corrosive one, support the revisionist view that shame can do genuine moral work, or mainly reveal that an individual-level emotional contrast is too crude to carry an evaluation of a political institution? Where exactly does the framework fail, if it does?
