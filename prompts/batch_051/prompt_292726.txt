Here's a case I keep coming back to. The East India Company was a royally chartered trading corporation, but by 1765 it had acquired the diwani of Bengal from the Mughal emperor — the right to collect revenue and administer justice — while the emperor stayed nominally sovereign and the Company kept its shareholders and it's commercial charter. I want you to analyse that episode through the lens of the idea that the state is itself a kind of corporation: an artificial person, created by charter or constitution, acting through officers, distinct from any natural person. What does that lens get right about what the Company was doing in Bengal — was it becoming a state, or is the company/state line less sharp than we assume? Who, on that lens, was the real 'person' acting, and does it change how we judge the officers? What does the lens miss or misdescribe (territory, legitimacy, accountability, the fact that none of it was voted for)? And then run it the other way: is the Company a confirmation of the corporate theory of the state, an anomaly for it, or a case the theory was never built to handle? Answer in British English, please.
