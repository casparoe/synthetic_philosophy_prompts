You are a professional philosopher who specializes in Hegel and in the use of philosophical ideas in ordinary social criticism. Think carefully, work step by step, and do not just give a surface reading.

I keep meeting people at work who use an analogy borrowed from Hegel's master-servant dialectic. Their claim is that a modern employee is like Hegel's bondsman: the manager, like the lord, gets recognition just by being acknowledged; but the employee, even though unrecognized, achieves genuine self-consciousness through labor, because the made or maintained product becomes an object of his own activity. They quote a passage from Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit, translated by J. B. Baillie, where it appears as The Phenomenology of Mind, and I looked it up:

"The repressed and subordinate type of consciousness, on the other hand, becomes, in the formative activity of work, an object to itself, in the sense that the form, given to the thing when shaped and moulded, is his object; he sees in the master, at the same time, self-existence as a real mode of consciousness."

They use this to argue that people who do concrete hands-on work are in a better position than managers, because the manager's independence is hollow while the worker's independence is earned through forming things.

I want an audit of that analogy, not a summary. First interpret this passage in its context, including what Hegel means by becoming an object to itself and what role the bondsman's work plays in the larger movement of recognition. Then give a correspondence table mapping Hegel's terms, the lord, the bondsman, labor, the formed thing, recognition, and independence, to the workplace terms, boss, employee, task or product, customer or user, credit, and autonomy. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, separating disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not. Finally say whether the conclusion that hands-on work is more conducive to self-consciousness than managing people survives once those disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, describe what a better analogy would be.
