I cover the AI beat and I keep noticing that the alarming arguments all run through a pair of ideas I can't quite nail down: the orthogonality thesis — roughly, you can have sky-high competence aimed at any terminal goal whatsoever, from paperclips to universal kindness — and instrumental convergence, roughly, that a whole lot of different terminal goals will produce the same sub-goals anyway: stay alive, ammass resources, protect your own goals, keep options open. I want to write the peice that says what a serious theory of instrumental convergence would actually have to get right, instead of just gesturing at self-preservation being automatically rational. Let's say the theory has to be good enough to underwrite the standard worry that a wide spread of advanced systems will, by default, try to accumulate resources and resist shutdown — not just to describe a few toy agents. So treat this like a spec: give me the list of requirements, and for each one tell me the motivating case or the false view that forces it. I especially want the distinctions a good theory can't collapse: between something being useful for one particular goal and being convergent across a broad class of goals, between convergence in the agent's world model and convergence in its policy, between the claim that most well-designed agents will do X and the claim that X is a theorem of instrumental rationality. For trouble cases I'm thinking of: the agent whose terminal goal is satisfied by being turned off, the agent that wants to make exactly one paperclip and then stop, the agent with a terminal preference against continuing to exist, the finite-horizon agent, the agent that wants to minimize its impact on the world. Which of those does the theory have to get right, and which are edge cases it's allowed to bracket? What does it owe the orthogonality thesis — if the only way to get convergence is to sneak in a terminal preference for existing or for winning, is that a red flag or a feature? Are there results from decision theory or economics it isn't allowed to contradict, like the fact that you can always write down a utility function that punishes staying alive, and which single constraint does the most to rule out the sloppy versions people throw around? I also keep worrying that the almost in almost any terminal goal is doing hidden work, since there's no obvious way to count goals or put a measure over all possible objectives — does a real theory have to specify the class of goals it's averaging over, or can it dodge that? Say which requirements are negotiable and which are load-bearing, and name the one requirement that knocks out the most popular loose accounts. Scoring the existing views can come at the end and stay brief; I'm mostly after the spec, not the review.
