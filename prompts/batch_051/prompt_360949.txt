I am caught in the middle of a nasty dispute between two friends, and I would be grateful for careful help thinking it through. One of them, Priya, is a screenwriter developing a limited series about a real documentary photographer — I will call her Elena Voss — who died almost twenty years ago. Voss was never famous, but her photographs of state violence have become an important historical record, and her adult daughter now runs a small foundation that preserves the archive and her mother's reputation. The daughter initially supported the project and gave Priya access to letters, contact sheets, and unpublished diaries, but the collaboration has collapsed over a single scene. In it, the young Voss, envious of a rival photographer's better access to a demonstration, anonymously tips off the security police about where the rival will be that evening. The rival is badly beaten and misses the event; the photograph Voss takes that night makes her career. Nothing in the archive even hints that anything like this happened, but nothing rules it out either.

Priya wants to keep the scene. She says it supplies the moral engine for the rest of the series, that betrayals of exactly this kind did occur in that milieu, and that the series will include a title card noting that some scenes are imagined. The daughter wants the scene cut. She says she never licensed a false accusation against her mother, that viewers will remember the incident as established fact no matter what disclaimers say, and that a life's work is not raw material to be bent into whatever shape sells. Priya's showrunner backs her: you cannot slander the dead, every dramatist since Sophocles has rearranged a life for dramatic form, and a biopic is a drama, not a biography. The daughter's reply has been sticking with me: if the dead cannot be wronged by what is said about them, then why do we care so much what will be said at our own funerals?

I have ended up as the mediator, and I am supposed to tell both sides what I actually think. Could you approach this as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of ethics and narrative? Please start by brainstorming the distinct considerations in play — even the ones that pull against one another — and then reason carefully to a verdict about whether the scene should be kept, cut, or changed, and under what conditions. I am not looking for a legal answer or a survey of how biopics usually proceed; I want the clearest possible map of what is morally at stake.
