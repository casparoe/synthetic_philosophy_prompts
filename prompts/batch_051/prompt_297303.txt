I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on philosophy of mind, around 20 students, one 90-minute session. I want an in-class game that makes the problem of other minds show up in what students actually do, not in what I say afterward. The thing I want them to run into: the only evidence we ever have about other minds is behavior, and the same behavior is compatible with very different inner lives — or none at all. Design a real game with rules and points (points buy a small course advantage, like picking the next reading or a deadline extension), where some students have private inside information no one else can access, and the others have to make a consequential call — bet points, decide who to trust in a task, something like that — about what's inside. Make it adversarial: give some players an incentive to fake or misrepresent their inner states, so observers can't just ask and take the answer on faith. Include materials and setup, an exact timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps the game onto the first-person/third-person asymmetry and underdetermination. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the philosophical problem the game can't reproduce. Don't fall back on the standard beetle-in-a-box exercise; if that idea appears at all, it should be the debrief backdrop, not the activity. Commit to a single design: pick the game, rules, and structure you think are best and defend them, rather than giving me a menu of options.
