I keep coming back to a claim Hofstadter makes about creativity—roughly, that what we treat as a bolt from the blue is mostly knob-twiddling: you have a theme, a kind of conceptual space you already understand, and creating is mostly turning parameters inside that space, hitting combinations nobody had tried, and once in a while discovering the space had an extra dimension you didnt know about. I was arguing about this with a friend who composes. He says his best work comes from deliberately staying inside a tight constraint and working every corner of it, which sounds exactly like knob-twiddling, but he also says the best ideas feel like the theme breaking open, which sounds like the opposite.

I cant decide whether the knob-twiddling view is a substantive hypothesis about how creative work actually happens, or just a way of redescribing any product after the fact—once youve heard the fugue or seen the painting, you can always pick some theme it counts as a variation on. So heres what I want: design the best test, or a small battery of tests, of the claim that creativity is variations on a theme, understood either as a claim about the process or as a claim about the structure of creative products—say explicitly which reading each test is aimed at. For each test, say concretely what result would favor the view and what result would count against it. Then be honest about the residue: what would even a strong positive result leave unsettled, for instance whether "theme" and "knob" are being read into the data after the fact, or whether knob-twiddling can explain why a variation is good rather than merely different.

After the main discussion, give me a markdown table with these columns: Proposed test; What you'd actually do; Result that supports knob-twiddling; Result that undercuts it; What it can't settle. Then a one-sentence bottom line. American English, please.
