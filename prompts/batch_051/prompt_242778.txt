Adam Smith writes in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Part II, Section I, Chapter I:

‘The sentiment which most immediately and directly prompts us to reward, is gratitude; that which most immediately and directly prompts us to punish, is resentment.’

Please argue for the following position: that this account of reward and punishment, if taken seriously, implies that resultant moral luck is not a defect in our moral psychology but a rationally intelligible feature of the way reactive attitudes such as gratitude and resentment pick out their proper objects. Your argument should interpret the quoted passage carefully, connect it to Smith’s later discussion of the influence of fortune on the sentiments of mankind, and answer the strongest objection that outcomes cannot properly reach back to alter the moral quality of an agent’s motive. Be explicit about where your interpretation rests on settled features of Smith’s view and where it is genuinely contested among people who know the topic; do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Write in British English.
