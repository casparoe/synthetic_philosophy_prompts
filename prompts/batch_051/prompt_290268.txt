I've got a political philosophy seminar, 16 undergrads, and a single 75-minute block I want to use for a game rather than a lecture. The theme is epistocracy versus democracy, and I want the students to feel the tension from the inside before anyone names it — the words 'epistocracy' and 'restricted suffrage' shouldn't come up until the debrief. The two forces I'm trying to get in the room are: (1) the common-sense pull of the competence principle — on factually loaded questions, someone who actually knows the relevant material should have more say than someone who is guessing — and (2) the moral resistance that shows up the moment you actually strip or shrink someone's vote because they scored badly: equal standing, respect, fear of a permanent know-it-all caste. I'd like the rules themselves to produce both reactions, not a setup speech, and theyre not allowed to hear the names until afterwards.

The students should be making real decisions with real but small stakes about the next couple weeks of the course — e.g., how participation gets weighted, whether laptops and phones are allowed in discussion, how a small pool of discretionary points gets spent. The game needs a mechanism that makes 'competence' measurable and genuinely uneven in the room (a briefing sheet given to a random subset, a short test on the actual consequences of the options, that sort of thing), and then it should have them work through the same type of decision under contrasting franchise rules: one-person-one-vote, restricted suffrage, score-weighted votes, an enfranchisement lottery, or whatever mix you think will actually force the tension into the open.

Please write it up as a runnable plan: exact rules, materials, the decision prompts we should actually use, a stakes scheme that needs no real money, and a timed debrief that maps what happened onto the conceptual structure of the epistocracy debate — the competence principle, the instrumental case for weighting votes by knowledge, the demeaning/equal-standing objection, the demographic objection (the knowledgeable will skew toward the already advantaged), and the civic-skill worry about the people who get excluded. Be honest about what the game can't reproduce — the stakes of actual government, the fact that any real 'competence' measure is itself contested — and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how you'd handle it.
