Since I retired from invertebrate zoology and took up philosophy of biology, I keep meeting the same move in debates about biological individuality, especially from people who study social insects and corals. The move is that a colony is not merely like an organism but is one — a superorganism. The mapping they offer is straightforward: the queen corresponds to the germ line, the sterile workers to the somatic cells, the age-based division of labor to cellular differentiation, the nest or mound to a body wall, and the nuptial flight or colony fission to reproduction of the colony itself rather than of its members. From this they draw a conclusion that matters to them: selection at the level of the colony is exactly as respectable as selection at the level of the individual bee or polyp, and the question of where, in a hierarchy of living things, the organism really is has no privileged answer. At a departmental reunion last month, a former colleague extended the same analogy to holobionts — a reef coral together with its zooxanthellae, a termite together with its gut protists — arguing that if the superorganism analogy is granted, then a holobiont deserves the name organism for the same reason, because the line between one living thing and its parts is blurred in the same way.

I would like you to audit that analogy rather than repeat it. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what in the colony plays the role of germ line, soma, development, metabolism, reproduction, and the death of an individual. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks or quietly imports something the colony does not have. I can think of worker policing and other within-colony conflict, the fact that in many species workers retain their own reproductive options, the lack of a single spatially bounded developing body of the sort a metazoan has, and the problem that what looks like colony death is often fission, abandonment, or takeover rather than the irreversible dissolution of an integrated whole. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not. Then tell me whether the conclusion — that colony-level selection and holobionts should both be admitted as organisms on the strength of this analogy — survives, or whether the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear; and if so, say what a better comparison would be. Throughout, please state how confident you are in your claims, and identify the point you are least sure of.
