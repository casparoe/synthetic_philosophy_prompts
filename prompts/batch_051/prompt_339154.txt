I have spent the last few weeks reading through the long-running dispute over clinical equipoise in the ethics of randomized trials, and what frustrates me is that the two sides seem to share almost all the relevant facts and still make no contact. The standard defense of randomization runs roughly like this: assigning a patient to either arm of a trial is compatible with a physician's duty of care only when the expert medical community is genuinely uncertain about which treatment is superior; under that condition, no patient is being knowingly given a treatment the physician judges inferior. The critics answer that this community-level uncertainty is being asked to do work it cannot do. A statistical state of divided opinion across a profession is not the same as any actual physician's judgment, and if this particular physician has a reasonable preference for one arm, enrolling and randomizing the patient still seems to require setting aside the patient's individualized interests. Defenders then appeal to the patient's informed consent to enter a trial; critics reply that consent cannot license treating a person as a means for producing generalizable knowledge if the physician simultaneously presents the trial as clinical care.

The deeper I go, the more the discussion seems to run in place. Each side accepts that some randomized trials are ethically necessary; each accepts that clinical care and clinical research differ in point; what they cannot agree on is whether "uncertainty" is the right concept to mediate between those two activities. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not about uncertainty at all, but about role morality — specifically whether a physician's therapeutic obligation simply persists through the act of enrolling a patient in a trial, or whether trial participation already places the physician in a second role whose norms are partly set by the scientific purposes of the trial. Diagnose this impasse for me. Is it a clash of bedrock intuitions about professional roles, a disagreement about what counts as a harm or a wrong to a patient, a hidden empirical question about what patients actually understand when they consent, or a dispute with two different questions buried under the single word "equipoise"? And given your diagnosis, say what would genuinely count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a kind of evidence, a change in the question, or an honest admission that the two sides derive their obligations from incommensurable starting points.
