I've been arguing with my housemate about carbon taxes for months now — he's an econ grad student and I'm the one reading degrowth stuff — and I've started keeping a running list of the standard objections so I stop repeating myself. As of last week it reads: (1) the distributional one: a carbon tax is regressive, and even with a dividend or a lump-sum rebate it doesn't fully track how different households are actually hit; (2) the leakage and competitiveness one: emissions just relocate to jurisdictions without the tax, and trade-exposed domestic industry gets punished while global emissions don't fall; (3) the political-durability one: a highly visible carbon price gets voted out the moment fuel prices spike, so it never stays high enough or credible enough to drive long-run investment; (4) the quantity-uncertainty one: a tax fixes a price, not an emissions path, and since we don't really know the relevant elasticities we might sail past the target; (5) the moral-framing one: putting a price on emissions turns a wrong into a purchasable commodity and can quietly license continued pollution as a normal cost of doing business; (6) the tax-interaction one: layering a carbon tax onto existing taxes on labor and capital makes the whole system more distortionary unless the revenue is recycled exactly right.

I keep feeling there's another objection that belong on the list, something I almost say in these arguments but can't ever pin down. What is it? It needs to be genuinely different from all six of the above — not a rewording, a special case, or a mash-up of two of them — and I'd want you to tell me straight whether its actually a strong objection or just a clever-sounding quibble.
