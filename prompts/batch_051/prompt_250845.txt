I teach at a small K-8 school and I've somehow become the person who reviews curriculum. Three times a year I get about an hour with each teacher to go over their next unit plan before they finalize it, and the thing I keep flagging is that the plan is full of ideas the kids will memorize, pass a quiz on, and never touch again — never use them, connect them to anything, question them, or bring them back later. I read Whitehead on this once, and the way I remember it, he thought the main disease of schooling was inert ideas: facts that sit in a kid's head without ever being used, questioned, or connected to anything. But I also remember he wasn't saying every idea has to be immediately useful in a grim job-skills way. There's a stage where a kid is supposed to fall in love with a subject before the precision and application come later. Which means the two failure modes look annoyingly similar from the outside: an idea that's genuinely inert, and an idea that's just not ripe yet.

Write me a reusable procedure — a checklist or a set of tests in a fixed order, maybe ending in a small decision tree — that a teacher can run down a unit plan and sort every idea in it into one of three piles: cut it entirely, rework it so the kids actually do something with it, or leave it alone because it's either foundational plumbing or still in that stage where the kid is falling for it. The teachers have no philosophy background and no enthusiasm for philosophy, so the procedure has to be usable by a tired fourth-grade teacher on a Sunday night in under 45 minutes without me in the room. My principal wants a one-page version she can hand to any parent who pushes back, so it can't read like theory. I also have to be able to defend it to a parent who thinks we've gone soft because their kid used to memorize dates and doesn't seem to anymore. And if the procedure just ends up saying make everything a hands-on project, you've lost the thread — the inert-versus-unripe distinction is exactly the part that has to survive the simplification.

Then give me two or three worked examples, one of which should be a case where the procedure tells a teacher to cut something they love — make it something with serious but-it's-on-the-test energy, like the stages of mitosis or the causes of the War of 1812. Include at least one case where the procedure defends leaving alone something that looks inert but isn't. And be explicit about the cases the procedure can't settle (where it really is a judgment call, and what I should be looking at when I make that call), and about which parts of Whitehead's idea you had to fudge or leave out to get it down to a page.
