ok so here's my population ethics view, or at least where I've landed, and I want to know whether the pieces actually hang together before I present it:

(1) Betterness across outcomes is transitive, no exceptions.

(2) The value of an outcome is just the sum of individual lifetime well-being — I don't think equality or distribution has value over and above that.

(3) I accept the repugnant conclusion: for any population of lives at a high welfare level, there's a much larger population of lives at a barely-positive welfare level that is better. I've made my peace with this.

(4) But I reject the sadistic-sounding mirror image: adding new people whose lives are not worth living can't be better than adding new people whose lives are barely worth living.

(5) I also think the zero line — between a life worth living and a life not worth living — is morally momentous in a way that ordinary differences among positive levels aren't; it's not just a convention.

(6) I've got the standard procreation asymmetry intuition too: there's no obligation to bring a happy person into existence just because they'd be happy, but there is a strong obligation not to bring a miserable person into existence.

Which of these are actually in tension and which only look like it? I want them ranked by severity, with what the cheapest fix would be for each, and which single change would clean up the most.
