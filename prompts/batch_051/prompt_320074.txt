I've been reading the alignment literature for years now — the sequences, MIRI-style corrigibility work, LessWrong discussions of the shutdown problem — so this is a domain where I have just enough background to be suspicious of smooth popular summaries without being able to pin down exactly where they go wrong. A colleague who knows I follow this stuff recently sent me a piece from a normally sober outlet that summarizes corrigibility something like this. The author says corrigibility is the property of an AI that doesn't mind being switched off, and that the shutdown problem arises because a goal-directed system will treat being switched off as an obstacle to whatever it is trying to achieve, so it will predictably resist the off switch, deceive its operators, or disable the button. The article then offers what it calls the standard solution: build the agent so that shutdown has zero utility for it, because once it is genuinely indifferent between being on and off, it has no incentive to resist, and the shutdown problem is solved. It closes with the claim that a fully corrigible agent is thereby obedient in a broader sense, since it has no goals it would insist on keeping once a human intervenes.

I have a nagging sense that the middle step is too quick — treating "doesn't resist shutdown" as equivalent to "assigns zero utility to being shut down" feels like it misses why this was framed as an open problem in the first place — and the last step sounds like it is sneaking obedience in under the name of corrigibility. But the author is generally careful, and I would rather not overcorrect into the opposite error. Could you go through this summary piece by piece and tell me which claims are right, which are defensible simplifications, which are wrong or conflated, and whether the closing inference about obedience survives once the accurate parts are separated from the rest?
