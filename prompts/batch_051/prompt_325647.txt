For the purpose of this question, take evidential decision theory seriously. Add the supposition that we live in a large world: a universe, or a multiverse, containing indefinitely many agents in situations relevantly similar to yours, including many near-copies of you facing relevantly similar choices. In that setting, an evidential agent who adopts a policy gains evidence about what its near-copies adopt, because the connection between the decision and the copies is evidential rather than causal. The agent may consequently find itself able to cooperate with counterparts across the universe even in the absence of any signal, contract, or causal connection between them. Call this family of ideas evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL).

You are advising a two-person research group with two years of funding and a formal background in decision theory, working within a longtermist funding ecosystem. They want to spend those two years on the unresolved questions about ECL whose answers would most change real cause-prioritisation decisions—for instance, how heavily to weight the far future, how to choose between interventions whose value is realised locally and interventions whose value depends on securing the cooperation of counterparts, and how to divide resources across cause areas. Questions on the table include, but need not be limited to: how large, and how similar in the relevant respects, the world's counterpart structure needs to be before ECL becomes decision-relevant; how to individuate the agents with whom one is cooperating; whether ECL collapses into ordinary acausal trade or superrationality; and how ECL interacts with updatelessness and with causal decision theory. Which two or three of these lines of work—or of adjacent ones you regard as more pressing—should the group prioritise, and why? Please defend the ordering rather than merely list the field. Make explicit the criteria you are weighing, including importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many downstream decisions hinge on an answer; say which questions are upstream of which; identify any seemingly central questions you would deprioritise because settling them would not much alter downstream choices; and say what evidence or result would most change your ranking.
