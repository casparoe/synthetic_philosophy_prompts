My brother and I are about to start a small business, and we need to decide how to structure our involvement. Each of us will choose between working in the business full-time and keeping a safer part-time job on the side. We both agree that we would each be better off with both of us full-time than with both of us hedging, and that the venture itself does best if we are both full-time. At the same time, each of us privately recognises that he would be best off if the other went full-time while he himself kept the fallback, and that he would be worst off if he went full-time while the other hedged.

Our accountant has proposed an unusual way to settle this before we sign the partnership agreement. Each of us would submit to him, in advance and in private, a short decision rule for ourselves. A rule may simply prescribe full-time or part-time, but it may also make the choice conditional on the content of the other person's rule — for example, it may say that I will go full-time just in case my brother's rule is word-for-word the same as mine. The accountant would then compare the two rules and apply each rule to the other, and the resulting choices would be treated as binding.

My brother objects that this is only a promise dressed up as procedure. He argues that the underlying temptation to hedge has not changed, and that the only stable outcome is the one in which we both hedge. I suspect that letting our rules refer to one another changes the logic of the situation, but I cannot say exactly how, and I do not know what rule to submit. Who is right, and what should our rules say? Please write your answer in British English.
