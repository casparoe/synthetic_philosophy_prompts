I'm putting together a which-theory-do-you-actually-hold quiz for my intermediate philosophy of language course, the unit on conditionals, and I keep coming back to this passage from Dorothy Edgington's Stanford Encyclopedia article "Indicative Conditionals":

"It is controversial how best to classify conditionals. According to some theorists, the forward-looking “indicatives” (those with a “will” in the main clause) belong with the “subjunctives” (those with a “would” in the main clause), and not with the other “indicatives”. (See Gibbard (1981, pp. 222–6), Dudman (1984, 1988), Bennett (1988). Bennett (1995) changed his mind. Jackson (1990) defends the traditional view.) The easy transition from typical “wills” to “woulds” is indeed a datum to be explained. Still, straightforward statements about the past, present or future, to which a conditional clause is attached — the traditional class of indicative conditionals — do (in my view) constitute a single semantic kind."

The passage is the hook for the handout because it shows the split I want the quiz to detect: whether the line between indicative and subjunctive is a semantic joint, a pragmatic convenience, or something that runs through the middle of the "indicative" category itself, around forward-looking will-conditionals. So I'd like an 8–10 question quiz with a scoring key. But build it from the logical space rather than from labels: start from the claims that genuinely separate the views (Do conditionals have truth values at all? Is the indicative/subjunctive difference semantic or pragmatic? If there are truth conditions, are they truth-functional, strict, or variably strict? Is there a unique closest antecedent world? Do forward-looking indicatives pattern with subjunctives?), then locate the named leaves in that space: the material conditional, the strict conditional, Stalnaker, Lewis, an Adams/Edgington no-truth-conditions suppositional view, and a Dudman-style bifurcation. I'd also like you to note which combinations of answers nobody occupies and say briefly whether that's because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and to flag pairs your quiz can't really seperate — I'm suspicious that Stalnaker and Lewis only come apart on ties between equally similar worlds, and that most simple cases won't catch that.

Each question should be a concrete little scenario with an actual conditional to judge, with two or three answer options, and the scoring key should map answer patterns onto the views without the questions ever using the labels or the technical vocabulary. The idea is to sort students by what they say about cases, not by which term sounds impressive to them. You are an expert on the semantics and pragmatics of conditionals; think carefully, brainstorm candidate cases first, then write the quiz so that someone who has never heard of Lewis or Edgington could still be classified cleanly. No need for citations, reading lists, or a bibliography — just the handout.
