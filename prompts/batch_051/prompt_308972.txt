Before I retired I spent my career as a working scientist, and I am accustomed to treating a well-designed statistical study as close to the gold standard of empirical support. Since beginning to read philosophy of law and formal epistemology, however, I have become preoccupied with a pair of standard examples that seem to show the law does not, and perhaps should not, run on that same principle, and I cannot determine how deep the difference goes.

The first is the blue bus case. A pedestrian is injured by a bus but cannot identify its color or operator; she establishes that the Blue Bus Company operates eighty percent of the buses on that route. If the civil standard is preponderance of the evidence, understood as more likely than not, the case against Blue Bus looks strong. Yet most people, and most legal doctrine, resist finding the company liable on that statistical showing alone. The second is the gatecrasher case. One thousand spectators attend a rodeo, and four hundred ninety-nine paid admission; the organizer selects a spectator at random and sues that person for an unpaid ticket on the strength of a 50.1 percent probability. The probability crosses the same threshold, and still something seems wrong with imposing liability on that basis.

What interests me is not the cases themselves but the structure of the debate around them. For decades, philosophers and legal theorists have proposed accounts of what is missing from these naked statistical proofs. Some argue that legal proof requires a causal or explanatory connection between the evidence and the particular event. Some argue that a verdict should express knowledge, and that evidence of this purely statistical kind cannot supply it. Some argue that the real issue is not what the evidence proves but the incentives and distribution of error that a rule of liability would create. And some bite the bullet, insisting that the law is simply irrational to reject a probability that crosses the stated threshold. Each family of views handles the familiar examples, each runs into trouble with modified ones, and as far as I can tell the debate has largely stopped moving. The same cases reappear, the same moves are replayed, and nobody updates.

I am not asking you to adjudicate the dispute. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it a substantive disagreement about what legal proof is for, an empirical disagreement about how fact-finders actually reason, a conceptual disagreement about the meaning of the word evidence, or a clash of starting intuitions about when it is acceptable to impose liability that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden question doing the work, for example whether a bare increase in probability can ever license a normative act like punishment, while the parties talk as if the question were purely epistemic? My working hypothesis is that the debate stalls because the two sides are quietly using proof for two different things, one an epistemic relation to a particular historical fact and the other an actuarial warrant for action, and that the phrase preponderance of the evidence conflates them. I do not trust that hypothesis entirely, since I can imagine variants of the dispute that would survive the distinction. I am also struck that the same unease reappears, in sharper form, around the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, where it is not merely a matter of a higher probability threshold.

Please give me your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, with reasons that engage the actual positions rather than survey them, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a sharper distinction, a formal result, an empirical study of fact-finders, a reformulation of the question, or something else. If you think no such progress should be expected, say why and tell me what should be done with the debate instead. I would like the response in American English.
