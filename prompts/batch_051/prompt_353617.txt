Write me a decision tree for a one-page handout in a metaphilosophy seminar on what thought experiments are doing when philosophers actually use them. Make sure these positions show up as leaves: strong armchair evidentialism, modal rationalism (the conceivability-as-evidence view), illustrationism, the intuition-pump view, experimental-philosophy skepticism, the argument view (cases are compressed arguments and carry only as much weight as their hidden premises), and pluralism. Build the tree from yes/no splits rather than from the labels. Every split should be a concrete judgment call someone can make without already knowing the positions, e.g. whether a single well-chosen case can force revision of a long-standing analysis, whether a case flipping under different order or framing means you should distrust that verdict rather than just control for it, or whether a case gives you something an explicit deductive reconstruction would leave out. Use at least four of these cases as anchors in the nodes or at the leaves: Gettier's stopped clock, Mary the color scientist, Thomson's violinist, Putnam's Twin Earth, Parfit's fission, the trolley cases. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't cleanly seperate; I suspect illustrationism and the intuition-pump view come out nearly identical unless you build in a question about whether the case is aimed at persuasion or at clarification, so handle that explicitly. Also say which answer-combinations in the logical space are empty, and for each one whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or underexplored. Keep it usable by students who know the classic cases but have never heard the labels.
