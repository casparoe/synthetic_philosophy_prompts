I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about what gives an oath its binding force—whether it is the god invoked, the words spoken, or the intention behind them. She never stated a principle, but her particular verdicts were steady. An oath made to a highwayman at knifepoint, she said, is void; so is an oath sworn to a god to carry out a massacre, because no oath can make an impermissible act obligatory. A god who swears on the Styx—the oath by which even the gods bind themselves most solemnly—is bound even when keeping the oath costs him dearly. A purely secular promise to a friend binds just as much as the most solemn oath, she thinks, and a public, ceremonial oath of office binds even when the officeholder later finds it ruinous and wishes she had never sworn it.

Now I want to anticipate how she will rule on a new case. A diplomat swears on the Styx, with full public ceremony, to observe a treaty whose terms are entirely permissible; but he privately does not believe in the Styx and never intends to keep the oath. Is he bound? More than the bare yes or no, I want the candidate principles that fit her track record, how those principles disagree about the diplomat, which principle you think best captures her and why, and one further case that would most cleanly separate any principles that survive this prediction. Finally, say which consideration for the rival reading would most likely change your mind, or where your prediction is most liable to fail.
