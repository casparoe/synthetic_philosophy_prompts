I keep coming back to Henry Sidgwick's argument in his 1882 presidential address to the Society for Psychical Research, where he says, roughly, that claims about post-mortem survival should be settled by the ordinary methods of evidence gathering—testing witnesses, checking conditions, ruling out fraud—and that rejecting them before investigation, just because the conclusion would be huge or weird, isn't a scientific attitude. He isn't saying survival has been proved; he's saying the question belongs to the same empirical tribunal as everything else.

I find that principle genuinely compelling: the same standards of evidence should apply everywhere, and the strangeness of a conclusion isn't itself evidence against it. But I also have a very firm intuition that there must be something wrong with the medium cases, even when I can't point to the flaw. Not a specific suspicion about any one report—I've read bits of the SPR work, and the precautions often sound careful—but a sense that the whole field is somehow rotten, that the dead are gone, full stop. i dont know whether this is a legitimate induction from history, since every previous supernatural claim has collapsed on close inspection, or just a prejudice wearing the clothes of common sense.

I want a negotiated verdict. In this clash between a tidy methodological principle and a firm intuition, which should give? What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—would I need to find a specific flaw, or could the principle just overrule it? What would it take for the principle to be the thing that goes? Is there a reading on which both survive, say by distinguishing methodological open-mindedness from assigning low prior probabilities on background knowledge? Answer in British English.
