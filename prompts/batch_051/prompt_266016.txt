I've been trying to figure out whether my gut verdicts about suffering and happiness actually hang together, and this bit from Schopenhauer's On the Sufferings of the World (the essay in Studies in Pessimism) keeps feeling like it's pointing at whatever principle I half-believe:

"It is the good which is negative; in other words, happiness and satisfaction always imply some desire fulfilled, some state of pain brought to an end."

If he's right, pain is the positive thing and pleasure is mostly just the ending or easing of a pain. My verdicts: I'm quite sure it's wrong for me not to prevent a coworker's week of severe back pain when I could do it with a two-line email, but I don't think it's wrong for me to skip surprising a friend with the concert tickets I know would make his month. I think it'd be monstrous to create a child whose whole life would be nothing but pain, but there's nothing wrong with deciding not to have a child who would probably be happy. I don't think later happiness can retroactively cancel earlier suffering: a happy adulthood does not make a miserable childhood okay. But then I also think it's completely fine for most people to have children whose lives will be a mix, and that seems really hard to square with treating suffering as the thing that does all the moral work. Whats the principle behind these verdicts—if there is one—and which of them does it force me to drop? And whats its verdict on the case I keep dodging: should I spend every spare dollar easing existing suffering before I spend anything on adding happiness to people who are already comfortable?
