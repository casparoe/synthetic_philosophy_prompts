You are an expert in formal epistemology, especially the theory of chance and credence, and you are good at turning technical principles into procedures that non-philosophers can actually run.

I direct the forecast-operations unit at a state emergency-management agency. During fire, flood, and heat season, my unit receives probabilistic model output every morning: a 20% chance that a river gauge will exceed flood stage within 48 hours, an 8% chance that a wildfire will reach a town, a 15% chance that a heat event will cross a public-health threshold. My staff have to fold these numbers into decisions about warnings, evacuations, and pre-positioning resources. The decisions are frequent — several per day in season — and high-stakes, and after-action reviews punish both overreaction and underreaction.

The recurring problem is that my analysts and field staff often hold information the model did not use: an engineer's report on a levee repair, a field observer's report of smoke, a hospital's report of heat-related admissions. We have two bad default modes. Some people treat the model's number as authoritative and suppress what they saw. Others override the model whenever their gut says otherwise. I want one reusable procedure that tells my staff when they should simply adopt the model's probability as their working probability, and when their extra information licenses moving off it.

Ground the procedure in the Principal Principle about deference to objective chance. As I understand it, the principle says roughly that if you know the objective chance of an event is x, your rational credence in that event should be x — but only when the rest of your information is admissible, where inadmissible information is information that bears on the event over and above the chance itself. I need you to make that operational for people who have no philosophy background and no probability theory beyond basic percentages.

Please produce the following.

1. A decision procedure in the form of an ordered checklist. For each step, give the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the stop rule that follows. Mark every step where human judgment cannot be eliminated. The procedure must be runnable in fifteen minutes by a single duty officer and must produce a written record that can be defended in an after-action review.

2. The procedure must begin by forcing the user to state what kind of number the model is producing — an objective chance, a base rate for a reference class, a calibrated forecast, a subjective judgment dressed up as a chance — and it must say which of those actually trigger deference under the Principal Principle and which do not. It must also operationalize the admissibility condition: how should a non-philosopher decide whether a piece of extra information is admissible or inadmissible with respect to the model's number?

3. An honest statement of what the procedure cannot do — for example, what to do when the extra information is inadmissible, how to combine the model's number with inadmissible evidence, or how to set the decision threshold once the probability is fixed.

4. A worked application of the procedure to each of the following three cases, running the checklist all the way to its stop rule:
   a. A calibrated riverine-flood model gives a 25% chance that a particular levee overtops within 48 hours. The duty officer thinks the chance "feels more like 40%" because the ground is already saturated. Saturation data were among the model's inputs.
   b. A dam-safety model, well calibrated on a large class of similar dams, gives a 5% chance of failure this season. An engineer has just inspected this dam and found active seepage that the model does not capture.
   c. A plume-dispersion model gives an 8% chance that a chemical release reaches a populated area. The agency director wants the staff to treat the probability as much higher, because the consequences of being wrong are catastrophic and "8% is too high to ignore."

Make sure at least one of the worked applications ends in an answer I will not like, and say explicitly which one it is and why the procedure gives that answer anyway.

Before you write the checklist, start by restating the principle in your own words and by explaining the admissible/inadmissible distinction as you will use it in the procedure. Think carefully about the difference between changing the probability and changing the decision threshold; the procedure should say, at each point, which of those it is doing, and it should say explicitly when it cannot separate the two.
