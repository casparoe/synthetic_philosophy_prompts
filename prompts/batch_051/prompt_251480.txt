I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on decision theory and the self — 16 students, 80 minutes. I want an in-class game that makes them feel the weirdness of assigning probabilities to their own choices: the way deliberating about what to do seems to crowd out treating yourself as a predictor of yourself. Design an activity where each student has to make a real low-stakes choice (points, candy, whatever) and, at some point, also has to report or bet on their own probability of choosing each option. The rules should put those two tasks in tension so the problem shows up in what actually happens, rather then in what I say afterward. Include the exact rules, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the conceptual point. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle that, and which part of the idea the activity just can't reproduce — the part I'll have to supply myself. They'll have seen Newcomb's problem before but not the self-prediction stuff. Write it in American English.
