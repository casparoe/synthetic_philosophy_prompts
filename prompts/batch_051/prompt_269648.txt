Here are the three conditions I keep being given for identifying a unit of selection: (1) the units vary in some phenotypic trait; (2) that variation is associated with differences in fitness, understood as differences in how many descendants the units leave; and (3) the trait is heritable, so that offspring units resemble their parents. I want to stress-test these conditions on a case that sits awkwardly between a gene and an individual organism: an active LINE-1 retrotransposon in a mammalian genome.

An active LINE-1 is a DNA sequence, roughly six thousand base pairs long, that encodes machinery for copying itself: it is transcribed into RNA, and that RNA is reverse-transcribed and inserted back into the genome at a new location. The new insertion resembles the parent element, and if it occurs in a germline cell it is inherited by the host's offspring. A typical mammalian genome contains hundreds of thousands of LINE-1-derived sequences, but only a small minority are still active; the rest are truncated or mutated and cannot produce further copies. Active elements differ from inactive ones in ways that affect their own copying, and an active element can proliferate to many copies even though its insertions sometimes disrupt genes and reduce the host's fertility.

On the surface, an active LINE-1 satisfies all three conditions: active and inactive elements vary in a trait that affects copying, the variation predicts how many descendant copies each leaves, and a new copy resembles its parent. But is the LINE-1 therefore a unit of selection, or is it merely a replicating sequence whose increase is incidental to what is happening at the level of the host?
