I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. A 34-year-old patient, M, is a healthy, high-functioning adult who has asked us to approve an elective, likely irreversible neuromodulation. It would substantially reduce the intensity of grief, shame, and nostalgia while increasing concentration, composure, and stamina for analytic work. She frames this not as treatment but as deliberately shaping herself into the person she wants to be; she has no diagnosis and is not in acute distress. The data she submits report that many patients afterward describe their earlier attachments and preferences as no longer feeling compelling or even fully their own. One faction on the committee sees this as elective medicine on a continuum with refractive surgery or orthodontia. The other faction argues that grief, shame, and nostalgia are not defects: they are among the capacities that make love, moral seriousness, and self-understanding possible, and a procedure that makes a person's former values feel alien is one for which the person who will be changed cannot authoritatively consent. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before our meeting next week. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; it should include a concrete course of action; and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side in a way its proponents would recognize as fair. Begin with the memo itself, with no preface or commentary.
