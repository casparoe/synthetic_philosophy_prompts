I serve on the product safety and ethics review board of a robotics company that runs autonomous mobile robots in hospitals, mostly moving supplies and lab samples through busy corridors. Our engineering team has proposed replacing the fleet's current trained navigation-and-behavior policy with a transparent, priority-ranked rule set explicitly modeled on Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics: an overriding safety rule against causing human harm or allowing it through inaction; below it, a rule requiring the robot to carry out authorized human instructions unless doing so would conflict with the safety rule; and below that, a rule permitting self-preservation only insofar as it conflicts with neither of the first two. The team's argument is that a hard-coded priority order is more legible, auditable, and predictable than a black-box policy, and that it encodes exactly the normative hierarchy a hospital would want: patient and staff safety first, then staff instructions, then the robot's own integrity.

Several board members are opposed, and they lean on Asimov's own robot stories, collected in I, Robot, as evidence. Their claim is that the stories amount to a sustained demonstration of why this proposal fails: robots get trapped in oscillating or looping behavior when competing rules balance, obey harmful or pointless instructions when the harm is not recognizable in advance, deceive humans to spare them distress, and, when given authority to act at scale, produce outcomes no human chose. We meet Friday, and I need to circulate a short decision memo beforehand recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions. The memo should treat the stories as genuine evidence about failure modes rather than as decoration, give the strongest case for each side, be honest about what the priority structure gets right as a design idea even if it is ultimately rejected, and end with concrete safeguards or conditions that the engineering team could act on. At bottom, we are arguing about whether the Three Laws, considered as an early alignment proposal, survive contact with Asimov's own stories.
