I'm a student in our school's Model United Nations program, and we are working through a crisis simulation in which an established power must decide how to respond to a rising power that has just absorbed a small neighboring territory. Two historical analogies keep coming up in the committee, and each is being used to support a specific policy. One side says the situation is Munich in 1938: the Western powers believed that letting Germany take the Sudetenland would preserve peace, but the concession only encouraged further German expansion, so any negotiated concession to the rising power now would likewise reward aggression and make later demands more likely. The other side says the situation is Vietnam: an outside power that commits troops and prestige to a distant conflict with weak local understanding can easily end up in a long, costly entanglement with no clear exit, so the committee should keep its commitment limited and avoid escalation. I have been asked to write a short paper for the club evaluating how far these two historical analogies actually hold, because the two conclusions are pulling in opposite directions.

I would like you to audit both analogies. For each one, give an explicit correspondence table—which features of the current scenario are supposed to map onto which features of 1938 Czechoslovakia or of the Vietnam War: the aggressor, the local state, the outside power, the stakes, and the mechanism by which the initial step is supposed to lead to disaster. Then show where each mapping is strained or breaks: what would have to be true about the rising power's aims, its internal politics, the balance of military power, and the credibility of the established power's commitments for the analogy to be reliable, and which features of the historical case are being imported silently even though they may have no counterpart here. Finally, say which of the two policy conclusions is actually supported once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether either side is genuinely learning a lesson from history or mostly using a vivid comparison to justify a position it already held. If neither analogy survives, say whether a better comparison is available or whether the decision has to be made without one.
