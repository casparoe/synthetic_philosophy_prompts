I am preparing a short presentation for my philosophy club on how ordinary words carve up the world, and the assigned anthology has a section on the ancient Chinese School of Names. The anthology summarizes Gongsun Long's white-horse dialogue roughly as follows. Gongsun Long argues that a white horse is not a horse, because 'white' picks out a color while 'horse' picks out a shape, and a color is not a shape, so the compound term 'white horse' cannot denote the same thing as the simple term 'horse.' According to the anthology, what Gongsun Long really means to show is that words never reach concrete particulars at all: 'horse' refers to the abstract universal horse-ness, while 'white horse' refers to the compound universal white-horse-ness, and these are different entities. On this reading, ordinary speech never names the particular animal in front of you, making the dialogue a skeptical argument about reference, and the anthology calls this the standard interpretation and the reason later thinkers dismissed the School of Names as logic-chopping sophists.

I had always heard the dialogue read much more narrowly, as a point about how terms relate to the demands they can satisfy: asking for a horse and asking for a white horse are satisfied by different things, so the two terms are not interchangeable, without any heavy theory of universals. I cannot tell whether the anthology is over-reading Gongsun Long, whether I have been missing his metaphysics, or whether the truth is somewhere in between. Could you audit the anthology's summary for me? Please separate what is directly supported in the dialogue from what is interpretive extrapolation, flag any conflation between a claim about meaning and a claim about what exists, and say how fair it is to treat the School of Names as mere sophists. Please also indicate how confident you are in your assessment and single out the point you are least sure of.
