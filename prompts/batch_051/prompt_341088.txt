I am a novelist, and I have never arrived at a settled view about aesthetic testimony: how far I should let other people's judgments about books, films, and music shape, replace, or correct my own. My behavior over many years suggests a pattern, but I cannot state it. Here are my verdicts on six concrete cases; I want to know what principle, or what small set of principles, captures them, how it decides cases I have not listed, and which of my verdicts I must give up.

1. A novelist I have known for years, whose ear I trust and who has rarely steered me wrong, tells me a recent debut novel is the best thing she has read in a decade. I buy it that afternoon, begin it that night, and open the first page already expecting it to be good. I regard this as reasonable.

2. An unsigned review in a magazine I never read says the same novel is a masterpiece. I do not adjust my expectations at all, and a week later I could not repeat the reviewer's words. I regard this as reasonable too.

3. The same novelist friend tells me a new restaurant is superb. I do not change my dinner plans, and I would not describe the restaurant as good on her authority. I trust her literary judgment completely, and this seems reasonable.

4. I read a novel and find it brittle and mechanical. Then three writers I esteem, whose taste has tracked mine closely for years, independently describe it as profound. Within a month I say, and believe, that it is profound but that I have not yet managed to see how; I treat my own first reaction as a failure of attention in myself rather than evidence against the novel. I think this is reasonable, but it makes me uneasy.

5. The same three writers warn me that a different novel is dull and dishonest. I decide not to read it, and I will tell friends it is probably not worth their time, but I will not say the novel is dull; I will say only that I have been told it is dull by people I trust. I think this is reasonable.

6. A friend whose taste matches mine on nearly every book tells me that I will love a particular novel. I do not treat this as a strong reason to expect that I will love it. I trust her about the book, but not about my future response to it. I think this is reasonable.

My only tentative principle is that I treat aesthetic testimony as admissible for directing my attention and calibrating my expectations, but not as a basis for a finished judgment of a work's quality. Yet the fourth verdict lets testimony overrule my own experience of a novel, while the fifth refuses to let testimony settle whether an unread novel is dull. I appear to let testimony beat experience when I have some, while refusing to let it settle matters when I have none; I cannot see how those two verdicts are consistent, nor which of them, if either, must go.

Please answer as exactly five numbered candidate principles, with no introduction and no conclusion. In each numbered item, within two sentences, state one candidate principle, say which of my six verdicts it captures and which it overturns, and give its verdict on one further case of your own choosing, chosen to discriminate among the candidates. Make at least one candidate the simple principle that forces a revision among my verdicts, and at least one the gerrymandered principle that saves all six at the cost of complexity.
