Okay so I've ended up pretty convinced by a Humean line about beauty, the one in "Of the Standard of Taste." I basicly hold that beauty isn't a real property in objects, but there's still a standard because adequately prepared critics converge: people with a fine sense, who've practiced, compared lots of works, and freed themselves from prejudice. I read the goal of the essay when Hume says, "It is natural for us to seek a Standard of Taste; a rule, by which the various sentiments of men may be reconciled; at least, a decision, afforded, confirming one sentiment, and condemning another." That's my position: the standard is the verdict of the ideal critic, and ordinary disagreements are noise or defect.

What I want to know is what would actually count against this view, in a serious way. Not the stock objections — circularity and all that — but the cases, results, or developments that would make a committed Humean nervous. For instance, if you could run the thought experiment of many independent communities of otherwise-ideal critics starting from very different aesthetic traditions, and they did not converge on the same rankings even after full exposure to each other's works, would that break the view? What if the conditions of ideal criticism turn out to alter taste itself — practice and comparison don't just remove obstacles but create new sensibilities that wouldn't exist without them — so "convergence" is really about a training procedure, not a discovery? What would a naturalized psychology of aesthetic preference have to show for the position to collapse into either pure subjectivism or a disguised cultural authority? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to tell me honestly how exposed or insulated the Humean position is. Don't just lay out options: take a definite stand on which of these you think is the real threat, and defend it.
