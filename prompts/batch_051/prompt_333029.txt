Here is an argument from a formal-epistemology reading group that troubles me, and I would like a diagnosis of it.

Take an agent who now assigns credence 0.5 to a proposition A (for example, that it will rain on a specified date). She knows that overnight a truth-irrelevant drug will be administered to her, that the drug will make her morning credence in A exactly 0.9, and that she will receive no information relevant to A in the meantime. She also knows that the drug's effect is independent of whether A is true. She is therefore certain that her morning credence in A will be 0.9.

The argument then runs:

(1) van Fraassen's Reflection principle states that, for any proposition X and any value r for which the relevant conditional credence is defined, one's present credence in X, conditional on one's future credence in X being r, should equal r.
(2) The agent is certain that her future credence in A is 0.9.
(3) From (1), her present credence in A, conditional on her future credence in A being 0.9, should be 0.9.
(4) When one is certain of a conditioning proposition, one's conditional credence equals one's unconditional credence. So, from (2) and (3), her present credence in A should be 0.9.
(5) By stipulation, her present credence in A is 0.5. Reflection therefore appears to require her to adopt 0.9 now, despite her knowing that the future credence is caused entirely by a truth-irrelevant drug.

The colleague concludes that Reflection cannot be right as stated. I cannot accept that verdict without seeing exactly where the reasoning fails and what the alternatives would cost. Which step or premise should be rejected, why did that step or premise look acceptable, and what does rejecting or qualifying it commit one to? Answer in British English.
