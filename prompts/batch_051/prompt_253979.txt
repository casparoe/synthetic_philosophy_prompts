I've gone down a rabbit hole lately about pain asymbolia—the condition where someone can point to a spot, say it hurts, describe it as sharp or burning, but insist they don't mind it at all, and don't pull away or want painkillers—and its been making me think that most of us have no clean account of what exactly the badness of pain is. I'd like you to write me a dialogue set in a pain clinic's case conference, between two people who disagree about the lesson of these patients. One should defend the view that pain's badness is intrinsic to the sensation itself: the burning/sharp quality is itself an experiential bad, and an asymbolia patient either isn't having the full-blown experience or is reporting it oddly. The other should defend the view that the badness is a separable layer—an aversive grip, a "stop this" kind of caring—that usually rides on the sensory core but can come apart from it, so the same sensation could in principle be neutral or even welcomed.

Make it a real back-and-forth rather than two monologues. Have them press each other on hard cases: the asymbolia patient who flinches to a pinprick but says it doesn't bother them, the person on morphine who says the pain is just as intense but no longer awful, someone who welcomes a particular pain in a chosen context, and (if it helps) a hypothetical case of all suffering and no localized sensation to test the other direction. Each should try to explain the other's favorite cases away in their own terms, and at least one should end up revising something. I'm not looking for one side to win; I want both views sharpened as much as possible.
