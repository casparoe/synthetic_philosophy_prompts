I have been working through John Stuart Mill’s *A System of Logic, Ratiocinative and Inductive*, in particular the section on classification where he contrasts two kinds of classes. One sort is just a group defined by a few shared marks: the class of all white things, for example, gives you no new information about its members beyond what went into the definition. The other sort he calls real Kinds: when you group gold, or a species, or a chemical element, the members turn out to share an indefinite number of further properties not included in the classification, and that is why the group seems to mark a boundary in nature rather than a boundary in our convenience.

What puzzles me is that this criterion seems to be doing different work in different parts of the contemporary natural kinds debate. Essentialists treat the indefiniteness as evidence that there is a hidden common nature or structure underlying the observed similarities. Pluralists treat it as evidence that there are many cross-cutting natural groupings, none of which has exclusive claim to be the joint. Conventionalists and pragmatists treat it as just a fact about how successful a classification is for our inferences, with no metaphysical weight. The debate has not moved much, and I suspect the same passage from Mill is being read three different ways without anyone saying so.

So my question is diagnostic: why has the argument over whether scientific categories carve nature at its joints stayed stuck, and what kind of disagreement is it really? Is it a verbal dispute about the word *kind*, a disagreement about what would make a classification natural rather than merely useful, a hidden disagreement about induction versus explanation, or a genuine metaphysical dispute with no shared criterion for what would settle it? My working hypothesis is that the impasse comes from an ambiguity in the inexhaustibility test itself, between an epistemic reading—we keep finding new properties—and a metaphysical reading—there is something in the world that guarantees those properties. Is that right, or is the disagreement better located elsewhere? I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and an account of what would count as progress given the diagnosis.
