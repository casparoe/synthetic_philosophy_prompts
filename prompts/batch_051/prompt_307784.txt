At a recent workshop on collective responsibility, two participants spent several hours in adversarial collaboration over whether a company can be blamed as a company. They do not dispute that companies can be fined, regulated, condemned in public reports, or dissolved; they dispute whether the company itself is a further subject of moral blame beyond the people who constitute it. One holds the corporate-differentiation view: when a firm's internal procedures aggregate and filter individual inputs into a decision that no single person fully intended or understood, the firm can bear blame for that decision even if no individual has the same blameworthy relation to the outcome. The other holds the individualist-translation view: corporate blame talk is always loose shorthand for blaming some combination of executives, board members, managers, employees, and shareholders, and positing a corporate subject adds nothing except rhetorical cover. They have agreed to produce a joint statement. Draft it for them.

Write the document they should have produced, keeping the two positions symmetrically stated and phrased so each side would sign the wording of its own view. The statement should have four sections in this order:

1. Agreed Claims (roughly 250 words): A numbered list of claims both can accept about how corporate decisions differ from individual decisions, what sanctions can attach to organizations, and what limits of information and control exist inside large firms.

2. Precise Disagreements (roughly 200 words): A table with columns for the issue, the corporate-differentiation formulation, and the individualist-translation formulation. Do not strawman either side.

3. Cruxes and Tests (roughly 350 words): Identify three or four sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side. For each crux, state a concrete case, policy intervention, or organizational design both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts. Cases may include: a senior management team that suppresses dissent before a harmful decision; a company whose offending division is divested before blame is assessed; a company whose harmful culture outlasts all its original members; or others you think are sharper.

4. Verbal or Near-Verbal Disputes (roughly 150 words): State any part of their disagreement you think is partly about the words 'blame' or 'intention,' and identify what would remain after those terms are clarified.

Do not conclude with a verdict; this is a joint statement, not an adjudication.
