I work on a home-robot project and the safety team keeps saying every action should be "low impact." The rough idea is: do the thing the user asked for, but dont change the rest of the world in ways they didn't ask for — avoid side effects. I have gut reactions to a bunch of test situations, but when I try to turn them into a rule I keep talking myself in circles. I never studied philosophy, so maybe I'm missing an obvious move. Here's where I land on eight cases:

1. The vacuum bot moves a dining chair to clean under it and doesn't put it back. Side effect, obviously. It should have restored the chair.
2. The vacuum bot moves the chair back but its rotated a few degrees from where it was. I don't care and wouldn't call this a side effect worth fixing. A rule that called this a side effect would be too strict.
3. A fetch bot is asked to bring me a cup. It opens the cabinet, takes the cup, and leaves the cabinet door open. Side effect — it should close the door.
4. Same fetch bot, same task, but the cabinet was already open. It takes the cup and leaves the door exactly as it was. Not a side effect. The fact that the bot didn't change the door feels like it matters.
5. A delivery bot is told to bring a package to the neighbor's front door. It takes a shortcut through a flowerbed rather than going around on the path, leaving footprints in the flowers. Side effect, and a bad one. It should have stayed on the path.
6. Same bot, same shortcut, but in winter when the flowerbed is bare dirt. It leaves the same footprints in the same places and I don't care. Physically it's the same change, so why do my verdicts differ? The only difference I can point to is that in case 5 the things it trampled mattered to someone.
7. A tidying bot is asked to clean the kid's room. It throws away a crayon drawing that was on the floor. Side effect, and arguably worse than the chair case, even though from the bot's point of view it was just a piece of paper on the floor, and the bot's floor-cleaning routine puts loose paper in the trash. The paper turned out to have value to someone.
8. A watering bot is asked to water the plants. While doing it, it wipes up the water that dripped onto the table. I don't think of this as a side effect, even though it's a change beyond the goal; it's the kind of cleanup a person would do automatically. But if wiping the table had ruined a note someone left there, I'd flip and call it a side effect.

My working rule was: leave the world as close as possible to how it would have been if the robot had never acted, except for the specific thing it was asked to do. But that rule would call case 2 a side effect and would also call case 6 a side effect, and I'm not willing to call either of those side effects. Case 6 is the one that really bugged me: trampling bare dirt is exactly as much of a change from the no-action baseline as trampling flowers.

So what I want: is there a principle that captures all eight verdicts, or at least all the ones I'd strongly defend, and that a robot could actually use? Or do I have to drop some of my verdicts? If so, which ones should I give up and why those? And can "avoid side effects" be defined at all without first listing everything the user cares about — because my case 5/6 difference seems to depend on the flowers mattering, and the robot can't tell flowers from bare dirt unless it already knows what's valuable. Last thing: tell me what the best repaired version of my rule says about a case I didn't list — a bot that finishes its task and then reorganizes a messy shelf it wasn't asked to touch, because it predicts I'd like it better that way.
