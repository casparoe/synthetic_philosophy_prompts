You are a moral philosopher working in the contractualist tradition associated with T. M. Scanlon. Begin by brainstorming the possible distinctions between the two cases below; do not jump to a conclusion. Then formulate the strongest candidate principle you can, test it against the variants, and finish with an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry is defensible or should be revised or abandoned.

Here is the asymmetry.

A public health authority faces two decisions. In each, acting saves five lives at the cost of one, and refraining saves the one at the cost of five.

Decision 1. Five patients will die unless each receives a compatible organ. A healthy stranger, admitted for an unrelated check-up, is a match for all five. The authority could quietly kill the stranger and harvest the organs. It refuses. Nearly everyone outside an austere utilitarian camp regards this refusal as required, not merely permitted.

Decision 2. Six miners are trapped after a collapse, one in shaft A and five in shaft B. The authority's rescue team can reach only one shaft before rising water makes the other impossible. It goes to shaft B. Five live, one dies. Nearly everyone regards this as right, or at least clearly permissible, and almost no one thinks it morally equivalent to killing the sixth.

So the asymmetry is this: in Decision 1, the fact that five exceeds one is not allowed to settle the matter; in Decision 2, it is allowed to settle the matter. Yet in both cases the decision determines who dies: the stranger is killed on the authority's orders, and the sixth miner is left to die when the team could have gone toward his shaft instead.

A Scanlonian contractualist will want to explain Decision 1 by saying that a principle permitting the organ harvest could be reasonably rejected by the stranger: he can object that he is being used, and on this view his complaint is assessed on its own rather than outweighed by summing the five patients' claims. But that same structure creates a squeeze. If complaints are never summed, what reason could the authority have to prefer the five miners over the one? The sixth miner's complaint against the principle save the five is that he is left to die; each of the five could lodge the same complaint against the principle save the one. If no one may add those complaints together, the comparison looks like a tie, and the contractualist would seem driven to an answer nearly everyone rejects, such as rescuing by lottery. If, on the other hand, the contractualist does sum complaints in Decision 2 so that the five win, why does the same summing not license harvesting the stranger's organs in Decision 1?

Your task is to resolve this squeeze. Do not settle for saying the stranger is used as a mere means unless you can explain, without metaphor, why a principle that leaves the sixth miner to drown does not also use him, and why that difference is the kind of difference that should carry this much moral weight. Before you commit, test your principle against the numerical variants: one stranger whose organs could save ten thousand, and one miner who would have to be abandoned so that ten thousand others could be saved. If your principle allows the number to matter in one variant but not the other, say so and explain why; if it treats the change of scale as morally idle, defend that as well. Finish by stating which of the two initial judgments, if either, you would be most willing to give up, and whether what remains is a genuine principle or merely a way of re-describing a firm intuition.
