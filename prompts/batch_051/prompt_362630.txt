I am a novelist, and for a book I am writing I need to work through a philosophical issue that keeps resisting the neat shape of a plot. In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book II, Chapter 2, Aristotle argues, as I understand the Greek in standard translation, that the subject matter of ethics admits of no exactness or fixity, and that the agents themselves must consider what is appropriate to the particular occasion, in the way that doctors and navigators do. This has always struck me as an unsettling, almost writerly thought. I would like an essay that takes that passage as its focal point. The essay should weigh whether this passage commits Aristotle to moral particularism—the view that moral judgment is not, and cannot be, codified into a set of exceptionless principles that determine the right action before the situation is encountered—or whether a generalist can plausibly read it as a claim only about the limits of ethical instruction and exposition, not about the underlying structure of moral judgment itself. As a novelist, I am especially interested in whether a person can be said to hold a genuine moral principle while still having to work out, scene by scene, what that principle amounts to here and now, and whether Aristotle’s comparison of moral judgment to craft settles the dispute or merely restates it. Reach a determinate verdict and defend it; do not end by saying the two readings are equally good. Write the essay in a formal register, around 1,200 words.
