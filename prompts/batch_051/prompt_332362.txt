I am giving a job talk this term at a department whose metaphysics leans away from Lewis: the senior metaphysicians work in a broadly Plantingan idiom of essences and haecceities, the philosophers of language are largely Kripkean about names and rigid designation, and several of the junior faculty are methodologically committed to respecting ordinary modal judgement and letting it constrain theory. My talk defends counterpart theory against transworld identity, but with a specific burden of proof. The claim I argue for is that a transworld identity semantics is only as explanatory as it is by quietly trading on counterpart-theoretic machinery, and that once this is made explicit the only live dispute is over which counterpart relation to use, not whether there is one. I press a dilemma: either the very same individual literally exists in other possible worlds, in which case the problem of accidental intrinsics forces the account toward world-indexed properties and the identity does no semantic work; or the account quantifies over ways-for-that-individual-to-be, in which case it has given up transworld identity in all but name. In the final third of the talk I defend an interest-relative counterpart relation, meaning that which similarity weights fix the counterpart relation for a given de re modal locution depends on the purposes of the conversation at hand. I claim this dissolves the Humphrey objection in its strongest form, but I suspect this audience will find it the most revisionary move in the talk.
