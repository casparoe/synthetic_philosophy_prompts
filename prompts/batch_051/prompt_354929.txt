Since returning to practice at a Buddhist community after years away, I have been struck by how much the moral conversation there turns on the quality of the volition (cetanā) behind an act, while the academic treatments I remember tended to read Buddhist ethics as broadly consequentialist. I would like a worked analysis of a case built from the standard lever trolley. Fix the outward act and its outcome across all variants: five strangers on the main track, one stranger on the side track, the agent pulls the lever, one person dies, five live. What varies is the agent's volition alone:

(a) the agent pulls from impartial compassion for all six, with no ill will toward the one and clear sorrow that a death is unavoidable;
(b) the agent pulls from a detached head-count rule, with no felt concern for the one;
(c) the agent pulls while half-concealing from himself a wish that the one person die, and consciously rehearses a justification about saving five.

For each variant, lay out how a flat outcome-focused consequentialist reading of Buddhist ethics would analyze it, and how a non-consequentialist reading that treats karmic gravity as primarily a function of cetanā would analyze it. I want to know whether this one parameter — holding the bodily movement and the consequences fixed while varying the volition — actually separates the two readings. Do they deliver different verdicts in some variants and not others? Does the consequentialist reading have to import character or intention to distinguish (a) from (c), and does the intention-based reading have to fall back on outcome to explain why a compassionate act that knowingly kills is not simply murder? End by saying what the case shows about whether these readings are rivals or complementary descriptions of the same act. Please mark which claims are settled and which are genuinely contested among specialists in Buddhist ethics, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
