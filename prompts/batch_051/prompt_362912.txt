I have been trying to decide whether a structural parallel I noticed is genuinely deep or merely an attractive analogy, and I would value a careful verdict. Dennett distinguishes a hierarchy of minds by where trial and error gets to happen. A Darwinian creature's only way of eliminating error is natural selection at the population level; a Skinnerian creature generates candidate behaviors and tests them against the actual world, keeping what is reinforced and discarding what is punished; a Popperian creature does the testing one step removed, inside an inner environment that represents features of the world, so that a bad hypothesis can die without the creature dying with it; and a Gregorian creature does the testing one further step removed, borrowing words, tools, and methods from its culture so that the accumulated trial and error of other minds does much of the filtering. When I think about how machine learning systems improve, the same ladder of removal appears, only located in where the selection happens. Evolutionary search is Darwinian; model-free reinforcement learning, which adjusts a policy through reward and punishment in the real environment, is Skinnerian; model-based reinforcement learning, which learns a world model and plans or searches inside it so that poor trajectories are never executed, is Popperian; and learning from demonstrations or from a corpus of other agents' experience, where much of the dangerous sampling has already been done elsewhere, is Gregorian.

The parallel seems precise enough to state as a single principle: the higher rungs internalize, or socialize, the selection process, so that error becomes cheaper because it occurs inside a model, or inside someone else's history, rather than in the agent's own interaction with the world. But I suspect this is also where the analogy weakens. Dennett's ladder is about what a creature must be able to represent and foresee if it is to be credited with a certain kind of mind, whereas the machine learning ladder is about optimizing an objective under computational constraints; a planning agent can be Popperian in my sense while being entirely mindless. I would like three things from you. First, state the mapping as carefully as you can, marking where it is exact and where it is loose. Second, say what would have to be true on both sides for the analogy to be more than a metaphor, and in particular whether the claim that the difference between model-free and model-based reinforcement learning is the difference between Skinnerian and Popperian minds carries real content. Third, identify at least one prediction the mapping makes about either side that turns out false, and explain whether that failure is a local blemish or a sign that the whole parallel is superficial.
