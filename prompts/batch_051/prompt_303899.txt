At a party last weekend, Dan and Priya got into a long argument about whether the US is near the end of its run as a major power. I stayed out of it, and I've no idea whether Dan's numbers were right, because the argument never went there. Dan had been reading about the outside view and base rates. He said: look at past regional hegemons, such as Rome, the Habsburgs, the Ottomans, the British Empire or the USSR. Take the rise-to-fall lifespans of three or four dozen of them and the average comes out around 250 years, give or take a century. The US has been an independent state since 1776 and the dominant global power since about 1945. So the outside view puts it deep into the historical range, and an American who says 'we are different' is doing exactly the inside-view thing the method is meant to correct.

Priya said the whole number was an artefact of the list. Why regional hegemons rather than constitutional democracies, nuclear-armed states, or states that survived a civil war and then industrialised? Count from 1945 and the US is only eighty; count from the Civil War and you get a different age again. Use all sovereign states and the median lifetime is tiny, which makes the US look ancient; use global superpowers and you have three or four cases, none much of an analogy. She said he had not found a base rate, he had built a list to produce a reassuringly round number. Dan replied that she was demanding perfect analogues, which would make forecasting impossible. The outside view, he said, does not need an exact reference class; it needs a broad one, to break the illusion that your own case is special. Take the rough prior and then update with case-specific evidence. Priya answered that he was skipping the two places the method actually bites: the class has to be specified before you look at the outcome and independent of what you are trying to predict, and his class looked selected from cases that did collapse, which makes the conclusion nearly tautological; and an average over completed lifespans says little about the remaining lifespan of a case that has already survived 250 years, since the early deaths are behind it. Dan said that cuts both ways: survivors are a selected group, but they are also old, and in his list nothing reached much past 400, so there is an upper bound somewhere and the US is closer to it than to year one. Priya said a missing case in the record is not proof of an upper bound; it may just mean the reference class is too small, and he cannot at once treat the class as broad enough to support a prior and too narrow to include novel risks like nuclear or enviromental collapse. It was past midnight and neither would give in.

Could you referee the whole exchange? Go through the moves in order and say who is right about what, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right; then say what both were missing about the difference between an unconditional base rate, a conditional one, and a reference class, and who is more right overall. Please answer in British English.
