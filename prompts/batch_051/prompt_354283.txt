I cover philosophy and the social sciences for a general-audience magazine, and I have been working on a feature about how people come to act on reasons they do not already feel. In reporting it I started collecting my own reactions to cases instead of relying on the theorists I quote, and I now have a set of verdicts that I cannot fit under a single rule. The running distinction is roughly this: some reasons seem to answer to what a person already cares about, once factual mistakes are corrected and imagination is given its due, while other reasons are supposed to bind regardless of what the person wants. I can gesture at the distinction; I cannot state it in a way that survives my cases.

Here are the cases as they struck me. Marta's doctor tells her that a daily walk would meaningfully slow the bone loss that threatens to keep her from visiting her grandchildren; she dislikes exercise but cares intensely about those visits. She clearly has a reason to walk. Leo is a fourteen-year-old with no curiosity about mathematics, no ambition that requires it, and no interest in seeming numerate. When his teacher says he has a reason to study it because the curriculum requires it and it trains the mind, I want to say the school has reasons to make him study it but Leo himself does not have a reason in the same sense. Cole is callously cruel to his partner, lacks any desire to be decent, and is indifferent to being found out; I nevertheless believe he has a decisive reason not to treat her that way, and I would say so without hedging. Rana is in a severe depression and wants only to stop her treatment, but her doctors and family are confident that if her illness lifted she would want the treatment to have continued and would want the future it would restore. I think she has a reason to continue even now, because the motivation is there in her once the depression is filtered out. Dev makes a considered promise to a stranger he will never see again, with no reputational risk and no anticipated guilt. I find I do think he has a reason to keep it, though I cannot locate that reason in anything he currently wants.

My working rule is something like: a person has a reason to act just in case the action serves or protects something they would want if they knew all the facts and deliberated clearly. That handles Marta and Rana and explains Leo, but it seems to fail Cole and Dev unless I quietly build the missing concern into the antecedent. I would like a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to test: which clauses do the work, which of my verdicts it must overturn or redescribe, what it predicts about cases I have not listed (for instance, someone with no present concern for their later self who would likely develop that concern after a significant life change), and which verdict I should surrender if I want the simpler principle. If the contrast I have drawn is confused, or if no single notion of having a reason can hold these cases together, please tell me that plainly rather than offering me a tidy principle that changes the subject.
