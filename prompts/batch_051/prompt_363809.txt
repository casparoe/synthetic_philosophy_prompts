Something I keep noticing with my otherwise-quantitative friends: nearly all of them carry term life insurance and treat it as obvious prudence -- you pay a modest premium and the policy only pays out in an unlikely state (you die during the term), and even then it's a capped amount for your family. Yet most of the same people think cryonics is obviously a silly lottery ticket, even though it looks structurally similar: you pay a modest ongoing premium, it 'pays out' only in an unlikely state (you get revived and restored to a decent life), and the upside there is a huge amount of extra life rather than a fixed sum. So we have two long-shot bets on unlikely states concerning your death, and ordinary judgment calls one responsible and the other a waste. What could justify that asymmetry? I want the strongest principled distinction -- hedging a bad state vs. speculating on a good one, a payoff to your dependents vs. a payoff to your future self, the shape of the probabilities, risk aversion over money but not over life -- tested against a few nearby cases (disability insurance, a 1% experimental cancer treatment, buying a cryonics policy for your child). Then give me an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry survives, or whether its mostly status-quo bias.
