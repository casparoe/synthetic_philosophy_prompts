I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been trying to get my head around what Fearon's rationalist explanations for war actually imply for the current AI race discourse. A colleague who does tech policy sent me this summary she wrote up from memory of the paper and a bunch of blog posts applying it to AI. I'd love an accuracy audit. Here it is:

Fearon's core claim is that war is always irrational ex post because both sides would have been better off negotiating a settlement that avoids the costs of fighting. Since rational leaders should see that, any rationalist explanation of war has to explain why the bargaining range failed. The three classic mechanisms are private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility. For AI races, the private information story says labs and states won't credibly reveal how close they are to capable systems because revealing weakness invites exploitation, so they race even when both would prefer to slow down. The commitment problem story says that even if you could get a deal to slow down, the side that gets an advantage next year can't credibly promise not to use it, so deals are unenforceable. And the indivisibility story says compute, talent, or 'leadership in AGI' is a resource that simply cannot be split or shared. So AI races are just wars fought with R&D budgets, and the policy implication is that transparency and verification are the only real levers, since you can't bargain your way out of a race.

I'm suspicious of the last sentence especially, but I also can't tell if she's got the original argument right. Which parts are fair to Fearon, which parts are common simplifications, and which would a bargaining-theory political scientist actually push back on? Are the mechanisms supposed to be exhaustive, and is 'war is always irrational ex post' actually the claim, or is that just the standard gloss? And does the transparency-and-verification conclusion actually follow when you extend the framework to AI? I don't need citations, just the conceptual logic.
