You are a professional philosopher specialising in moral psychology and ancient ethics, with a close interest in how Stoic ideas circulate in contemporary self-help culture. Please work through the following question slowly and explicitly: first canvass the nearby concepts, assess whether each captures the phenomenon precisely or only approximately, and only then either identify the established concept that fits best or propose and defend a new term with a careful definition and boundary cases. Write your final answer in British English.

A reader writes:

I keep noticing a pattern among people who have absorbed a particular strain of popular Stoicism, and I wonder whether it has an established name. The strain teaches a distinction between what is up to us and what is not, and it urges people to care only about the first. In practice, though, the line between the two seems to move. A friend stakes his savings on a speculative business. When the bet fails, he says the failure was never up to him: markets are outside his control, and he did everything a reasonable person could. When an identical bet pays off, he says the success was his judgement and discipline paying off. Same person, same type of bet, but the outcome is assigned to whichever side of the line flatters him. I have seen the same thing with health: a setback after aggressive self-treatment is a misfortune outside anyone's control, but an eventual recovery proves that the regimen was within the patient's power all along.

I do not think these people are lying, and I do not mean ordinary bragging or a brute tendency to claim credit and dodge blame. The distinction itself seems to be doing the work: a principle that was supposed to keep the self in check gets used to keep it comfortable while preserving a sense of agency and credit. Does this pattern have an established name, something like the self-serving redrawing of a control/indifference distinction under cover of a principled teaching? I know a few neighbouring ideas: self-serving bias, sour grapes, learned helplessness, plain hypocrisy. None seems to capture it exactly, but I cannot articulate why. If an established concept fits, I would like its definition and an account of how it differs from those neighbours; if none fits, I would like a proposed name, its definition, and some boundary cases that separate it from them. The label matters less to me than the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, and where its edges are.
