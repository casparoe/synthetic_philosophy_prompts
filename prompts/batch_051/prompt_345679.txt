You're a professional philosopher of science who has thought hard about why societies fund knowledge that has no short-term payoff—not the pat "technology came from physics" story, but the harder choices about option value, serendipity, negative results, and knowledge that is worth having for its own sake. Think carefully and brainstorm before committing to a procedure, including the places where the honest answer is "a human has to decide."

I run the small-grants program at a family foundation that gives away a few million dollars a year to scholarly projects, mostly two-year grants in the humanities and sciences. Every cycle we get a dozen or so proposals that look useless to our board, and one trustee in particular wants every applicant to answer "what is this useful for?" I'm convinced that's the wrong screen, but I don't have a reusable procedure—I just have a feeling and a few anecdotes. I need a decision procedure we can actually run on proposals that promise no near-term application.

The procedure will be used by me and one junior program officer. It has to be run in about ninty minutes per proposal, three times a year, and then be defended in a two-page memo to a skeptical board of businesspeople and lawyers who's instinct is to kill anything without a market. So it can't lean on phrases like "intrinsic value" or "knowledge for its own sake" unless you unpack them into questions someone can answer while reading the proposal and talking to the applicant.

Please give me the procedure itself, not an essay about why utility is hard to predict. It should reliably separate at least three things: (a) work that looks useless but is likely to pay off in ways no one can predict, (b) work that is genuinely useless by any defensible standard but still worth funding on principle, and (c) work that is just obscure in a fashionable way. Include the negative case: the board worries that "just knowing it" lets in crackpots and hobbyists, so the procedure should say when "useless but worth knowing" fails. Also be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, where the procedure stops and a person has to decide, and where two reasonable people could run it and still disagree.

Then run the procedure on three actual-looking proposals, and show every step: (1) an ethnographer who wants two years to write a grammar of a language with eleven remaining speakers and no writing system; (2) a pure mathematician who wants to classify a family of low-dimensional geometric objects that nobody outside a subfield has heard of; (3) a historian who wants to reconstruct how nineteenth-century actuaries printed and corrected life tables—the trustee calls this ancestor-worship, and I happen to love it. Make sure at least one of these comes out "don't fund" in a way I won't like, and explain what the procedure is doing there. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and what the board would have to accept on non-procedural grounds for the procedure to make sense.
