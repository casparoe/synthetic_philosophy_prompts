I'm in a metaethics reading group that has been chewing on the internal versus external reasons debate for close to a year now, and it's began to feel like we're not so much arguing as repeatedly re-convincing ourselves of the same two positions. The internalist in the group keeps saying that for anything to count as a reason for an agent, there has to be a sound deliberative route from where the agent actually is—the desires, projects, and commitments she already has—to the thing's counting in favor of acting. If a person has no motivation that would survive informed, error-free deliberation pointing toward giving to charity, then on this view there just is no reason for that person to give to charity. The externalist keeps saying that's a conflation of what rationally requires with what an agent happens to be able to get herself to do; there can be perfectly real reasons—say, that an act is what justice requires—that apply to someone regardless of whether any amount of sound deliberation from her current motivational set would get her to care. And then they go around again. What's odd is that they agree on nearly all the lower-level facts: they agree that some agents won't be moved by the relevant considerations, agree that deliberation has to correct for false beliefs and bad inference, agree about all the standard cases except the ones at the boundary.

I'm starting to think the debate has stalled because the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about the same question—or maybe they are, and it's a bedrock clash about whether normative authority has to be answerable to the agent's own motivational standpoint. I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a verdict about who's right. Is it verbal, with two notions of a reason—one for guiding deliberation from the inside, one for evaluating agents from the outside? Is it a pragmatic disagreement about which concept earns the name for which theoretical job? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question about what sound deliberation actually converges on? Or is it a genuine first-order normative disagreement that no amount of conceptual hygiene will dissolve? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a distinction drawn, an empirical finding, a formal model of the deliberative route, a change of question—or is this the sort of dispute where we should expect no movement, and why?
