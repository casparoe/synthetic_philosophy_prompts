When the topic of grit and resilience comes up, I find myself endorsing all five of the following claims in different conversations, and I cannot tell whether they form a defensible package or whether I am glossing over a conflict. Before I try to write them up as a single position, I want an audit.

(a) Perseverance and self-control, measured as traits, are among the best purely psychological predictors of long-run outcomes such as educational completion, employment stability, and health, even though the correlations are modest in absolute terms.

(b) The person–situation critique is substantially correct: a global trait is a weak predictor of what someone will do on any single occasion, and once the immediate situation is specified, knowing a person's trait score adds surprisingly little.

(c) The public discourse of resilience and grit, especially in schools and workplace wellness programs, largely functions as ideology: it re-describes structural failures as individual deficits and treats early quitting as a character flaw rather than as a reasonable adaptation to a discouraging environment.

(d) Schools should nonetheless deliberately teach perseverance, self-regulation, and adaptive quitting—the ability to recognize when effort should be redirected—because these capacities are learnable and causally improve later outcomes.

(e) As a matter of virtue theory, fortitude is a virtue only when it is aimed at a worthwhile end and calibrated by practical wisdom; uncalibrated persistence is mere stubbornness, not a virtue.

Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating all of them as equally serious. Separate the pairs that only appear inconsistent from those that genuinely conflict, and for each genuine tension say what distinction or revision would be the least costly way to restore consistency.
