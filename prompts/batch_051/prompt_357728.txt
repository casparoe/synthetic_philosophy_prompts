Ive been going back and forth with someone on a forum about whether a small online game economy that rebases its currency by a factor of ten is actually richer, and I keep coming back to this passage from David Hume’s essay “Of Interest”:

Were all the gold in England annihilated at once, and one and twenty shillings substituted in the place of every guinea, would money be more plentiful or interest lower? No surely: We should only use silver instead of gold. Were gold rendered as common as silver, and silver as common as copper; would money be more plentiful or interest lower? We may assuredly give the same answer. Our shillings would then be yellow, and our halfpence white; and we should have no guineas. No other difference would ever be observed; no alteration on commerce, manufactures, navigation, or interest; unless we imagine, that the colour of the metal is of any consequence.

I get the argument in the abstract, but I need a thought experiment that makes the point land for someone who treats nominal balances as real wealth. So here’s the spec. Build a case set in an ordinary online community with its own local credit system, no central bank and no annihilating all cash: increase the total credit supply by 10x in a way that leaves every real quantity—goods, labor, relative prices, interest—unchanged, while a naive observer looking only at balances would say the community got richer. Then show the reasoning: what the naive quantity-of-money view says, what Hume’s argument says, and which structural feature of the case makes them come apart. Finally, say what happens if you remove that feature and how a defender of the naive view would attack the case. This is for a blog post, so the scenario should be ordinary enough not to feel like a trick.
