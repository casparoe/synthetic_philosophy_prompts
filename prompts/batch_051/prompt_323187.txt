Okay, here's the situation: my seminar keeps splitting into Benthamites and natural-rights people over the French Declaration business — the bit where Bentham says talk of natural rights is nonsense on stilts. Every year the argument loops around the same few moves, so I want to hand them a document that a smart Benthamite and a smart defender of natural rights could both sign after an honest attempt to pin down the real disagreement rather than perform it. Draft that document for me.

Use this format. First, a numbered list of substantive claims both parties sign — not truisms like "rights-talk exists" but actual commitments about how legal rights differ from moral rights, about what Bentham was and wasn't objecting to, and about what rights-language does in normal political argument. These have to be strong enough that the document has content, but phrased so neither side would cross one out. Second, the residual disagreement stated twice: once in wording the Benthamite would own (something in the neighborhood of "a sentence of the form 'there's a natural right to X' is either a disguised proposal about what the law ought to be, or it's empty") and once in wording the natural-rights person would own. Don't make the two statements mirror images just for symmetry; if one side insists the other can't even state its view coherently, mark that as a place where the collaboration stalled. Third, a table of cruxes, one per row. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about that case in advance. Make sure the cruxes include: (a) whether "it's their right" does any justificatory work beyond a welfare calculation, tested on a hard conflict case (scapegoat-to-prevent-a-riot is fine, or find a better one); (b) whether the debate is partly just verbal, given how often the two sides agree on the all-things-considered judgment and only split on what the word "right" is adding; (c) a scope stress-test — claims made on behalf of future people or nonhuman animals, with the Benthamite's prediction about what is really doing the work in such claims and the natural-rights person's prediction about whether those claims come out true. Fourth, in a seperate short section, tell me which two cruxes would move the most weight if settled, and which part of the whole fight you'd mark as genuine rather than verbal.

No reading list and no citation-chasing, please — I want the document a good arbitrator would push the two sides to co-sign, not a survey of the literature. Keep it readable enough that my students can see the architecture without wading through jargon.
