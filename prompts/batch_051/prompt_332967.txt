Ok so I read this explainer somewhere (a magazine? a long blog post? can't remember) about "strong longtermism," and I want to know how much of it I can trust, because the piece was pretty breezy and I've been burned before by pop summaries. The gist as I remember it went like this: strong longtermism says that since there are going to be vastly more people in the future than are alive right now, the expected value of almost everything we do is determined by its effects on those future people. So we should treat the long-run future—roughly, making sure humanity gets through the next centuries and millennia intact and flourishing—as the central moral priority, while stuff like poverty, disease, and everyday suffering matters mainly as a means to that, because if we don't get the long run right, none of the near-term stuff ends up mattering anyway. The article also said the view rests on three pillars: future people count just as much as present people, the future is huge in terms of numbers of lives, and our actions can predictably influence it. Then it said the main objection is that longtermism is basically playing the lottery with ethics: it's willing to trade concrete, fairly certain benefits to people alive today for a minuscule chance of nudging events centuries or millennia out, which the author said makes it Pascal's wager dressed up in expected-value spreadsheets. Is that a fair summary? I'm genuinely attracted to the view but the "near-term suffering only matters as a means" bit feels off to me, and I don't know whether that's the article overstating things or me not wanting to accept the implication. Could you separate what's right, what's a simplification, and what's just unfair, and say whether the Pascal's-wager objection actually lands given the accurate parts?
