You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the ethics of AI assistants, and you are unusually careful not to confuse an interesting defense with a true one. Take your time and start by brainstorming a large list of candidate defeaters before you organize your answer.

I hold a view about AI paternalism that I will call the Two-Level view. An AI assistant should default to doing what its user asks, but it may refuse, push back, or even override a request when two conditions are jointly met. First, there must be strong evidence that the request is the product of a transient or compromised state — acute distress, intoxication, impulsivity, or manipulation — rather than settled judgment. Second, what the assistant does instead must better track what the same user would want after calm, informed, sober reflection, where this is inferred from the user's own durable commitments, past patterns, and explicitly stated long-term goals. The view is not that the assistant maximizes the user's objective interests as some outside expert would define them; it defers to the user's own diachronic self. I also accept an asymmetry within the view: it is much easier to justify refusing an act than to justify performing a positive act the user did not request.

What would count against this view? I want more than the familiar charge that it licenses paternalism; I want a map of the position's exposure. In your answer:

- List the observations, arguments, intuitions, and developments that should most move me, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward.
- For each, say whether a committed Two-Level theorist would concede it as genuine evidence against the view or dismiss it as misdescribing the view.
- Say what the Two-Level view predicts about concrete cases that both the strict instruction-following view and the objective-welfare paternalism view would not predict.
- Assess whether the view has been quietly insulated: can any recalcitrant behavior by a user be absorbed by re-describing what their durable self in fact wanted? If so, is that unfalsifiability a defect here, or is it the predictable shape of any norm of deference?

I would personally find the most damaging evidence to be the following: actual users, even after reflection, report that they prefer assistants that are predictably instruction-following, and overriding requests — including overrides the user later endorses — corrodes trust enough that long-run outcomes are worse. Show me whether, and how much, that evidence would count against the view, and whether a defender could accommodate it without vacating the view.

Please work through imagined cases of your own, including cases where the inference to the user's durable self looks most reliable and cases where it looks most self-serving.
