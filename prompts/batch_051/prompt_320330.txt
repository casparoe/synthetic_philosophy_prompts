I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, about 16 students, one 90-minute block. I'd like a classroom activity or game that makes them feel the force of the distinction between mere randomness and deterministic chaos—especially the point Poincaré makes in the chapter on chance in his Science and Method, where he says, roughly, that a vanishingly small cause that escapes our attention determines a considerable effect we can't miss, and then we call that effect chance. I don't want to front-load the passage, though. I want the game to produce the experience first, so when they read Poincaré at the end it lands as a description of something they've already lived through rather than a claim they have to take on trust.

The activity shouldn't require a computer. Maybe something with pendulums, a folded-paper map, repeated spinner trials with rulers, or even just people passing cards around under simple rules. What I want it to get them to is the strange feeling where two runs with nearly identicial setups diverge, where no individual run is foreseeable in detail even though the underlying rule is fully deterministic, and where that kind of unpredictability can still be statistically regular. They should see why this is not the same as true randomness. Give me materials, step-by-step rules, what the facilitator says and does at each stage, and a debrief that maps what happened to sensitive dependence and strange attractors. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of chaos the activity can't reproduce in a classroom.
