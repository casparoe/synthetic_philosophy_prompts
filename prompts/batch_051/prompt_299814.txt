Here's an asymmetry I keep catching myself in, and I genuinely cant tell wich way it should go. When I make a hard choice—say, between staying late to finish a lecture and coming home for family dinner—I describe myself as having a committee of values squabbling inside me. One part cares about the job, another part cares about the family, and the choice didn't reveal that I value work more than dinner; dinner mattered the whole time, it just lost that evening. But when a colleague misses his kid's recital for a faculty meeting, I immediately read him as having a ranking: meeting outranks recital, full stop. Same with students: if a student skips my class for a march, I catch myself saying her behavior shows what she really values, but if I skip a chore to help a friend, I insist my values were simply in conflict and I did the best I could. So in my own case I treat values as irreducibly many, and resist putting them on one scale; in everyone else's case I treat choices as evidence of a single stable value ranking. The obvious justification is that I have direct access to my own inner plurality but only outward behavior from others. But I don't think that defense is as good as it looks: I have years of behavioral data about myself, and I still refuse after the fact to accept the ranking my own behavior would imply—and when I describe others as plural, I often do it only as an excuse for them, not as a description I really believe. So is the asymmetry tracking something real, or is it just a way of keeping my own values flattering and complicated while reducing everyone else to one thing? I can see three moves: keep the asymmetry, flatten it by treating myself as also having a single revealed ranking, or flatten it by treating everyone else as having the same many-value mess I feel in myself. Build the strongest case for each, then tell me which of the three I should keep and what I'd be giving up by keeping it.
