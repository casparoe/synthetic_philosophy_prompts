I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been reading my way through the philosophy of science — in particular the literature on reduction, supervenience, and emergence. I now sit on the scientific advisory panel of a private foundation that funds research on complex systems, and a proposal before us has turned into one of those philosophical disputes, except that this one comes with a budget line and a five-year appointment.

The proposal is for a theory group that would work on what the applicants call causal emergence. The empirical core is defensible and, frankly, standard: they will compute critical exponents and finite-size scaling for several lattice models, develop coarse-grained descriptions of a few biological and geophysical systems, and try to say systematically when macroscopic variables — order parameters, hydrodynamic modes, coarse-grained fields — allow predictions that are infeasible from the microphysics alone. On its own, that part would sail through review.

What divides the panel is the stated interpretation. The applicants insist these macroscopic variables do real causal work that the microphysical story omits: not merely that coarse-grained descriptions are more tractable, but that they capture genuine causal powers absent from the lower-level description. They propose to formalise top-down causation and claim a purely bottom-up research programme is not just impractical but conceptually mistaken. Two panelists think this is standard condensed-matter physics dressed in philosophical clothing and want to reject the proposal as overclaiming; a third thinks rejecting it would legislate an armchair reductionism and forgo a genuinely novel research programme.

I have been asked to draft the panel's written opinion for the meeting. It should be usable by scientists who have not read this literature — every piece of jargon must pay its way — and it should be philosophically serious rather than a way of avoiding the issue. It needs an actionable bottom line and must take the strongest version of the losing position seriously.

Please draft the memo, roughly 1,000 words, with these sections in this order:

1. Background and decision requested (~120 words) — the proposal, the panel's split, and exactly what is being voted on.
2. The respectable core (~180 words) — the strongest thing the applicants could be right about even if one grants that microphysics is causally complete: predictive intractability, universality, multiple realizability, and the autonomy of coarse-grained regularities.
3. The case against the strong reading (~200 words) — what top-down causation would have to mean to be more than a manner of speaking; the conflict with causal closure and supervenience, the double-counting worry, and why a new causal power at the macro level is hard to state without contradiction or miracle.
4. The case for the strong reading (~200 words) — the best case that the modest account sells the phenomenon short: explanatory autonomy, why the renormalization-group selection of relevant variables is not a merely descriptive matter, and what would make the strong claim a scientific rather than a metaphysical one.
5. How much of the fight is real (~200 words) — separate what is verbal from what is substantive; say what each side would have to be shown before one of them should give ground; indicate the conditions under which both readings can coexist.
6. Recommendation (~150 words) — a concrete course of action with conditions, time frame, and reporting requirements.
