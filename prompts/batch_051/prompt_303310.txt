One of the standard arguments against paying kidney donors is not that payment is dangerous or that it would fail to increase the supply of organs. It is that introducing a cash price would corrupt the meaning of donation: the same act, performed for compensation, would no longer express altruism, and that loss of meaning is itself a reason to forbid the market even if it would save lives. Suppose a pilot program now offers donors not cash but a post-operative health-insurance credit, assigned only through a public lottery, capped at ten years, and explicitly framed in the consent process as society’s reciprocal obligation rather than as a price. Does the anti-commodification argument survive this development? Which premise of the argument—if any—does the change bear on, and can the objection be restated in a form that still rules out the lottery-credit system without also condemning ordinary compensated plasma donation, tax deductions for charitable tissue gifts, or paid research participation? If it cannot, say whether the remaining objection is really about commodification or about something else.
