At the company where I work, we recently adopted a mandatory style guide for incident reports and postmortems. The rules are concrete: state the cause of an outage in active voice, avoid nouns built from verbs ("a latency degradation was experienced"), and, wherever a failure is described, name the specific component and the specific action or sequence of actions that failed. The stated rationale is that vague phrasing lets responsibility evaporate and lets problems get laundered into neutral-sounding abstractions before they reach the people who could actually fix them. I have read Orwell's essay "Politics and the English Language," and the overlap is hard to miss. Orwell argues there that stale metaphors, jargon, and euphemism are not just symptoms of careless thinking but actively make further careless or dishonest thinking easier, and that political language in particular leans on abstract, passive constructions to make the indefensible sound tolerable. Our internal policy seems to be betting on a stronger version of that claim: if you force the language of an incident report to be concrete and active, the thinking behind it will become more precise and more honest as well.

Two things bother me, though. First, some of the incidents I write up have genuinely unclear causation — a checkout failure may emerge from an interaction among several services owned by different teams — and the style guide's demand for a single named actor can manufacture a clarity the facts do not have. Second, the company's external communications follow the opposite rule: after the same outage, customers are told they "may have experienced a brief interruption in service," and no component or decision is ever named. The language we are forbidden to use internally is exactly the language the company uses outward, presumably on purpose. So my question is: what view of the relationship between language and thought does this internal policy actually presuppose? Does it presuppose the strong view that language shapes or constrains thought, or would a weaker view — that language is a useful diagnostic for thought but not a driver of it — be enough to justify the style guide? Is the policy coherent given the external-communications carve-out, and does that carve-out reveal what the policy is really for? And what would have to change in how we write and review incident reports if the company actually held the weaker view instead?
