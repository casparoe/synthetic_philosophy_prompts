For a paper I'm writing for my high school's philosophy and literature seminar, I want to read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the debate between communitarianism and liberalism. By liberalism I mean the view that individual persons are the basic units of moral concern and that a person's rights cannot simply be sacrificed to the happiness of others; by communitarianism I mean the view that the self is largely constituted by community and that a shared form of life has moral weight that is not reducible to the interests of the individuals in it. I'm deliberately setting aside the usual utilitarian reading. My question is whether the story sides with, complicates, or reframes this debate. Does the citizens' shared acceptance of the child's misery look like a community in the communitarian sense, or like a liberal society that has simply made one person an exception whose suffering underwrites everyone else's private happiness? Do the ones who walk away represent a liberal act of individual conscience, a communitarian search for a different shared life, or something neither framework captures? Which scenes or details should I focus on as evidence? And what does the story add to the debate that the theories themselves miss—for example, in its unexplained claim that the child's suffering is necessary, which makes the reader unable to weigh any actual trade-off?
