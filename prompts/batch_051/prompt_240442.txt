Take the two-envelope puzzle, but with a twist: you get to open your envelope before deciding, and you know the smaller amount was chosen uniformly from 1 through 100 dollars, with the other envelop holding twice that. So the possible pairs are (1,2), (2,4), ..., (100,200), each equally likely. You pick an envelope, open it, and see 70. Your friend insists the old argument still works: the other envelope is either 140 or 35, each equally likely, so switching averages (140+35)/2 = 87.5, which beats 70. Is your friend right? Now run the same calculation if you'd seen 140 instead. Finally, consider this rule: switch if and only if the amount you see is at most 100. Does that rule beat never switching in expected value? If it does, why doesn't the usual symmetry argument—before you look, the two envelopes are symmetric, so switching can't be systematically better—rule it out? And where exactly does the original "always switch" argument go wrong in this version?
