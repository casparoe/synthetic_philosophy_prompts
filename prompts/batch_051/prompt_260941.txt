Some background before the question itself. I've been going down a slow G. E. Moore rabbit hole after seeing him repeatedly invoked in rationalist-adjacent discussions of the open-question argument and naturalistic ethics; the forum threads tend to quote the same few pages of Principia Ethica, so I went looking for a more patient overview. I ended up with an introductory companion to early analytic ethics that gives what it calls a plain reconstruction of Moore's chapter on the ideal. I've read only secondary discussion of Moore before, and I don't trust my memory of the original enough to judge the reconstruction myself, so I'd like you to audit it.

The companion's reconstruction, as I would paraphrase it, runs like this. Moore held that there are exactly two kinds of thing worth having for their own sake: loving personal relationships on the one hand, and the enjoyment of beauty on the other. Everything else — knowledge, virtue, achievement — is good only insofar as it brings about or sustains those states. His method for reaching this conclusion was an isolation test: ask whether a state of affairs would still be judged good if it existed entirely alone, with no consequences beyond itself. What makes friendship and aesthetic enjoyment pass the test, according to the companion, is that they contain the most intense and enduring pleasures, so their value consists in that pleasure. The author concludes that Moore is best read as a hedonist who simply objected to defining good as pleasure, because his axiology just is hedonism with a more liberal view of which objects can produce the relevant pleasures; whether the beloved person really has the qualities one admires, or whether the painting is really beautiful, matters only as a causal contributor to the admirer's pleasure.

Two parts of this seem off to me, though I don't know whether I'm confusing Moore with one of his critics. I had the impression that Moore spent a great deal of effort denying that the value of these states reduces to their pleasantness, and that he thought it mattered whether one's admiration or enjoyment is directed at qualities that are really there — that a friendship based on a flattering illusion is less good than one that tracks its object correctly, even if the felt pleasure is equal. Could you go through the reconstruction claim by claim? I'd like to know which parts are accurate, which are defensible simplifications, and which Moore would explicitly have rejected, with the reasoning for each. And I'd like a verdict on the author's final claim: is Moore's position hedonism with a broader catalog of pleasant objects, or does that conclusion depend on the parts of the reconstruction that fail?
