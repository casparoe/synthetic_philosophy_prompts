I'm running a 75-minute session for a rationalist-adjacent reading group (about 18 people, mostly software and policy types, no military background) on autonomous weapons and the slogan "meaningful human control." I've seen the phrase used a lot, and I've internalized the standard worries--automation bias, moral crumple zones, the responsibility gap--but I don't think the group has really felt why a human in the loop can be a compliance ritual rather than control. So: design a structured role-play or simulation that makes them run into that problem themselves, before it gets named.

I want a runnable activity with roles, materials, and a debrief, not a discussion prompt. The learning objective: by the end, people should feel the pull of "a person has to own the decision" and simultaneously see that control might be a property of the whole system--who sets the constraints, what information the approver sees, what happens after a mistake--rather than of the final button-press. A nice outcome would be someone saying, half surprised, "I just approved a strike I don't understand." I don't mean you should script that line; I mean the structure should produce that state.

Concretely I'd like two rounds along these lines, though adjust if you have something better: (1) a round where one participant is the "human controller" who must approve or veto AI targeting recommendations under time pressure using information the system provides; (2) a contrasting round where control moves upstream--people set rules of engagement or constraints in advance and no one reviews individual shots. Build in small incentives (points, status, avoided blame, not real money) so rubber-stamping and blame-gaming happen on their own.

The debrief should map what happened onto the actual conceptual joints: control-as-veto versus control-as-shaping-the-system, attribution versus influence, the responsibility gap, moral crumple zones, and the levels-of-abstraction point (is the human controlling a decision, a policy, a design, or a training distribution?). Also be frank about what the simulation can't reproduce--the stakes of an actual strike, institutional pressures, the fact that real operators are trained and accountable--and say what the facilitator has to supply in words afterwards.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I'm imagining someone who reflexively rejects every recommendation to prove a point, a table that treats it as a referendum on wether to ban autonomous weapons, or a group that concludes the human veto was good enough and the worry is overblown. If those happen, what do they show and what do I change? Also say where your design leans on a contested reading of meaningful human control, and what would make you redesign it--for example, if the contrast between round 1 and round 2 mainly confuses people, or if participants mostly conclude the upstream round gives less control rather than more, what revision would that argue for?

Format: hard cap of 1,200 words, and lead with a two-sentence summary of the activity and the learning objective before the details. No throat-clearing.
