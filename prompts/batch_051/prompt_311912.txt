My friend, who's been reading some experimental philosophy, summarized the Knobe effect for me like this. In the famous setup, people hear about a CEO who says he only cares about profits and is told his policy will have a side effect: in one version it helps the environment and in another it harms it. When the side effect is harmful, almost everyone says he brought it about intentionally; when it's helpful, almost everyone denies he brought it about intentionally. Knobe's takeaway, according to my friend, is that moral evaluation bleeds into our factual judgement of what was done intentionally, so judging mental states is actually doing two things at once: tracking mental states and tracking blameworthiness. That means when someone says the CEO acted intentionally they aren't reporting a neutral fact, they're condemning him. My friend then said this is why the legal distinction between intention and motive is hopeless, because jurors can't help but read blame into intent. This feels plausible but also to clean, and I suspect the real experimental philosophy literature is messier. Is his summary fair? I want to know which parts are accurate, which are subtle overstatements, and whether the legal conclusion survives once you've corrected anything he got wrong.
