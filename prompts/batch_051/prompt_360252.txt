I have been working on a puzzle about moral criticism, and I would like to work through it by way of an explicit definition. We routinely distinguish between the judgment that an act is wrong and the judgment that a particular person is in a position to voice that criticism without some further defect. I want a working definition of the second condition, standing to blame, understood not as the mere correctness of the criticism but as a normative relation between the blamer and the wrong or the wrongdoer.

Please test your definition against the following cases, saying for each whether the definition returns the right verdict, returns a clearly wrong verdict, or leaves the case undecided, and then revise or defend the definition accordingly. The cases: a passing stranger with no connection to either party who condemns a genuine wrong; the direct victim of that same wrong; a person who committed the same kind of wrong years ago, openly acknowledges it, and now criticizes others for it; a person who privately commits the same wrong now, conceals it, and publicly condemns others for it; a person who has never committed the wrong but has quietly profited from it and still benefits; a critic whose accusation is accurate but whose motive is spite; and an institution, a state or a museum, say, that publicly condemns a historical injustice while retaining property acquired through that injustice.

I suspect the notion of standing is doing two jobs at once: one backward-looking requirement on the critic's own record, and one relational requirement governing what the critic may do toward this particular wrongdoer. If you share that suspicion, please give the two conditions separately and say where each case falls relative to both; if you do not, defend the single definition that handles them.

I would like the response in continuous prose, as one would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
