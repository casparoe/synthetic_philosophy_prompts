I am a novelist doing background reading on historical explanation for a book set during the French Revolution, and a popular account of the covering-law debate gave me the following summary, which I am reconstructing from memory. Carl Hempel's covering-law model says an event is explained only when its occurrence can be deduced from universal laws together with statements of initial conditions. Since historians never have such laws, Hempel concluded that historical writing consists at best of explanation sketches pointing toward laws that will never be supplied, and that the narratives historians actually produce have no explanatory force. Philosophy of history has therefore moved to narrative explanation: to explain the French Revolution is to emplot its events into a coherent, temporally ordered story, and this storytelling explains without appeal to general laws or causal regularities.

I find the deductive part plausible, and I am inclined to think narrative explanation captures something real, but the claim that Hempel dismissed narrative as empty feels like a caricature, and the final claim that narrative explanation owes nothing to causal regularities seems too strong. Am I right to be suspicious? Please separate what in this account is accurate, what is a common simplification, what a defender of Hempel and a defender of narrative explanation would each reject, and what the corrected picture would mean for how I think about explanation in historical fiction.
