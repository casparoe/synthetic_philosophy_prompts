I'm not an academic, just someone who's been reading about the Nordics on my own since the Ukraine war started, so forgive me if I've got some of the categories wrong. The thing I keep chewing on is this. For my whole lifetime Sweden and Finland were held up as proof that armed neutrality is a workable strategy: stay out of formal alliances, but keep a serious military and a whole-society defense setup so that attacking you costs more than it's worth, and don't look like you're about to side with anyone. Then Russia invades Ukraine in 2022 and both of them drop it — Finland into NATO in 2023, Sweden in 2024. I want you to analyze that flip through the lens of the armed-neutrality playbook as it's defenders used to describe it. What does that playbook actually assume — about your neighbors, your own credibility, the kind of war you'd actually be in — and where do the Finnish and Swedish setups fit those assumptions and where do they break them? Finland's long land border with Russia and its conscription-based army put it in a different spot than Sweden's industrial base and two centuries of staying out of wars. Then run it the other direction: what does the flip say about when armed neutrality is stable and when it comes apart? Is the honest lesson that they were never really neutral, or that the model only works under background conditions nobody bothered to spell out? And please keep straight what's settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who study this — don't pass off one contested take as the consensus.
