Until a few months ago I would have said I had no worked-out view about moral worth. Then a relative helped me through a long recovery, and I learned afterward that the help had been driven mostly by how it would look to the rest of the family. I felt my admiration curdle, but I could not tell whether that reaction was fair or just wounded pride. I have spent the time since trying to get clear about when a right action is admirable in the specific way we mark by saying it has moral worth.

The view I have arrived at, and want stress-tested, is this: an action has moral worth just in case the agent performs it partly because of the features that actually make the action right. The explanatory connection matters, not the agent's fluency. An agent can be moved by those features under a modest description — "she is in pain," "I promised," "that is not fair" — without framing them in the terms of any moral theory. But the features doing the motivational work must be the right-making features themselves, not surrogates that merely coincide with them. On this view, the following lack moral worth: doing the right thing from self-interest; from concern for reputation; from unreflective rule-following that does not register why the rule exists; from a desire to follow a correct principle as such, since duty-for-duty's-sake does not by itself put one in touch with what makes the particular act right; and from a mistaken moral view. I am tempted by one exception: an agent acting from a mistaken principle can have moral worth if the principle was itself formed as a good-faith generalization from concern with the right kind of features, or if the mistake lies elsewhere in her reasoning while the right-making features still do the motivating work. I worry that this exception is already evidence of insulation, and I want that worry examined rather than soothed.

What would count against this position? I do not mean what merely sounds like a counterexample; I mean what the view genuinely risks. Please sort the material this way. First, the cases or arguments that should be decisive against it, if any exist: a convincing example of moral worth without the right-making features doing the work, or an action that meets my conditions yet clearly lacks moral worth. Second, the evidence that would be merely awkward, forcing refinement rather than abandonment — and tell me how much refinement already amounts to a concession. Third, what someone who shares the view should in honesty concede counts against it, as opposed to what its critics claim. Fourth, what the view predicts that its main rivals do not — the acting-from-duty view, the acting-on-principle view, a quality-of-will view indifferent to the agent's description of her reasons, and a mere reliability view — and where these views would come apart in practice. Fifth, whether the view still risks anything, or whether clauses like the modest-description allowance and the mistaken-but-derived exception have insulated it from refutation; and if it is insulated, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or simply what one should expect of a view about something as elusive as acting for the right reasons.

Finally, I would like your own exposure on the table: what argument or example would change your mind about your assessment, and where do you think your reasoning is most likely to fail?
