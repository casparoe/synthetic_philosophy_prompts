I run the science portfolio at a mid-sized family foundation — about thirty grants a year, spread across psychology, ecology, materials science, and the occasional pure-math-adjacent thing. The board signs off on every slate, and they've started asking why some fields seem to move fast while others publish paper after paper and nobody can say what it means. The working view among the program staff is that fields with tight, fast feedback loops — where a wrong idea gets killed quickly and unambigiously — progress faster than fields where you can be wrong for a decade and the literature just gets more confused. I want to turn that slogan into something our grant screeners can actually use, not another thing we say at retreats.

Write me a reusable procedure a program officer can run in about twenty-five minutes on a single proposal, before the full review, to score how much real feedback a project would produce and how much weight that should carry. Our reviewers are smart generalists, not methodologists, so it has to be operational: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops and puts a "weak feedback" flag on the file, and — just as important — where it should hand the decision back to a person, because not everything worth funding can tell you fast that it's wrong. I suspect there's a distinction between feedback that arrives quickly and feedback that actually changes your mind, and between a project that could falsify its own main claim if it's false and one that just accumulates data either way; make sure those distinctions survive the translation.

Run the procedure on three proposals: (1) a five-year observational study of long-term forest recovery after fire, which won't give a clean verdict for years but is the only way to get the data; (2) a series of rapid classroom experiments on a tutoring method, each designed to kill the approach within six weeks if it doesn't work; and (3) a theory-building project in cognitive science with no experiment at all, just a formal model plus qualitative predictions. I expect the procedure to favor the second and to tell me no on at least one of the other two — probably the forest study, which will annoy me, since I think it's important — and I want you to say plainly what the procedure can't capture, what this feedback lens systematically undervalues. Lead with the bottom line: put the single rule you'd write at the top of the procedure in your first sentence, then give the steps and the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
