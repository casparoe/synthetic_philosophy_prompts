Two members of my philosophy reading group spent an evening arguing about whether every meaningful statement must be true or false. One of them kept returning to a sentence from Aristotle's On Interpretation, Part 4: "Thus a prayer is a sentence, but is neither true nor false." Their exchange, as I noted it down, went roughly through five moves.

(1) He quoted the passage and said it shows that a grammatically ordinary sentence can fail to be truth-evaluable, so if the principle of bivalence is meant to cover all meaningful sentences, it is false. (2) She replied that this is trivial for Aristotle on his own terms: he has already defined a proposition as that which has truth or falsity, and a prayer is not a proposition. The serious question, she said, is whether every proposition—every assertoric sentence with truth-evaluable content—must be either true or false, and the passage does not touch that. (3) He answered that her reply merely relocates the problem: many apparently assertoric sentences, such as "That film is good" or "Her action was brave," function more like prayers or expressions of attitude than like descriptions, so she owes a principled account of which sentences express propositions and which do not. (4) She said such sentences become truth-evaluable once an evaluative standard is supplied, and where no standard is supplied they are incomplete rather than sui generis truth-value gaps—unlike a prayer, which is complete as a speech act but not the kind of speech act that can be true or false. (5) He countered that if their truth-evaluability depends on a standard the speaker has not supplied, then the sentence as actually uttered is not truth-evaluable; and by her own incompleteness test, ordinary future-tense predictions about undetermined matters would likewise fail to be truth-evaluable as uttered, which is a substantive concession to the opponent of bivalence.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who has the better of the argument as stated—and what, if anything, both of them are missing about Aristotle's own sentence/proposition distinction and its bearing on bivalence.
