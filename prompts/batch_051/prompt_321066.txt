Benedict de Spinoza, in Part I of the *Ethics*, offers this definition:

> That thing is called free, which exists solely by the necessity of its own nature, and of which the action is determined by itself alone. On the other hand, that thing is necessary, or rather constrained, which is determined by something external to itself to a fixed and definite method of existence or action.

Later in the *Ethics*, Spinoza argues that everything that happens follows from the divine nature with strict necessity, and that the highest human good consists in the intellectual love of God, an active state of the mind that arises from adequate knowledge. Spinoza clearly wants to say that someone who attains the intellectual love of God is freer than someone driven by passions, even though the necessitarian metaphysics seems to leave no events that are not determined by prior causes.

Please explain the conceptual structure that keeps Spinoza’s account of freedom from being a merely verbal trick: what exactly distinguishes being the adequate cause of one’s affects from being an inadequate cause, and why should that difference amount to a difference in freedom under Definition VII? Then test the view on the intellectual love of God: can the intellectual love of God properly be called an action of the mind itself rather than a passive affect? Does that classification carry genuine normative weight within Spinoza’s system, or does it simply relabel the best causal chains as “free”?
