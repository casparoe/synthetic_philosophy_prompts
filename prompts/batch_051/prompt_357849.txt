Act as an expert in nineteenth-century German moral philosophy and contemporary moral psychology. I have been co-leading a reading group on the Schopenhauer–Nietzsche dispute about pity and compassion (Mitleid), and after several weeks the conversation has become frustratingly circular. We have reconstructed the standard positions clearly enough: Schopenhauer treats pity as the one genuinely non-egoistic moral motive and as the foundation of morality, grounded in a kind of metaphysical insight into the underlying unity of self and other, whereas Nietzsche treats pity as a pathology that deepens suffering, corrupts both the one who pities and the one pitied, and sneaks a feeling of superiority in under a virtuous name. Yet knowing each side better has not moved anyone. Each week we return to the same standoff, with some members convinced that Schopenhauer has identified the emotional root of moral concern and others convinced that Nietzsche has unmasked what actually goes on when people congratulate themselves for feeling sorry for someone.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really one debate. It seems possible that Schopenhauer is asking a grounding question—how can morality have a non-egoistic basis at all, and what kind of experience could supply one—while Nietzsche is asking a different, more diagnostic question—what does pity do to the people who feel it, what kind of life does it express, and what social and psychological functions does it serve under the appearance of virtue. If that is right, the standoff is partly a category confusion; if it is wrong, then somewhere underneath the rhetoric there may be a substantive psychological or ethical dispute, perhaps about whether pity reliably increases or reduces suffering, or about whether status and condescension are essential to it or merely frequent accompaniments.

I want a real diagnosis, not a survey of the two philosophers. Think carefully and start by brainstorming the different sources the impasse could have: a hidden empirical disagreement about the psychology of compassion, a difference about what an account of pity is supposed to do, a difference about which cases count as central examples of pity, a difference in bedrock intuitions about whether feeling another's suffering is admirable or suspect, or a difference about the underlying metaphysical picture of self and other. Then commit to the diagnosis you find best supported by what each side actually says, note where the diagnosis fits imperfectly and where it fits well, and explain what would actually count as progress under that diagnosis—whether that is a sharper distinction, a change of question, a psychological finding, or a reasoned conclusion that no convergence should be expected and why.
