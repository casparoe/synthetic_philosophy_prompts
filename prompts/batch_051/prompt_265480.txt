I'm a novelist, and there's an asymmetry in my own reactions I can't get straight, and I'd like help getting it straight. For years I've helped raise money for a land trust that protects a kind of salamander — a brown, unglamorous thing that lives under rocks — and I've watched otherwise practical people treat the thought of it going locally extinct as a disaster and a loss to the world itself, not just to us. Meanwhile, a neighbor of mine died last year. She was one of the last fluent speakers of a language that's been spoken in the valley where I set my novels for as long as anyone can remember. With her went a whole body of proverbs, songs, and a way of marking respect I can't render in any language I write. At the wake everyone was sad about her; almost no one was sad about the language, or if they were it was the quiet sadness you feel about a damaged fresco, not the stop-everything feeling the salamander produced. So here it is: we seem to treat a species's disappearance as a permanent loss to the world, while a language's disappearance — which is, if anything, a denser and more singular thing — gets treated as a loss only to the people who happened to hold it. Is there a principle that really draws that line, or is it just sentiment, and should I get over the salamanders or get less fatalistic about the languages? Give me the strongest candidate justification you can find, test it against nearby cases — a tradition nobody practices anymore, an art form kept alive by a single person, a manuscript no one will ever read, the last members of a species kept going in a zoo — and then tell me honestly whether you'd keep the asymmetry, give it up, or reverse it. And please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
