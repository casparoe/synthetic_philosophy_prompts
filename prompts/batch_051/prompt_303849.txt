Exam question: Many theists hold that God is omniscient, with complete foreknowledge of what each person will do, and that God is immutable, incapable of any change. Explain as precisely as you can why petitionary prayer—prayer that asks God for something—is puzzling given these two commitments. Then evaluate the common reply that petitionary prayer is best understood primarily not as an attempt to move God to act but as a practice that transforms the person who prays. Does this reply solve the puzzle, or does it give up something essential to petition as such? Defend your verdict.

Student answer:

Petitionary prayer is puzzling because it is an act of asking. Asking normally conveys information the hearer lacks, or tries to move the hearer to do something he would not otherwise do. But an omniscient God already knows everything about our needs, so our asking cannot inform him. And an immutable God cannot be moved: if God would not grant a request apart from our asking but grants it because we ask, then God's will differs depending on what we do, and that is exactly the kind of change immutability rules out. So either our asking makes no difference to what God does, in which case it is pointless as a request, or it does make a difference, in which case God is mutable.

The standard reply is that prayer changes us, not God. Prayer is a way of acknowledging our dependence, correcting our desires, and coming to accept that outcomes are in God's hands. Its value lies in this transformation rather than in moving God to act. On this account, God's will is settled from eternity. Since God already foreknows everything that will happen, whether we ask cannot be a factor in what God does; it can only be a factor in what we become. And because what we become is a genuine good, the practice is fully coherent as a form of petitionary prayer. The puzzle is therefore dissolved.
