Spent most of my life doing chemistry, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy of action and criminal law theory — particularly the stuff on complicity and whether you can share in a wrong without actually causing it. Here's a complicity clause of the sort you see in modern penal codes, simplified a bit:

SECTION 11. A person is complicit in an offence if, knowing that another intends to commit it, he does any act for the purpose of assisting that other to commit it, or he aids, abets, counsels, procures or encourages its commission. It is immaterial (a) that the offence is not in fact committed, (b) that the principal is never identified or convicted, or (c) that the person's conduct was not necessary to the commission of the offence or did not in fact contribute to it.

Now here's the case thats bothering me. A retired chemist runs a small open-access site where he puts up careful bench procedures for compounds that mostly have teaching uses. One procedure is for a compound used legitimately in undergrad labs, but also usable as a precursor in an unlawful synthesis. From reader comments and download patterns he knows with near-certainty that a sizeable chunk of the people downloading it intend to use it unlawfully. He never learns their names, never corresponds with any particular one, and his purpose in posting is the ordinary scientific one — keeping an older technique from being lost and helping honest instructors. In a given offence, the paper would usually not have been necessary (other routes exist), and some offenders download it but never actually follow the procedure.

Does SECTION 11 make him complicit? I'd like the chain of reasoning worked through for the different approaches — the plain words, the purpose of the rule, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader takes the words to cover — and then a verdict on which approach you think is right. And here's the thing I actually want to get at: clause (c) settles the 'no causal contribution' prong, but does it just beg the deeper question, whether complicity is a matter of causing (or nearly causing) the wrong at all, or whether it's about something like making the other person's conduct yours — joining your will to the wrong?
