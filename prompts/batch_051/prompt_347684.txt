I recently started taking long-termist reasoning seriously—like actually reallocating donations and half-planning my career around the far future—and then a friend made an offhand comment about the simulation argument that has been messing with my head. I tried to turn the worry into an argument, and now I can't tell whether I've found a real problem or just tied myself in a knot. Here it is:

(1) If we're in an ancestor simulation, the simulators could shut it down soon, or could be running only a slice of history around the present; nothing guarantees our world has a long future ahead of it.
(2) The simulation argument gives us strong reason to think we're in an ancestor simulation.
(3) So we should have high credence that there's no long future ahead of us.
(4) Far-future fanaticism—the idea that the astronomical number of possible future lives makes the long-run effects of our actions dominate everything—only makes sense if our actions now can be expected to affect a huge number of future lives.
(5) If there's probably no long future ahead of us, then that expected effect is mostly gone.
(6) So, if the simulation argument is right, far-future fanaticism is undermined.

I have a vauge sense the fishy step is (3)—something about conflating the base-reality future of whoever simulated us with the future we'd actually experience if the sim keeps running—but I can't pin it down. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading? If (3) is the problem, what should it say instead, and would the conclusion still go through?
