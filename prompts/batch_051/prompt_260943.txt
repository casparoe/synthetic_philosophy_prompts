I'm on the faith-and-science review committee at a small Christian college. Our statement of faith says God created the world good, and that human sin brought death and corruption into the world, but it doesn't define any of those terms, which is why were stuck. A biology professor wants to teach a unit arguing that animal pain and death existed for hundreds of millions of years before humans, and that this doesn't touch the doctrine of a good creation, because calling creation good was never meant to mean that each individual creature lives comfortably—a creation can be good while still including predation, parasites, and extinction. A theology professor on the committee is furious. On her view, if pain and predation are original equipment, then the Fall does no work: sin doesn't introduce death into the world, and the whole narrative about a broken creation that God promises to renew stops making sense. Worse, she says, it makes God the direct author of a world built on suffering, and she thinks that's a bigger problem than the one it solves. The biology professor says the alternative is even worse: you have to pretend the fossil record is misleading, or invent a Fall that reaches backward through time to insert death into a history that happened before the sin—which sounds like special pleading.

Draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: recommend whether the unit should be approved, rejected, or approved with conditions, and give conditions specific enough that both professors could actually comply. Engage the theology professor's worry seriously rather than treating it as anti-science, and be honest about the strongest reason to reject the unit.
