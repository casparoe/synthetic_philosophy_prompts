I have been thinking about how a group sometimes resolves a crisis by putting blame, guilt, or bad luck onto a single person or animal, and then acting as if removing or punishing that target repairs the original problem. I have my own reactions to the cases below, but I cannot tell what principle, if any, lies behind them, and I would like help making it explicit. I am setting aside cases in which the person blamed really did cause the harm; those feel simpler to me.

Case one. A company loses a major client after a delayed product launch. The real cause was the founder's repeated changes to the scope. The project manager had warned about the delays, but the founder fires the project manager. In the following weeks the team's morale recovers and the next launch goes well. My verdict: this is an injustice to the project manager, yet I condemn it less than I expected to, because something real in the group did get repaired.

Case two. A small town is frightened by a series of unsolved burglaries. Suspicion settles on a harmless man with a decades-old conviction. He is harassed until he moves away, and afterwards people say the town feels safe again. My verdict: clearly wrong.

Case three. A professional sports team has a terrible season. The players are aging and the general manager made poor trades, but the head coach is fired. My verdict: I feel little outrage. Coaching seems to include absorbing blame for the team; it is almost a service the coach performs.

Case four. A rural community holds an annual ceremony in which it symbolically places the community's misdeeds onto a goat and drives the animal away from the village. I do not think a goat can literally carry wrongdoing. My verdict: not morally wrong; it looks like a shared gesture that lets people mark a fresh start.

Case five. In a family I know, whenever there is conflict, the eldest child is blamed, and punishing her is treated as the resolution. She has learned to accept it, and the household remains peaceful. My verdict: wrong, and more wrong than the manager case, even though the structure seems similar.

Case six. A soldier throws himself on a grenade, absorbing the blast and saving the people around him. My verdict: I admire it, and it feels entirely different from the other cases, though I cannot say exactly why.

Case seven. A cabinet minister resigns after a policy failure that was mostly caused by the civil service and unlucky timing. The government continues, and public anger largely dissipates. My verdict: acceptable in a way, though not entirely right; something like this may be required for a government to keep functioning.

I would like to know three things. First, whether there is a single principle, involving consent, role, actual fault, effectiveness, or something else, that captures all or most of these verdicts, stated precisely and with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Second, which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to overturn or soften. Third, what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: after a destructive riot, prosecutors charge a small number of rioters harshly while releasing many others who were equally involved, on the grounds that a public example is needed.
