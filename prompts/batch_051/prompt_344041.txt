I've been thinking about what's usually called Chesterton's fence: the principle that you shouldn't tear a fence down until you know why someone put it up, because the builder may have had a reason that isn't obvious. The argument seems to take for granted that an institution is like a fence—something deliberately put in place by an agent who knew something. But many of the things people defend this way were never deliberately put in place at all. Common-law doctrines, etiquette norms, customs, market conventions, even features of language accumulate over long stretches of time, and no one ever chose the whole thing for a reason. So here's my question: if an institution wasn't built by anyone, does Chesterton's fence still apply, or does the change of assumption—from designed artifact to emergent accumulation—kill the argument? If it applies, which premise has to be revised, and does the revised principle come out weaker or just differently grounded? If it doesn't apply, what's the right default for a reformer facing an emergent institution? End with a single line of the form \answer{...} that gives your verdict in a short phrase, e.g., \answer{survives with repair}.
