As a journalist who covers ideas about governance and economic development, I once interviewed a senior economic official in a prosperous authoritarian state. Defending the political system, he offered an analogy: the state is like a corporation. A corporation is not run by asking shareholders to vote on every operational decision; it is run by appointing competent managers, setting performance targets, and judging them on results — profit, growth, and stability. A government, he argued, should be judged in the same way: not by whether it is selected through competitive elections, but by whether it delivers prosperity and order. Audit this analogy. Map the correspondences: who or what plays the role of the shareholders, the board, the executives, the employees, the customers, and the quarterly report? Then mark where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the official's argument from the incidental ones, and identify which features of the corporate setting the analogy quietly imports — exit, competition, a purpose fixed by owners, fiduciary duties, a mechanism for firing management — that a state lacks or has only in distorted form. Finally, say whether the conclusion it was used to support — that demonstrated prosperity and order can justify non-democratic rule — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along, and, if so, whether some other analogy would carry it better.
