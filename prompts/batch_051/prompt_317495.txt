I recently joined the governance committee of a small professional association, and I have spent the past few months trying to revise the default terms under which members provide services to one another. I expected the arguments to be about fairness. They were mostly not. Newer members wanted defaults oriented toward mentoring and flexibility; senior members wanted defaults oriented toward autonomy and relief from supervision. Everyone sincerely appealed to the golden rule, and everyone reached a different answer. That is what pushed me toward a proposal I have begun calling the default golden rule.

The proposal rests on one claim: the golden rule is not a reliable test for individual, one-off conduct toward a particular person, because people often want different things. It becomes workable only when it is applied to default arrangements rather than particular transactions — to the rule you would adopt before you know which position you occupy among those the rule affects. In practice, the default golden rule has two parts. First, a proposed default is acceptable only if a person could accept it while not knowing which affected position they would occupy: the new member or the senior one, the client or the provider, the occasional user or the heavy user. Second, once a default is in place, a deviation from it in a particular case is permissible only if the person in the least-advantaged affected position could reasonably accept it.

An example from our committee: the initial proposal for project assignments let seniority choose first. It failed the test, because someone who did not yet know their seniority would not accept a default that always gives the newest members the least convenient work. We replaced it with rotation and multi-year balancing. That felt like progress, but I worry that I have quietly stopped doing ethics and started doing contract drafting. It may be that I have merely reinvented a veil-of-ignorance or social-contract test and attached the golden rule's name to it, or that the phrase "could reasonably accept" is doing all the work while the random-position device does almost nothing.

I want an honest assessment rather than encouragement. Is the default golden rule a coherent and distinctive fix to the golden rule, or does it collapse into a familiar idea? Has it already been worked out under another name — and if so, what does the standard formulation capture that my two-part test misses? Where does my version break, either as a reconstruction of the golden rule or as a practical test for defaults and exceptions? What are the strongest objections, and are any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
