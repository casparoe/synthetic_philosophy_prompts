Heres a puzzle I keep going back and forth on. Take a developer in a city where the permit office is slow. In one case she hands a €2,000 envelope to the official who signs the permit, and the permit gets signed that week. In the other case she gives the same €2,000 to the mayor's re-election campaign, gets a fifteen-minute meeting with the mayor, and the permit gets signed after the mayor's office calls down to "check on things." Almost everyone calls the first move bribery and the second one politics, and the law in a lot of places more or less codifies that verdict. But both moves transfer money with the clear intent to get a favorable exercise of public power; the only differences are whether the money goes to a person or a campaign, and whether it's disclosed. I see the same asymmetry when a customs officer takes a bottle of whisky to wave a shipment through — obviously corrupt — while a trade association flies a minister to Singapore for a conference the week before a tariff decision and everyone calls it engagement. This line-drawing is exactly the kind of thing anti-corruption reformers export as a universal standard, so it had better survive scrutiny. Here's what I want: take a definite position on whether there is a real moral line here, or whether we've just legalized one channel of the same transaction. Don't give me a map of the debate. If you defend the asymmetry, you have to deal with a hypothetical jurisdiction that legalizes direct cash gifts to officials but requires that every gift be logged in a public register with real penalties for failing to log — what exactly is still wrong with it? If you attack the asymmetry, you have to deal with the worry that campaigns need money and that restricting contributions will just protect incumbents. End by saying whether we should keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or drop it.
