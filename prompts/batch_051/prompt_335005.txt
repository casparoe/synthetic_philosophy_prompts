Consider a position worth taking seriously: the no-free-lunch theorems are a formalized statement of the problem of induction. Averaging uniformly over all possible target functions is the correct formal rendering of complete absence of prior constraint on the world, so the theorems show that without substantive assumptions about the data-generating process — simplicity, smoothness, compressibility, or the like — no inductive strategy outperforms any other, including random guessing. On this view, the inductive biases that make contemporary learning theory work (restricted hypothesis classes, regularization, priors) are simply the principle of the uniformity of nature rewritten mathematically, and they require a non-circular justification no less than that principle did. Learning theory therefore does not dissolve the problem of induction; it sharpens it by making the smuggled assumptions explicit.

I want a map of what would count against this position. Which formal results, arguments, or empirical considerations should genuinely move someone away from it — the status of the uniform averaging distribution, algorithmic-prior or compressibility-based responses, online and non-uniform learning guarantees, the actual success of inductive practice — and which would only be awkward? Distinguish decisive defeaters from those a defender can absorb through qualification, indicate what defenders would themselves concede as counting against the position rather than what critics merely allege, and close with an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a position of this kind.
