I've been turning over an argument I found in a blog comment and can't figure out where it steps on a rake. It's supposed to show that if split-brain patients are two subjects, then so are the rest of us, which feels both right and absurd.

(1) Two simultaneous mental states belong to one subject only if they can in principle be brought together in a single act of consciousness — compared, reported together, held in one viewpoint at once.
(2) In a callosotomy patient, a left-hemisphere state and a simultaneous right-hemisphere state are not co-conscious in this way: the speaking hemisphere will confabulate an answer about what the other side saw rather than actually access it.
(3) So, at least during those tasks, a split-brain patient is two subjects, not one subject with reduced communication.
(4) But the difference between that case and a normal brain looks like a difference in degree of integration, not kind. Normal brains have massively parallel processing, attention bottlenecks, and plenty of activity that never becomes reportable; the corpus callosum is just a narrow channel that can be degraded by fatique or overload.
(5) So if imperfect co-consciousness makes the split-brain patient two subjects, then a tired or distracted normal person is, by parity of reasoning, several subjects at once — and how many people are in the room depends on how much coffee they've had.
(6) That seems to blow up the concept of a subject in a way I can't accept, but I also can't find a principled cutoff.

Tell me which step to reject and what I have to swallow by rejecting it. I'd rather keep (1) and (2) and insist the patient remains one subject, but I'm worried that just makes my unity criterion unfalsifiably weak. Give me a committed answer in American English: name the failure, explain why it looked fine, and say what the main alternatives cost.
