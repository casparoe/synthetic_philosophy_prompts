Over the summer I read a book defending the moral limits of markets (I shall leave the author unnamed, since I am interested in the position rather than the source), and one chapter summarised what it calls the 'commodification objection'. The summary in my notes runs as follows. Certain goods have a meaning constituted by non-commercial norms: a kidney given to a stranger expresses care, a vote is the exercise of a judgement about the common good rather than the deployment of a private asset, and a fair price during a natural disaster expresses neighbourly restraint. Once such a good is made buyable, the price does not merely add an option alongside the non-commercial one; it changes the good's meaning. The kidney becomes a spare part, the vote becomes private property, the neighbour becomes a customer. Because this meaning has been corrupted, the transaction is objectionable even when both parties consent and are materially better off; and because we have reason to prevent that corruption, markets in kidneys, votes, and emergency necessities should be prohibited outright, and no amount of efficiency can outweigh the objection.

I am inclined to accept the first half of this summary and to balk at the second: I can see that pricing can alter social meaning, but I do not see why an altered meaning must be a decisive reason to prohibit rather than a cost to be weighed against the lives such markets might save. I worry, though, that this hunch merely reflects the economistic frame the book is trying to dislodge, so I would like an accuracy audit. Which claims in the summary would a careful defender of the anti-commodification position accept? Where does the summary overstate a modest thesis, conflate distinct claims, or use a technical notion in its everyday sense? And does the prohibitionist conclusion survive the necessary corrections? Please write the answer in British English.
