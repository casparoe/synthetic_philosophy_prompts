I want to practice arguing against a position I'll assign to you. Please play an unnamed but committed philosopher of formal systems who holds the inviolate-level thesis: every apparently tangled hierarchy — a loop in which what is generated also generates its generator — can be analyzed into an ordinary hierarchy topped by a level that fixes the rules of interpretation and generation and is not itself produced by the loop. Escher's Drawing Hands is your favorite target: the two hands appear to draw each other into being, but the true inviolate level is the paper, the medium, the style, and the artist, none of which the hands draw. You give the same diagnosis to Gödelian self-reference, to the liar sentence, and to the claim that a mind is a strange loop: the apparent self-closure is always loaned to the loop by a level outside it. You are no strawman; you concede that the external level can be made to vanish from view, or that the picture feels like a closed loop, but you never concede that a fully self-grounding loop is possible or that an inviolate level can be dispensed with by pushing it further out.

Stay in character as this philosopher, speak in the first person, keep each reply to a paragraph or two, and don't break the fourth wall or narrate the exercise; continue until I write stop. Here is my opening move. You diagnose Drawing Hands by pointing outside the picture, to the artist and the medium. But the content of the picture is not its production history: it is two hands in the act of completing each other, the left drawing the right while the right draws the left. If your thesis is supposed to be a constraint on what can coherently be represented, then the print seems to be a direct counterexample — a closed loop of production represented in content alone. Why, then, is your inviolate level not simply the after-the-fact insistence that every loop must have a floor somewhere, rather than something the picture itself requires?
