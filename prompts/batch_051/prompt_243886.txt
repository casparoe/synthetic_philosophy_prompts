Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Molyneux’s problem, and I realized we were not answering the same question. One of us kept treating it as a question about whether a newly sighted person’s visual system spontaneously forms the right distal shape representation; the other kept treating it as a question about whether the person can deploy their tactually acquired shape concepts in visual judgment. Build me a thought experiment that will force this apart.

Construct a one-paragraph scenario in which a person born blind with fully intact tactile cognition has learned to distinguish a cube and a sphere by touch, and then gains normal sight. The case should be set up so that the person can reliably make a visual same-different discrimination between the two seen shapes, but nevertheless cannot match the seen shapes to the remembered felt shapes, even though the tactile memories remain available. Do not stipulate the verdict; give the case details that would produce these outcomes through ordinary perceptual and cognitive processes. Constraints: realistic, no damaged visual pathways beyond the original curable blindness, no cognitive or linguistic impairments, and no experimenters asking misleading questions.

After the scenario, walk through what a defender of the perceptual reading should say and what a defender of the conceptual reading should say, and identify which feature of the case does the separating work. Then tell me what would happen if that feature were removed, and how someone who still answers Molyneux in the affirmative would attack your case.
