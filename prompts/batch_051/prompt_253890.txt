I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and lately I keep running into the phrase "the banality of evil." An editor used it to kill a pitch about a corporate compliance system that quietly pressures employees into denying legitimate claims; his point, as I understood it, was that real harm doesn't need cartoon villains—just ordinary people doing their jobs. Then I heard it used to argue that mid-level bureaucrats in a genocidal regime are somehow more dangerous than the true believers, and later to criticize Hannah Arendt herself for being naive about how bad Eichmann really was. Thats at least three different claims wearing the same coat.

I know the phrase traces to the subtitle of Arendt's Eichmann book, Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil, and that she had something particular in mind about Eichmann's thoughtlessness. But I don't trust my memory of what she actually claimed, and honestly the slogan has taken on a life of its own, so I'd rather treat it as free-floating language. What are the distinct precise claims "the banality of evil" could be making? Lay them out as a menu, from the almost tautological to the strong and probably false. For each one, say whether it's plausible and whether it actually does explanatory work or just re-describes the thing it's supposed to explain. Which reading, if any, supports my editor's move that everyday institutional harm is worse—or less blameworthy, or more blameworthy—than ideological evil? And for each of the three uses I ran into, which reading does the argument actually need, and does that reading survive scrutiny? If the request rests on a confusion or the slogan can't be pinned down without the book's context, just say so.
