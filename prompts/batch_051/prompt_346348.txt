Next month I'm presenting a paper at a workshop on anti-discrimination law, and I'm already dreading the Q&A. The paper argues that the standard factors for deciding whether a ground of discrimination is "suspect" in equal-protection law — immutability, political powerlessness, a history of deliberate discrimination against the group, and the group being unable to protect itself through the ordinary political process — are all just noisy proxies for one underlying thing, which I call status-lock. Status-lock is when a state classification marks a group as fitted for a subordinate place in a social hierarchy and then makes that place sticky, so the classification both expresses and reproduces the group's lower standing. I argue the doctrine should be collapsed into this one factor. The payoff cases: race, national origin, and religion get heightened scrutiny because they are canonical status-hierarchy markers, while age and disability mostly don't, even though age is immutable and older and disabled people are politically weak, because those classifications are usually about competence rather than about marking a group as lesser. The same move, I claim, explains why sexual orientation and gender identity count as suspect without pretending they are "immutable" in the old sense, and why poverty doesn't count as suspect.

Who will be in the room: mostly U.S. constitutional-law professors, plus a handfull of political philosophers. About a third are originalists who think the whole tiers-of-scrutiny apparatus is invented and will ask why my one factor isn't equally invented. A third are critical race / structural discrimination people who think a clean one-factor theory launders the history out of the doctrine and makes racism sound like a classification problem. The rest are liberal doctrinalists who basically think the four-factor test is fine in practice and just needs tinkering.

Prepare me for the questions they'll actually ask. For each camp, give me the objection the way they'd phrase it, say which ones have real force and which are just turf-defending, tell me what to concede up front so I don't burn Q&A time on claims I don't need, and tell me the one reply that has to land if the paper is going to survive the room.
