I keep coming back to "what if everyone did that?" and I'd like to know whether there's one principle that explains when it actually generates a moral reason. Here are my gut verdicts.

(a) A friend lends me $200 and I promise to repay on Friday, but Friday comes and I'd rather ghost him. If everyone broke low-stakes promises whenever it was easy, promising would collapse. I think that settles it: I should pay.

(b) I'm about to skim $500 off some consulting income on my taxes. If everyone underreported by a few percent, public services would degrade badly. That feels like a real reason not to, and I'd call it wrong.

(c) There's a national election Tuesday and I don't want to stand in line. If everyone who didn't feel like it stayed home, turnout would crash. But I cant make myself think my one skipped vote is wrong; it feels like a fallacy of composition, since my staying home doesn't make anyone else stay home.

(d) I cut across a roped-off lawn at night to save two minutes. If every student did that, it'd be a dirt path in a month. I resist this one, but honestly I take the long way around; the argument seems to work here.

(e) My nephew prefers becoming a dive instructor to medical school. If everyone chose fun jobs over high-need ones, society would lose essential workers. But I don't think he's obligated to become a doctor; the argument proves too much.

(f) I get why Kant says the test applies to maxims, not acts: "what if everyone married Alex?" is absurd, but that doesn't make my marrying Alex wrong. But then I can't see how to describe the maxim. "Marry the person I love" universalizes fine; "marry Alex" doesn't; and there seems to be no principled way to choose the grain of the description.

My working hypothesis: the test works only when my act is parasitic on some background practice or shared arrangement I still want in place, and general defection would wreck it. That covers (a), (b), and (d). But it seems to overapply to (c)—democracy is a practice I want—and maybe to (e) as well.

So: is there a coherent principle that captures which cases generalize and which don't, or do some of my verdicts have to go? And what does the principle you land on predict about my flip-flop case: I never pledge to the public radio station I rely on every morning, and if everyone who relied on it did the same it would fold. Does that pattern with taxes or with voting?

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
