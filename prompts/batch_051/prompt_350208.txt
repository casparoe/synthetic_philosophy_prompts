I am a PhD student in philosophy of technology, writing a chapter on how platform rules drafted before generative AI behave when they encounter post-mortem chatbots. I'd like you to act as an expert in the interpretation of normative texts — someone at home in both statutory construction and the philosophy of language — and to think through a puzzle carefully rather than delivering a quick verdict. Before you commit to a conclusion, take a few passes at the language: brainstorm the candidate readings of the key phrases, then work through the interpretive approaches one by one.

The rule. A social platform's Terms of Service include Section 4.2: "You may not use the Service to create any account, profile, or bot that impersonates a real person, living or dead, or that otherwise holds itself out as being a real person or as speaking for a real person without that person's verifiable consent." The clause was added in 2016 after a wave of parody accounts and impersonation complaints, and the drafting history and staff guidance make clear that the harms the drafters had in mind were deception of third parties, damage to the impersonated person's reputation, and the use of fake identities to defraud or harass.

The unforeseen case. A widow, using a separate bereavement service that connects to the platform through an API, has created a "continuation bot" trained on several years of her late husband's text messages and emails. The husband died suddenly in 2019 and never consented; the widow is the executor of his digital estate. The bot announces at the start of every conversation that it is a simulation, converses only with the widow and the couple's two adult children, all of whom have consented, and does not post on the platform or interact with anyone else. It sometimes offers advice in the husband's familiar style, and the widow says it helps her talk through practical decisions as she would have done with him. The platform's moderation system flags the bot under Section 4.2.

I am less interested in what the platform would do as a business matter than in what the clause, properly interpreted, says. Please walk me through at least four approaches: (1) the plain meaning of the words; (2) the purpose of the clause as evidenced by the 2016 drafting history; (3) what the drafters would most likely have said if asked, in 2024, about a private, clearly labeled, consenting-family griefbot; and (4) what a reasonable user reading the clause today would understand it to prohibit. Then tell me which approach should control here and why, and whether any defensible reading of Section 4.2 reaches this bot without also reaching a clearly acceptable use of the platform. End by identifying the point where interpretation genuinely runs out — the question no amount of textual or purposive analysis can settle, which a court or platform would have to decide as a matter of policy.
