Here's a toy version of the classic old-evidence problem, just to fix numbers. Let T be a theory and E a piece of data. Background knowledge gives P(T)=0.1. T entails E, so P(E|T)=1. If T is false, E is still somewhat plausible: P(E|~T)=0.2. That means P(E)=0.28 before E is seen, and if E arrives as fresh news, P(T|E)=0.36. But in the old-evidence setup, E was already known, so P(E)=1, before anyone formulated T. So conditioning on E does nothing, and the puzzle is why scientists still treat E as strong support for T.

Now here's the variation I want you to work through. A scientist once knew E with certainty. She is then given a drug that deletes exactly one randomly selected belief, and it happens to delete E. On waking, she has no memory of ever knowing E, and her remaining background information supplies the same P(E)=0.28 as before. She then hears T proposed together with the proof that T entails E. Finally she goes to the lab and observes E again.

At which point or points, if any, does E (or the proof that T entails E) confirm T for her, and by how much? Does the old-evidence problem survive in this variant, or does the variant show the problem is really about certainty rather than about the evidence being temporally old? What should a strict conditionalizer say about the drug-induced forgetting—is forgetting even a move in the Bayesian formalism—and does that matter for whether we should trust the confirmation she gets when she re-observes E? Finally, compare this to the counterfactual way of handling old evidence, where instead of conditioning on E itself you ask how probable E would have been if T were false. Does that approach give the same verdict in the classic case and in the drug case, or do the two cases come apart?
