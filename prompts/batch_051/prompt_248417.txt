I am a high school student who has been working through game theory independently, and I have settled, at least provisionally, on a position that I want pressure-tested rather than simply rehearsed. The position is this. In a finite centipede game, two players alternate between taking a growing pot and passing it to the other player; the pot increases whenever a player passes, and the payoffs are arranged so that both players would end up better off if both kept passing to the final node, while at each node the player whose turn it is would get a larger immediate payoff by taking than by passing if the game were to end there. Under common knowledge of rationality, with players who care only about their own material payoffs, the backward-induction play — take at the first node — is the uniquely rational prescription. I am also inclined to accept the companion claim that the well-known experimental result that real subjects often pass, and therefore reach mutually better outcomes, does not by itself refute that normative claim; I am inclined to treat those results as evidence of bounded rationality, failures of common knowledge of rationality, or other-regarding preferences, not as evidence that backward induction misidentifies what a rational player should do when its assumptions in fact hold.

What I want is a careful account of what could count against this pair of claims, not a restatement of the rollback argument, which I already understand. In particular:

- Which arguments, formal results, or possible observations would be the strongest defeaters? I want them ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an account of what each one would have to establish in order to dislodge the position.
- Which assumptions of the backward-induction argument actually carry the weight — for example, common knowledge of rationality rather than merely high confidence in one another's rationality, common knowledge of the game's structure and payoffs, the finiteness of the game and the players' knowledge of that finiteness, the absence of outside options or repeated interaction, or the reduction of rational choice to maximizing one's own material payoff at each node — and which of these assumptions could fail in a way that is a live possibility rather than merely a logical one?
- Under what conditions would experimental evidence of cooperation bear on the normative claim rather than only on the descriptive claim? Is there a kind of experiment, real or merely conceivable, whose outcome ought to move me, and what would it look like?
- What would defenders of this position themselves concede counts against it, and which objections should they regard as irrelevant or question-begging?
- Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — for example, by making it always possible to explain away non-backward-induction play as a failure of one of its assumptions. If it is insulated in that way, is that a defect in a normative account of rational play, or is it exactly what such an account should look like?

For what it is worth, I would be most moved by a formal result showing that the rollback inference is not forced by the stated assumptions, or by an experimental design that carefully induces common knowledge of rationality and still produces sustained cooperation; I am less moved by the standard low-stakes laboratory results on their own.
