I'm taking a course on the epistemology of testimony, and I've been keeping a running list of my professor's verdicts on cases so that I can predict how she will rule on a new one before she discusses it next week. Her verdicts so far: when an unfamiliar stranger at a station tells you, unprompted, that the 4:15 is departing from track 8, and you believe her without checking the departure board, you can know it, assuming it is true; she denies that you first need to check this particular stranger's reliability or formulate an induction from past strangers. When a friend glances at her normally functioning wristwatch and tells you the time, you can know it without first having established a track record for that watch or for watches in general. When a colleague you have caught lying repeatedly tells you that the meeting has moved to Room 4, then even if it is true and you have no particular reason to distrust this utterance, you do not come to know it by taking his word. When an affable stranger at a dinner party confidently tells you a true but mildly surprising fact about a mutual acquaintance and answers your follow-up questions, you can know it. And when a seven-year-old tells you that there was a fire drill at school that day, you can know it.

I understand that this is supposed to be the old fight between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimonial justification, with reductionists requiring some non-testimonial reason to accept what you are told and anti-reductionists treating acceptance-by-default plus defeaters as sufficient. But her earlier cases do not cleanly pick out one of those slogans, and I want to know what rule she is actually running.

The new case is this: she arrives at her building and finds an unsigned note, in handwriting she does not recognize, taped to the elevator door saying that the north elevator is out of service and that she should use the south. She believes the note and takes the south elevator; the north elevator really is out of service. Would she say that she knew, or at least that she was justified in believing, that the north elevator was out? Please act as an epistemologist who specializes in testimony. Before committing, enumerate each rule that could rationalize all her earlier verdicts, and say what each rule predicts about the note. Then give your best guess about her verdict on the note, with the reason you prefer that reading of her. Finally, describe one further case, not one she has already been asked about, that would decisively separate the candidate rules that remain live. Start with a one-sentence bottom line, and keep the whole answer under 600 words.
