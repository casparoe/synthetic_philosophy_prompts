I have been trying to figure out whether my judgments about reparations and compensation are held together by any principled rule or merely by a jumble of intuitions. Here are the cases as I actually find them.

1. A city demolished an elderly woman's house in 1996 under a corrupt eminent-domain proceeding, paying her about a tenth of its value. She is still alive, the records are complete, and the city is solvent. I think the city should put her as nearly as possible in the position she would have been in: the value of the house at the time of the taking, plus the return she would plausibly have earned by reinvesting that value over the decades since. I am not much moved by the objection that the reinvestment path is a counterfactual construction.

2. A university excluded a man in 1968 from its only program in his chosen field under an openly discriminatory admissions rule. He is alive, and he claims the difference between his actual earnings and the career earnings he would credibly have had. The figure reconstructs a life he never got to live, but I think he has a strong claim to substantial compensation for lost earnings.

3. A museum holds a painting that was confiscated from a family on state orders in 1938 without compensation. The original owner died in 1948, and the grandchildren now ask for the return of the painting itself. I think they should get it. The passage of time and the fact that the original owner did not live to reclaim it do not weaken my judgment.

4. The same family, instead of asking for the painting, asks for a cash payment equal to what the painting would be worth today if the family had sold it in 1950 and put the proceeds into an ordinary stock portfolio, compounding until now. I would not grant this. At most I would give them the painting itself or its current market value as a stand-in for the object.

5. In 1929 a state seized a family's farm under a statute that was later repudiated as unjust. The original owner died in 1950, and the land is now the site of a public university campus. The great-grandchildren ask for the return of the land or its present value. I find their claim much weaker than the claim to the painting, and I lean toward saying they get nothing—even though the land is just as identifiable as the painting and the original wrong seems just as clear.

6. A descendant of a person enslaved in the 1840s asks for a payment calculated as the wages the ancestor would have been paid, compounded to the present. I resist this almost entirely, even though I can hear that it runs on the same structure that makes me confident in cases 1 and 2.

My working hunch has been something like this: wrongfully taken identifiable property should be returned no matter how long it has been held, while compensation for lost prosperity may be reconstructed only within the life of the wronged person and should not be projected through the later choices of heirs. But case 5 seems to break the first clause, since the land is identifiably still there, and case 4 makes me suspect the second clause is just a way of drawing a line where I happen to feel comfortable.

Is there a single principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? State it precisely, tie each of its clauses to the cases, and say what it yields for the following unadjudicated cases:

(a) In 1910 a state liquidated a perpetual trust that had been created to pay for the schooling of a family's descendants in perpetuity and that had a written, prudent investment mandate. The current generation asks the state to reconstruct the fund as it would stand now under the original mandate. Does your principle grant the claim, deny it, or distinguish something I have not noticed?

(b) The painting from case 3 was destroyed in a warehouse fire in 1940 rather than surviving in the museum. The grandchildren now ask for its 1940 value, adjusted at a reasonable rate to the present. What does your principle say, and does that vindicate or overturn my verdicts in cases 3 and 4?

(c) Two grandchildren of the farm owner in case 5 come forward separately: one asks for the return of the land itself, and the other asks for the income the farm would have produced for the family's business since 1929. Does a principled account treat these two requests differently, as my intuitions about the painting suggest, or should they rise or fall together?

Finally, be explicit about which of my six verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate, and say whether you would reject those verdicts or reject the principle. Please write your answer in American English.
