Two people in my philosophy reading group got into it over Frankfurt cases the other night, and I still can't sort it out. The setup they were both assuming is roughly this: Black wants Jones to do X, Black can detect in advance whether Jones is about to decide not to do X, and if so Black will step in and make Jones do X anyway. But Jones decides on his own and does X; Black never has to intervene.

(1) Dan said that shows the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP) is false: Jones is responsible for doing X even though he couldn't have done otherwise, because the actual sequence is just an ordinary responsible act and Black never intervenes. (2) Priya replied that Jones still had some alternative: he could have shown the sign Black was watching for, whatever it was (a blush, a hesitation, some neural signal). So there's a flicker of alternative possibility left, and PAP survives in that thinned-down form. (3) Dan said a flicker can't be what responsibility is about. A person isn't responsible for an act because his face could have twitched differently. If that's the only alternative left, the principle has been watered down to nothing. (4) Priya said that just pushes the question back: you now need an independent story about which alternatives are the ones required for responsibility, and once you supply one, PAP itself stops doing the work. The real work is being done by an actual-sequence account of responsibility, and alternatives drop out. (5) Dan said that's exactly his point. The only difference between the Frankfurt case and an ordinary case is a counterfactual intervener who never shows up. If the actual causes are the same, responsibility can't turn on something merely possible, so the ability to do otherwise isn't what matters, whatever ends up replacing it.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't, and then my housemate and I argued about it for another hour and got nowhere.
