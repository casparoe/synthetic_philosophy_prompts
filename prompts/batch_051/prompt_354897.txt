I teach a philosophy of computation seminar, and I keep seeing the same tangle in my students' work, and I realize I don't have a name for it. They're fine with pure functional code — they can trace it, compose it, even prove little properties of it. But the moment mutable state appears, a very specific confusions recurs. A student writes something like a = [1,2], then b = a, then mutates b, and is genuinely shocked that a changed. But it's not that they don't know the rule; you can tell them that assignment is not copying and they'll nod. The deeper thing is that they don't seem to have anywhere to put the idea of one and the same object that can be reached under two different names and altered while remaining itself. The same student will happily invent two distinct objects with identical contents and treat them as the same thing, or take one object under two names and treat it as two. Properties, names, and time slide around in a way that never happens for them with numbers or strings in the pure parts of the course.

I don't think this is merely a programming-language issue. It looks like a distinction they are failing to hold in place — something like the difference between a thing, a way of picking that thing out, and a description the thing happens to satisfy — with identity over time mixed in. I know the nearby vocabulary: aliasing, referential opacity, the frame problem, maybe sortals from metaphysics. But none of those seems to name quite the cognitive failure I'm describing, and in any case I'm not satisfied with a bare name. I want to know how the phenomenon should be carved up: what exactly it is, where it stops, and how it differs from those neighboring ideas. Is there an established name or literature for this specific difficulty, and if not, what would you call it?
