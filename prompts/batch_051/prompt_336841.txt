I have been reading Plato’s Laws, and I find myself returning to the discussion in Book 1 about drinking. The Athenian stranger, as I understand him, does not treat wine simply as an impairment of reason. He suggests that conditions such as anger, lust, insolence, ignorance, covetousness, and the head-turning pleasures that come from wealth or strength can be tested in a comparatively cheap and harmless way by wine, and that drinking can even serve as practice for resisting them. The thought seems to be that drunkenness temporarily weakens the rational part of the soul and thereby reveals the underlying dispositions a person normally keeps under control, without plunging them into a state where they are no longer recognizably themselves. I want to know whether this picture can survive contact with my own settled judgments about particular cases, because my gut keeps pulling in different directions.

Here are my verdicts, as I actually have them. A normally guarded colleague who gets drunk at a party and starts boasting about his achievements: I don’t think he is putting on an act, but I also don’t feel I have learned some easily usable hidden truth about him. A friend who becomes tearful and confesses long-standing anxieties while drunk: I treat the disclosures as partly true and worth following up, but I would not hold him to specific promises he made in that same state. A man who beats someone while drunk and claims he was not himself: I find the excuse nearly worthless, and in fact the alcohol seems to have shown exactly what he is capable of, though I would still judge him less harshly than someone who planned the beating sober. A recovering alcoholic who relapses and drinks a full bottle alone: I feel pity rather than reproach, and I would not treat the relapse as a revelation of his character. An elderly relative who drinks wine at structured dinner parties and becomes more animated and argumentative, but never cruel: I think that is good for him, even admirable in its way, not a failure of self-control. A person who, drunk, insults a friend and later says they did not mean it: I hold them responsible, though less than if they had said it sober, and I believe the insult would have been impossible had the disposition not already been there. Finally, a usually stingy man who, drunk, promises a large sum to a charity and retracts the next morning: I do not think the charity can reasonably hold him to the pledge, but the promise tells me something favorable about his character that I would not otherwise have guessed.

Can a single principle capture all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them? What would that principle be, and what does it say about cases I have not mentioned, such as a crime committed during a blackout or someone who deliberately drinks to loosen their own tongue during a difficult negotiation? Which of my verdicts would the best principle force me to revise? And does the Platonic idea of wine as a test of the soul earn its keep as a general guide here, or should my case-by-case judgments push against it?
