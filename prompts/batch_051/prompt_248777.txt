I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm stuck on the central relationship in my next book. The book is about a woman who loves something the wrong way, and the trouble is I can't get clear in my own head what "wrong way" means. Every draft I write just makes her excessive, or misinformed, or a little cruel, which is not the book I want. I want the wrongness to be real and structural — not that she loves too much, and not that she's factually wrong about what the thing is. She should be accurate about the object, she should be meeting her obligations, and the thing she loves should be genuinely good. And yet something in how she values it is off. I keep circling this idea people call idolatry, but I don't mean it religiously — I mean treating a finite, creaturely good as if it were the sort of thing that could be the ultimate source of meaning in a life, the one thing that must not disappoint. This is for the novel, but I don't want the philosophy to be wallpaper; the case has to earn the verdict rather than just assert it.

So here's the task. Construct a thought experiment to my specifications: a realistic scenario — a family, a farm, a small business, a clinic, whatever — where two accounts of misplaced valuing come apart.

Account A, the role account: valuing something the wrong way is assigning it the wrong role in your evaluative economy. It's treating a conditional, finite good as an unconditional, meaning-conferring source — the thing against which other things get measured, the thing whose loss wouldn't just grieve you but make the world unintelligible. On this account idolatry is a structural mistake, not a mistake about the facts and not a matter of intensity.

Account B, the error-and-consequences account: you can only value something wrongly by being factually mistaken about it, or by letting the valuing lead you to harm or neglect something you owe. If the beliefs are true, the love is strong, and nobody's neglected, then "wrong valuing" is just empty moralizing about someone else's tastes.

Build a case where A and B come apart: A says the woman's valuing is idolatrous and wrong, while B must say nothing is wrong with it. The object has to be genuinely good — not money, not fame, not a person used as a status symbol. Make the outward behavior, the intensity, and the factual accuracy all compatible with a perfectly healthy version of valuing the same thing; the difference has to be in the role the thing plays for her, and the scenario should show that rather than just tell me its there. Nothing should turn on whether God exists.

Then walk through what each account says about the case and why, tell me which feature of the scenario is doing the work and what breaks if you remove it, and how a defender of the error-and-consequences account would attack the case. And please be honest: if the two accounts as I've described them can't come apart in a realistic case without cheating, say so and fix the setup instead of handing me a rigged example.

Structure the response as:
1. The scenario (one paragraph, 80–120 words)
2. What the role account says (2–4 sentences)
3. What the error-and-consequences account says (2–4 sentences)
4. The feature doing the work (a short paragraph, including what breaks if removed)
5. Pushback from the error-and-consequences side (a short paragraph)
6. Confidence check (2–3 sentences on where the construction is softest)
