For the last several years I have kept notes on how my neighbor votes on our town's conservation commission, because she holds strong and fairly consistent views about what is worth protecting, but I have never managed to state the principle that explains them. A vote is coming up next month, and I want help predicting how she will vote and — more important — understanding what rule her record commits her to.

Her votes, as close as I can get them from memory:

1. A proposal to allow selective logging in a remote, rarely visited stand of old-growth forest. No rare species are known there and almost nobody uses it for recreation. She voted against the logging.

2. A town program to control a native beetle that was killing street trees. She voted to control the local outbreak, but only on the condition that the species not be eradicated from the region.

3. An effort to remove a non-native wildflower that had spread through a local meadow. The flower is beautiful and supports a great many bees, but she voted to remove it.

4. A proposal to cut down a long row of old, non-native sycamores in front of the library. She voted to keep them, and in discussion gave a single reason: the people of the town care about them.

Next month the commission votes on whether to keep paying for a hatchery that holds the last surviving population of a fish native to a single lake in the area. The lake has become too warm for the fish to survive or reproduce, and no return to the wild is realistically possible for decades, so the proposal is to keep it in the hatchery indefinitely. Some commissioners argue that a native species should be kept alive no matter what; others argue that a species surviving only in tanks has already lost whatever the commission is supposed to protect.

How should she vote if she wants to be consistent with her record, and which earlier vote is the closest analog? Please spell out the two or three rules that fit everything she has done, say what each one predicts about the hatchery fish, which rule her vote would amount to adopting, and which further case — one she has not faced — would cleanly separate the candidate rules if this vote, on its own, could be read either way.
