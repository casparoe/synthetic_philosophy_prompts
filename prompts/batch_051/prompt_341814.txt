For an upper-level undergraduate epistemology course, write a take-home exam question on the epistemology of coincidence and anecdote. The question should present a short invented anecdote — someone reports a striking coincidence, such as two strangers at a party sharing an unexpected connection — and ask the student to assess, using the birthday paradox and the idea of a relevant opportunity set, whether and in what way the report is evidence of a hidden cause or design rather than mere chance. The crux should be that a student who has only memorized that coincidences are more common than they seem cannot earn full marks: the question must force students to state what, if anything, would make the anecdote evidence, and what would be merely post hoc surprise. Set the question for 90 minutes, about 1,200 words, and 25 marks. Then provide the deliverable in exactly three titled sections: "Exam question" (about 150 words); "Model answer" (about 900 words), giving a full-credit response that distinguishes a specific prior prediction from a post hoc match, estimates the relevant opportunity set, and explains when a coincidence counts as evidence of a hidden cause; and "Marking scheme" (about 350 words), allocating all 25 marks to named moves and identifying two or three mark-losing errors, such as treating a large number of trials as an automatic debunking, inferring design from vividness alone, or confusing psychological surprise with evidential weight.
