I'm writing up a short "where I stand" statement for a reading group on self-examination and mental health, and before I circulate it I want a consistency check. Here's roughly what I believe:

1. Self-examination is in general a virtue — an unexamined life, even a comfortable one, is not one I'd call well-lived. I don't think this is just taste: to be genuinely your own person you have to understand what you're doing and why.

2. Rumination is a form of self-examination, not a completely different activity. It's self-focused, it tries to explain and evaluate your feelings and motives. What marks it off from healthy reflection is mostly its style: it's repetitive, it treats "why am I like this?" as a question with a final answer, and it keeps pushing toward a settled negative verdict.

3. That style is the harmful thing. Rumination maintains low mood, predicts later depression, and worsens problem-solving — and it does this even when the thoughts are mostly accurate. The problem is the process, not that the person has the facts wrong.

4. Even so, there are cases where accurate self-examination is itself demotivating. Take someone who discovers that his good work is partly driven by wanting admiration; he may stop doing the work even though he still endorses it. For people like that, more self-knowledge can make their lives go worse, not better.

5. But I also hold that self-deception is a defect even when it's useful. A person who believes flattering falsehoods about himself is less free, and "this truth would be hard to live with" is not by itself a sufficient reason not to know it.

6. And yet I think the constant demand to know yourself is often self-indulgent: someone who is endlessly auditing his own motives is usually just avoiding action. So self-examination has an optimum, and for a chronic ruminator the right advice is usually to stop trying to explain how you feel and just do the next concrete thing.

I get that there's some friction in there, but I don't want to write it off as productive nuance unless I can say where the real conflicts are. So: go through these and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other and which only seem to. Order the real tensions by severity — dont tell me they're all equally bad — and for each, say what I'd have to give up and what it would cost me. If there's one revision that would do the most to make the whole set hang together, tell me that too, but don't assume I'm committed to keeping any particular one of these.
