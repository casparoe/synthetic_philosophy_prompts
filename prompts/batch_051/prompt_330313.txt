I'm trying to figure out whether making reasoning into a competition helps or hurts the truth, so here are my gut verdicts. High school debate teams assigned arbitrary sides: fine as training, it forces you to inhabit both positions, but I don't think the round's "winner" tracks who's right. A courtroom with two lawyers each trying to win: fine, mostly; it surfaces evidence and arguments, though both sides hide what hurts them. An "argument league" with judges scoring rebuttals and docking points for concessions: not fine; I'd expect it to train people to never update. Prediction markets on real claims: good. Red-team exercises where a group is paid to attack a plan: good. Adversarial collaborations where two researchers who disagree write up what they learned together: very good. The two cases that look alike but feel different to me are the courtroom and the argument league: in both, competitors are rewarded for winning, not for being right, but I trust one and not the other. My tentative principle is that competition improves reasoning when the output goes to someone with a stake in accuracy (a jury, a bettor's wallet, a planner) rather than to spectators who want a show. But it feels too clean. What principle actually captures my verdicts? Which verdicts does it force me to drop, and what would it say about an app that ranks public debaters by viewer votes? Also say what would change your mind about the principle you settle on.
