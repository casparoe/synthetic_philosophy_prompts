Ok so I'm a senior in high school and I volunteer at this small county museum, and we're in a mess. The museum was founded in 1947 when a local guy left his whole collection to the county with a clause in his will that says: 'The said collection shall be kept together and displayed to the public in perpetuity, and no single object in it may be sold, lent, or given away.' Fast forward to now: two plaques in the collection -- everyone calls them Benin Bronzes even though I don't know if that's exactly the right term -- came from the founder's great-uncle, who was a seargent in the British force that sacked Benin City in 1897, and the accession records literally just say he 'acquired them in the field' during that campaign. Basically it's now accepted they were looted, and the Nigerian museum authorities have formally asked for them back. The board is split, and my job is to write a memo on what the will actually lets the museum do. Some board members say returning the plaques means 'giving away' collection objects, which the clause forbids. Others say returning stolen stuff isn't 'giving away,' and anyway the founder never imagined his don't-break-up-the-collection clause would be used to keep looted objects from the people they were taken from.

Act as an expert on legal interpretation and museum restitution. Start by brainstorming every plausible reading of the clause you can think of, then go through the main approaches one by one: the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the founder would have said if someone had sat him down in 1947 and told him where the plaques actually came from, and what a reasonable visitor to the museum today would expect. Tell me what each approach says about whether the museum may return the plaques, must return them, or must keep them. Then say which approach should win here and why, and if the words and the purpose cut in opposite directions, what the museum should honestly do since somebody's going to be angry either way.
