I am a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy late, and I convene a reading group of mostly retired scientists and physicians that has been arguing about whether purity and sanctity deserve any place in ethics. I would like you to construct a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on this question from what they say about concrete cases, not from the label they prefer. Anchor the tree in three cases we keep returning to: two adult siblings who have fully consensual, contraceptive sex and tell no one; a janitor who, alone and with no desire to make a statement, uses the national flag to scrub a toilet; and a restaurant that serves meat grown from human cells to fully informed, willing customers, with a consenting cell donor and no deception. Each branching node should pose a question answerable without prior philosophical vocabulary and should track one of the fault lines that genuinely divide the positions: whether an action can be wrong with no victim, whether disgust is a form of moral evidence or a biologically shaped reaction to be explained away, whether apparent desecrations wrong someone or show contempt rather than defiling the object itself, and whether purity-based considerations may ever justify constraining other people. The leaves must include at least a harm-based monism on which disgust is never a moral reason; an expressive/respect view that reconstructs taboo and desecration as wrongs against persons; a Haidt-style pluralism on which sanctity is one genuine moral foundation among several; and a sanctity realism on which some things really are sacred, defiling, or profane. As you map the logical space, note the combinations of answers that no living position occupies and say for each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; also flag any two leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate.
