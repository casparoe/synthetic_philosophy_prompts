I have been reading John Stuart Mill's On Liberty alongside recent debates about whether liberal democracies should recognize neurorights, and I would like a committed reconstruction of what Mill would say about a concrete legislative proposal rather than a survey of the different directions his principles might point. Suppose a jurisdiction is considering a law with three parts. First, neural data collected by consumer neurotechnology would become a specially protected category, requiring explicit and granular opt-in consent before companies may collect, retain, or sell it. Second, companies would be forbidden to use neural data to infer moods, attentional states, political attitudes, or other sensitive mental features for advertising, hiring, or insurance decisions. Third, the law would guarantee cognitive liberty, understood as the right of competent adults to use neurotechnologies to alter their own cognition, provided this does not directly harm others, even when the alterations are widely regarded as degrading or repugnant. The law is framed as a restraint on companies and a protection of mental self-determination, not as a paternalistic protection of individuals from their own choices.

I keep going back and forth about where Mill would land. The self-regarding/other-regarding distinction that does so much work in On Liberty seems to presuppose that what goes on inside a person's mind is private and inert until it issues in conduct; neural data crosses that boundary the moment a device records it, and a company's use of it is plainly a social act. Does that mean the first two parts of the law fall on the other-regarding side of the line and are permissible restraints on how one party may treat another, or is the use of such data simply a consequence of the consumer's own voluntary use of a device, so that restricting it would be the kind of paternalism Mill rejects? Mill's discussions of commerce, contracts, and dangerous articles suggest he thought society may regulate how things are sold and how they are used against others without thereby claiming jurisdiction over a person's private good; does that analogy carry over to the sale and use of neural data? And on the third part, is cognitive liberty simply an application of the sovereignty he assigns to individuals over their own minds, or does the prospect of deliberately dulling or rewiring one's own higher faculties create a real strain, given how central the cultivation of those faculties is to his defense of liberty in the first place?

Give me your best single reading of Mill: a definite verdict on the law as a whole and on each part, with the reasoning that produces it. Do not give me a menu of possible Mills; take a position and defend it, and tell me where you think the framework is doing the most work and where it starts to break under the weight of neural data.
