I run a philosophy club at a high school, about 15 kids aged 16-17, one hour, and we've spent the last two meetings doing ordinary expected value with poker chips. Next I want to sneak up on Pascal's wager, but if I say the name someone will roll their eyes and we'll get the usual argument about whether you can choose to believe. So I want an hour-long game that makes them build the argument themselves and then find the cracks in it from the inside. Design something runnable: teams, rules, a payoff scheme using chips or candy rather than real money, and a timeline. I'd like three movements if you can manage it. The first sets up finite choices among invented 'ways of life' with hidden payoffs so they get comfortable with expected value. The second introduces an option with an infinite or unbounded payoff and a tiny or unknown probability, arranged so that someone in the room eventually says the probability doesn't matter because the payoff is infinite. The third should make them hit the problems themselves: rival options can claim the same infinite payoff, new options can always be written down and force a re-choice, the probabilities are neither known nor comparable, and the whole choice becomes hostage to which options happen to be on the menu. Then a debrief that maps those moments onto Pascal's actual argument and its standard objections, with no lecture up front and no mention of Pascal until after. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the game can't reproduce — actual infinite utility, the threat of real hell, the fact that Pascal has one very particular god in mind, and the gap between deciding something is a good bet and actually believing it. I should be able to run the whole thing with stuff I can print out or buy at a dollar store.
