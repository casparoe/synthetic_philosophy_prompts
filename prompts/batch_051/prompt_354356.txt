I'm a philosophy professor teaching a unit on the attention economy this fall, and I keep tripping over what we're even claiming when we say a platform "captures" or "steals" attention. William James gets me partway there. In the "Attention" chapter of The Principles of Psychology (1890), after describing the zoned-out, vacant state that attention has to awaken us from, he writes:

"The abolition of this condition is what we call the awakening of the attention. One principal object comes then into the focus of consciousness, others are temporarily suppressed. The awakening may come about either by reason of a stimulus from without, or in consequence of some unknown inner alteration; and the change it brings with it amounts to a concentration upon one single object with exclusion of aught besides, or to a condition anywhere between this and the completely dispersed state."

That two-route picture—awakening by an external stimulus versus by some inner alteration—feels like the whole persuasive-design problem in miniature, but I want a working definition of attention built on it, not just a gloss. Build a definition that gets these cases right: a student working through a proof so absorbed she doesn't hear her phone buzz; a driver's eyes yanked to a flashing billboard on a dark road; a guy 40 minutes into an infinite-scroll feed, swiping steadily, who later can't recall a thing he saw; a platform dashboard counting "engaged minutes"; a problem gambler who has deleted the app but still finds himself reaching for his phone every evening without deciding to. I'm fairly sure the first case is attention and the dashboard metric isn't, and I honestly have no settled view on the middle three. Give the definition, run it through all five, tell me where it surprises you, and say what it does with James's "anywhere between" degrees.
