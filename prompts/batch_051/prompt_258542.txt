An article I read gave the following account of Hempel's ravens paradox. Observing a black raven is evidence for the claim that all ravens are black; observing a green apple seems to say nothing either way. But "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, "all non-black things are non-ravens." A green apple is a non-black thing that is not a raven, and so it is precisely the kind of observation that counts in favor of the contrapositive. Since logically equivalent claims must be supported by the same evidence, the green apple turns out to be evidence that all ravens are black — as is any non-black non-raven one bothers to inspect, which in practice is nearly every non-black object. According to the article, Hempel drew the bold conclusion. He allegedly held that a green apple confirms the raven hypothesis in the same sense, and to about the same degree, as a black raven does, and he is said to have dismissed the feeling that this is absurd as a mere psychological illusion caused by confusing the truth of a hypothesis with its confirmation.

I can follow the early steps, but the final two claims — the same-sense-and-same-degree conclusion and the diagnosis of the illusion — feel stronger than anything the argument by itself establishes, and I do not trust my ability to say exactly where the slide occurs.
