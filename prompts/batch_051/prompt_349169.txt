A mate of mine has got himself into a tangle reading Woodward on interventionism, and now I'm in it too. He's cobbled together an arguement that seems to show the whole interventionist approach to causation is empty, which is more than he actually wanted to prove. Here's the argument roughly as he gave it to me:

(1) Interventionism says X causes Y just in case there is some possible intervention on X that would change Y, or change the probability of Y.
(2) But an intervention on X with respect to Y is itself defined as a change to X that switches X off from its other causes, and that influences Y only through X rather than through some route that bypasses X.
(3) The definition in (2) is doing the real work, and it plainly uses causal notions twice over: talk of switching X off from its "other causes" and talk of "influences Y only through X" both presuppose facts about what causes what.
(4) So the whole account is circular: cause gets defined in terms of interventions, while interventions get defined in terms of causes.
(5) A circular definition doesn't tell us anything new about what causation is; it just shifts the problem from one place to another without solving it.
(6) Therefore interventionism cannot be a genuine account of causation, and using manipulations to test causal claims in science is just helping yourself to the very thing you were supposed to be discovering.

I'd much rather give up almost anything than accept (6), because carefully manipulated experiments obviously do teach us about causes, and the talk of invariance under interventions seems to capture something real about how that works. I also wouldn't like to give up (1), since that's the core of the view. But after that I can't see what's left to reject. Where does this go wrong, if it does? Wich step should I reject, and what would rejecting it cost me, so I don't end up quietly abandoning interventionism itself?
