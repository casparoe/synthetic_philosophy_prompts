Analyze the hot hand controversy in basketball as a two-way case study in the three leading interpretations of probability: frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity. Do not retell the history or give me a literature review; take the following facts as given. The standard 1985 study by Gilovich, Vallone, and Tversky examined shooting data from NBA games and a controlled shooting experiment and found no evidence that the outcomes of successive shots cluster more than chance predicts, concluding that belief in the hot hand is a cognitive illusion driven by people's inability to recognize random sequences. A later reanalysis by Miller and Sanjurjo showed that the customary way of estimating the conditional probability of a hit given k previous hits is systematically biased in short sequences, so that genuine streakiness can look like independence; this reopened the question. Subsequent work has been mixed: some studies report a small hot-hand effect, often restricted to particular shots, contexts, or a subset of players, while others do not.

For each interpretation, say exactly what the hot-hand claim has to mean under that interpretation, where the probability is supposed to live, and what would settle the question in principle. Then:

- For frequentism, identify the relevant reference classes: all shots by a player, shots after k hits, shots from the same spot or under the same conditions, shots by similarly situated players. What does the selection-bias correction change about what the original data show? And can a frequentist even say that this player is hot right now, as opposed to merely making a statement about a class of similar moments?
- For subjectivism, distinguish the player's degree of belief, the coach's, the observer's, and the statistician's. Is the original accusation of a fallacy a claim about irrational updating, or a claim about an unreasonable prior over nonstationary shooting processes? What should a Bayesian observer have done with the 1985 findings, and how should the Miller–Sanjurjo correction alter that?
- For propensity, specify what would have to be true in the world for a player to have an objective time-varying chance of hitting. What would count as evidence that a propensity is present rather than a constant underlying probability whose streaks arise by chance, and does the corrected frequency data by itself establish anything about a propensity? What would a propensity theorist need to add—mechanism, controls, predictions about when hot streaks begin and end—to escape merely re-describing the observed frequencies?

Then run the case in the other direction. Say which features of the controversy each interpretation explains well, which it distorts or leaves out, and what the episode shows about the interpretation itself. In particular, I want to know whether the measurement artifact is a problem specifically for frequentism or a problem for anyone who uses observed frequencies as evidence; whether the durability of the hot-hand intuition is a problem for subjectivism or just a fact it predicts; and whether propensity theory is doing indispensable work in this debate or only supplying a word for what people already believed.

Finally, separate what is now reasonably settled about the hot hand itself—such as the existence of the original measurement bias—from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading of the evidence as the consensus.
