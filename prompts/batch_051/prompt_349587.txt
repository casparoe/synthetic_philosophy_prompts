In my civics class we were assigned to design a rule for what happens when a constitution runs out of specified officeholders—for example, after a disaster, a disputed election, or a coup attempt leaves no one with a clear legal claim to govern. The mechanism I want to stress-test is this:

A country’s new constitution provides that if the presidency, the legislature, and the constitutional court are simultaneously unable to act—because all seats are vacant, all members are incapacitated, or all bodies are deadlocked beyond their legal quorum rules—a caretaker council automatically forms. It is made up of the mayors of the thirty largest municipalities by population. The council may not amend the constitution, dissolve the state bureaucracy, or call elections. It may issue decrees with the force of law, but each decree expires after 180 days unless a newly convened legislature ratifies it. If the council fails to act, existing law stays in force, but taxes cannot be collected and public spending is frozen except for debt payments and emergency services. The council is immune from ordinary courts for acts within its authority, but any citizen can challenge a decree before a panel of retired judges, who may suspend the decree but may not replace it with one of their own.

Walk through how this would actually behave, not just whether it is a good idea in the abstract. What does it do to the incentives of mayors before and during a crisis, especially since the rule selects them by municipal population? What happens to the bureaucracy, the military, creditors, and political parties when they see this council take over? Where would the mechanism be gamed first, and can its failure modes be fixed without giving the caretaker council a path to permanent power?
