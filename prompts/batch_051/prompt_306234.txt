Take on the role of a political philosopher who teaches a seminar on Rawls and social movements, and think through this carefully. I got into an argument with a friend about Occupy Wall Street: he kept insisting the whole thing was basically envy — middle-class kids angry they did not get to be bankers — and I found myself defending the protestors. I have been rereading A Theory of Justice since, and I am struck by how much machinery Rawls devotes to exactly this dismissive move, probably because he wanted to head it off. As I remember it, he distinguishes envy proper (hostility toward a greater good that does not actually subtract from your own goods) from resentment (a genuinely moral response to being wronged), and then he carves out a third category, excusable envy, for cases where the gap between the worse-off and the better-off is so large, and social life so constantly makes the worse-off feel their inferior status, that the envy is not morally blameworthy even though for Rawls it remains a vice and is not by itself a claim of justice.

I want to use Occupy as a case study for that framework, not to relitigate whether the movement succeeded. Treat the participants as the agents and their publicly expressed grievances — the 99 percent framing, the anger about the bank bailouts, the emphasis on inequality as such rather than on any discrete theft or broken promise — as the evidence. First, reconstruct the Rawlsian distinction in your own words and state, as precisely as you can, the criteria that would let someone classify a real episode as resentment versus excusable envy versus plain envy. Second, apply that test to Occupy, flagging where the evidence is too thin to be decisive. Third, say what the framework implies the protestors would have needed to do for their complaints to register as claims of justice rather than as excusable envy. Finally, run the analysis in the opposite direction: what does Rawls's framework illuminate about Occupy, and what does it miss or force — for example, the movement's own insistence that it was about justice and democracy rather than envy, or its famous vagueness about policy demands? Does the episode make excusable envy look like a useful diagnostic category, or does it show that the category gives observers an easy way to dismis principled complaints as mere emotion? Brainstorm a few possible readings before settling on your final answer.
