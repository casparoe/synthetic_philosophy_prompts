I've been arguing with a birder friend, and we've gotten tangled in a distinction neither of us can draw cleanly. She claims that after she learned to recognize a certain sparrow by its faint facial stripes, the birds started to look different to her: the face pattern is more vivid and the markings seem to pop out, and she insists it's not that she looks more carefully but that the visual experience itself changed. I think it's much more likely that the new knowledge changed where she points her eyes and how she parses the pattern afterward, while the early visual signal stayed exactly as it was. So we're in the territory people call cognitive penetration of perception: whether beliefs, concepts, and expertise can reach down into perceptual experience itself, rather than just steering attention, eye movements, and later judgment.

I'd like you to design the strongest experiment you can to test whether acquiring a recognitional concept like that literally alters early vision, as oppossed to merely guiding attention and post-perceptual interpretation. Be concrete about the stimuli, the task, and what you'd measure. What pattern of results would push you toward "perception itself changed" versus "attention and judgment did the work"?
