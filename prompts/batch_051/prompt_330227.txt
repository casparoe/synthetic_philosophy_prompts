Here is an argument intended to show that the ordinary idea of changing an object in place is incoherent. Consider a record `acct` with a field `balance`, initially 0, and this statement:

```
acct.balance = acct.balance + 100
```

(1) Before the assignment, the expression `acct.balance` evaluates to 0; after the assignment, it evaluates to 100.
(2) On the ordinary understanding, the assignment changes `acct` rather than replacing it: the object referred to by `acct` before the assignment is identical to the object referred to by `acct` after it.
(3) If A and B are identical, then A and B have exactly the same properties; a thing cannot differ from itself.
(4) The object before the assignment has the property of having a balance of 0; the object after the assignment has the property of having a balance of 100.
(5) From (2) and (3), the object before the assignment and the object after it must share all properties; from (4), they must differ in at least one. Contradiction.
(6) Therefore the ordinary notion of in-place update cannot be right. No statement can alter an object while preserving its identity. What programmers call mutation is really the production of a new object; the persisting object is a convenient fiction.

The conclusion is hard to accept, yet each step looked plausible. Work through the argument and say whether it is valid. If it is not valid, identify the first step that fails and explain what that failure shows about how property-ascription must be understood when we reason about state over time. If you think it is sound, state what we would have to give up in our ordinary talk about mutable objects.
