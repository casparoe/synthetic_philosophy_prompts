I keep going back and forth on a line of argument I sketched out after a seminar on Frankfurt-style counterexamples, and I would like a step-by-step diagnosis of it. The standard claim is that Frankfurt cases refute the principle of alternative possibilities — the principle that a person is morally responsible for what she did only if she could have done otherwise. But I suspect the cases only seem to work because we individuate the action too coarsely, and that once we individuate it finely enough, an alternative possibility is still there. My reconstruction goes like this:

(1) In the standard Frankfurt case, Jones is morally responsible for pressing the button even though Black, the counterfactual intervener, would have caused him to press it had he shown any sign of not doing it on his own.
(2) What Jones is responsible for, in the case, is not the bare outward button-pressing; it is pressing-the-button-on-his-own — the action understood as an exercise of his own unforced agency.
(3) Jones could not avoid pressing the button simpliciter, since Black stands ready to produce the press if necessary. But he could avoid pressing-it-on-his-own: had the actual course of his deliberation gone differently, Black would have taken over, and Jones would have pressed the button only because he was made to do so, not on his own.
(4) So Jones retains an alternative possibility with respect to exactly that for which he is held responsible. He could either press-the-button-on-his-own, or he could press-the-button-only-because-compelled — the same outward conduct, but not the same action.
(5) Therefore the Frankfurt case does not give us responsibility without alternative possibilities. It only shows that the principle of alternative possibilities should be applied to the fine-grained act for which the agent is responsible, rather than to the coarse-grained bodily behavior. The original principle survives.

I have the nagging sense that step (3) or step (4) is quietly substituting something else for the ordinary notion of "could have done otherwise," but I cannot make the charge stick precisely. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of that step does it fail? If you think it is valid, tell me which premises you would resist and whether step (5) overstates what the argument establishes.
