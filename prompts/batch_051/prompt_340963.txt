I'm taking a philosophy of promising seminar and we just spent a week on oaths, and there's an argument I've been turning over since class that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the exit. (1) An oath is supposed to add binding force on top of the underlying promise — swearing by something sacred makes you more bound than you were before. (2) But the classic mythological case is strange: the gods themselves swear by the river Styx rather than by their own names, because even they need the penalty attached to that river — forswear such an oath and something bad actually happens to you. (3) So whatever extra force an oath has comes from the sanction or enforcer it invokes, not from the act of swearing itself. (4) Then an oath is just a statement of intent plus a threat, and it binds only as well as the enforcement does. (5) That seems to prove too much: it makes a promise with a big enough external penalty count as an oath, and makes a solemn oath with no enforcement empty, which is the opposite of how we actually treat someone who swears on a dead parent's grave. (6) So swearing never adds anything, and oaths are a kind of trick we play on ourselves. I'd much rather keep the idea that oaths do something real than accept (6), but I can't tell where the chain breaks. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the rest of what we believe about promising?
