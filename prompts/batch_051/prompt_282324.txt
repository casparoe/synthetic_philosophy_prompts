Okay so I just had a long argument with a friend about the Confederate lost cause stuff and I think I want to turn it into a class thing. He kept saying the Cornerstone Speech is a smoking gun that makes all the “heritage not hate” stuff impossible to take seriously. Which, yes, but also I think the speech is more useful as a problem about how national myths get built around an inconvenient text. There’s this passage in Alexander H. Stephens’s Cornerstone Speech, delivered in Savannah on March 21, 1861, where he says the founding generation expected slavery to just sort of fade away, and then he says:

Those ideas, however, were fundamentally wrong. They rested upon the assumption of the equality of races. This was an error. It was a sandy foundation, and the idea of a Government built upon it—when the “storm came and the wind blew, it fell.”

That’s not the super-quoted cornerstone line, but it’s the passage I’d want the activity to turn on because it is so explicit about the Confederacy being a correction to the founders’ supposed mistake.

Anyway, I want a runnable activity for a college seminar, 18 students, 2.5 hours, on historical memory. Probably undergrads, mixed history and philosophy. The goal is for participants to come away seeing how a national myth gets assembled through selection, redescription, emphasis, and embarrassment—not just through lying. The idea should show up in what they do, not in what the facilitator says afterwards.

Could you design the activity? I’m imagining something where they have to produce a small piece of public history—maybe a museum label or a memorial plaque or an exhibit blurb—that has to include or respond to the Stephens passage. But don’t feel locked into that if you have a better setup. Include roles or rules, a timeline, and a debrief. The debrief should map moments in the activity onto the mechanics of the Lost Cause and say what predictably goes wrong when it’s run and how to handle it. Also be honest about which feature of actual lost-cause myths the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator has to supply afterward.

One more thing: I don’t want it to become a role-play where people pretend to be Confederates. The discomfort should come from the structural problem—what do you do with a founding text you want to honor but can’t fully quote—not from inhabiting a racist position.
