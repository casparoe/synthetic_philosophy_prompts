I have a standing rule for how I handle the sacred when it enters an argument. When someone objects to a claim, a text, or an action by saying that it is blasphemous, sacrilegious, or offensive to what a community holds sacred, I assign that objection zero weight. I bracket the sense of violation and ask only whether the claim is false or the action wrong on ordinary evidential or moral grounds; if no such ground is offered, I treat the objection as empty. I apply this in practical cases: a student objecting to a set text, a colleague objecting to an exhibition, a public row over a cartoon or a remark about a sacred site. My rationale is that felt violation is cheap to invoke, often tracks group membership more than truth, and lets a community veto inquiry simply by being wounded. A colleague I respect says this is my own piety wearing the costume of critical thinking: a widespread sense that something must not be said can be real evidence that it should not be said, at least here and now, and my rule converts my lack of reverence into an epistemic virtue. I no longer know whether the rule is defensible. If it is partly defensible, is there a principled rate, or set of conditions, at which offensiveness to the sacred should count in my deliberations, short of deferring to whoever is most easily wounded? And am I entitled to apply the rule as I currently do?
