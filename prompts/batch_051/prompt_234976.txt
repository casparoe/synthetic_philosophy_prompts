I want you to reconstruct the philosophy that a piece of current technology practice presupposes. The practice: a major AI lab ships a general-purpose chat assistant that answers factual questions in a fluent, declarative style indistinguishable from a knowledgeable human's, with no calibrated confidence signal. The lab knows, and labels, the system's fluent falsehoods as hallucination — a failure mode to be reduced, not a lie to be attributed. The lab ships it anyway: the answer is presented directly, with the user made responsible for checking anything important, and the interface warns against trusting it only for narrow high-stakes uses. Nowhere is any person, model, or institution marked as the asserter of what the interface says.

What theory of linguistic communication does this practice presuppose? Specifically, does it presuppose that the system's output is not assertion at all — merely text from which users may extract what is true — or that it is assertion but of a special, unowned kind? What does the practice commit the lab to about the distinction between lying, error, and bullshit, in the now-standard sense following Harry Frankfurt: speech produced without concern for whether what is said is true? Can the lab hold simultaneously that the model cannot lie because it is not a speaker, and that hallucination describes a mere error of fact? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and say where the design is genuinely underdetermined between rival views. Then spell out one concrete change to the product and one change to the terms under which it is offered that would follow if the lab instead treated the system's answers as speech for which someone is accountable.
