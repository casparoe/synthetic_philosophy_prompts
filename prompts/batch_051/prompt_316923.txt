I am a PhD student in political theory, and in the distributive-justice literature I have begun to notice a manoeuvre that I cannot name, though I suspect it must be a familiar one. It concerns two ways of characterising what is wrong with an unequal distribution. On the first characterisation, the gap between the better off and the worse off is itself the thing to be regretted: a wider difference is, in itself, a worse state of affairs. On the second, the gap carries no independent weight; what matters is only that some people are badly off, and that an improvement to someone badly off counts for more than the same-sized improvement to someone well off. In almost every case that people actually discuss—a growing economy in which the rich gain faster than the poor, a new treatment that reaches the better-off first—these two characterisations issue in identical verdicts. They part company only in the sort of case where the gap narrows because the better off are made worse off while nobody is made better off: there, the first characterisation seems committed to saying that something good has occurred, whereas the second denies it.

What I keep witnessing is this. A writer who begins from the claim that widening differences are themselves objectionable, when confronted with that unwelcome implication, retreats to the claim that only the position of the worse off matters—and then, once the troublesome case has left the page, returns to the language of gaps and widening differences as though no retreat had occurred. The two views are being run together without any acknowledgement of the difference, and the case in which they would diverge is quietly set aside as a curiosity. A bare label for either view would not settle anything for me, and I am aware that the standard objection to the gap-based view has a recognised name. What I want to know is whether this dialectical pattern itself has an established name: the practice of slipping between two standards whose verdicts coincide in nearly every case anyone bothers to discuss, borrowing the intuitive strengths of each while deflecting the costs of either, and treating the divergence cases as too recherché to be probative. If no such name exists, I would welcome a proposal, together with a definition that marks its edges—above all, how the pattern differs from changing one's mind under pressure, from a motte-and-bailey, and from the innocent fact that two distinct positions can recommend the same policies across the range of cases that are actually debated.
