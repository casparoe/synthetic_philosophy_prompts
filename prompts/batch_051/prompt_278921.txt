I follow a rule for distinguishing manipulation from legitimate persuasion, and I want to know whether the rule itself is defensible. The rule is this: when someone tries to change my mind, I ask whether the considerations they offer would still move me if I encountered them stripped of our relationship, their tone, and their evident investment in getting me to agree. If I judge that the appeal depends on trust, liking, or the fact that they know me well enough to press on my particular history, I classify it as manipulation—or at least as non-rational influence—and I set it aside until I can reconstruct the argument in my own words as a bare sequence of considerations that would make sense to a stranger. Recently I applied the rule to a close friend who urged me to turn down a job. She knew the employer, she knew my temperament, and much of what she said took the form of reminders about my own past rather than new arguments. I could not find anything false in what she said, and in the end I still set her advice aside partly because I could not convert it into the kind of anonymous, disinterested case my rule demands. My worry is that my rule quietly defines manipulation in a way that makes any exercise of relationship-dependent judgment look suspect, as though persuasion is legitimate only if it could work on someone with no history with me. Is the rule a sound way to protect myself from manipulation, or is it a disguised demand that all legitimate persuasion be impersonally valid? Under what conditions should trust and knowledge of one another count as legitimate parts of persuasion rather than as pressure, and is there a version of the rule that keeps its protection without treating personal influence as a contaminant?
