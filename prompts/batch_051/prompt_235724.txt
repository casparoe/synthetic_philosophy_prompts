I hold the following view about cultural value. A language, tradition, art form, or similar cultural practice has no value over and above the value it has for persons: for those who currently participate in it or care about it, for future people who would be enriched by it, and more broadly for anyone whose well-being it touches. On my view, saying that a cultural form is valuable in itself or as an end is a way of saying that persons appropriately value it for its own sake; if every relation of that kind to persons were removed, the value would be removed with it. Consequently, when a language dies or a tradition disappears, the loss is fully accounted for by the losses to such persons, and if no one was or would be made worse off by the disappearance, nothing of value has vanished.

Tell me what would count against this view. I want the considerations sorted from fatal to merely awkward: the strongest arguments and cases against it, which of them a defender of this position should concede weigh against it, and which should be dismissed. In particular, engage with (a) the claim that some cultural forms have final value — value as ends rather than merely as means — even when no actual or future person is affected; (b) the case of a practice known to only a few people whose current bearers are indifferent to its survival and that no future person is likely to take up; and (c) the worry that the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by appeals to hypothetical future appreciators. End with an honest assessment of whether the view, in its best-qualified form, risks anything at all, and whether that insulation is a problem.
