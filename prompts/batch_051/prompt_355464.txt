ok so I'm in a climate ethics seminar and we keep running into the same move: it's fine to discount future climate damages because our descendants will be way richer than us, so a dollar of avoided future harm counts for less than a dollar spent now. Thats the argument I want to stress-test, because something about it has been bugging me.

The move seems to lean pretty hard on how the big climate-economy models handle damages. As far as I can tell in the models we looked at, warming shaves some percentage off GDP in a given year but leaves the underlying growth rate alone. So no matter how bad warming gets, future people still end up richer than us—just less richer than they would have been. But we read some newer empirical stuff (summarized for us, not the original papers) suggesting heat and climate shocks don't just lower output in a bad year; they slow the growth rate itself, because they hit learning, capital accumulation, migration, institutions, all the compounding stuff. If damages compound through slower growth, then under high-warming scenarios future people could be poorer than us, not richer.

So does the 'discount because they'll be richer' argument survive that? It feels like its main empirical premise is exactly the thing the new damage functions undermine. But maybe it can be repaired: maybe you only need growth to stay positive in expectation for the relevant population; maybe the real work was always being done by pure time preference and the growth term was a side detail; or maybe some climate damages should get a much lower—or even negative—discount rate and the standard framework just has no way to say that.

There's also a distributional version of the same worry. Even if future average income goes up, the people actually dying from heat or losing their land to sea level rise might be poorer than us, not richer. Does the argument still go through if you apply it to the relevant victims instead of the global average?

Where I'm stuck: am I just relocating the debate from one parameter (the discount rate) to another (the growth forecast)? If the argument is dead, what should replace it? And if it survives, what exactly does it need to be true, and is that actually true under the newer damage functions?
