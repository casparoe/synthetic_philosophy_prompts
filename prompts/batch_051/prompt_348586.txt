I'm taking a course on collective decision-making and we've been going through puzzles where a group has to decide something by majority vote on several sub-questions, and I've found that my own gut reactions don't line up. You're an expert on social choice and group epistemology — please help me find the principle behind my verdicts, or tell me which verdicts I have to abandon. Start by brainstorming a few candidate principles before you commit.

Here are my cases. (1) Three judges decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract; liability requires both a valid contract and a breach. All three say yes on both and yes on liability. No issue: the group believes the premises and the conclusion. (2) A second panel splits: Alba says yes/yes and votes liable; Bao says yes/no and votes not liable; Chen says no/yes and votes not liable. A majority says there was a contract (Alba and Bao), a majority says there was a breach (Alba and Chen), but a majority votes not liable (Bao and Chen). My verdict is that the not-liable outcome is the right legal result — courts vote on the outcome, full stop — but the panel's collective reasoning feels plainly incoherent: the group seems to accept both premises yet deny what follows from them. (3) Same three judges as in (2), but under a different rule: the panel first votes on each issue and the outcome is then derived from those votes. Now the result flips to liable, since both issues pass. I find that unacceptable too: whether the defendant is liable shouldn't depend on whether you poll the judges on the conclusion or on the premises, when nothing about the judges has changed. (4) A five-member hiring committee requires all three of strong research, good teaching, and good fit; all five members vote reject, but if they vote seperately on each criterion, each criterion has a majority in favor (three of five on research, three on teaching, three on fit). Here I don't feel the scruple from (2) at all — I just think the committee should reject and the criterion votes are a kind of trick. But structurally it looks like the same phenomenon, just larger, so maybe I'm being inconsistent.

The best principle I can articulate is that a group's judgment is just the majority view on each proposition, and the group thereby believes the logical consequences of those summary judgments. But that gives liable in (2) and hire in (4), which I said were wrong. So maybe the group's decision is just the majority on the final question, with no group-level beliefs at all. That seems to get the verdicts right, but it feels like a bullet I don't want to bite: it amounts to saying there is no inconsistency when a panel announces both premises while denying the conclusion, because the "we" in "we find" doesn't refer to anything in particular. Is there a third principle, or must I bite that bullet? And what does your preferred principle predict for the case I keep going back and forth on: a grant panel must fund a proposal if the research is either highly novel or highly feasible; Dr. A says novel yes, feasible no, and votes fund; Dr. B says novel no, feasible yes, and votes fund; Dr. C says novel no, feasible no, and votes reject. The direct vote is 2–1 to fund, but the panel's written findings would read "the board finds the research is not novel and not feasible, therefore it funds." Must the board fund, reject, or rewrite its findings, and why?
