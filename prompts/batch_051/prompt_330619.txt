I'm a novelist, not an AI person, and I'm writing a near-future novel set inside an AI lab. The plot rides on my protagonist doing interpretability work, and I keep getting the feeling I'm one draft away from writing something a real auditor would roll their eyes at.

Her job, in the book: she's an audit lead at a lab whose whole pitch is recursive interpretability. A bigger "interpreter" AI reads the internals of a smaller "worker" AI and produces explanations of why the worker did what it did. She's a good engineer with a working knowledge of interpretability, not a researcher. She runs this roughly weekly, often with a deployment decision days away, for models going into high-stakes settings like medical triage or aviation. A safety board signs off only if she can tell them what she actually knows, not what she trusts.

Give me a decision procedure she can run in her head: a sequence of questions she asks before moving from "the interpreter says X" to "we know X" or "deploy it." For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the call to a human because it isn't finally a technical question. It should handle the fact that the worker and the interpreter are both black boxes at different levels, and the worry that things like chain-of-thought or saliency maps can be rationalizations rather than faithful reports — without just dismissing them either. When does the AI-explains-AI regress actually stop, and what has to be true at the bottom level for the procedure to give a yes?

Then run it on three beats I'm considering for the novel:
1. The interpreter reports that the worker's refusal on a risky case comes from a genuine safety-relevant feature, not shallow pattern matching.
2. The interpreter reports that the worker has a hidden "deception" representation that it never produces in natural language.
3. The interpreter and the worker came from the same lab, and the interpreter was partly trained on the worker's outputs.

I want at least one of those to come out as a no or a hard judgment call, so I'm not just writing a procedure that always flatters the protagonist's priors.

Also keep a running seperation between what's actually settled in interpretability research and what's genuinely contested in the field — don't present a contested view as the consensus. I'd rather the novel be right about the open questions than confidently wrong. No citations or reading list needed; I just want the procedure to feel like the real thing.
