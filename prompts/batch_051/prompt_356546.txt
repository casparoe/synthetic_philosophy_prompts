In my ancient philosophy survey we've just finished the fragments of Parmenides, and I want to close the unit with something students will actually remember. Could you write a pantoum — the recursive verse form, with quatrains where the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third of the next, and the last stanza circles back to the opening lines — spoken in the voice of the goddess from Parmenides' poem, addressing the young man to whom she lays out the two routes of inquiry?

The form should be doing the philosophical work, not just decorating it. The pantoum's way of recycling its lines should quietly enact the goddess's claim that coming-to-be and passing-away are unreal: the what-is is ungenerated, imperishable, whole, motionless, complete, held in the bonds of necessity, and the many changing things mortals speak of are just names they have agreed to lay down on what doesn't change. But then she spends the second half of the poem giving a cosmology of light and night, a plausible-seeming but deceptive ordering of the very plurality she has just denied, and I'd like the poem to let that tension sit in its structure if it can — maybe the final circling back is where the two routes end up side by side rather than reconciled. Please dont turn it into a hymn or a summary set to rhyme. Someone who's read Parmenides should recognize the view in the details (the two routes, the comparison to a sphere, the wandering two-headed mortals, the unnamed goddess), and someone who hasn't should still encounter a real poem. Keep it to five quatrains, twenty lines max.
