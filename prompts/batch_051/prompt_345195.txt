I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and a visiting speaker recently left our reading group with an argument that seems to show that the standard reading of Nozick's entitlement theory proves too much. I find the individual steps hard to deny, and yet the conclusion strikes me as impossible to accept. The argument, as I have reconstructed it, runs as follows.

(1) According to the entitlement theory, leaving aside rectification for past injustice and stipulating that the original acquisitions were just, a distribution of holdings is just if and only if it has arisen through a sequence of just transfers; no feature of the resulting pattern matters as such.
(2) A voluntary exchange, gift, or bequest made by someone whose holdings are just is always a just transfer, regardless of the pattern that results. This is meant to be the lesson of the Wilt Chamberlain case: if a great many spectators each freely hand over a small sum to watch a celebrated athlete play, and the sums accumulate in his hands, then the resulting concentration is just, and any principle that required a more equal pattern would be forbidding an innocent liberty.
(3) Since each individual transfer preserves justice, any finite sequence of voluntary transfers preserves justice. The length of the sequence and the extremity of the eventual pattern make no difference, for at every stage the distribution is just and the next transfer is voluntary.
(4) So if, beginning from a just distribution, a society passes through a long chain of uncoerced sales, gifts, and bequests until a single family owns all the land and everyone else, owning no land, can only contract for work on terms set by that family, the resulting manorial situation is just. A later state that expropriated the land to restore wider ownership would act unjustly.
(5) That consequence seems to discredit the theory. An adequate theory of justice must be able to condemn, or at least permit the correction of, the voluntary reconstruction of a feudal order.
(6) Therefore the Chamberlain lesson is not acceptable in the unrestricted form given in (2), or the entitlement conception in (1) must be abandoned.

I should add that I find the original Chamberlain case genuinely persuasive and would like to keep the anti-pattern lesson it is taken to establish, but I cannot accept (4). I can feel the conclusion outrunning the premises, yet each attempt I make to resist it seems to reintroduce exactly the pattern-based restriction that the Chamberlain case was supposed to exclude. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
