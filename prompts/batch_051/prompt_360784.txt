I have been turning over a parallel between Moore's paradox and an ordinary fact about replicated distributed systems, and I cannot tell whether it is a real structural identity or only a misleading likeness in wording.

Moore's paradox, stated loosely, is that a person can hold two claims that are logically consistent and even jointly true — the state of affairs p obtains, and the person does not believe that p obtains — and yet the person cannot coherently assert the conjunction: p, but I do not believe that p. The familiar diagnosis is that assertion expresses, or is governed by a norm requiring, the asserter's own belief; so the first conjunct, as asserted, already declares belief that p, and the second conjunct denies it. The absurdity attaches specifically to the first-person, present-tense case.

Now consider a replicated key-value store arranged with a leader and followers, of the kind that aims at linearizable reads. A client writes a new value x over an old value y. The leader commits the write. Before one particular follower has received or applied the new entry, a second client sends a read to that follower. A linearizable system may not silently hand the stale value y back. One common arrangement is for the follower to forward the read to the leader and relay the leader's answer to the client — returning x even though the follower's own local state machine still records y. If we treat the reply sent to the client as an assertion and the follower's local state as its belief, then the follower has just issued something of the form: the current value is x, but I do not believe that the current value is x. Moore's paradox says a single subject cannot coherently say this; the follower appears to do exactly that, and its reply is not absurd but correct.

I suspect the parallel gives out somewhere, but I want to know exactly where. In particular:

- Is the follower really asserting the first conjunct at all, or is it merely relaying the leader's answer, the way a secretary who passes along a message does not thereby assert the message? If it is only relaying, Moore's paradox never arises, because the paradox attaches to first-person assertion.
- If the follower were instrumented to return both the system-committed value and its own local state in the same reply — a literal readout that says, in one field, the current value is x, and in another field, the current value is y — would that be a genuinely Moorean utterance, and if not, what is missing?
- Does the follower's failure to be paradoxical show something about Moore's paradox itself — for example, that the paradox depends on a unified epistemic subject with a first-person point of view, which a distributed system lacks — or does it show only that the follower's two speech acts belong to two different subjects, the system and the replica, so the analogy was never doing real work?

So my question is whether this parallel is deep, superficial, or somewhere in between, and whether anything transfers in either direction — for instance, a sharper formulation of the assertion norm, or a machine-design insight about self-reporting. Evaluate it without assuming that one side is merely a metaphor for the other.
