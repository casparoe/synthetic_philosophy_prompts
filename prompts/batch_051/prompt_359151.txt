Draft the joint statement that a committed computationalist about the mind and a committed Searlean could both sign after a year-long, good-faith adversarial collaboration on Searle's Chinese Room argument. The computationalist holds that a suitably programmed system literally understands — it has semantics, not merely simulated semantics — while the Searlean holds that no purely formal program is sufficient for understanding, because formal symbol manipulation alone cannot yield meaning and understanding requires the biological causal powers of a brain. Both parties accept that their debate has partly been a matter of talking past each other, and neither will sign anything that caricatures their view. Begin the document itself, with no preface or commentary. Include a numbered section of agreed claims, covering the original thought experiment, the canonical objections, and the logical structure of Searle's argument as both sides would accept it; then a disagreements table giving each remaining dispute in neutral wording and again in wording each side would accept for its own position; then the cruxes, listed in order of how much their resolution would actually change someone's mind. For each crux, give a concrete case, experiment, or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Do not let the cruxes be limited to obvious points: at minimum, engage with whether the systems reply illegitimately changes the subject from the person to the room-as-a-whole, whether the robot reply answers the objection or merely relocates it, whether the argument would equally rule out a neuron-by-neuron simulation of a brain, and whether Searle's conclusion requires consciousness. Flag any disagreements you judge to be purely verbal and say why. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
