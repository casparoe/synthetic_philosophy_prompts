International democracy-monitoring bodies and foreign ministries routinely characterize certain elected governments as undergoing "democratic backsliding" when, without abolishing elections, they use formally legal instruments—court-packing, redrawing electoral districts, replacing senior civil servants, taking over independent media, invoking emergency powers—to entrench the incumbent and weaken future competition. The same bodies often treat substantively similar institutional changes, when carried out by an allied government through an ordinary legislative majority, as routine democratic politics. The practice therefore seems to track something beyond the formal content of the changes themselves: it appears sensitive to their direction, motivation, and competitive effects, and it is applied unevenly across cases. What conception of democracy, of legitimate constitutional change, and of who may classify a regime does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits; say which rival views of democratic legitimacy it quietly rules out and where it remains underdetermined; and assess whether the category "backsliding" stays coherent if its application is shown to track the classifier's own political interests. What would have to change in the practice if each of its presupposed commitments were replaced?
