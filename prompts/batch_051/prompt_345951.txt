I'm writting up a short essay — really more of a set of notes — and I'd like comments before I sink more time into it. Epistemic status: maybe 60% on the central claim, would not be surprised if large chunks are just relabeling.

The claim: the usual contrast between intuitions about cases and intuitions about principles is much less clean than people pretend. A case judgment is just a low-bandwidth principle — a generalization with a huge, mostly unverbalized ceteris paribus clause and a jurisdiction of one scenario (or a few nearby ones). A principle like 'value comparisons are transitive' is the same kind of thing, just with a much bigger jurisdiction and a smaller exception clause. The difference is mostly about the fit-to-noise tradeoff, not about what kind of evidence you're looking at. So 'should we trust case intuitions or principle intuitions' is the wrong question; the right question is inductive: which set of judgments generalizes best from the examples we've actually checked to the new cases we care about.

I use transitivity as the stalking horse. It gets treated like a principle you'd need heroic evidence to overturn, but really we've only ever confirmed it on easy, local, heavily structured choices, and there could be cyclic preference structures in messy high-dimensional comparison tasks that we never notice because we don't collect data there. Same move for simplicity: people treat simplicity as a neutral tie-breaker between theories, but it's presumably just another under-articulated intuition, an expectation about what kinds of patterns the world tends to serve up, so using it to adjudicate between principles and cases is just using one more item from inside the same system.

(b) does the transitivity example pull its weight, or does it quietly confrim the picture of preferences I'm trying to defend? (c) is the upshot interesting, or does it dissolve into 'everything is an intuition, just calibrate'? Most important: tell me what would change my mind — which argument or consideration would get me to abandon the 'same kind of thing, different bandwidth' thesis, and where you think the reasoning is most likely to fail.
