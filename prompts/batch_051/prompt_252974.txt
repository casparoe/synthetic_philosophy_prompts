After reading some Hofstadter I've tied myself in knots over what counts as the same self persisting over time. He seems to think the self is a pattern, and that after you die a kind of low-resolution copy of you keeps going in the brains of people who knew you well, and that this is literally survival rather than just a comforting way of talking. I'd like a working definition of 'survives' (or 'is the same self', if you think that's the better term) that sorts these cases.

Clear yes: you suffer total amnesia but your body carries on with your habits and temperament; your brain is scanned in perfect detail and run as a simulation after your biological death.

Clear no: an identical twin who shares your genome but none of your memories; a stranger who happens to share your name and reads your biography.

Borderline: your spouse of forty years, after your death, carries around a mental model of you that can finish your sentences and predict how you'd react; an AI trained on all your messages and diaries that your friends say still sounds like you; a split-brain patient whose right hemisphere seems to have its own stream of thought while the left one speaks; and the Hofstadter-ish case of your self being smeared across dozens of other minds at various resolutions, none of them very detailed.

Give me the definition, run it through every case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. I'd rather you commit to a definate position with reasons than survey the options. And please write it in British English.
