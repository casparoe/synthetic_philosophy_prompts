I'm a philosophy professor, and next month I'm giving a brownbag talk to our cognitive science group. The audience is mostly computational modelers and cognitive psychologists, plus a couple of philosophers of mind, and there are two interpretability people who keep saying the only honest way to know what a model "thinks" is to read the weights, not the outputs. So I have a decent guess about where the trouble will come from.

The paper defends a deflationary version of the intentional stance about LLMs. The one-paragraph version: belief-talk about an LLM is neither literally true nor a category mistake; it's a modeling stance whose license is predictive payoff. An LLM earns more belief-ascription than a chess engine or a thermostat because it was trained on the public traces of human belief-talk — arguments, retractions, hedges, corrections — so its outputs track the inferential texture of belief ascriptions. But it earns less than a person because it lacks the stable dispositions that make belief ascriptions robust: it doesn't act on its "beliefs" across contexts, and it will flip it's stated position with a trivial change in prompt. So "the model believes P" is a graded, as-if claim, true in roughly the way "the model is confident" is true, and the interesting question is what sets the grade.
