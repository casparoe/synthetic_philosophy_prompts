I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar that puts philosophy students and computer science students in the same room, and I am about to run a ninety-minute session on Gödelian arguments against mechanism: Lucas's original claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind cannot be a machine, and Penrose's later revival of that line. The situation is uneven in a specific way. The CS students can state the first incompleteness theorem and have worked through a proof; the philosophy students mostly arrive with the vague slogan that Gödel's theorem refuted computationalism. My worry is that neither group will see where the argument actually does its work: the step where a human, presented with a consistent formal system M that a mechanist claims captures the mind, assents to M's Gödel sentence because they trust that M is consistent, and why that step does not automatically transfer to a formal system that would capture the human's own output — which is Benacerraf's problem. This is the first time I am teaching the topic to a room where the mathematical background is this unevenly distributed, and I expect the real difficulty to be philosophical rather than technical.

Please do not explain Lucas's argument to me; I need a plan for how I should explain it. I want the order in which the ideas must be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be in place before the anti-mechanist inference can even be stated, and what has to be in place before each objection can properly land. Build the entire session around one carrying example — I am imagining a single toy formal system M and its Gödel sentence G_M — and say what this example must have, and must avoid, if it is to carry the weight of the session rather than collapse into mere notation. Then give me the misconceptions you expect and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I especially want the plan to intercept these: that unprovability is relative to a system, so Gödel's theorem alone does not refute mechanism; that the move of simply adding G_M as a new axiom appears to refute Lucas but in fact relocates the argument to the question whether the human's arithmetical output is captured by any one system whose soundness the human can recognize; that consistency is the load-bearing assumption, since an inconsistent system would prove everything and look nothing like a mind, yet the human has no special proof of their own consistency; that Penrose's suggestions about how a brain might physically escape mechanizability are separate from the Gödelian core and should not be allowed to substitute for it; and the slide into a lazy skepticism along the lines that if the human cannot prove their own consistency then nobody knows anything. Tell me what to leave out of a ninety-minute session and to say so aloud, and finish with one check — a question or a small task — that would tell me whether a student has understood the structure of the argument rather than merely being able to summarize Lucas.
