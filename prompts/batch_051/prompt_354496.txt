I'm several months into reporting a feature on how public-health policy handles trade-offs—contact-tracing apps, vaccine passports, quarantine rules—and in interviews the same sentence keeps being deployed as a conversation-ender: rights are trumps. A privacy advocate used it to preempt any weighing of the benefits of location data during an outbreak; a civil-liberties lawyer used it to cut short a cost-benefit argument for mandatory quarantine; a bioethicist used it to dismiss a model showing that a screening program would save many lives at some cost to autonomy. Each time, the invocation of a right was treated as ending the calculation rather than entering it.

I understand the slogan's rhetorical force, but I don't know what proposition my sources are actually asserting, and I suspect they don't either. I plan to follow up with several of them and want to force some precision. Please lay out the distinct claims the slogan "rights are trumps" could express, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical, and for each say how defensible it is, what argumentative work it does, and what it would commit a person to in a policy dispute. I especially need to know which readings would genuinely let someone stop a cost-benefit analysis rather than merely shift its burden of proof, and which readings the public-health cases could not sustain. Present it as a menu I can carry into interviews; indicate which item the privacy advocate would have to mean for the contact-tracing dismissal to go through; and, if useful, locate the readings against the neighboring positions people seem to invoke—that rights are side constraints, that they are especially weighty interests, or that the vocabulary of rights is doing no real work at all.
