I keep going back and forth on a tension in how the big AI assistants are actually set up, and I want to know what theory of belief and intention attribution the practice implicitly commits to. The same system that will tell you, if you press it, that it doesn't have beleifs or experiences and is just predicting likely text will, a minute later, tell you it thinks the second option is safer, or say it didn't mean to mislead you, or admit it was wrong earlier. The labs also train these things to be helpful and avoid harm and to figure out what the user wants to hear, and their own engineers talk in public writeups about what the model knows or is trying to do, while the official line stays that there's nothing it's like to be the model. So the existing practice looks like: deny mental states when asked, but freely use mental-state language and mental-state-shaped training everywhere else.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of belief and intention attribution that makes this practice coherent, if it even is coherent. Does the practice strictly presuppose that belief requires some extra inner ingredient that LLMs lack, so all the first-person talk is just performance? Or does it presuppose the opposite, that we're entitled to attribute beliefs whenever a system behaves reliably as if it had them, in which case the disclaimers are just one more piece of behavior to interpret? And be careful to separate what the practice actually presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and to say where it's philosophically underdetermined so several theories would license it. Also tell me what would change your mind about your reading: which argument or consideration would push you to the opposite one, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail.
