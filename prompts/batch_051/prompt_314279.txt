I serve on the capital planning committee of a regional water management authority. We are about to issue the discount-rate guidance that will govern our evaluation of a proposed 100-year storm-surge barrier and wetland restoration program, and the committee is genuinely split. The staff's baseline analysis discounts all future costs and benefits at a uniform 4 percent per year, of which 1.5 percent is explicitly defended as a pure rate of time preference. A dissenting planning memo argues that a positive pure rate of time preference is simply a formal policy of counting a person born in 2125 as less important than a person born today, and it urges us to set that parameter to zero while keeping the component that reflects expected growth in per-capita consumption. The finance staff responds that zero pure time preference will make the analysis hostage to speculative benefits far in the future and will quietly bias us toward enormous capital projects rather than cheaper, more adaptable interventions. A further complication is that much of the project's benefit is back-loaded, so the choice of discount rate nearly flips the sign of the net present value, and some committee members are now arguing that the question cannot be settled by cost–benefit arithmetic at all.

I keep wanting to insist that things be kept separate: an ethical parameter for how much later utility matters, an empirical parameter for how much richer future people will be, and a probability term for whether the project will still be delivering benefits at all. But I can see that the baseline rate bakes at least some of these together. Please draft the formal decision memo I will circulate to the full committee. It should recommend a concrete discounting treatment for this project and for the next revision of our evaluation manual, reason through what the discount rate actually does, distinguish the ethical from the empirical inputs, and give an honest, serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the side you do not recommend. The memo should be written in American English.
