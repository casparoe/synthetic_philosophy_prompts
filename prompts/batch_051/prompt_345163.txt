So I spent two hours last night arguing with my friend (philosophy PhD student, ABD I think) about whether the kind of system I build for a living could ever be conscious, and I've been turning it over alot since. My day job is staff engineer on event-driven services: a bunch of processes with their own private state, a message bus in the middle, publish/subscribe topics, event logs, consumer groups, schemas, backpressure, all of it. I'm fluent in that vocabulary, but the only philosophy I know is from talking to her.

She kept insisting on a distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, which as far as I can tell goes like this. Access consciousness is when a piece of information is globally available to the system: other parts can read it, reason with it, report on it, act on it. It's a functional, third-person notion, something you could in principle see in the message flows. Phenomenal consciousness is the raw felt part, the something-it-is-like for the thing itself, not the report and not the downstream use. Her claim was that a system could be access-conscious with zero phenomenal consciousness, and that phenomenal consciousness is exactly the part that resists any functional story. Then she brought up global workspace theory: a bunch of specialized processors compete, a winner gets into a shared workspace, the workspace broadcasts that content back out to the whole system, and that global availability is what makes a content access-conscious (or at least reportable and controllable). She also says global workspace theory is a theory of access, not of phenomenality, and that if you think you've explained felt experience by explaining broadcasts you've confused the function with the thing itself.

Here's my problem. The whole time I was translating it into what I know day to day. Access consciousness sounds exactly like a well-formed event being on the bus where every subscriber can consume it and each consumer group keeps its own offset; the workspace sounds like a pub/sub broker or a broadcast topic; the competition sounds like producers and some priority, routing, or contention scheme; the specialized processors sound like downstream services with private state that can be changed by the event but can't poke at each other except via messages; reportability sounds like an orchestrator or logging service or some service that emits an external response. I think that translation is mostly faithful to her view, but the phenomenal half evaporates in my hands. There's no obvious counterpart in the message-queue world for the felt quality itself: a message has fields, producers, consumers, a schema, but no is-it-like-something property attaches to the message, and the bus doesn't have anything it is like to be the bus. That gap is what we kept circling.

So here's what I want. Restate the access/phenomenal distinction and global workspace theory in my vocabulary as precisely and honestly as you can, using services, buses, topics, broadcast, event logs, private state, subscribers, orchestrators, offsets, and the rest of it. Don't hand-wave and don't smuggle in your own framework's assumptions and call it a translation. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which only translate with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all. I especially want a verdict I can defend in the next argument: when the phenomenal half fails to translate, is that because my vocabulary is genuinely missing something, or because that half of the distinction was never really a claim about systems in the first place, or because it's a claim my vocabulary actively obscures? And tell me the exact line in the translation where it stops being translatable, not just that the hard problem is famously hard. Also, don't patch the gap by simply renaming something, like declaring that the payload of the event just is the feeling, unless you can say what that would mean for the system's behavior.

Please write in American English.
