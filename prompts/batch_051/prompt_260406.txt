I am a philosophy professor working on the epistemology of historical prediction, and I have been teaching Montesquieu's Considerations on the Causes of the Greatness of the Romans and Their Decline in a seminar on whether the past can discipline forecasts about political order. My students and I keep returning to Chapter XVIII, where Montesquieu says, in substance, that fortune does not rule the world; there are general causes, moral and physical, that act in every monarchy to raise it, maintain it, or precipitate it; all particular accidents are subordinate to these causes; and if the chance of a battle, that is, a particular cause, ruins a state, there was a general cause at work that made that state have to perish by a single battle.

I would like you to restate this passage in the vocabulary of reference-class forecasting and the outside view: reference classes, base rates, inside versus outside view, distributional reasoning, and the advice to replace story-specific explanation with class-wide statistics. Treat Montesquieu as offering, or perhaps failing to offer, an early outside-view argument about the life and death of empires. Then give me a residue report: which components of his account translate cleanly, which carry over only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in the forecasting vocabulary at all. I am particularly interested in whether the residue indicates a real limitation of the modern vocabulary—something it cannot express about causal structure or the moral physiology of a regime—or whether it shows that Montesquieu's contrast between general causes and particular accidents is less precise than it looks. In short, is my suspicion correct that his general causes are not base rates, and that the outside view's central counsel does not survive the translation intact?
