I've been moderating a reading group on Hume's account of causation, and two members have been going at eachother for weeks now. One reads Hume as a full regularity theorist: causation just is constant conjunction plus the mind's habit of expecting the second event after the first, and there's nothing more in the objects themselves. The other is, I gather, what people call a New Humean (or skeptical realist): he says Hume is only committed to the idea that we can't observe or know any necessary connection, not that there is no such connection in the world. So on his reading, Hume could believe in real necessary connections or powers that are permanently hidden from us.

I want the document these two should have produced instead of another evening of arguing. Draft the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration. Include: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign about what Hume actually says, e.g. about constant conjunction, habit, the absence of any observed tie between cause and effect, the role the mind plays in producing our idea of necessity, and what his official definitions of cause actually state; (2) a disagreements table with each camp's real position, phrased so each side would accept its own wording; (3) the cruxes — the genuinely contested sub-questions. I suspect one crux is how to weigh the passages where Hume talks about hidden or secret powers in nature, and another is whether the definitions of cause are meant as exhaustive analyses of causation itself or just as the best we can do from experience; a third might be whether Hume's account commits him to the maxim that every event has a cause. For each crux, say what textual or argumentative evidence both sides would agree bears on it, and what each side predicts about that evidence. Finally, flag any part of the dispute that is just verbal — I have a hunch some of the disagreement comes down to how the word 'real' is being used, or to how much the two people actually care about pinning Hume down versus defending their own views about laws of nature.
