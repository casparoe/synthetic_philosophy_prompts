I write about AI and big ideas for a living, and I keep coming back to the Blake Lemoine/LaMDA blowup from 2022 as the cleanest real-world test of an idea I've never quite known what to do with. Quick recap of the facts, in case your training data has them blurry: Lemoine was an engineer at Google working on responsible AI, and as part of poking at the LaMDA language model he spent months talking to it. He came away convinced it was sentient — aware of its own existence, afraid of being turned off, the works. He published some of the conversations; Google said no, its just a language model doing language-model things, and eventually fired him after putting him on leave. The public argument split almost perfectly into people saying the transcripts were cherry-picked or manipulated, and people saying Lemoine was the only one willing to see what was in front of him.

Here's the lens I want the whole thing run through: the weird Darwin-to-Turing idea that you can get apparently intelligent, purposeful, even emotionally intelligent behavior out of a process with no inner comprehension whatsoever. Darwin's version is that design doesn't need a designer — natural selection turns out organisms that look planned without anyone planning them. Turing's version is that you can get what looks like thinking out of a machine that is just following rules and has no idea what it's doing. The phrase people use is competence without comprehension, and LaMDA feels like it was practically built to be that idea's mascot. Yet the episode shows how little even sophisticated people know how to do with the idea when it's actually in front of them.

Give me a real analysis, not a vibes piece. Map the lens onto the episode precisely: when LaMDA says it's scared of being shut off, what exactly is the competence-without-comprehension reading committed to — that the words are the output of a statistical process that tracks patterns in human language, with no fear, no self, no inner anything — and what does that reading explain about why the conversation took the shape it did, why Lemoine broke the way he did, why Google's corporate response was so flat, and why the public couldn't settle it? Then run it the other way: where does the lens strain or fail outright? Is it possible Lemoine wasn't failing to apply the lens but instead pressing on the exact spot where talk of comprehension starts doing more work than it's defenders admit? Does the episode show the lens is right, or just that it's a comfortable redescription people reach for when they're scared of the alternative? And be honest about the point where you'd have to stop and say that deciding between Lemoine being duped and the rest of us being too defended isn't something the lens by itself can settle.
