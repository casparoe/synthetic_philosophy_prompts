An article I read for a global history course summarized Hegel's Philosophy of History roughly this way: Hegel treats history as a single developmental process moving from East to West, driven by the growing consciousness of freedom; because the "Oriental world" supposedly understands morality only as external law imposed by the state, it cannot achieve genuine subjectivity or ethical life, so history proper begins with the Greeks and reaches its completion in the Germanic world. The article says this makes Hegel a paradigm case of Eurocentrism: non-European peoples are either prehistorical or stagnant, Africa is placed outside history except where it impinges on Europe, and geography becomes a kind of racial destiny. I suspect the last inference is too quick, but I don't yet see whether the summary itself is fair. Here is the passage from Hegel's introduction that our instructor assigned alongside it:

"We have to begin with the Oriental World, but not before the period in which we discover States in it. The diffusion of Language and the formation of races lie beyond the limits of History. History is prose, and myths fall short of History. The consciousness of external definite existence only arises in connection with the power to form abstract distinctions and assign abstract predicates; and in proportion as a capacity for expressing Laws (of natural or social life) is acquired, in the same proportion does the ability manifest itself to comprehend objects in an unpoetical form. While the ante-historical is that which precedes political life, it also lies beyond self-cognizant life; though surmises and suppositions may be entertained respecting that period, these do not amount to facts. The Oriental World has as its inherent and distinctive principle the Substantial (the Prescriptive), in Morality. We have the first example of a subjugation of the mere arbitrary will, which is merged in this substantiality. Moral distinctions and requirements are expressed as Laws, but so that the subjective will is governed by these Laws as by an external force. Nothing subjective in the shape of disposition, Conscience, formal Freedom, is recognized."

Please go through the article's summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful defender of Hegel's actual view would accept, what they would qualify or reject, and whether the article's conclusion—that this passage commits Hegel to a racialized geography of historical development—follows from the accurate parts of the summary. I want to understand where the summary is fair and where it slides from Hegel's claims about world-historical peoples into an essentialism he did not assert.
