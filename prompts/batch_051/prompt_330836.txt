I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting turns on a consumer-technology company that submits, under investor and regulatory pressure, to an independent certification scheme for what its backers call non-exploitative attention design. I need the scheme to behave the way real institutions behave, not the way its founders intend, and I would rather go into revision knowing its failure modes than have a reader catch them in the published book. The scheme, as I currently imagine it, runs like this. A non-profit certifier, the Fair Attention Committee, awards an annually renewable seal to apps that satisfy four conditions: no autoplay or automatic queuing of the next item; no infinite scroll, only paginated displays or a hard session cap that the user sets in advance; a recommender whose training objective excludes every proxy for session length (watch time, dwell, scroll depth, completed auto-advances) and instead maximizes a post-session survey score on whether the session felt worth the time; and a ban on ranking features calibrated to detected emotional vulnerability, such as sadness or late-night restlessness. Certified apps receive a lower commission from the major app stores, a public badge, and a slot in a curated storefront section; the Committee charges for its audits and must re-certify each app yearly.

What I need is not a verdict on whether such a scheme is a good idea in the abstract, but an account of how it would actually play out. What would it push the product teams, the certifying body, the app stores, and the users themselves to do? How would each of them game it, and how quickly? What second-order effects would follow, whether in the uncertified apps, in the meaning of the seal, in how the surveys themselves are engineered, or in how the emotional-vulnerability rule is arbitraged? And which of the failure modes, if any, could be fixed by tightening the criteria or changing the incentives without collapsing the whole thing? Please state your overall verdict first, then work through the incentives level by level, in no more than 900 words.
