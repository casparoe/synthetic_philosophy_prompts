Take a standard Frankfurt-style case: Jones is deciding between doing X and doing Y. A neurosurgeon, Black, has rigged Jones with a device that will intervene and make him choose X if Jones shows any sign he's about to choose Y; but Jones chooses X on his own and the device stays idle. The usual upshot is that Jones is responsible for choosing X even though he couldn't have done otherwise, so the principle of alternate possibilities is false.

Now change the device. It doesn't wait until Jones is about to choose Y. It works one step upstream: it scrubs Y from the set of options that ever make it into Jones's conscious deliberation. Jones never considers Y at all. He deliberates between X and some third option, or just X, and chooses X for his own reasons. Black never has to fire, because the possibility of Y never gets a toehold.

Does the Frankfurt argument get stronger or weaker when the unavailable alternative is removed from deliberation entirely, rather than being overridden at the moment of decision? I want a committed answer, not a survey. In particular: does this variation give a cleaner case for the claim that moral responsibility doesn't require alternate possibilities, or does it just become a manipulation case that raises new worries? And which underlying picture of free will is doing the most work in your answer — that what matters is the actual causal source of the action, that the agent identifies with the motive, that the agent is reasons-responsive, or something else? Say where a libertarian critic should push back hardest, what change to the case would most likely flip your verdict, and flag the step you're least confident about.
