AI labs sometimes train language models using something they call process supervision instead of outcome supervision. With outcome supervision, you basically just score whether the final answer is right or useful, and the model gets updated on that. With process supervision, the training signal scores each intermediate step of the response as it goes—'this step is good,' 'this step doesn't follow'—before anyone knows where the whole chain is heading. The stated motivation is usually to make the model's reasoning more legible, rather than just correct.

What philosophical commitments does that training practice presuppose about what reasoning, explanation, or understanding is? For instance, does it presuppose that good reasoning can be choped into individually checkable steps, the way a proof or a calculation can? What would it imply about reasoning that works by analogy, reframing, hunches, or a leap that only looks justified in retrospect? And is the practice consistent with the same labs' admission that they don't really know what's going on inside the model—that the actual reasons for a response may not be the words printed on the screen?

I want the implicit theory here, not just the engineering rationale: not only what the method rewards, but what it has to be committed to about thought in order for the training objective to make sense at all. Where is it genuinely neutral between rival pictures of reasoning, and where does it quietly rule some out?
