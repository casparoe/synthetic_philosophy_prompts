I'm a philosophy of science professor, and at a workshop last month I watched two of my colleages — Marta, an economist who works on epidemic modeling, and Ravi, a philosopher of science — go at it over a paper that modeled a city's disease waves by treating the whole city as a perfectly mixed tank of people: no neighborhoods, no commute patterns, no superspreader events. The model had nonetheless predicted the last two winter waves almost exactly. Their argument went roughly like this:

M: "The tank assumption is false, obviously. People aren't molecules. But Friedman's whole point was that we don't judge a model by whether its assumptions are realistic; we judge it by whether it predicts. This model predicts, so it stays."

R: "Predicts what, exactly? Two data points? A model with a totally wrong mechanism can fit two seasons and then blow up. Without a story about why the waves look the way they do, you don't know when the model will fail or where. That's not a model, it's a mnemonic."

M: "It has quite a lot of internal structure — contact rate, recovery rate, seasonal forcing. The tank part is one assumption among many. And every model has false assumptions. The planets aren't point masses either, but that didn't stop Newton."

R: "Point masses are an idealization: they leave out a small factor that doesn't matter for the question. The tank assumption doesn't leave something out; it asserts a mechanism — random mixing — that is known to be importantly false. Here, probably two errors are cancelling. That's not idealization, it's luck."

M: "Calling it luck is just a way of saying the model does better than you think it's allowed to. If the cancellation is stable across different cities and years, then maybe, at the relevant scale, the city really does behave as if it were well mixed. That 'as if' is all Friedman ever needed."

R: "But you don't get to say 'behaves as if' for free. You have to show that the details wash out — that aggregation makes the mixing assumption harmless. Showing that is a substantive claim about the real structure of the city. It isn't a license to ignore structure; it's a demand to understand it."

M: "So no model gets used until its assumptions are certified true? Good luck building any policy model then. You're comparing applied science to an impossible standard of truth, while the rest of us compare models by their forecasts."

R: "I'm not asking for true. I'm asking for an account of why the false parts don't matter for the prediction. Friedman's 'as if' was never supposed to be an excuse to skip that question. That's the difference between a useful fiction and a just-so story."

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate it move by move: which points are right, which are overreach, and which of them is more right when the dust settles?
