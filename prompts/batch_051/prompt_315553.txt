I'm a PhD student in statistics, working mainly on causal inference and out-of-distribution generalization, and I've been reading some development economics on the side. The material that keeps drawing me in is the long-running dispute over why some countries are rich and others are not: the institutions camp says it is mostly about the rules of the game and how they are enforced (extractive versus inclusive), the geography camp points to climate, disease burden, and natural endowments, and the culture camp points to trust, norms, and values that support markets and education. History is not my field, but the debate feels strangely familiar, because I keep wanting to translate it into the machinery I actually speak fluently: potential outcomes, DAGs, d-separation, do-calculus, instrumental variables, local average treatment effects, SUTVA, set identification versus point identification, treatment-effect heterogeneity, transportability, path dependence, and the learning-theoretic idea of trying to generalize from a single high-dimensional, non-exchangeable trajectory.

My own first pass runs something like this: institutions are a partially manipulable treatment; geography works as a confounder or a set of effect modifiers; settler mortality or similar historical accidents serve as noisy instruments for institutions in some regions; and today's income is the outcome, with long-run growth as the time-varying process. But that first pass makes me uneasy: it feels like it might be quietly changing the subject of a debate that is also about why a particular country ended up on a particular path.

I want you to restate the institutions-versus-geography-versus-culture debate as precisely as you can in that vocabulary, and tell me what each substantive position turns into once the translation is made. Then give me the residue report. Which claims map cleanly onto the causal and distributional machinery? Which ones map only with distortion, and what exactly gets distorted? Which ones cannot be expressed in the target vocabulary at all? And, finally, I want your honest view on where the failure lies: is the residue a defect of my machinery, which is built for modular, repeatable interventions and may simply be the wrong tool for one-off historical processes, or is it a sign that the historical question itself is less precise than its participants assume, or some of each? I'm trying to work out whether my instinct—that a large part of this debate is really an argument about identification and external validity—is right, or whether there is content in the question that my framework cannot even see.
