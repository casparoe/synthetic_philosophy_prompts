Here's something I've been turning over. The standard story about the socialist calculation debate is that Mises said you can't have rational economic calculation without real market prices for capital goods, Lange answered that a Central Planning Board could just run prices by trial and error—raise them when there's a shortage, lower them when there's a surplus—and Hayek's deeper objection was that the knowledge you'd need is scattered across millions of people and much of it is tacit, the kind of thing that shows up in what people do rather than in anything they could write down.

But Hayek made that argument before anyone had systems trained on all of recorded text plus a firehose of transaction and sensor data. Suppose the Planning Board, instead of trial-and-error pricing, trains something like a foundation model on every reciept scanned, every shipping manifest, every search query and job posting, and uses it to continuously estimate demand and set prices. Does Hayek's argument survive that?

My instinct is it cuts both ways. Hayek said the information doesn't exist anywhere in concentrated form; a model trained on everything is, in principle, a new place where it gets concentrated. But Hayek also said the problem was never really computation—its that the relevant knowledge only gets revealed when people act under competitive pressure, and that prices are a discovery mechanism, not just an estimator.

So I want a careful postmortem. Go through Hayek's argument premise by premise and say which premise this development actually puts pressure on, which ones it leaves untouched, and whether there's a repaired version that's weaker but still does real work. And be honest about which parts of Lange's side genuinely benefit from the ML.
