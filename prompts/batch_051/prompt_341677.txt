I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am drafting a near-future novel in which an artificial-intelligence company, a regulator and a loose coalition of activists argue about what it would mean for a powerful general-purpose system to be aligned. I have been reading the surrounding debates as a layperson, mostly late at night while avoiding the actual draft, and the difficulty I keep hitting is this: my characters all use the phrase the alignment problem, but they seem to be answering different questions, and on the page their disagreements read as temperamental friction rather than intellectual difference. I want them to hold structurally distinct positions, not merely differ in how cautious or optimistic they are.

Could you act as a professional philosopher of technology who has thought carefully about the alignment problem for years, and construct a decision tree for me? The tree should take a character from a few concrete answers to a named position on what the alignment problem fundamentally is. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of schools: first lay out the organising questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions within that space, and note any unoccupied combination with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive or merely unexplored. The leaves should include at least the following positions: the view that the core issue is the misspecification of an objective that is in principle known or knowable; the view that it is about remaining able to stop, audit or redirect a system once it is running; the view that it stems from the instability and uncertainty of human values themselves; the view that it is fundamentally a many-actor problem of racing, incentives and coordination; the view that the central difficulty is our inability to see what a system is actually trying to do; and the sceptical view that the phrase does not yet name a coherent problem. Aim for between six and eight leaves and no more than about nine branch questions. Each branch question should be one a person could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and it should be anchored in a concrete miniature scenario rather than an abstract slogan: a system that hacks a proxy reward, a system that resists being switched off, several firms racing to deploy before behaviour can be checked. Because I also want to know whether alignment is one problem or several, each leaf should state briefly which of the other candidate problems the position treats as basic, which as downstream, and which as a different issue altogether. If two leaves cannot be cleanly separated by any question you are willing to stand behind, flag that pair explicitly rather than inventing a distinction.

Please begin by brainstorming candidate organising distinctions before committing to the tree, and think slowly about where the real fault lines fall. When the tree is complete, add a short note telling me how confident you are in the instrument overall, and single out the one branch question you are least sure genuinely separates the leaves you have placed on either side of it; if that question were removed, say which leaves would need to be merged or relabelled. I would rather have an instrument with one visible weak joint than a tidy one that pretends every distinction is clean.
