Two people in my study group got into an argument about whether a jury's majority verdict is more reliable than a single juror's. One of them, Al, said obviously yes, and trotted out the Condorcet jury theorem: if each voter is even slightly better than chance, the majority's chance of being right increases fast as the group grows. Bea said that's exactly the problem—the theorem assumes the votes are independent, but real juries hear the same evidence and deliberate, so their errors are correlated; if everyone is wrong for the same reason, adding more voters is just counting the same mistake more times. Al replied that the independence assumption doesn't need to hold perfectly, since there are extensions of the theorem where the conclusion survives under modest correlation, so Bea was raising a caveat about degree, not a refutation. Bea said 'modest' is doing all the work: whether actual juries fall within the regime where the theorem still applies is an emperical question, and the theorem itself is silent on that. Al said that's not a flaw—the theorem is a conditional claim, and what it tells you is precisely which conditions to go check, not how to predict any particular jury's accuracy.
