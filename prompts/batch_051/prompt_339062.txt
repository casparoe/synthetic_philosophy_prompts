Suppose a state's election code contains the following provisions, enacted in 1974. Section 12-204(a) provides: "It is unlawful for any corporation, or any person acting on behalf of a corporation, to make a contribution to any candidate for state or local office." Section 12-101(c) defines "contribution" as "a gift, subscription, loan, advance, or deposit of money or anything of value made for the purpose of influencing the nomination or election of any candidate."

Last year Meridian Systems, a corporation, spent substantial sums to train a large language model and then released the trained weights to the public under a license permitting any use, commercial or otherwise, without charge. A candidate for the state legislature downloaded the model, fine-tuned it on her own speeches and position papers, and now uses it to draft fundraising emails, canvassing scripts, and debate preparation; her campaign estimates this replaced roughly sixty thousand dollars in consulting and copywriting. Meridian has never communicated with the candidate or her campaign, and its license imposes no restrictions.

Does Meridian's release of the model amount to a prohibited corporate contribution to the candidate under the statute? Work through at least three approaches—the ordinary meaning of the statutory text, the evident purpose of the ban, and what the 1974 drafters would likely have said if asked—and say which should control and why. In particular, address whether "anything of value" was "made for the purpose of influencing" the election when the corporation released the model to the public at large and had no contact with the candidate.
