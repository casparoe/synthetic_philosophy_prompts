I've been turning over something that feels like it should pattern-match to a single phenomenon but keeps not doing that. Germany makes Holocaust denial a crime and has built this whole memorial culture around not letting the past settle into something comfortable. Japan, same war, same losing side, lets its prime ministers visit Yasukuni, a shrine that honors convicted Class-A war criminals, and its official apologies keep reading to the rest of East Asia as hedged. Turkey has a law against denigrating Turkishness, which gets used against people who bring up the Armenian genocide. And the American South still puts up Confederate monuments and defends them as heritage, not hate. What does each of these practices presuppose about collective memory and collective responsibility—does a country inherit guilt, can a nation be a subject that owes something to the dead, does national identity depend on a flattering origin story? For each, I want to know what it strictly presupposes as opposed to what it's merely compatible with. Are the four actually inconsistent with each other, or is there one coherent view that licenses all of them? And what would actually have to change in, say, Germany's practice if you swapped in the view that only individuals, never nations, can be responsible for the past?
