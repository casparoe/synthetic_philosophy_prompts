I work in philosophical aesthetics, and I have been developing a view about what makes a game artistically valuable. I would like a candid assessment of the view before I pursue it further.

The view is that the primary aesthetic object of a game — including video games, board games, and card games — is neither its audiovisual presentation nor its narrative, but the structure of agency it offers the player, which I have been calling its 'decision texture.' By a game's decision texture I mean the felt shape of the space of choices as one plays: how options present themselves, how the consequences of a choice are registered, how different considerations pull against one another, and how the options not taken linger after the fact. My central claim is that a game's artistic merit is largely a function of the quality of its decision texture, and I have been trying to isolate the dimensions along which decision textures differ. So far I distinguish three.

First, tension: the degree to which local and global interests diverge, so that what is good for the present situation is costly in the long run, or conversely. Second, salience: how vividly the foreclosed options remain present after a choice is made, rather than vanishing the moment it is made. Third, legibility: how well the player can discern the causal structure connecting a choice to its consequences, so that the choice is experienced as a choice rather than as a shot in the dark.

I am inclined to say that a game is artistically good to the extent that its decision texture scores highly on these three dimensions — that depth is, roughly, the density of salient, legible, high-tension decisions. This would explain why a game can offer many choices and still feel shallow, when the choices are equivalent in their texture, and why a game with few choices can feel deep, when each choice is heavily loaded. Chess, on this account, is artistically significant not because of the look of the pieces but because at nearly every move several genuinely different plans are legible, each with its own local and global costs, and the plans not chosen remain salient as the position develops; a visually lavish game in which every choice is a busywork toggle between equivalent outcomes would have a thin decision texture and, by my lights, little artistic merit.

Before I commit to the view, I want an honest assessment. Is 'decision texture' a coherent concept, or is it a metaphor with a taxonomy attached? Has anything close to it already been worked out under another name in philosophical aesthetics or game studies, such that my version would be redundant? And where does the account break — what does it count as artistically valuable that is not, and what does it miss about what makes games valuable as art?
