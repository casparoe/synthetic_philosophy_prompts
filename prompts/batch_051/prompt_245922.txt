I've been reading about theories of the atonement and somethings been bugging me. You've got Anselm's satisfaction theory from the 11th century — sin is an offense against God's infinite honor, a debt humans can't repay, so Christ's death makes satisfaction and repairs the order. Then the Reformers' penal substitution view — sin deserves punishment, God's justice demands that the penalty be inflicted, and Christ absorbs it in our place, which is what lets God forgive without being unjust. And then the moral influence view that's usually traced to Abelard — the cross isn't about paying a debt or absorbing a penalty at all; it's God's love put on display to melt us and actually change us. These positions have been argued over for close to a thousand years, everyone's reading more or less the same texts, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved an inch.

My working theory is that this looks like one debate but is really three different questions sharing a heading: penal substitution is about what makes forgiveness legally possible, satisfaction is about repairing an offense inside a feudal honor framework, and moral influence is about what transforms the sinner. If that's right, the impasse is mostly people talking past each other and there's less actual disagreement than it looks like.

But I'm not sure, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict for one side. Is there a real substantive disagreement under the hood — for example about whether God's justice is fundamentally retributive, about whether the atonement is mainly something that changes God's stance or something that changes us, or about which biblical passages are load-bearing? Or is the whole thing underdetermined by the texts so that no amount of exegesis could settle it? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a sharper statement of the underlying problem, a way of telling when two of these theories genuinely conflict versus just using different vocabulary, or something else entirely? I'd rather have an honest account of the structure of the disagreement than another argument for one of the three positions.
