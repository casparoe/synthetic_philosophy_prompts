Below is an argument I have been working through. It is meant to show that the notion of a truth-value gap—a meaningful declarative sentence that is neither true nor false—is unstable, at least in the case where a sentence's subject term fails to refer. I want to know whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and on what reading.

(1) Let S be the sentence "The present Emperor of Antarctica is left-handed." Let N be its ordinary negation, "The present Emperor of Antarctica is not left-handed." Let E be the more external formulation, "It is not the case that the present Emperor of Antarctica is left-handed."

(2) The gap theorist holds that S is meaningful: a competent speaker understands it perfectly well. But because Antarctica has no emperor, S fails to say anything that could be true or false. It is therefore neither true nor false.

(3) Since Antarctica has no emperor, E is true. After all, it is not the case that the present Emperor of Antarctica is left-handed; there is no such person, so the sentence cannot correctly be affirmed.

(4) But E and N are the same statement in different notation. "It is not the case that the present Emperor of Antarctica is left-handed" and "The present Emperor of Antarctica is not left-handed" say the same thing; the second merely places the negation inside the sentence.

(5) Therefore, N is true (from (3) and (4)).

(6) Yet the gap theorist must also hold that N is neither true nor false. On the standard presuppositional view, a failed presupposition survives ordinary negation: if S presupposes an Emperor of Antarctica and there is none, then N likewise presupposes one and likewise lacks a truth-value.

(7) The gap theorist is therefore committed to saying that N is both true and neither true nor false.

(8) Since this is incoherent, the gap-theoretic treatment of S must be abandoned; the sentence is better regarded as simply false.

I find the argument forceful, but I also suspect it is too quick. Is the reasoning valid? If not, identify every step that could be resisted and say which resistance succeeds, being specific about the reading of the negation at each place and about whether any distinction the gap theorist draws is principled rather than ad hoc.
