You are a specialist in contemporary egalitarian political philosophy. I am a graduate teaching assistant marking a midterm in an undergraduate course on distributive justice, and before I finalize the grade I would like a careful second opinion on one student's answer.

The exam question was: "Explain the luck egalitarian distinction between option luck and brute luck and the role it plays in luck egalitarian theories of distributive justice. Then critically discuss Elizabeth Anderson's objection that, so understood, luck egalitarianism fails to treat citizens with equal respect and concern."

The student wrote the following:

"Luck egalitarians hold that inequalities are just when they flow from people's choices and unjust when they flow from luck, so distributive institutions should be sensitive to the difference between chosen and unchosen disadvantage. The central conceptual tool here is Ronald Dworkin's distinction between option luck and brute luck. Option luck covers the results of deliberate gambles: cases in which a person knowingly accepts a risk that might have turned out either way, such as declining insurance or investing in a speculative stock. Brute luck covers fortunes and misfortunes that do not arise from this kind of chosen risk, such as being born with a serious impairment or being struck by lightning. Dworkin's claim is that bad option luck gives a person no claim of justice against others, while bad brute luck always generates a claim of justice to compensation. He then proposes that this be implemented through a hypothetical insurance market: we ask what coverage people would have bought if they had not known which brute misfortunes would befall them, and the premiums that prudent people would have chosen set the level of compensation society owes. In this way, the scheme converts brute luck into option luck.

Anderson's objection is that this picture is unforgiving in practice. If the option/brute luck distinction were made the test of entitlement, the state could deny assistance to the victims of bad option luck, such as a homeowner who opted out of flood insurance and then lost their house in a storm, and would have to investigate whether applicants for aid were themselves responsible for their need. For Anderson, this treats citizens in need with blame or condescending pity rather than with equal respect and concern. Her own positive proposal, democratic equality, is best read as adjusting Dworkin's resourcism rather than rejecting it: it keeps the option/brute luck split as the criterion for when inequalities are unjust, and adds only a relational requirement that compensation be delivered without stigmatizing its recipients."
