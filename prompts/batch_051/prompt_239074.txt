I've been turning over a thought experiment lately and have the nagging sense that it corresponds to something recognized, but I can't put a name to it, and every term I try fits only partway. Imagine a device that can produce any experience a human being is capable of having, drawing each time from an enormous but finite catalog of distinguishable experiences. Now imagine someone using it without any time limit. After enough use they will have worked through the entire catalog, and from then on anything the device produces is, from the inside, indistinguishable from something they have already lived through. I've seen people take this as a reason to think an endless life would be unbearable: once the catalog is exhausted, novelty is gone and you are left with reruns. But I've also seen the reply that reruns are not automatically worthless or unpleasant; people rewatch films and re-listen to songs, and still get something out of them. So exhausting the catalog seems to settle less than the pessimistic conclusion claims.

What I actually want to identify is the middle claim that both sides appear to share: that a finite mind has, in effect, only finitely many distinguishable experiences available to it, so that the space of possible experience can be used up. I know 'eternal recurrence' is something different, and plain boredom isn't quite right either, since boredom is a felt episode while this is a claim about what is possible in the first place. Does this middle claim — or the overall pattern of arguing from it to the badness of an immortal life — have an established name? I would like the boundaries drawn properly: where the descriptive claim ends and the evaluative step begins, what has to be added to get from exhaustibility to the conclusion that an endless life would be bad, and which nearby named ideas this should be distinguished from. A bare label won't settle anything for me.
