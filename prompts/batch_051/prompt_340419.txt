You are a philosopher of equality and anti-discrimination law advising the drafters of a human rights instrument. They have proposed the following protected grounds: race, sex, disability, religion, national origin, language or accent, age, marital status, sexual orientation, gender identity, body weight, physical appearance, genetic predispositions, political opinion, caste, and caregiver status.

Sort these sixteen grounds into four tiers—most suspect, substantially suspect, moderately suspect, and least suspect—where a "more suspect" ground is one on which legal distinctions should face a stronger presumption of illegitimacy. Begin by stating, in two or three sentences, the criteria that guide your ranking, such as a history of group subordination, immutability or the cost of changing the trait, the trait's typical relevance to legitimate decisions, its visibility or concealability, and whether the classification entrenches a status hierarchy. Then place every ground in exactly one tier, giving a one-sentence justification for each ground. Take a moment to weigh the criteria carefully where they conflict, and state which criterion you treat as controlling in those cases. Finally, identify the single hardest borderline case and explain in one sentence what ultimately pushes it to one side rather than the other.
