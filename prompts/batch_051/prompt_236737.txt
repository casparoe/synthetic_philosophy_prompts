I've just come from a long argument with a friend about whether people can properly be praised or blamed for things that were, at least in part, out of their hands. The argument left me convinced that my own judgements on particular cases are more settled than any principle I can state, and I would like you to help me find the principle — or to tell me which of my judgements I should give up. Let me set down the cases and my verdicts as honestly as I can.

Two drivers each glance down at a text message while passing a nursery at closing time. One looks up, nothing has happened, and he drives on. The other, in otherwise identical circumstances, strikes and kills a child who stepped into the road. My verdict is that the second driver deserves far harsher punishment and should carry far more blame, even though I can name no difference in what each did.

Two builders knowingly use substandard joists in a roof they are renovating. In one case the flaw is discovered and repaired before anyone is hurt. In the other the roof collapses during a party and kills several people. I think both builders did exactly the same wrong, and yet I cannot bring myself to say the surviving builder's situation is just as grave: the one whose roof collapsed seems to owe something — a debt, a punishment, a degree of guilt — that the other does not owe, and that difference seems to me to be made entirely by luck.

Two surgeons perform the same innovative and only half-tested procedure on similar patients, against the same level of evidence. One patient recovers, and the surgeon is praised as bold and skilful. The other patient dies, and the surgeon is blamed as reckless and incompetent. I share both reactions, at least partly, and this bothers me, because the difference in how we speak of them was caused by the outcome rather than by anything in their decisions.

But now a case that pulls the other way. Two friends each betray a secret that they promised to keep. In the first case the secret, once out, does no detectable damage. In the second it destroys a marriage and costs someone a job. I do not think the second friend is the more disloyal of the two, and I am reluctant to say the second deserves more moral blame, even though the damage is very different.

And a final case, the one my friend pressed hardest. Two people each fire a gun at someone with the settled intention of killing them. The first person's gun jams; the second person's gun fires and kills. Here I resist the idea that the successful killer deserves a different sentence from the would-be killer, because the distance between the outcomes was a mechanical accident. Yet my own verdicts on the drivers, the builders, and the surgeons all seem to allow outcome to change blame, punishment, or reputation.

I realise, then, that I am pulled between two thoughts. The first is that a person cannot properly be praised or blamed for what they did not control, and that identical choices, care, and intentions deserve identical assessment. The second is that what actually happens matters to how much blame, punishment, or praise is owed, and that the person who causes a death stands under a burden that the person who merely risked a death does not. I would like you to tell me what principle, or what small set of principles, best captures my pattern of verdicts. In particular, what should that principle say about a negligent driver who is caught before any harm occurs — should he be punished as if the harm had happened, or only as if he had risked it? And what should it say about a case my friend and I never resolved: an arsonist whose fire is put out by a sudden rainstorm next to one whose fire burns down an occupied house? Which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to abandon, and is there a way to keep all of them without making the principle so gerrymandered that it explains nothing?

Please write your answer in American English, and please write it in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter or a long email.
