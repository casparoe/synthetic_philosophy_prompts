I have been trying to get clear about something, and I would like your help turning a pile of my own judgments into a principle, if one is even there to be found. The question is what makes something a disease. My rough working idea is that a disease is a condition in which some part or process of the body or mind fails to do what it is for, in a way that makes the person worse off. But I keep discovering that my own verdicts do not respect that idea very cleanly, so I want to lay out what I actually think about a range of cases and see what principle, if any, captures it.

Here are my verdicts. Pneumonia and type 1 diabetes are diseases, without hesitation. Severe depression is a disease. ADHD is a disease, or at least close enough that the difference does not bother me; the attentional system keeps failing a person in ways that harm them. Alcoholism and gambling addiction are diseases too, even though they begin in choices. Mild shyness is not a disease, but social anxiety that keeps a person housebound is. Lactose intolerance in an adult is not a disease to me, just a normal variation with unpleasant consequences. Nearsightedness, by contrast, feels like a disease to me because the eye is failing at the very thing it is for. Male pattern baldness is not a disease, and neither pregnancy nor menopause is. Grief after someone dies is not a disease, even when it is intense. Here is where I start to wobble: I want to say high blood pressure is a disease because it quietly damages the body, but I do not want to say obesity is a disease, or only in extreme cases. And I certainly do not think the old diagnosis of drapetomania, applied to enslaved people who ran away, was ever a disease, even though the doctors who invented it thought the behavior was abnormal. Likewise, the old diagnosis of hysteria, which lumped together whatever in women's emotional lives men found inconvenient, was not a disease in my book.

I would like you to tell me whether there is one coherent account of disease behind these verdicts, or close to one. State the principle precisely, tie its clauses to the cases that motivate them, and tell me what it says about cases I have not listed: infertility in a person who has never wanted children, moderate hearing loss in old age, a genetic condition that causes no symptoms but would be fatal if the person ate a common food, and a very low but perfectly harmless IQ. Be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and say which ones I would have to revise if I adopted it. Please write in continuous prose only, as an email or letter would be written, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
