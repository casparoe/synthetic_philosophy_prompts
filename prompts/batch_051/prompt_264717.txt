I am helping to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two researchers who have spent the past year talking past one another about whether contemporary large language models can properly be said to hold beliefs. One party works broadly in the intentional-stance tradition associated with Dennett: she holds that belief-talk about an AI system is justified just when adopting the intentional stance towards it yields reliable predictions, and that there is no further, stance-independent fact about whether the system 'really' believes. The other party is a representational realist about the attitudes: he holds that there is a fact of the matter about whether a system literally believes something, and that this fact depends on the system's internal architecture — in particular on whether it contains stored, recombinable, action-guiding representations with the right causal roles, or something in that vicinity — and not merely on the success of a predictive stance.

The collaboration's deliverable is a joint statement that both can put their names to while making their residual disagreement exact. Please draft it. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both accept, covering at least what the intentional stance is, what current large language models demonstrably can and cannot do, and what would have to be true for belief ascriptions to them to be merely useful fiction; (2) a disagreements table in which each side's position is stated in wording that side would accept, and the other side's position is stated fairly enough that it too would sign off on the characterisation; and (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I suspect the cruxes include whether the intentional stance can be systematically misleading while remaining predictive, and whether a system lacking the architecture for storing and recombining representations could nonetheless pass belief-attribution tests. For each crux, specify a concrete case, test, or experimental result that both would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each party predicts about that case. If part of their disagreement turns out to be verbal — for example about how much internal architecture the word 'belief' already requires, or about the force of 'really' — say which part and why.
