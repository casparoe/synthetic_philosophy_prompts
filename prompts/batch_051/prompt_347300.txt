I'm a philosophy professor, and I teach a philosophy of philanthropy seminar where the same thing happens every year. We cover cause prioritisation and the students walk out treating importance, tractability, and neglectedness as a three-column checklist: rate each cause one to five on each, add them up, done. I want to break that reflex with a game before we ever open the reading. The thing I want them to feel, not hear me explain, is that the three factors are not independent scores you can sum. Neglectedness only does work because of diminishing returns and because other people are already pouring money into some things while ignoring others; a cause can be the most important one in the room and still be a lousy place to put the next dollar, because the cheap, tractable wins have been used up and the remaining work is grindingly hard. So I want a game where that shows up in the payoffs rather than in my framing.

Here's the setting: it's the philosophy club I advise, twenty undergrads, 75 minutes, and almost none of them have seen the framework before. Could you design the whole thing? Split them into funding teams, each with a fixed budget to allcoate however they like, and give them four or five cause cards with short descriptions that don't advertise the right answer — disguise them enough that nobody can just import their politics. Hidden, exactly specified payoff schedules underneath, with actual numbers rather than vibes: include at least one big important cause that saturates quickly after a little money, and at least one boring-looking cause with steady room for more funding round after round. I want two or three rounds, with payoffs revealed between rounds, and something in the mechanics that lets the players themselves change a cause's neglectedness by where they already spent money, so they generate the lesson before they're told it. No lecture up front, and the name of the framework shouldn't appear until the debrief. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto importance, tractability, and neglectedness, says what predictably goes wrong when you actually run this (teams bidding at random, someone shouting the payoff schedule across the room, students treating it as a guessing game) and how to handle it, and is honest about what the game can't capture about real cause evaluation.
