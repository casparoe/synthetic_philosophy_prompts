I keep seeing the standard econ argument that a union is basically a cartel for labor: by bargaining collectively, workers push wages above the competitive rate, which means fewer jobs and deadweight loss, same as if a bunch of sellers colluded. But that argument seems to be doing all its work under the assumption of a competitive labor market. A lot of the places where union fights are actually happening now don't look like that—think a single big employer that dominates a region, or the gig platforms where your pay is set by some closed-source algorithm and can change under you week to week. In a labor market that's monopsonistic or "algorithmic monopsony"-ish, does the anti-union cartel argument still go through? What I'm after is which premise the new setting actually pressures, and whether the argument can be repaired into a weaker but still somewhat useful version, or whether it just collapses into an empirical question about how competitive the specific market is. I don't have any econ or philosophy background, just a dev who reads about this stuff, so maybe I'm missing something. If you think the argument survives, give me the strongest version that still works in a genuinely one-employer town; if you think it's dead there, say what's left of the objection to unions once the cartel point goes.
