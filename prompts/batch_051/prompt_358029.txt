I'm a novelist working on a book where one of the main characters is a retired philosopher who, after years of teaching on moral responsibility, has come to accept something like Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — that nobody is ultimately morally responsible for what they do. In a scene near the end she reads a poem at a prison education program graduation, and I want the poem to be an actual villanelle that does the argument instead of just gesturing at 'free will is dead.'

So: the form has to carry the content. The two refrains should be the two joints of the regress — one line saying that what you do flows from the way you are, the other saying that you never made yourself the way you are. Let the five tercets push one step further back each time: the action, the character behind the action, the formation of that character, the heredity and early luck behind the formation, and finally the impossibility of finding any first cause. The repetitions should start to feel like the argument refusing to let go whenever the speaker tries to stop. In the closing quatrain the two refrains should collide, and the final line — the second refrain — should land on the idea that to be responsible we would have to be causa sui, the cause of ourselves, which nothing can be. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher who works on this stuff recognize the Basic Argument in its structure, not just its mood — the argument bites whether or not the world is deterministic.
