An appraisal theorist and a bestowal theorist about love's reasons have spent the term arguing past one another, and they have agreed to produce an adversarial-collaboration document rather than another round of polemic. The appraisal theorist, in the spirit of Velleman, holds that love is an evaluative response to value the beloved already has: not a tally of attractive properties, but an appreciation of the beloved as a person, which is why love presents itself as answerable to how the beloved actually is and can survive the loss of contingent charms. The bestowal theorist, in the spirit of Frankfurt, holds that love is an attitude that constitutes importance rather than tracking it: the beloved matters to the lover because the lover loves, which is why love need not reflect any independent ranking of merits and why it is not a judgment the lover can revise at will. Both also know a third, relational view in the spirit of Kolodny, which denies their shared assumption that love's reasons must be either detected properties or conferred importance, and neither is sure whether that view is a genuine third option or reduces to one of their own.

Write the document they should have produced. Include: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign about the phenomenology of love, its resistance to direct voluntary control, and the failure of ordinary merit-based explanations of whom one loves; (2) a disagreements table in which each row gives the residual disagreement in neutral wording, followed by each camp's own preferred formulation of its position; (3) the cruxes, understood as sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side, and for each crux a concrete case or argument both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Attend especially to the equal-merit problem, the persistence of love through the loss of admired qualities, and the apparent non-transferability of love's reasons. If any part of the dispute now looks merely verbal, say which part and why.
