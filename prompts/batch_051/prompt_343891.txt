The old claim about scapegoating, as I understand it, is that it only works when the community doesn't know it's doing it. A group under internal stress redirects its aggression onto a single victim, and on this story the relief is real only if the participants genuinely believe the victim is the source of their trouble; the moment they see the ritual as a social technology for dumping aggression, it stops relieving aggression and starts looking like exactly what it is. I'm not asking whether that story is correct as history or anthropology, I want to know whether it survives a specific development.

Suppose a secular, therapy-literate community reads the argument and decides to run a transparent version anyway. Once a year they hold a grievance session, write the year's accumulated resentments into a single written charge, select a scapegoat by lottery from among volunteers (the scapegoat gets a generous payout and a month off from community duties), and then publicly and ritualistically transfer the blame. Everyone involved, including the scapegoat, knows the function: it's an explicit release valve for aggression the community can't otherwise discharge. Does the old argument predict this fails, and if so which premise is actually doing the work — genuine belief in the victim's guilt, the victim's isolation, spontaneity, secrecy, or something else? If it does survive, what would have to be true for a self-aware scapegoat ritual to reduce conflict? Or is there a repaired version of the argument that says something weaker but still important, like "transparency changes what the ritual can transfer but doesn't eliminate it"? I'm interested in what happens to the argument, not in whether the community should do it.
