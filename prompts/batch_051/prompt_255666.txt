I recently retired from a career in biology and have been working through some ancient philosophy, mostly because I got tired of modern thinkers assuming the pre-moderns had nothing to say about economics. A friend of mine, who is an economist and rather dismissive of anything before the nineteenth century, sent me a short summary of Aristotle on money and exchange that he picked up from an online essay. This is what he sent:

'Aristotle was basically a conservative agrarian snob about the economy. He divides things into what's natural and what's conventional, and he puts money on the conventional side, which for him means it's sort of unreal, just a social agreement with no real substance. Barter was natural, he thought, because you're swapping use-values, while trade for profit is unnatural because it treats money as an end in itself rather than as a mere medium of exchange. He even says interest is the most unnatural thing of all, because it lets money breed money, and nothing natural can do that. So his whole account can be summed up as: production for use is good, money is a necessary evil, and making money from money is monstrous.'

I suspect this is a bit of a caricature, but I dont know Aristotle well enough to say precisely where it goes wrong. Could you go through it roughly sentence by sentence and tell me which bits a careful reader of the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics would accept, which bits are overstatements or conflate two different things Aristotle keeps apart, and whether the final one-liner actually follows? I'm less interested in textual scholarship than in understanding what the natural/conventional distinction is actually doing in his argument, and whether there's anything in his account a modern economist could still find usefull.
