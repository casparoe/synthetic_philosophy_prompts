I am a third-year undergraduate in a course called "Philosophy and Future Technologies," and our final assignment is an adversarial-collaboration document rather than a position paper. I have been arguing for weeks with a friend in the machine learning group, and I have chosen the dispute between two camps that the course treats as live. On one side, the prosaic alignment camp holds that systems built from currently dominant methods — large-scale pretraining, reinforcement learning from human feedback, red-teaming, scalable oversight, and mechanistic interpretability — can in principle be made aligned and safe if those methods are refined and applied rigorously and incrementally. On the other side, the prosaic-skeptic camp holds that these methods can at best produce systems that behave well on the distributions we can evaluate, and that genuinely aligning powerful systems requires new conceptual foundations — a real theory of agency, value, or goal-directedness — before we can responsibly build and deploy them.

Please write the joint statement that a thoughtful, good-faith representative of each camp could sign after an adversarial collaboration. I am not interested in caricatures; I want each side's claims worded so that its own advocates would accept them as fair and faithful. I suspect, without asking you to defer to this, that one crux concerns whether behaving as intended under evaluation is the right target to aim at in the first place, and another concerns how much weight to place on the possibility of distribution shift revealing goals that the training signal never ruled out. The document should be constructive: it should separate what is genuinely load-bearing from what has been a fight about words, and it should leave the unresolved parts sharp rather than papering them over.

Structure the response in the following sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths. First, "Agreed claims" (around 500 words): a numbered list of substantive, non-vacuous claims both sides can sign about what alignment must secure, what current methods demonstrably do and do not do, and which failure modes are real; phrase each claim so it does not smuggle in one side's conclusion. Second, "Precise disagreements" (around 400 words): for each major point of divergence, give the question in neutral language, then state the prosaic position and the skeptic position in wording each side would accept as its own. Third, "Cruxes" (around 350 words): the sub-questions whose resolution would genuinely move at least one side, with a sentence each on why they matter to the disagreement. Fourth, "Tests and predictions" (around 400 words): for each crux, at least one concrete empirical or theoretical development both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Fifth, "Verbal or apparent disagreements" (around 250 words): any points where part of the debate looks terminological — for example, disputes over what counts as new insight, understanding, or alignment itself — and the statements both camps could accept once the terms are fixed.
