At a seminar last week, a behavioural economist was defending the view that the best model of risky choice is a form of risk-weighted expected utility, in which the objective probabilities of expected utility theory are replaced by decision weights: a non-linear function w(p) that overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate and large ones. Her stronger claim was that w(p) is psychologically real—that it corresponds to something in how people actually process risk, rather than being a mere mathematical convenience. I came away sceptical that this stronger claim is testable at all. After any pattern of choices, I suspect, one can always find some function w that makes the model fit, which would make the function a redescription of the data rather than an explanation of it.

What would count as genuine evidence that the weighting function is a real feature of the decision-maker's psychology?
