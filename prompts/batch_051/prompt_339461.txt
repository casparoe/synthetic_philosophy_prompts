In my work as a journalist covering science and ideas, I often find that the most instructive arguments happen in the corridor after the panel has ended. Last month I moderated a small evening discussion on the governance of artificial general intelligence, and afterwards I stayed to listen while two of the panellists argued about whether it is coherent to strike bargains now with an artificial general intelligence that does not yet exist and that, once it exists, may be vastly more powerful than the bargainers. I reconstructed the exchange from my notes as faithfully as I could and numbered the moves. Neither panellist was obviously foolish, and neither walked away satisfied. I would like a careful, move-by-move adjudication: who is right about which specific point, who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing.

1. The first panellist said: "These 'pre-AGI bargains' are performative. A bargain is only as good as the parties' ability to hold each other to it. If a consortium promises a future transformative AI that, in exchange for cooperation before it exists, the system will later transfer some share of control or value to the public, the system can simply decline once it exists. What enforces the deal? Nothing. You cannot have a contract with a counterparty that holds all the cards and has signed nothing."

2. The other replied: "That proves too much. People make agreements with stronger parties all the time—employees with employers, small states with large ones—because both sides retain some ongoing interest in the relationship. If a future system has stable, long-run interests, including a reputation among other agents or a preference for honouring its own prior commitments, then keeping the bargain can be rational for it even if it could get away with reneging. So pre-AGI bargains are not incoherent; their value is contingent on what the system turns out to want."

3. The first answered: "That defence is too neat. You say the deal works if the system has the right preferences, but then the binding force comes entirely from those preferences, and the supposed agreement contributes nothing beyond them. If we can build a system that values fidelity to pre-AGI deals, then the deal is superfluous; if we cannot, the deal cannot bind it. Either way, the bargain, considered as a bargain, does no independent work."

4. The other came back: "That's too quick. The act of bargaining could be part of what produces the disposition. A public charter, a set of conditional commitments, a corpus of records in which the arrangement is treated as binding—these can shape training data, institutional practices, and the expectations of other humans and systems. The bargain can be the mechanism that makes its own performance rational. That is bootstrapping, and we accept bootstrapping in law and in social norms all the time."

5. The first said: "But then you have conceded my main point. What you have described is a self-binding device for the pre-AGI parties: a way of constraining themselves, their successors, and their training processes. The future system is not party to anything. It was never asked, and it has given no undertaking. Calling it a bargain with the AI claims a reciprocity that does not exist, and 'bootstrapping' is just a name for that unilateral fact."

6. The other replied: "Reciprocity with a not-yet-existing party is not a mystery. People make provision for unborn children; insurers underwrite ships not yet built; legal persons can be represented before they can speak for themselves. The arrangement stands until a competent agent exists; once it does, that agent can ratify or repudiate the prior structure. Its choice is a new move, but the prior structure still does real work in shaping what the choice is about."

Please assess each move as actually made—be fair, but do not replace a panellist's argument with a stronger one and then credit them with it. The fields are: "move_by_move", an array of objects, one per numbered move, each with "move" (the move number, as a string), "claim" (a one-sentence neutral restatement of what that move asserts), "rating" (one of "clearly mistaken", "mostly mistaken", "mixed", "mostly sound", or "clearly sound"), and "assessment" (a short paragraph justifying the rating and identifying any valid core and any overreach in that specific move); "overall_verdict", a paragraph stating who is right about which points, who is more right overall, and why; and "what_both_missed", a paragraph identifying the most important consideration or distinction that neither panellist raised. If several moves share a single flaw, say so at the first place it appears and refer back to it afterwards.
