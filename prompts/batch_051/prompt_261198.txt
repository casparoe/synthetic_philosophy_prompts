Over the past year I have been using a distinction I have not seen drawn quite this way, and I would like it checked rather than indulged. I call the two sides frontier acquisitiveness and rank acquisitiveness. Frontier acquisitiveness is wanting more of a good considered on its own: more years of health, more reliable crops, a larger repertoire of skills, higher yield from the same land, more problems solved. Rank acquisitiveness is wanting more relative to other people: a larger house than one's neighbors, a higher place on a list, the inability to tolerate being outdone. My thesis is that the ordinary moral case against greed is, almost everywhere, a case against rank acquisitiveness in disguise, while frontier acquisitiveness is not only innocent but is the main engine of the gains in human welfare we usually praise. A farmer alone on an island who labors every year for a better harvest seems to me admirable; it is only when the farmer needs the harvest to beat a neighbor's that the word greed starts to feel apt. From this I have also drawn a policy conclusion: we should discourage positional arms races and status competition through taxation and regulation, but deliberately protect and even celebrate open-ended absolute wanting, because that is what medicine, technique, and institutions actually run on.

I suspect the distinction may collapse under pressure. Maybe all wanting is eventually comparative, because even more health is measured against a baseline other people supply. Maybe frontier acquisitiveness has its own characteristic vices, such as a grinding inability to be satisfied or a blindness to costs one is not personally paying. And maybe some rank acquisitiveness is harmless or even useful. Before I keep using the terms, I would like an honest assessment. Is the distinction coherent, or does the absolute side quietly reduce to the relative side? Has this already been worked out under another name? And where exactly does it break: what is the strongest case that frontier acquisitiveness can be a vice, and that rank acquisitiveness can be a benefit? Please write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a thoughtful letter.
