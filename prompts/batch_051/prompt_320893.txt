I sit on the conduct committee of the professional body that registers clinical-trial statisticians. Our role is to decide which complaints about long-past conduct should proceed to a full hearing and which are time-barred. Our recent record:

- We declined to hear a complaint that a statistician had misappropriated research funds fourteen years earlier. The accounting records had been destroyed, and the respondent retired five years ago.
- We heard a complaint that a statistician had falsified a safety-monitoring report eighteen years earlier. The report had recently been recovered in full, and the respondent still signs safety reports for the same sponsor.
- We heard a complaint that a statistician had harassed a junior colleague sixteen years earlier. The colleague had kept a contemporaneous file of messages, and the respondent now supervises junior staff.
- We declined to hear a complaint about a single, isolated failure to keep proper records for one trial visit twelve years earlier. The documents could be recovered, the respondent remains in post, and there has been no repetition since.
- We heard a complaint about a decision, twenty-three years ago, to change a trial's primary endpoint after seeing the data. The statistical code and correspondence survived, and the respondent still designs trials.

We now face a new complaint. A statistician is alleged to have invented data for a commercial trial nineteen years ago. The fabricated data, the analysis code, and a contemporaneous internal note pointing to the fabrication all survive and are unambiguous. The respondent left trial work shortly afterwards and now only teaches statistics, with no contact with patients, sponsors, or trial data.

Should the committee hear the new complaint if it wishes to remain consistent with its own record?

I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in the principle our record seems to embody, the rival rules that fit our record equally well, and what each requires here, which of our earlier decisions is the closest analogue, what principle we would be adopting if the decision went either way, and which further case would best settle the rule we have really been applying. Please lead with your recommended decision in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; do not preface your answer or restate the question.
