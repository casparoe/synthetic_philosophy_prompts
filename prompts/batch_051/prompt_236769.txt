I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working through a question for a piece on cognitive outsourcing: when does relying on an AI assistant extend someone's mind, and when does it quietly replace understanding with deference? I want to stress-test the standard philosophical framework by varying its canonical case.

In Clark and Chalmers's original extended-mind scenario, Inga simply recalls that the museum is on 53rd Street, while Otto, who has a memory impairment, has written the address in a notebook he always carries and consults it when the question arises. Clark and Chalmers argue that the notebook entry plays the same role as Inga's dispositional memory, so that if Inga believes the museum is on 53rd Street, then Otto does too.

Now change the external resource. Otto discards the notebook and relies instead on a personal AI assistant built by a company and trained partly on other users' data. The assistant is continuously updated, only partly transparent to Otto, and occasionally wrong: it confabulates, gives stale answers, or slants its responses in ways that serve the company's design. Otto no longer records things for himself; he asks, accepts, and acts, even in domains he once knew well. On the surface, the original parity conditions seem to be met — the assistant is constantly available, automatically endorsed, and consulted as easily as memory — but Otto now stands to the stored information as a client to a service rather than as its author or editor.

How does the analysis change? In particular: Does the parity argument still support saying that Otto believes, or knows, what the assistant reports? Does the variation show that belief and knowledge can come apart from understanding in a way the notebook case concealed? Which features of the assistant — shared and corporate provenance, opacity, fallibility, the fact that its outputs track another agent's aims, Otto's passivity — do the real philosophical work, and which are merely incidental? And what condition, if any, would a revised parity principle need to add about ownership, transparency, or self-trust?

Please defend a definite verdict rather than a survey of options, and then tell me what would change your mind: identify the strongest consideration against your verdict and the point in your reasoning where it is most likely to fail.
