My friend and I were up half the night arguing about whether courage still means anything once you take war and fighting out of the picture. He kept leaning on this line from Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, III.6: "But even death, we should hold, does not in all circumstances give an opportunity for Courage: for instance we do not call a man courageous for facing death by drowning or disease." His reading was that courage in the proper sense needs a noble death, or at least a sudden deadly peril where you can act and defend yourself or die well, and war is the fullest case. But I think that's too hasty.

Here's the case I kept pushing. Take an ICU fellow in March 2020, before vaccines, in a hospital with no backup staff. She's terrified, cant sleep, could quit for a telehealth job the same day, and has enough PPE to be careful but not enough to feel safe; she goes in anyway and intubates one hypoxic patient after another. She isnt fearless or reckless—she's scared and does it anyway, mostly because they'd die if she didnt and she cant make herself leave them. That means she's risking exactly the kind of death by disease that the quoted line says doesn't give an opportunity for courage.

From the passage and the chapter around it, the criteria we settled on for courage in the strict sense are: the thing faced must be genuinely fearful, with death as the paradigm; it must be faced for the sake of the noble; the person must fear and endure in the right way and at the right time, not rashly or insensibly; and the danger must be one where the person can act well rather than just passively undergo it. Go through these one by one for the ICU fellow and say whether she qualifies. Then say whether the "drowning or disease" line is doing real work or whether it's just a battlefield-shaped bias that should make us amend the criteria. Which feature of her case does the real work either way?
