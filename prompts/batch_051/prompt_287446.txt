I write about logic, language, and the occasional AI story for a general-audience science-and-ideas magazine, and I've agreed to give a thirty-minute talk at a local ideas evening next month. The audience will be around forty curious adults with no formal logic background, one whiteboard, and the title the organizers requested is "Does every statement have to be true or false?" I want to do better than a greatest-hits tour of the usual examples. From reader mail and past Q&As I've noticed that people run together at least three very different cases: a statement about tomorrow, a statement that quietly presumes something false (the bald king of France is, I suspect, now more familiar than useful), and a borderline case such as whether a particular chip is blue. My own hunch is that the genuine stumbling block is the step after the idea is introduced: once you say a statement is neither true nor false, you seem to have just made a true-or-false claim about that very statement, and the puzzle comes back. I'd like the talk to land on that feeling and let it sit, rather than resolve it too quickly.

Plan how I should teach this. I'm not asking you to explain truth-value gaps to me; I need the order in which the pieces have to go so the idea feels earned rather than asserted, the single carrying example and why it should be that one (including which of the other two cases must be kept away from it and handled at what point), the misconceptions the audience will form — I expect at least "neither true nor false" means "meaningless," "it just comes down to opinion," "a gap is just our ignorance," and the conflation of the law of excluded middle with bivalence — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. End with one check that would tell me they have understood the idea rather than merely nodded along, and say plainly what I should refuse to explain at this level.
