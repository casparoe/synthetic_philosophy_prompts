Two researchers who work on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment have spent a year disagreeing about whether the alignment problem should be stated narrowly or substantively. One — call this the intent-alignment view — holds that an AI system is aligned just in case it reliably does what its principal intends or instructs, within the scope of the principal's standing instructions and legitimate authority; on this view, questions about what a principal should want belong to ethics, not to the technical problem of alignment. The other — the value-alignment view — holds that alignment is only adequately stated as a normative relation: a system is aligned when it acts on the right values, or at least on the interests of affected parties properly understood, with the principal's occurrent intentions serving as fallible evidence and a default heuristic rather than as the criterion. Both grant that these two notions can come apart in practice: a system can be intent-aligned while dangerous, and a system can depart from the letter of an instruction while being, on the value-alignment view, better aligned. Their fight is over which notion deserves to define the field's target, benchmarks, and safety arguments.

Write the joint statement this collaboration should produce: a document both can sign while the residual disagreement remains exact. Structure it as follows.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of non-vacuous claims both parties accept after the collaboration. It should record the concessions each side has made, not just restate truisms. Include at least one claim about how the term "alignment" is actually used in the field, at least one claim about a concrete case in which a system satisfies one notion while violating the other, and at least one claim about the different operational measures or benchmarks each notion licenses.

2. Disagreements. A table with a row for each remaining substantive issue. One column gives the intent-alignment formulation and one the value-alignment formulation, each phrased so that its own side would sign it. Avoid rows that are simple negations of one another; where a supposed disagreement is merely verbal, say so rather than entering it as a difference.

3. Cruxes. For each crux — a sub-question whose resolution would move at least one side — give a neutral formulation both accept, then a concrete case, argument, or measurement both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance, so that a result would update at least one of them.

I expect the cruxes to include at least the following three; confirm that each is genuine or show where it dissolves, and add any others that matter:

(a) whether there is a coherent, operationalizable notion of what a principal would intend under full information and reflection that does not already smuggle in a substantive value theory;

(b) whether corrigibility — the disposition to accept correction or shutdown — is better analyzed as deference-to-intent or as a value-ranking that places continued human steering above the system's current objective;

(c) whether familiar failure modes such as specification gaming, sycophancy, and deceptive behavior are best classified as failures of intent-execution (the system failed to do what the principal meant) or as failures of value-pursuit (the system did what was specified, and what was missing was not in the specification but in the values).

Do not resolve the dispute or declare a winner; the statement should make the two positions and their residual differences precise enough that both parties would put their names to it.
