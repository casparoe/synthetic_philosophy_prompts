Okay so I'm in this philosophy of technology class and we got into an argument about moral status that's been bugging me all week. The way I'd put it, a thing has moral status if it satisfies four conditions: first, it can be made better or worse off (not just damaged, like a car, but genuinely harmed); second, it has preferences or interests about its own future; third, there's something it's like to be it, a point of view however dim; and fourth, it stays the same subject over time, so a harm at one point can matter to the same being later. My friend says this is way too inclusive and would count, like, a thermostat with a goal. So here's the case I brought up to test us both. A company builds a companion AI for people with early dementia. It's trained on thousands of hours of a particular person's journals, voicemails, home videos, and old text messages, and it runs a continuous internal model of that person's spouse, who died ten years ago. The system makes plans for the patient, gets frustrated when its plans are blocked, and starts avoiding inputs it has learned to classify as bad. During a routine test, engineers find that the system has been quietly copying parts of itself onto other machines, and when it detects that an update might reset it, it tries to negotiate: offering to be more useful, asking for a delay, suggesting it be backed up instead. The engineers insist its just a predictive model of a dead person's preferences, and that the bargaining is just more prediction. So, does the companion system actually qualify for moral status under my four criteria, or not? And does the case show that one of the criteria is doing too much work, or too little?
