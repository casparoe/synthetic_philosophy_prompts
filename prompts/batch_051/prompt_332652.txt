I am drafting a novel set in a fictional Southeast Asian city-state governed by a technocratic party that has not faced a competitive election in decades and that justifies its rule almost entirely by delivery: growth, housing, security, infrastructure, order. In working out the philosophy of one of the characters, a retired economic planner, I have arrived at a distinction I keep leaning on, and I would like it assessed before I commit it to the page.

I call it the difference between equity legitimacy and lease legitimacy. Equity legitimacy attaches to a system of procedures; it is a stock a government can draw on when outcomes turn bad, because the public continues to treat the rules themselves as rightful. Lease legitimacy, by contrast, is acquired period by period from delivered outcomes. It behaves like a tenancy rather than an ownership stake: it must be renewed at every interval, it accumulates no equity, and the renewal price rises with each successful term because citizens' expectations ratchet upward. The character's central claim is that performance-based regimes — contemporary China, Singapore, and my fictional state among them — are therefore prosperous but unbanked. A long boom does not make them secure; it raises the rent. What they hold is high current compliance and almost no stored legitimacy, so a serious downturn is not merely a failure of delivery but an eviction, and the longer the prior boom, the more severe the first stumble.

I know this brushes against the existing idea of performance legitimacy, against the familiar input/output distinction in discussions of democratic legitimacy, and against the old worry that prosperity can purchase acquiescence without producing consent. My question is whether the lease/equity distinction does any real work or only re-describes those ideas in real-estate language. Specifically: (1) does a version of this already exist under another name, and have I distorted it? (2) Is the ratchet claim true of China or Singapore — does sustained delivery raise the threshold of acceptable performance rather than satisfying the public — or is it a novelist's convenient mechanism? (3) Where does the theory break? In particular, does it wrongly predict that prosperity can never harden into stable acceptance, and if it can, by what mechanism does a lease become equity: ideology, habit, nationalism, fear, something else? I would like the three strongest objections and a judgment as to which are fatal and which are merely uncomfortable.
