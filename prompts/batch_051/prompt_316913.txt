You're a professional philosopher of science, and I'd like you to think carefully about this — maybe brainstorm the hardest test cases before you answer. I'm a retired physicist who's only recently gotten into philosophy, and I've been trying to improve on Popper. I call my idea the residual strain criterion. Falsifiability, I now think, is aimed at the wrong level (statements or theories), because what actually seperates science from pseudoscience is how a field behaves after a failed prediction. In a material, stress causes either elastic deformation (it springs back) or plastic deformation (it stays bent). Fields do the same thing. In a real science, when a risky prediction fails, the field deforms plastically: a model gets patched or scrapped, a textbook sentence gets rewritten, the training of the next generation changes, and the failure leaves a permanent trace. In a pseudoscience, the deformation is elastic: there's a flurry of excuses, reinterpretation, blaming confounding factors, and then the practice returns to it's exactly prior shape, ready for the next failure. The Michelson-Morley null result eventually bent physics permanently; astrology survives any number of failed forecasts without permanent deformation. So my claim is: science is whatever undergoes plastic deformation under predictive failure, pseudoscience whatever only deforms elastically. I can already see this might just be Popper with extra steps, or something somebody worked out under another name. Give me the three strongest objections to it as a demarcation criterion, say whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable, and tell me where it breaks — especially whether some elastic response (tweaking auxiliary assumptions) is legitimate inside real science, and whether my criterion misclassifies any obvious cases.
