I work in a group that studies mechanistic anomaly detection: deciding, when a monitoring system flags deviations in a complex system such as an engine, a cell, or a hospital workflow, whether two flags issue from the same underlying mechanism or from different ones. The field has been stuck for years on the question of what makes two anomalies count as the same mechanism. One camp holds that two anomalies are the same mechanism just in case they involve the same network of entities and activities, the same causal wiring, even when their surface symptoms differ. Another holds that a mechanism should be individuated by its function: two flags have the same mechanism when they represent the same functional failure mode, such that the same kind of intervention would address both, however different their physical realization. A third holds that mechanism identity is relative to the detection model: two anomalies count as the same mechanism when the same model or monitoring signature handles both, and there is no deeper fact. Each camp produces pairs of cases it finds decisive, such as same wiring with different function, same function with different wiring, and models that lump or split depending on granularity, and yet nobody moves. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a verbal dispute about the word mechanism, a substantive dispute about the causal structure of the world, a dispute about standards of evidence or about which cases count as data, or are the three camps answering different questions under one heading? If it is a mixture, say which part is which; if it is a disguised disagreement about starting intuitions neither side can argue for, say that as well. Then tell me what would count as progress given the diagnosis, such as a distinction, an intervention study, a formal result, or a change of question, or argue that no progress should be expected and why. My own hypothesis, which you may reject, is that the first camp is answering an ontological question about what mechanisms are, the second an engineering question about what ought to be repaired, and the third a modeling question about what a detector ought to output, and that the debate has stalled because each camp keeps treating its answer as an answer to the other two questions. Please write the response as continuous prose, in the form of an ordinary email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
