I serve on a government commission considering whether our state should issue a formal apology for the forced removal of a minority population nearly a century ago. The facts are not in dispute and no one defends the removal, yet after months of deliberation we are deadlocked, and I suspect we are not really arguing about the apology at all. One faction says a present-day state cannot sincerely apologize for deeds done by people long dead without either empty theater or falsely implying that today's citizens are guilty. A second faction says the state is a continuous corporate entity that can perform speech acts across generations, and that refusing to apologize is itself a continuing wrong. A third faction says both camps are fixated on words and the only real question is whether to transfer land or pay compensation to descendants. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this is — a factual one about the persistence of harm, a conceptual one about collective identity or what an apology does, a difference in what counts as the relevant moral wrong, or something else — and tell me what, given that diagnosis, could actually move the commission.
