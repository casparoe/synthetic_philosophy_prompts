I've been reading Hobbes's Leviathan, especially chapter 14, where he argues that in the state of nature covenants are void because there is no common power to enforce them—something like words alone are too weak unless a sword stands behind them. Compare that with the standard game-theoretic account of self-enforcing agreements, where a deal can hold without any third party because the long-run cost of defecting, through lost reputation or future retaliation, is bigger than the short-run payoff. On the surface they look opposed: Hobbes says no enforcement by a common power means no real covenant, while the game-theorist says future interactions are the enforcement. But I suspect Hobbes could except the game-theoretic story as a kind of enforcement, just not the sovereign-backed kind he has in mind. So my question is where exactly the disagreement lies. Is Hobbes implicitly assuming a one-shot state of nature, or at least people with high discount rates and no reputation? If people are patient and care about being trusted, does his argument against covenants still go through? What would he have to change about his psychology for a reputation-only covenant to count as an actual covenant? And if he changes nothing, which step in the game-theoretic argument is he denying—the rationality of keeping promises to preserve future dealings, the ability of people to coordinate on reputational punishment, or something else? I dont care whether you think Hobbes was right; I want the precise point of collision.
