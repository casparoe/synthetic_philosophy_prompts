I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether his appeals to purity and loyalty were real moral reasons or just strong feelings dressed up as reasons. He turned the charge back on me: he said my appeals to harm and fairness are just as much the product of a particular moral psychology, and that moral foundations theory — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty — predicts exactly this kind of mutual deafness. The argument made me aware of an asymmetry I was relying on but could not defend, and I want you to examine it directly.

In ordinary political and moral argument, we tend to treat harm-based and fairness-based considerations as if they need no further justification. If someone can show that a policy would hurt people, or would treat relevantly similar people unequally, that is enough to place the matter on the moral agenda. But we do not extend the same standing to the other foundations. We treat disgust at a degrading practice, concern about the subversion of an authority, or partiality toward one's own group as things that need a harm-based translation before they can count as reasons at all — otherwise they are dismissed as bias, mere emotion, or rationalization. The asymmetry is not exactly between left and right; it is between foundations. Care and fairness get to be reason-giving in their own right, while sanctity, authority, and loyalty get only borrowed authority, if any.

I want to know whether anything justifies that asymmetry. Does moral foundations theory itself justify it, or does its descriptive finding that self-identified liberals and conservatives weight these foundations differently give me no normative license at all — leaving the asymmetry as merely my own moral psychology wearing philosophical clothes? What is the strongest principle that could generate the asymmetry, and does it survive hard cases? Consider at least these: a loyalty-based obligation that produces no measurable harm to outsiders; a purity-based prohibition that tracks a real contamination risk or serves to bind a community over time; and an authority-based rule that prevents a coordination failure. Test the principle against them rather than just appealing to them.

I want a committed response: either keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up, and say exactly what that verdict implies about whether the left–right difference in moral psychology tells us anything about who is right.
