Alright, here's one Ive been mulling over. Fuller famously argued that law has an internal morality: eight desiderata a system has to meet to count as law at all — generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, no contradictions, no demanding the impossible, reasonable stability over time, and congruence between the rules as announced and what officials actually do. The last one always struck me as doing most of the work, because its what separates a legal order from a rigged game where the published rule is just decoration.

Now suppose a jurisdiction moves to what people call 'rules as code'. The statute is drafted as an executable program, and the code, not the natural-language text, is the authoritative version of the rule. Officials apply the law by running the program. On the face of it, Fuller's congruence desideratum becomes trivially satisfied: the same artifact both states the rule and applies it. But I suspect the problems have only migrated. Legislators may not understand what the code does in edge cases, dependencies get patched, and the rule a citizen reads on the portal can drift from what the runtime actually enforces after an update.

So here's the real question: does Fuller's account of the rule of law survive 'rules as code', or does it need repair? If it survives, show how the eight desiderata translate when rules are executable; if it doesn't, identify which desideratum breaks and say what, if anything, should replace it. Don't summarise Fuller for me — I want the argument.
