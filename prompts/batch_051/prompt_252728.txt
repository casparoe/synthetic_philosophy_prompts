Design a classroom activity for an upper-level metaphysics seminar — twelve students, ninety minutes — whose goal is to make participants feel the force of Bradley's regress about the unity of the proposition as a structural trap they walk into themselves, rather than as a piece of philosophy they hear described.

Set up the target idea as follows. A simple subject–predicate sentence, such as the claim that a particular die is cubical, seems to be about at least two things: the die and the property of being cubical. But a proposition is not merely its constituents listed side by side. Something must combine them, whether one calls it predication, exemplification, or instantiation. If a relation does the combining, then that relation appears to stand in need of being combined with each of the things it relates; for that, a further combining relation seems required, and the same question arises again. Each added piece reopens the gap it was introduced to close. Denying that any combiner is needed, in turn, seems to amount to giving up the very distinction between a unified fact and a mere bundle or list. The activity should get students to generate this dilemma from inside a task that refuses to let them finish it.

The activity must not begin with a lecture, and the term Bradley's regress should not be spoken until the debrief. It should be a role-play, construction task, or staged deliberation with concrete rules and materials: something participants do with their hands, their movement, or their decisions, not a worksheet and not an open discussion. Arrange the central mechanic so that the regress appears in what happens. A task of assembling, linking, or completing a fact or proposition should succeed at every local step while failing globally: each connection is easy to make, but the completed whole can never be reached, because the same move is called for at every level and the rules provide no level at which a stopping point is available. The facilitator should not have to explain the regress in order for participants to experience it; explanation belongs to the debrief.

Design the activity so that a facilitator could run it from your description alone, without further reading. Prescribe the following sections in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths:

1. Concept and intended insight — about 150 words. An overview of the activity, its objective, and exactly what participants should come away seeing.

2. Materials and setup — about 100 words. What to prepare, how to arrange the room, and any instructions or role cards to print in advance.

3. Rules and timed sequence — about 350 words. The rules participants operate under, the stages of the session with timings, and what the facilitator should and should not say at each stage.

4. Debrief — about 300 words. How to elicit in participants' own words the structure they have just enacted, and how to map specific moments of the activity onto the philosophical structure of the regress, including where a solution might block it and what that solution costs.

5. Likely failure modes and fixes — about 150 words. What will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run, and concrete corrections.

6. What the activity cannot reproduce — about 100 words. Which feature of the philosophical force of Bradley's regress the activity inevitably leaves out, and what the facilitator must therefore supply.

Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation; the design should stand on its own.
