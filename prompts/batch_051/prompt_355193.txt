I'm a PhD student in linguistics (historical syntax, mostly), and I've realized I can't give a coherent anwser to a question that keeps coming up in our morphology reading group: what actually is a grammatical rule? My advisor says "rule" all the time, and everyone nods, but as soon as I think about the messy cases the word stops doing any obvious work for me.

Could you propose a working defintion of "grammatical rule" that gets these cases right, or at least sorts them in a principled way? (a) the English plural -s; (b) go/went; (c) sing/sang; (d) good/better; (e) a kid saying goed or foots; (f) a borrowed plural kept as an irregular form, like cacti, especially once speakers also start saying cactuses; (g) a one-off speech error like starting to say the cat are and self-correcting; (h) a prescriptive rule like don't split infinitives; (i) a sound change with no exceptions in a given environment, like final devoicing in German or Grimm's law in the history of English; (j) a pattern that applies to only a handful of lexical items, like ox/oxen.

I'm confident (a) is a rule and (g) isn't, and I don't know what to do with (b), (d), (h), or (j); I also can't tell whether (i) is a rule in the same sense as (a). Propose the definition, run it through all ten, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Where I'd especially value your honesty is separating what working linguists largely agree on from what is genuinely contested (for example, whether rules are psychologically real, or whether a sound change is a "rule" in the same way a morphological pattern is). Please don't present a contested view as the consensus; tell me where the field actually splits. Not looking for a literature survey — I want your best reconstruction of the distinction itself.
