For a grad seminar I'm teaching on epistemic logic, I want a handout that works as a decision tree sorting the main responses to the problem of logical omniscience. Quick setup for context: in standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge, if an agent knows P and P entails Q, the agent automatically knows Q, and every agent knows every logical truth — which is obviously false of real agents. The tree should start from the commitment "I accept that real agents are not logically omniscient, and I want a formal model that reflects that."

Each branch node should be a claim or choice someone can answer without already knowing the labels. Things like: should closure under known entailment be kept as an idealization, or should the semantics genuinely invalidate it? Do we need worlds where classical logic doesn't hold, or can we keep the worlds classical and locate the failure elsewhere? Should the model distinguish what's implicit in the agent's information state from what the agent can explicitly report? Is the limitation best modeled semantically (in terms of which worlds are accessible) or syntactically (in terms of what sentences are stored or derivable)?

Leaves should include at least awareness-style models, impossible-worlds semantics, and syntactic or algorithmic models, plus any other genuinely distinct position you think earns a leaf (resource-bounded views, for instance, if you judge them distinct rather than a special case). For each leaf, a sentence or two on what it buys and what it costs.

Also: mark unoccupied combinations of answers and say briefly whether they're empty because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. For example, I suspect "give up logical omniscience but keep only possible worlds" is just incoherent, but I want you to say why rather than hand-wave. And flag any pairs of leaves your tree cant really seperate without loading in extra assumptions.

Audience is grad students who've seen Kripke semantics but not the literature on this problem. Aim for maybe six to eight leaves and a couple pages total. And one thing I really want: keep the settled stuff (standard possible-worlds semantics does yield logical omniscience) clearly separate from the contested stuff (which repair is best, whether giving up closure is too high a price, whether the right fix is semantic or syntactic). Dont present one camp as the consensus when the field genuinely disagrees.
