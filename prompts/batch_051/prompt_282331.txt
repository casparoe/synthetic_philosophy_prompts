I teach a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and after last week's class I can't shake a worry one of my students raised. The big cross-national democracy monitors—Freedom House, V-Dem, the Economist's index—regularly score countries like Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and, in the more contested recent case, the U.S. as "backsliding democracies" or "flawed democracies." What struck me is that the individual changes that get a country coded as backsliding are almost always individually bland: a judicial-appointments reform, a redrawn electoral map, a media-ownership rule, an emergency decree, a constitutional court being packed. Each of these things happens in plenty of countries that are not coded as backsliding. So the practice is to aggregate a pile of individually-defensible procedural changes into a single alarm-bell label, and then insist that the label isn't merely a partisan judgment about the incumbent, even though every backsliding government describes it's reforms as just one legitimate democratic choice among others.

I'd like you to put on your professional-political-philosopher hat (someone who works on democratic theory, not just punditry). Before you answer, brainstorm out loud a few rival definitions of democracy that could be running in the background: threshold vs. continuum, liberal vs. majoritarian, procedural vs. substantive. Then tell me: what does the practice of coding some declines as "backsliding" strictly presuppose about what democracy is, as opposed to what it merely fits well with? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined—where would several different theories of democracy license the same coding? And can the practice coherently distinguish backsliding from ordinary majoritarian disagreement about what a democracy should look like, or does it smuggle in the very liberal-democratic baseline that its targets contest? I'm not asking for a verdict on any particular country; I'm asking what the practice itself commits you to.
