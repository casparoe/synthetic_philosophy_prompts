You are a philosophy professor who works on collective responsibility and moral psychology. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm the criteria for blameworthiness before you apply them.

In my ethics class we learned that for something to count as blameworthy it needs at least: (1) it can act intentionally, (2) it can understand that what it's doing is wrong, and (3) there's some single "who" that owns the decision and can be the target of blame. That makes sense for people. But here's the case bugging me. Suppose there's a big online marketplace whose pricing and moderation calls get made by dozens of semi-independent teams plus automated systems; no single human, not even the CEO, sees the whole chain that produced a harmful outcome (say, systematically squeezing desperate users, or banning innocent sellers). Afterward, the internal review just says "that's what the system produced," and every individual employee did something that looked reasonable locally.

So: does the company itself qualify as blameworthy under my critera, or not? Go through each criterion and check it. Where it fails, say whether the criterion is doing real work or just sneaking in a bias toward individuals. Then, at the end: does this case show my criteria need fixing, or does it show companies are just the wrong kind of thing to blame in the first place?
