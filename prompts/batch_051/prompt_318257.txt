I am legal officer for the Republic of Velsuna, a territory that declared independence from its former metropolitan state nine years ago after a referendum, and which has governed itself continuously ever since. Velsuna has a permanent population of roughly two million, a settled and demarcated boundary, an effective government that levies taxes and operates courts, and, on paper at least, the capacity to enter into relations with other states: it has sent trade missions abroad and concluded memoranda of understanding with foreign commercial bodies. Yet not a single state recognizes it. The state from which it seceded still claims the territory and has persuaded every other government to withhold recognition, with the result that Velsuna is excluded from the regional union, cannot accede to the standard conventions on aviation, environmental cooperation, or banking supervision, and its passports are refused at every border.

Our position has long been that recognition is not what makes us a state. The 1933 Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States, which nearly every state in our region has ratified, provides in Article 1: "The state as a person of international law should possess the following qualifications: (a) a permanent population; (b) a defined territory; (c) government; and (d) capacity to enter into relations with the other states." It also provides, in Article 3, that "The political existence of the state is independent of recognition by the other states." The drafters in 1933 were preoccupied with the recognition of new governments after revolutions and coups in the Americas; it seems plain that no one at the conference had in view a functioning territorial government denied recognition by every other state for nearly a decade. I want a careful analysis of what the Convention's text, its purpose, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader today would take it to mean each yields in our case—and which of those approaches should govern. In particular: does the Convention treat Velsuna as a state now, or does statehood remain somehow incomplete while others refuse to acknowledge it?
