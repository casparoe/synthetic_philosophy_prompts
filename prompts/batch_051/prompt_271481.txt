I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep coming back to the standard meritocratic argument for steep inheritance taxes: a society that rewards effort and ability rather than accidents of birth cannot allow large fortunes to pass directly to the next generation, because inherited wealth gives some people an unearned head start. I'm trying to figure out whether this argument survives a shift in how advantage actually gets transmitted. Suppose the main channel of dynastic advantage is no longer a direct financial bequest but the early conversion of wealth into children's human capital — schools, tutoring, neighborhoods, networks, parental attention — reinforced by increasingly assortative mating, most of which happens long before an inheritance tax would apply. On that picture, does the equal-opportunity argument for inheritance taxes still go through? If so, which premise has to be true for it to survive, and does the argument now point somewhere else? If not, does it collapse into a different argument, for example about inequality of consumption or political power, and what is lost in that shift?
