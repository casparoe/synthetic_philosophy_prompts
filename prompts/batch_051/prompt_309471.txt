Im running a third-year undergrad seminar on republics, 18 students, 75 minutes, and next week we start Polybius before moving to the late Roman Republic. I want the students to come in having felt, in their own decisions, why the cycle of constitutions might actually happen, not just read about it. Design a classroom game/simulation built around the passage in Polybius, Histories Book VI, chapters 4–9, where he distinguishes the three good forms and their corrupt doubles (monarchy-kingship-tyranny-aristocracy-oligarchy-democracy-mob-rule) and then says Rome's mixed constitution checked the cycle. The game should not mention Polybius or the anacyclosis by name. It should be playable by 18 students in about 50 minutes, with 25 minutes of debrief. Each student gets a role in one of a few social groups, with concrete payoffs they can count. The rules should be arranged so that every good regime they try, when people act on their payoffs, mutates into the corrupt twin, and the only stable arrangement they can discover is something like a mixed one with multiple veto points. Include exact role cards, scores, voting rules, a timeline, and what to do if a group gets stuck or a faction exits. Then give a debrief script that maps each game phase back to a specific claim in that Polybius passage. End the debrief with a transfer question where students pick a current democratic crisis and say whether it is anacyclotic slippage or something else. Be honest about what the game cant reproduce: the passage is about long-run natural decay, not a 50-minute choice, and the facilitator will have to supply the scale. Also, if someone realizes the game is rigged to force a cycle, tell the facilitator how to handle that without pretending it isnt rigged.

Format: lead with the bottom line in the first sentence—one sentence on the core insight the game is meant to make them feel—then give the design and reasoning, no preamble, no restating of the question.
