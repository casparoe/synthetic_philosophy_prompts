I am drafting a novel in which a small coastal land trust plans to re-flood a drained salt marsh, and my most articulate objector is a field veterinarian who insists the project is not the mercy it appears to be. Her refrain is "Nature, red in tooth and claw": flooded again, the marsh will generate more voles and killdeer, more herons and raccoons, more parasitism and starvation—more sentient life, and therefore, she says, more suffering. Early readers warn me that she occasionally collapses into sloganeering, so I want to understand exactly what she is compressing. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that line could be used to make—for instance, empirical claims about whether wild animals' lives are on balance good or bad, claims about whether "natural" suffering counts morally, and claims about what restoring a wetland actually does to the number and quality of animal lives—and say for each whether it is trivial, plausible, or false, and which of them her conclusion actually needs? In particular, does the slogan by itself support "so we should not restore the marsh," or does that inference require further premises she has not stated?
