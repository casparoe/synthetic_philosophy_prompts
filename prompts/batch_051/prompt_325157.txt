Many legal systems still have criminal statutes on the books that are in practice never enforced — old offences concerning adultery, blasphemy, or Sunday trading, say. Some systems treat prolonged non-use as eventually extinguishing a law (desuetude); others insist that a validly enacted statute remains law until repealed no matter how long it has gone unenforced, while prosecutors simply leave it dormant. Reconstruct what each stance presupposes about what makes something a law and about what the rule of law requires, distinguishing what is strictly presupposed from what is merely compatible; then say whether the two stances rest on conflicting commitments or can be reconciled within a single account of legal validity. In particular, consider whether the second stance commits a legal system to the view that a rule can bind citizens even while officials treat it as a dead letter, and how that sits with the usual principle that ignorance of the law is no excuse. Finally, indicate what a legal system would have to change if it replaced the presupposition you find least defensible. Please write in British English, and end by saying how confident you are in your answer and singling out the point you are least sure of.
