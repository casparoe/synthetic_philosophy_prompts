I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, working on a feature about whether physics could have an end: whether there is, or could be, a final theory of the fundamental interactions, and — the question I most want to get right — whether we could ever know that we had reached it. Before I start interviewing physicists, I need the evaluative backbone for the piece. Write me the desiderata: what a candidate final theory has to deliver in order to count as final, or at least as a serious candidate for finality, and what would count as evidence that we had found it.

You are a philosopher of physics who has written on theory choice, underdetermination, and the limits of physical knowledge. Think carefully. Begin by brainstorming candidate requirements without censoring for conflict, then trim and sort them. For each requirement you keep, give the case or consideration behind it: the phenomena it must save, the inconsistencies it must resolve, the results it must be consistent with, the explanatory work it has to do. Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular research program — background independence, perturbative finiteness, uniqueness of the vacuum, computability of predictions, and so on.

The part I most need is the conflict check. Is any subset of the requirements jointly unsatisfiable? I am especially worried about four tensions. First, finality as uniqueness or self-explanation versus the apparent contingency of the constants and the possibility that they are environmental. Second, the demand for empirical confirmation versus the fact that a final theory's distinctive predictions would plausibly sit at energies we can never probe, so the theory could be true and final while being empirically indistinguishable from current physics. Third, the demand that the theory close off further "why" questions versus the historical pattern in which every theory has turned out to be effective and provisional. Fourth, the thought that a theory could be final and yet its distinctive predictions might be computationally intractable to derive, so finality in principle would be epistemically inert. Tell me which of these are real conflicts, which dissolve on inspection, and whether some requirement on the list forces a dilemma deeper than all four. Throughout, separate constraints on a theory's being final from constraints on our being justified in believing we had reached the end; these might be two different things, though I think they are often run together.

I am not asking for a reading list or bibliography, and I am not asking you to name the final theory. A brief scorecard of how current candidates such as string theory and loop quantum gravity fare against the list is welcome only insofar as it sharpens the criteria; keep the weight on the criteria themselves, and if a single requirement does the most exclusionary work, say which one and why.
