i've been collecting the standard ways people try to close the is-ought gap (hume's point that you can't get an 'ought' from a pile of 'is' premises) and the neighbouring naturalistic fallacy (moore's point that you can't, or shouldn't, define 'good' in purely descriptive terms). here are the big moves i have so far:

1. searle's promise move: from the fact that jones says he promises to pay smith five dollars, under ordinary conditions, plus plain facts about the institution of promising, you can derive that jones ought to pay smith five dollars.

2. the function move: from 'the function of a heart is to pump blood' and 'this heart isn't pumping blood' you get 'this heart is defective' — an evaluative conclusion out of flatly descriptive premises, via a thing's proper job (foot, macintyre, that style).

3. prior's trick: from 'london is the capital of england' infer 'either london is the capital of england or you ought not to torture babies' by disjunction introduction. valid inference, ought in the conclusion, no ought in the premise.

4. the instrumental move: from 'you want to catch the 7 bus' and 'the only way to catch it is to leave now' you get 'you ought to leave now' — merely hypothetical, but it's the standard baseline.

5. naturalistic reduction: 'good' just is some natural property like pleasure or desire-satisfaction, so an 'is' claim about that property is an 'ought' claim; moore's open question argument is the usual pushback.

i think that's basically all the standard ones. is there a sixth move that's genuwinely different — not a rewording of any of these, not a special case of #4, not just slotting a fresh natural property into #5? if there is, name it and say how it differs from all five; if there isn't, say why these five exhaust the options. start with the name of the move, and keep the whole thing under 250 words.
