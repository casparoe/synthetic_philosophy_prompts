I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been reading around the metaphysics of causation because the topic keeps surfacing in work on machine learning interpretability and debugging complex systems. The position I keep coming back to is associated with Russell: at the level of fundamental physics, the equations simply relate the state of a system at one time to its state at another, and nothing like a primitive relation of one event producing another shows up in that description. From that point of view, causation looks like an outdated holdover from prescientific habits of mind—the sort of fuzzy, higher-level notion you would expect to disappear once you have the real, lower-level equations in hand.

That picture appeals to me, but I am not sure what I am supposed to do with it in my own work. My day-to-day practice is saturated with causal talk: I write postmortems saying a misconfigured retry policy caused a thundering-herd failure, I choose which experiment to run by reasoning about what would happen if I intervened on one component, and I read distributed traces as showing how latency in one service propagates into another. If causation is genuinely a relic at the level of physics, it seems as though continuing to rely on it in engineering is a bit like discovering that my whole codebase depends on a deprecated API and simply refusing to refactor. So my question is: what would the mature, Russell-respecting version of engineering reasoning actually look like—how far should I go in eliminating causal language from postmortems, debugging, and system design, and where would I even start?
