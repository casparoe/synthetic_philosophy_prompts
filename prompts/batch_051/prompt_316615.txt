The Lucas–Penrose debate over whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems refute the claim that the human mind is a Turing machine seems to have been stalled for decades in a peculiar way. The formal core is uncontroversial: for any consistent, recursively axiomatizable system containing enough arithmetic, there is a sentence true on the standard interpretation of its symbols that the system cannot prove. Lucas and Penrose infer that, because a human can recognize the truth of such a sentence for any proposed machine while the machine cannot, the mind is not any such machine. The standard replies—that the inference requires the human to know the system is consistent, that the machine can reason about its own formalized limitations, or that the argument defeats only our ability to know which machine we are, not the possibility of our being one—have all been met with counter-replies, and yet the dispute remains unresolved. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is over whether introspection can supply premises precise enough to support a formal conclusion, but I want that diagnosis tested rather than assumed. Is this a disagreement about mathematical fact, about the limits of introspection, about what prove or see means here, or about which idealizations of human reasoning are legitimate? And, given the right diagnosis, would anything—a formal theorem, an epistemic principle, a sharper account of self-knowledge—actually count as progress?
