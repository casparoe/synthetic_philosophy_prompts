Over the past year I have been trying to understand population ethics, and I keep returning to the repugnant conclusion. As I understand it, the problem is this: for any population of, say, ten billion people with excellent lives, there is some vastly larger population — trillions of people, each with a life that is only barely worth living — such that, if we simply total up welfare, the second world comes out better. I cannot accept this, and I think I have found a way out, though I assume something like it already exists.

I call my proposal the Principle of Unrepeatable Goods. It rests on a distinction between two kinds of good in a life. Generic goods are fungible across persons: relief from pain, mild comfort, small pleasures. Unrepeatable goods are bound to particulars: a particular friendship, a particular achievement that only existed once, the experience of having made this specific thing. My first substantive claim is that the goods in an excellent life are not merely more of the goods in a barely-worth-living life; an excellent life is excellent partly because it contains unrepeatable goods. My second claim is that a life that is only barely worth living is, for that very reason, overwhelmingly generic in its goods — if it carried a substantial unrepeatable good, it would not be barely worth living in the morally relevant sense.

The principle itself says that the value of a world is the depth-weighted sum of its unrepeatable goods, plus a per-person allowance of generic goods that counts only up to a decent baseline. Generic goods beyond that baseline add zero, no matter how many lives carry them. I have not yet worked out exactly how to specify the baseline, and I expect that is a weak point.

Applied to the comparison: the first world has a vast number of unrepeatable goods, so its value is enormous. The second world has almost none, and its generic goods are capped, so adding trillions of barely-worth-living lives adds nothing. The first world wins, and the repugnant conclusion is blocked.

I know this can sound like Mill on higher and lower pleasures, or like a lexical view. I think it is different because I am not ranking one kind of good above another in advance; I am saying generic goods and unrepeatable goods aggregate according to different rules, because duplicating a generic good preserves it while duplicating an unrepeatable good is impossible — like a performance that cannot be repeated.

I want a candid assessment. Reconstruct the view in its strongest plausible form, identify the closest existing positions if any, say what it gets right, and then give the sharpest objections you can find, distinguishing those that are fatal from those that are merely uncomfortable. If I have misunderstood the repugnant conclusion or framed the question in a confused way, tell me that plainly rather than working with my setup.
