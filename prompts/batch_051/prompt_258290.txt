One move in the torture-vs-dust-specks debate that has always struck me as too fast is the assertion, made with the confidence of a mathematical result, that there must be something discontinuous in the trade-off between one large harm and an enormous number of tiny harms: that either the small harms are rounded down to zero, or the comparison flips sharply at some unlocatable threshold, and therefore no smooth moral arithmetic is available. That assertion is doing double duty — it is partly a descriptive claim about how human judges actually order such trade-offs, and partly a normative claim about how the trade-off ought to be structured — and I want to see how far an experiment can push on the descriptive half before the normative half has to be granted. Please design the strongest test you can of whether the apparent discontinuity and rounding-to-zero reflect a real feature of evaluative judgment or an artifact of the original example's setup. In particular, I want a design that measures where between one speck and the original 3^^^3-person version subjects place the crossover with a single fifty-year torture, whether the crossover is sharp or gradual under forced choice versus separate ratings of badness, whether substituting a concretely imaginable small harm (a paper cut, ten seconds of a headache, a stubbed toe) changes the pattern, and whether subjects report an explicit policy of rounding small harms to zero or merely lose track of large numbers. For each component, say what result would favor a lexical or threshold structure, what result would favor a smooth additive structure, and what result would indicate that the original puzzle is mostly scope insensitivity to numbers too large to represent. Then say exactly what a clean result would and would not settle: could it ever bear on the normative question of how harms of different sizes ought to be weighed, or would it only characterize the measuring instrument that the thought experiment was supposed to bypass? Reply in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion.
