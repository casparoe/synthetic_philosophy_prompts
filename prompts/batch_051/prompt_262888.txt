I do compliance for a small startup that offers "destructive whole-brain emulation": we scan a client's brain at a resolution fine enough to later run it as a simulation, but the scan destroys the biological brain in the process, so the client either dies or moves into the machine, depending on who you ask. Our national Human Research Act says: "No person shall perform, or assist in performing, a procedure on a human being that is reasonably expected to result in the permanent loss or irreversible alteration of the subject's consciousness, memory, or personal identity." The Act was drafted in the early 90s and was clearly aimed at psychosurgery, deep-brain stimulation, memory-altering drugs — not at uploading, which nobody foresaw. We have a fully consenting patient with terminal cancer who wants to be uploaded before the disease kills him. Are we going to be in breach of that clause? I'm less interested in a quick yes/no than in seeing the readings seperated out: what the plain words say, what the purpose of the clause says, what the people who drafted it would have said if you'd described our machine to them, and what a reasonable reader today would take it to cover. Then tell me which reading should control, and say explicitly what your answer commits you to about whether the brain is a computer and whether the simulation is him. (For this, assume the simulation, once it's actually run, passes every behavioral test for being the same person, but no one knows whether it's conscious or just behaving exactly right.)
