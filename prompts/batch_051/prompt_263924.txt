I'm working through Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life for a reading group, and I got into an argument about whether a pretty popular intro book on him is being fair. The book sums the essay up roughly like this. Nietzsche's target is the modern historical man, who is sick from knowing too much and feeling too little. History, Nietzsche says, has value only insofar as it serves life, and it can serve life in three ways. Monumental history picks out past greatness to inspire present action, but it risks turning the past into a distorted gallery of heroes and making the living feel small by comparision. Antiquarian history preserves the small, local, inherited stuff that gives a community its identity, but it risks mummifying the past and refusing to let anything go. Critical history hauls the past before a tribunal and condemns it, which the book says is necessary if a people is ever to free itself, but it risks pretending we can slice ourselves off from the very inheritance that made us. The book then says Nietzsche's takeaway is that a healthy culture needs a mix of all three, tailored to whatever the present needs, and that Nietzsche has no problem at all with a history that bends the record if the lie is life-enhancing, because for him truth about the past matters only as a means to vitality. Something in the last part bothers me: I thought Nietzsche was also obsessive about honesty, and I can't tell whether the book has actually said something false or just collapsed a subtle point into a slogan. Could you go through which parts of that summary are accurate, which are simplifications, and which Nietzsche would explicitly push back on? And does the book's takeaway survive once the sloppy parts are fixed, or is it mostly carried by the misreading? Also, flag where the scholarly consensus is solid and where this is genuinely contested, so I don't go repeating a contested reading as if it were settled.
