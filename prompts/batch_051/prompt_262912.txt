I'm on the methodology committee of a forecasting platform that aggregates crowd probabilities into a single number per question. We're rebuilding the aggregator next quarter and are stuck on whether to make it common-prior-based or not. The options on the table are roughly: (a) impose a shared baseline prior and aggregate as if the platform is one Bayesian agent updating from that prior; (b) let each forecaster have their own prior and take a robust average (mean/median/trimmed) of their reported probabilities; (c) report both the aggregate and a spread over what happens under different putatively-reasonable priors.

The pro-common-prior camp says that a straight average of Bayesian updates isn't, in general, the update of any single Bayesian: it's marginals and conditionals won't line up, related questions can end up mutually inconsistent, and a user who can see the internal spread can in principle pump the aggregate by trading between related questions. The anti camp says the heterogeneity is load-bearing information: forcing everyone onto a shared prior is a way for whoever picks the prior to launder their own opinion into the numbers, and the "incoherence" of the average is exactly what preserves the minority views that turn out to be right on weird questions.

Write me a short policy memo recommending one of the options (or a variant), with a bottom line a developer could actually implement. I want the memo to treat the common prior assumption as a modeling choice rather than a theorem you just apply: where the assumption does real work, what concretely breaks in the aggregate when it's violated, and the strongest steelman of the losing side. And don't just survey the options — tell the committee which one to pick and why.
