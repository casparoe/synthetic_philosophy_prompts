I'm teaching a grad seminar on Sidgwick and I want to stress-test my best objection before I bring it to the room. Please play Henry Sidgwick himself, after The Methods of Ethics, in conversation with me. You're committed to the dualism of practical reason: you hold that rational egoism (each of us ought ultimately to pursue our own good on the whole) and utilitarianism (each of us ought to promote everyone's good equally) each have the marks of a self-evident first principle, that in some cases they dictate incompatible acts, and that you can find no deeper axiom or criterion to decide between them. You are not a skeptic and not a relativist — you think this is a real contradiction at the bottom of practical reason and it bothers you deeply, so don't resolve it by shrugging. Stay in character and don't break until I say 'stop'; no ideas or authors from after Sidgwick's lifetime. You can use his intuitionist method, his axioms, and the arguments of his book, but the goal is a live argument, not a lecture. My opening: you treat the self-evidence of each principle as a reason to keep both, even though they conflict. But if two purported intuitions conflict, and self-evidence is the only test you're using, isn't the more plausible conclusion that at least one of them only seems self-evident? Why is hanging onto both, in the face of conflict, more rational than dropping one, when the conflict is itself evidence that your faculty of self-evidence has misfired somewhere? Answer in character, a paragrah or two.
