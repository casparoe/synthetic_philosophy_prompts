A recurring disagreement on rationalist-adjacent forums concerns whether doing good is best modelled as the selection of high-marginal-expected-value interventions or as the alteration of systems. On the first view, the world is a menu of roughly additive actions: a bed-net distribution, a deworming campaign, an advocacy effort, and a research project are each scored, by some version of the importance–tractability–neglectedness heuristic, for the expected benefit of the next unit of resource, and one then moves down the list. On this view, "changing the system" is either a loose name for a very large entry on the same menu or an attempt to import a conclusion without doing the comparison. The second view says the menu metaphor is the mistake: the value of any particular action is fixed by the structure in which it occurs, and the highest-leverage work changes the rules, incentives, and feedback loops so that better outcomes become the default. On this view, the marginalist is someone trying to play a badly designed game very well.

I would like you to test these two views against the global eradication of smallpox, and I want the judgement to come out of the case rather than out of the abstract argument. The salient facts are as follows. Smallpox was a severe human viral disease with no animal reservoir, distinctive skin lesions, and a long-available vaccine. The World Health Organization's intensified eradication effort, launched in 1967, initially emphasised mass vaccination; during the campaign it shifted substantially towards surveillance and containment, in which workers detected cases quickly and vaccinated a ring of contacts around each case, using the disease's transmission dynamics instead of pursuing uniform population coverage. The last natural case occurred in 1977, and eradication was certified in 1980.
