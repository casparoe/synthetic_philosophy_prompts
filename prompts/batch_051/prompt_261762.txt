I keep coming back to the original demon passage in Maxwell's *Theory of Heat* (1872), where he says:

Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and only the slower molecules to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics.

Suppose, counterfactually, that in 1874 somebody actually builds a working version: a partition with a tiny flap, driven by a newly discovered photochemical effect, that really does sort faster from slower air molecules in a closed cylinder. No one can detect any work being done by the sorting mechanism, and the flap itself doesn't heat up or wear out. The effect is small but repeatable. I know this probably can't happen in our world, but I want you to take it as the premise and predict how physics and technology develop over the next eighty years, with the reasoning visible. Which parts of thermodynamics get abandoned or reinterpreted, and which survive as approximations? Would information-theoretic entropy still be discovered, or does the demon-as-real-machine make it less likely? What could a Victorian engineer do with a cost-free heat pump, and what would the practical limits actually be? Then work backwards: which of your predictions depend on the demon being genuinely cost-free, and which would still hold if the information-processing cost merely happened to be very small? I'm not looking for a story; I want the chain of mechanisms, incentives, and constraints.
