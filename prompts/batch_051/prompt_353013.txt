I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the problem of evil, and most of my students can already trot out the standard free will defence for human evil. I want this year's final essay question to push them into the fall of Satan / the fall of the angels, because that's where the free will story really gets tested. Could you draft one for me?

The crux I want the question to force them into is this. We're told the angel is created wholly good, with full knowledge of God, no bodily appetites, no bad upbringing, and no prior temptation. If its first sin is genuinely free, and if God is not the author of sin, then we seem to need an explanation of how a good will can choose evil without already having an inclination to evil—and any such inclination would itself be the very evil we were trying to explain. I'd like the question to push them either to supply that explanation or to identify which of the background claims has to be given up, and at what cost. I'd also like the question to make them seperate two things candidates always run together: explaining the motive of the first sin (pride? love of one's own excellence?) versus explaining the enabling conditions that make such a choice so much as possible (a will with a two-way power? a gap between what the angel knows and what it attends to? a privative rather than positive cause?). And I'd like the model answer to set aside at least one paragraph for the regress worry: if pride is meant to explain the fall, is pride itself the fall?

Please write: (1) the exam question, aimed at 90 minutes, deliberately not answerable by reciting the standard free will defence; (2) a model answer of about 600 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 20 with five or six must-hit points, the mark each is worth, and for each point a one- or two-line note on how weaker candidates typically miss or mangle it. Use British English throughout (analyse, favour, defence, and so on). The question should stand on argument alone—please don't set a question that needs citation or knowledge of particular historical texts.
