I have been trying to situate Buddhist ethics within the consequentialism/deontology/virtue-ethics taxonomy that structures the moral-philosophy discussions on rationalist blogs and forums, and I have constructed an argument that seems to show Buddhist ethics collapses into consequentialism. Since this conclusion sits badly with the way the tradition usually describes itself, I take it that something has gone wrong; I want to know what. Here is the argument, with the steps separated.

(1) On the standard modern account, the moral quality of an action in Buddhism is settled by the volition (cetanā) behind it: the same outward deed may have a different moral character depending on whether the intention is wholesome or unwholesome, and karma is standardly described as tracking the quality of one's volitions.

(2) Yet Mahāyāna sources also teach that a bodhisattva — a practitioner on the path dedicated to the liberation of all sentient beings — may break a precept, such as lying or stealing, and in exceptional cases even kill, when the act is motivated by compassion and prevents substantially greater suffering. This is presented as skilful means rather than as a regretful compromise.

(3) If (2) is accepted, then what makes the forbidden act right in those cases cannot be its intrinsic character or its conformity to a rule. It must be the combination of a compassionate intention and the better state of affairs the act produces for sentient beings. That has the form of a consequentialist account: acts are evaluated by reference to the welfare they bring about, with intentions entering as causes, instruments, or evidence rather than as independent constraints.

(4) The obvious reply is that the intention, not the outcome, is doing the work: the bodhisattva's act is right because it is what a compassionate person would do, and the good consequences merely reveal what compassion amounts to. But a dilemma follows. Either we spell out compassion in terms of the welfare it tends to produce, in which case the doctrine is consequentialist in substance; or we give an outcome-independent account of compassion, in which case it is obscure why a compassionate response should systematically select the act that prevents the greater suffering — yet that is exactly the pattern the examples in (2) display.

(5) The appeal to karma offers no escape. If karma tracks intention, then the bodhisattva who lies to save lives is karmically clean, or even meritorious, and karma is functioning as a ledger that rewards motives because they produce good outcomes — consequentialism with a retributive accounting system. If karma instead tracks the outward act regardless of intention, then the tradition is committed to saying that the compassionate rescue-lie is both an expression of virtue and karmically bad, which severs karma from the guidance of action.

(6) Therefore, once the bodhisattva exception is admitted, Buddhist ethics has the structure of consequentialism, and the usual contrast between Buddhist virtue ethics and consequentialism cannot be sustained.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the premise or step you would reject, explain why it looked sound, and make explicit what your preferred resolution commits you to. If forced to choose, I should like to keep (1) and (2): I take the intention-based account of action and the bodhisattva exception to be non-negotiable features of the tradition as I understand it. But then I need to know whether there is a stable position between the horns of (4) and (5), or whether I have simply described a form of virtue consequentialism and should accept that label. Please write the answer in British English.
