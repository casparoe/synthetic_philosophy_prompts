I have been working through the sorites paradox and find myself pulled in two directions. On reflection, I am drawn to the epistemicist treatment of vagueness because it is the cleanest option I know. Vague predicates like 'heap' and 'bald' get to keep a fully classical semantics: bivalence is preserved, every sentence receives a definite truth value, and the sorites paradox is dissolved not by revising logic but by treating the paradox as a case of unavoidable ignorance about where a sharp cutoff lies. The cutoff is not merely unknown; it is unknowable in principle, and that unknowability is supposed to fall out of the margin-for-error structure of our epistemic access to observational predicates. On this picture, vagueness is a species of ignorance rather than a third semantic status, and the world remains sharp while our ability to locate boundaries does not.

But I cannot make myself accept the sharp cutoff. When I consider a single grain of sand or a single hair, it seems not just that I cannot know whether it makes a heap or makes someone bald; it seems that there is nothing to know, because the predicate's meaning has not settled the case either way. Borderline cases strike me as genuine semantic indeterminacy, not questions with hidden answers. That intuition pushes me toward supervaluationism, on which a sentence is true if it holds on all admissible precisifications of the vague predicate, false if it holds on none, and neither in borderline cases, where precisifications disagree; classical logic is retained for determinate cases at the price of truth-value gaps. I am also aware that neither side gets off cheaply. Supervaluationism must say something about higher-order vagueness and about what makes a precisification admissible without sneaking vagueness back in, while epistemicism must earn the claim that the boundary is unknowable in principle rather than merely unknown in practice, and must explain away my sense that there is no fact to be known.

Write an essay that presents the strongest version of each position and then gives me a negotiated verdict rather than a mere catalogue. In particular, explain what it would take for the intuition that there is 'nothing to know' to be debunked and dropped, what it would take for the sharp-boundary picture to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of 'no fact of the matter' on which the two views are less opposed than they first appear — for example, if the disputed boundary lies in the semantics rather than in the non-linguistic world, or if the disagreement is ultimately about which theoretical costs are tolerable. Reach a conclusion about which side should yield, or about what residual disagreement remains, and justify it.
