Consider Aristotle's sea-battle argument. In its usual form, the future is unsettled because whether there will be a sea battle tomorrow depends on decisions not yet made: if the statement that there will be a sea battle tomorrow was already true today, the battle seems fixed in advance, and deliberation would be idle. Now change the source of the unsettledness. Suppose the battle will occur just in case a storm clears the strait before dawn, and suppose the meteorological system is irreducibly chancy: by the best physics of the imagined world, at the time of the prediction the proposition that the storm will clear has an objective probability, no determinate truth value, and no hidden variables that will settle it beforehand. An oracle nonetheless says that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. How does the standard analysis carry over? Does the fatalistic conclusion follow only on the assumption that the oracle's utterance is already true, and is that assumption now unavailable or at least in tension with the physics? If we instead insist that the utterance must be true or false, what exactly does that force us to concede about chances, truth, and the future? Say whether the variation dissolves the original puzzle or merely relocates its weight into a different premise, such as bivalence, excluded middle, or the link between truth and fixity. Then state where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what consideration—for instance, a demonstration that the meteorological system is deterministic after all, or a proof that a chancy process can still ground a determinate future truth—would most change your view.
