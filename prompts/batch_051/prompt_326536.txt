A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether the world is becoming less violent, and the argument kept shifting because we were pulling on different threads. I defended what I’ll call the decline view: the long-run fall in violence is a real and central fact about the modern world; per capita rates are the correct denominator, since what matters is an individual’s risk of dying violently; and the historical broadening of what counts as serious violence—for example, coming to treat domestic violence or cruelty to animals as violence rather than as private misfortune—is itself evidence of the trend rather than a confound. My friend defended what I’ll call the metric-dependence view: the sentence “the world is becoming less violent” has no determinate truth value until we fix what counts as violence, over which population and period, and per capita versus absolute; absolute numbers and the reclassification of harms as violence can reverse or erase the apparent decline; and the real disagreement is in part about which metric is worth caring about, which is not a question of fact alone. Rather than let the argument go in a circle, we decided to write the document that a well-conducted adversarial collaboration between those two positions would have produced.

Please draft that joint statement, written in neutral language that the two positions could both sign. It should contain:

- a numbered agreed-claims section: only claims both positions can genuinely endorse, precise enough to have content but not so framed that either side is silently conceding the dispute;
- a disagreements table stating exactly where the positions come apart, in wording each side would accept for its own view, with a column indicating which rows are substantive disagreements and which are partly or entirely about words—and for the verbal ones, which term is doing the work and why;
- the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, with the single crux you judge to be load-bearing for the entire argument identified and justified as such;
- for each crux, the argument, case, or kind of evidence both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance.

Do not include citations, titles, or statistics that would have to be looked up; the document should stand on its own, and where an empirical claim is contested because the underlying data are contested, make that contest one of the cruxes rather than pretending it is settled. Be committed: make definite choices about formulations, about which disagreements are verbal rather than real, and about which crux is load-bearing, and defend those choices rather than laying out options.
