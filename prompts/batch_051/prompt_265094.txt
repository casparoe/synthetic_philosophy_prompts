I serve on the committee revising our federation's constitution. Last year the federation spent six weeks without a lawful executive: a disputed election left the presidency vacant, every named successor had resigned or been disqualified, and the constitution was silent about what came next. We have drafted a clause to govern such gaps, and I want a hard analysis of how it would actually behave, not a judgment about its intentions. Under the draft, when the head of state and all named successors are simultaneously vacant or incapacitated, power passes to an Interim Council of the three most senior judges and the two most senior nonpartisan civil servants. The Council may take only decisions classed as routine or urgent; every other decision requires a two-thirds vote of the legislature and operates provisionally for ninety days, then lapses unless ratified by a citizens' assembly of one hundred and fifty people selected by lot. Walk through the incentives this creates. Who would gain from triggering, extending, or ending an interregnum, and how might they act to do so? How should we expect judges and senior civil servants to behave once membership of the Interim Council is a foreseeable prize or burden? What happens if the legislature is captured or deadlocked, or if the citizens' assembly becomes the real bottleneck? Identify the most likely failure modes, and tell me which of them can be fixed by amending the clause and which would argue for abandoning the design.
