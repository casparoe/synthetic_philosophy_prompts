I follow a policy about reflecting on my own past decisions that I think of as a search rule, and I want to know whether it can be defended as epistemology rather than avoidance. The rule: I may re-examine a past choice only while I am still looking for information about what happened and why. The moment I notice that I have started looking for a particular feeling — either that I was in the right, or that I alone caused the harm and therefore have a stable identity as the guilty one — I stop. I treat anything further as a known unreliable process: memory becomes a constructive storyteller rather than a record, my so-called insights become predictions that confirm themselves, and each pass through the episode makes me more certain of whatever narrative I entered the loop with, not more accurate. A friend objects that this is a self-serving stopping rule: I quit exactly when introspection becomes uncomfortable or destabilizing, so I systematically avoid the revisions that would actually teach me something. My answer is that I have enough data on my own rumination to know that by then it is a closed loop — the tenth pass has never surfaced a new fact or corrected an old one, only deepened whichever feeling I started with. Is my rule defensible as a policy for acquiring self-knowledge, or is it a bias that has learned to describe itself in epistemic terms? What principled conditions distinguish introspection that tracks the truth about one's past from introspection that manufactures certainty, and is there a version of my stopping rule that preserves the real asymmetry without being self-serving? Treat self-examination as an information-processing practice with costs, reliability conditions, and diminishing returns, not as something good in itself.
