I've been chewing on an asymmetry in how people talk about simple living and frugality, and I cant tell if it tracks a real distinction or is just class aesthetics.

Two people live in nearly identical 400-square-foot apartments. Both own about a hundred things, don't run a car, mend their clothes, cook from scratch, rarely eat out or travel. Person A has a high income or an inheritance and could trivially afford a lot more; they've chosen this life after getting into minimalism or asceticism, and their friends call them disciplined, intentional, maybe a little thoreauvian. Person B has never really had a choice; this is just what their wage allows. Person B doesn't get called disciplined. Person B gets called poor, and is more likely to be lectured about financial ambition than praised for living light.

Same material behavior, same daily routines. The main difference in how we evaluate them seems to be the size of their option set, not anything about the life itself. That's odd if simplicity and frugality and non-attachment are supposed to be ideals in themselves, which people also claim. If a simple life is good, why does everyone's verdict depend this much on the person being rich enough not to need it?

I can think of a few candidate principles. (a) We're actually praising renunciation: the exercise of self-control against a live alternative, so the ideal only bites for someone who has the alternative. (b) What we value is autonomy or optionality, not material simplicity as such. (c) It's mostly signaling: minimalism is a costly signal of discipline or taste only for someone who could consume more, and we've mistaken the signal for the virtue. There are probably better ones I'm missing.

I want the strongest case for the asymmetry, not just a debunk, and then an honest verdict: should it be kept, reversed, or given up? Test the candidates against edge cases too: a rich person who is forced into simplicity by a court order (assets frozen, say), or a poor person who is genuinely unattached to the goods they can't afford and wouldn't want them if offered. And if the poor person can display the same underlying virtue, does the asymmetry just fall apart?

Also, please seperate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's still a live fight, and don't present a contested take as the consensus. I've noticed virtue-ethics people especially do this.
