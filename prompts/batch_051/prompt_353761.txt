So I had a long argument with my friend last night about generics — sentences like "ducks lay eggs" or "mosquitoes carry malaria" that say something about a kind, not about every member of it. My friend had a simple view: a generic is true when enough members of the kind have the property. I kept saying it can't just be about the numbers, but I realised halfway through that I couldn't state the rule I was actually using. We ended up with a pile of cases where I trust my gut, and i'd like to know whether there's a coherent account that captures my verdicts, or close, and which ones it forces me to drop.

Here are my verdicts:

1. "Ducks lay eggs" — true, obviously. Even though the ducks actually doing the laying are a small minority at any given time (adult females, in season).

2. "Ducks are female" — false. Which is awkward, because a much larger proportion of ducks are female than are currently laying eggs. If it were just prevalence, #2 ought to come out more true than #1, not less.

3. "Lions have manes" — true. Again only the adult males, and that doesn't bother me.

4. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" — true. A tiny proportion of mosquitoes carry it; doesn't matter.

5. "Sharks attack surfers" — true, I think. Vanishingly rare, but the attacks are so vivid that I can't bring myself to call it false.

6. "Ravens are white" — false. There are albino ravens, but the sentence just seems wrong.

7. "Books are paperbacks" — false. But most books in any bookshop probably are paperbacks, so my friend says this one shows I'm not even taking my own prevalence criterion seriously. If I accept #1 and #4 on low numbers, why do I reject this? All I can say is that being a paperback feels accidental in a way that laying eggs doesn't.

8. "Greycaps are poisonous" — false. We made this one up: a greycap is a mushroom, and exactly one greycap in a million is fatally poisonous, not a subspecies, no way to tell, just a freak. I want the generic to be false. But then why is #4 true? My friend thinks I'm just being soft about mushrooms.

9. "The Barchetta X has a faulty airbag" — true. Suppose one in a thousand Barchetta Xs has an airbag that fails to deploy, and two people have died. I'd say the generic is true, and so would the papers. But that's rare as well, so what makes it different from #8?

I don't have a principle that gets all of these. So: is there a coherent account of generics that captures most of them? State it as precisely as you can, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why, and tell me what it predicts about a case we kept going back and forth on. That case is a species of bamboo — call it century bamboo — where every individual flowers exactly once in its life, all in the same year, and then dies. Is "century bamboo flowers once in its life" true on your account? It's nowhere near a majority at any moment, but it also feels essential rather than accidental. I genuinely can't decide.
