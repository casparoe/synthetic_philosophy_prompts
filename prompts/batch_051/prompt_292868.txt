I've been reading Davidson's essay on metaphor alongside work in the conceptual metaphor tradition, and I keep finding that my intuitive verdicts about particular metaphors don't line up neatly with either camp. I'm hoping you can help me extract a principle from my cases, or tell me which verdicts I'd have to abandon for a coherent view. Here are the cases, as concretely as I can put them.

If someone says, "My lawyer is a shark," I think the sentence communicates something about the lawyer—ruthlessness, predatory competence—that goes beyond the literal claim that a human is a fish. I don't think it's just a cause of my noticing those features; the features seem part of what was said. But if someone else says, "My lawyer is a barracuda," I feel the two say almost the same thing, even though the literal animal properties differ. So maybe the specific image matters less than a general schema. When a poet writes that a city street "was a vein carrying the city's blood" (my own invented line), I get a vivid, fairly determinate picture—traffic as circulation, the street as a vessel—and I'm inclined to say the metaphor has a kind of content, close to a proposition. Yet when someone says, "The committee is a sieve," I can't decide between two contents: that the committee leaks information, or that it filters out bad proposals. If I don't know which, maybe the metaphor has no determinate meaning at all. For a dead metaphor like "He kicked the bucket," I have no temptation to say it means anything beyond died, even though historically it was metaphorical. So whatever special content metaphors have, it seems to fade with conventionalization. For a sentence like "No man is an island," it is literally false, but I strongly feel it expresses something true—maybe that no one is self-sufficient—yet I can't say the sentence itself is true; only that the metaphor conveys a truth. For a tautology used metaphorically, like "Business is business," I feel it conveys resignation or inevitability, but I can't articulate any content beyond what the literal tautology says; the effect feels almost purely causal or attitudinal.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all or most of these verdicts? Which verdicts, if any, would it force me to give up? Does the resulting account end up closer to Davidson's denial that metaphors have any special meaning beyond the literal, to the conceptual metaphor view that metaphors express stable conceptual mappings, or to something in between? Finally, please say what your principle predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on: a friend says, "Her voice was sandpaper on my nerves." I both feel that it conveys a precise unpleasantness and worry that any paraphrase loses everything.
