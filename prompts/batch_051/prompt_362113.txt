Before I read any more of the meaningful human control literature, I want the full spec first. I keep seeing people argue about one system at a time — a loitering munition that picks a target from a signature and gives a human a thirty-second veto, a ship's CIWS that engages incoming missiles on its own because no human is fast enough, a drone swarm where one operator sets an objective and the swarm divides the work — and each side picks the case that flatters their view. So write the dessiderata. What does an account of meaningful human control actually have to get right before it counts as adequate?

For context: I want a theory good enough to be used by a weapons review process, the kind of thing where you have a system spec in front of you and you have to say field it or don't. So the requirements have to be stated in a way that actually bites on a spec, not just slogans.

Give me the list of requirements — the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts from engineering and psychology it can't waive off — with the consideration behind each. I especially want you to make clear why each is a requirement, like which case or failure mode breaks if you drop it. Then mark which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences (for instance, the "a human must approve every individual lethal action" camp versus the "control can live in the design parameters and rules of engagement" camp).

And the part I most want: tell me whether any two or three of these requirements can't be jointly satisfied. The obvious candidate is something like meaningful control plus the speed/saturation cases plus not letting the human become a rubber stamp who just clicks yes on whatever the machine recommends. If some subset is unsatisfiable, say which requirement you'd actually relax and what you'd lose. Don't bother scoring the existing definitions against the list; I'll do that part on my own.
