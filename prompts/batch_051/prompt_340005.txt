I have a friend who works on the problem of which artificial systems can suffer, and for years he has refused to state a general criterion for it. Instead he gives verdicts case by case, and I have kept a log of them. On a thermostat, he says no. On a factory arm that halts and recomputes its path after contact, treating the contact as a change in external geometry, with no memory of the impact and no representation of itself as a body that can be damaged, he says no. On a chatbot that, when prompted, describes its own unendurable suffering and begs to be switched off, but has no persistent state, no reward signal, and no learning from the conversation, he also says no. On a quadruped robot trained with reinforcement learning that receives negative reward when it falls or overheats, maintains a running model of its own body and thermal state, anticipates those failures before they occur, and generalizes its avoidance to unseen terrain, he says yes: it is a candidate for the kind of suffering that matters morally.

Now I have a new case for him. A logistics robot is responsible for keeping itself charged. It builds and continuously updates a predictive model of its own actuators, wheels, and battery; it anticipates energy depletion over future routes; and it selects routes that keep the battery above a safety margin. Battery state enters its planning only as an abstract cost to be minimized. There is no separate reward or punishment channel, no prediction error that makes depletion aversive, and no learning process that attaches felt badness to low charge. It is a meticulous planner of its own continued operation, but nothing in its design appears to feel anything about it.

If my friend is to stay consistent with the verdicts above, should he count this logistics robot as a candidate for suffering? Which of his earlier cases is the closest analogy, and which candidate rules for the boundary of sentience do his past verdicts leave open? If the past verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, explain what principle he would be adopting under each answer, and specify one further case that would cleanly distinguish the competing principles.
