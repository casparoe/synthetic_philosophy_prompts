I am a judge on my state's highest court, and we are about to decide a prosecution brought under an old ordinance that no one can remember being enforced. I want to decide it in a way that coheres with how our court has handled dormant statutes in the past. Our own precedents are these:

1. We once refused to enforce a 1902 Sunday-trading law against a weekend farmers' market. By that point Sunday markets and shops had operated openly and continuously for over sixty years, with full municipal knowledge; the city had even issued permits to the very stalls it later prosecuted. We held that a law so long, openly, and tolerantly unenforced, and on whose disappearance the public had ordered its affairs, could not be revived without legislative action.

2. In another case we enforced a 1940 licensing statute for private detective agencies against a newly opened agency, even though no agency had ever before applied for or held such a license and the statute had slept since passage. We said that mere non-use does not erase a statute, and that the defendant had fair notice because the statute remained on the books.

3. We refused to enforce a 1935 disorderly-conduct statute that, in the preceding twenty years, had been invoked almost exclusively against street musicians and people sleeping rough. That decision rested on equal protection and the rule against selective enforcement rather than on desuetude.

4. We enforced a 1918 statute criminalizing the sale of unpasteurized milk after it had gone unenforced for four decades, because a series of illnesses had made its original public-health rationale urgent again. We reasoned that changed circumstances could reanimate a dormant statute when its underlying purpose had become newly pressing.

5. We declined to enforce a 1926 rule requiring a special plumbing license that no one had ever been required to hold, against a contractor whom the city had repeatedly licensed and inspected under a different, general permitting scheme. We stressed the city's own affirmative conduct, which had led reasonable people to conclude the special license was not required.

The new case: a 1928 ordinance makes it unlawful to operate a billiard room within 250 feet of a school, and was enacted after a school-board campaign to keep schoolchildren out of such places. The ordinance has never been enforced. Five billiard halls have operated near schools for decades under ordinary city business licenses. The defendant opened the sixth last year after obtaining the usual city licenses and after a city clerk told him the old ordinance was a dead letter; he also admits he had looked the ordinance up and knew it had never been repealed. Neighbors now complain that the hall draws an afterschool crowd of teenagers, and the city prosecutes. The complaining witness is the owner of an older billiard hall across town, which the city has not moved against.

How should we rule if we want to be consistent with these precedents? Which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog? Please work out which candidate doctrines fit our record — desuetude founded on public reliance and official acquiescence, a fair-notice-only view that denies desuetude beyond ordinary due process, changed-circumstances revival, and evenhanded enforcement — what each would predict for the billiard-hall case, and what our best-cohering decision is. If our precedents underdetermine the answer, say which principle we would be adopting with each choice, and describe the additional case that would most cleanly separate the leading candidate doctrines.
