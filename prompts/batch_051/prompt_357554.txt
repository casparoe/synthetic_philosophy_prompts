In a reading group on divine simplicity and petitionary prayer, someone kept returning to the slogan "Prayer changes things" to explain why prayer makes sense even if God is omniscient and immutable. The conversation stalled when I pressed her on what, exactly, changes and in what sense. I later came across this passage in Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Second Part of the Second Part, Question 83, Article 2, Objection 2:

"Further, by prayer we bend the mind of the person to whom we pray, so that he may do what is asked of him. But God's mind is unchangeable and inflexible, according to 1 Samuel 15:29, 'But the Triumpher in Israel will not spare, and will not be moved to repentance.' Therefore it is not fitting that we should pray to God."

Take the slogan "Prayer changes things" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making—about God's will, God's knowledge, the petitioner's state, the world's course, or the relation among these. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false on classical theism, and say which readings the Aquinas objection actually dislodges and which it leaves untouched. Which reading, if any, makes petitionary prayer to an omniscient and immutable God coherent without collapsing prayer into mere self-therapy? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—a one-sentence statement of which reading of the slogan survives the objection—and then give the menu and reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
