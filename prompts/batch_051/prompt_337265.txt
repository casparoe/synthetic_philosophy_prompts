I spent my career in environmental science but have recently gotten into philosophy of science, and I keep circling the same puzzle: when a long-run prediction fails on schedule, is the theory falsified or just 'early'? I've got strong gut verdicts on particular cases but no principle behind them, and I suspect some of my verdicts can't all be kept.

Here's roughly where I stand.

Malthus: population grows geometrically, food only arithmetically, so famine and mass misery are baked in. I want to call this wrong, because it turned on ignoring how much agriculture and demography could change, and the English collapse he gestured at never came on his terms. But I also can't shake the feeling there's a true core about finite systems, so maybe he's 'right but early' — unless that phrase is just another way of never being wrong, which I also half-believe.

A geologist in the 1950s predicts US oil production peaks around 1970 and then declines. It does. I count that as a real success, even though the curve was fit after the fact and the mechanism was shaky.

The global peak-oil folks predicted a world peak around 2005, then 2010, then 2020. Fracking broke the pattern. I say each new date made the theory weaker, and by now it's disconfirmed, not early.

The 1960s population-bomb predictions of mass starvation in the 1970s and 80s: wrong, and I won't let 'right but early' rescue them, even though 'finite planet, finite growth' is trivially true and has worked out locally plenty of times.

The Limits to Growth business-as-usual scenario pointing at collapse around mid-century: I go back and forth on weather it's even testable, since it's scenario-based, but if collapse arrived roughly on schedule I think I'd want to give the model some credit even with a partly wrong mechanism.

And the case that really bothers me: suppose someone in 1900 predicts the North Atlantic cod fishery collapses by 1990 because of a spreading parasite. It collapses in 1992, but from overfishing. My gut says not confirmed — right outcome, wrong mechanism. But that seems inconsistent with the oil-peak success, where I was happy to accept the outcome despite a shaky mechanism.

What I'm after: is there a defensible principle about long-run predictions, falsification, and rescue that captures most of these verdicts? Which ones does it make me give up, and what does it say about someone who keeps moving their collapse date back a decade each time it fails?
