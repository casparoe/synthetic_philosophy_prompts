In The Querist, George Berkeley argues in the form of a series of questions that money is best understood as a ticket, counter, or instrument for transferring real wealth, not as wealth itself, and that a country with idle labor and scarce gold and silver should create a national bank to issue paper money so that those idle hands can be set to work. His underlying picture is that true wealth consists in industry and the production of goods, while money is merely a social device for recording and circulating claims on that productive power.

Assume, for the sake of argument, that Berkeley is right about the ontology of money: it is not a stock of real value but a transferable marker of claims on future production. The contemporary monetary system, however, differs sharply from the one Berkeley addressed. Most of what now functions as money consists of bank deposits created by private banks when they make loans; central banks issue fiat reserves and manage payment infrastructure; most payments are digital and can be cleared instantly; and private digital monies and alternative payment systems now exist alongside state-issued currency.

Does Berkeley’s argument that money is a mere ticket or counter survive this change, or does it need repair? Identify which step in his argument the modern system puts pressure on: the claim about what money is, the claim that a public issuer should supply the counters, or the claim that issuing more counters in a slack economy need not merely devalue them. Then state whether a repaired Berkeleyan view can still say something weaker but important, and what that would be. I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in a policy recommendation.
