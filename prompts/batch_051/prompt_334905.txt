Take Parfit's harmless torturers case: a thousand people each turn a dial that adds one imperceptible increment of pain to a single victim, no individual turn makes a detectable difference, and by the end the victim is in agony. Now change one thing. Suppose the victim's pain system doesn't sum smoothly but has a hard threshold: below 1000 units nothing is felt at all, and the 1000th unit triggers the entire day's pain at once as a single brief agony. The thousand turns happen in a random order that no one can observe, everyone knows the setup, and no torturer ever finds out their own place in the order. So each person's turn stops being the certain addition of an imperceptible increment and becomes a 1-in-1000 shot at being the one that triggers the agony.

Work through the moral arithmetic of the altered case next to the original. On expected-value reasoning the two cases are supposed to come out the same, aren't they? Each turn's expected contribution to total pain is identical, but in the original no individual turn makes anyone's experience worse, while in the threshold version exactly one turn literally causes the agony. Is that a real difference or just a description difference? And what does the comparison do to the inefficacy objection as it actually shows up: does voting look like the threshold version, does eating meat or flying look like the incremental version, and is someone who treats the two versions differently making a real distinction or fooling themselves? I dont want a survey of what people have written about this; I want you to work the two cases through in your own terms and say where the moral difference, if any, actually lives.
