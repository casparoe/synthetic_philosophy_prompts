I am a PhD student in philosophical theology, finishing a paper on the logical problem of the Trinity. I have been trying to reconstruct the strongest version of the standard charge that the doctrine is inconsistent, and I have ended up with an argument that now unsettles me for a slightly different reason: on the reading that gives it its force, it seems to prove far too much, because the very same move would turn any two members of a kind into a single thing. I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis of where this argument goes wrong, or, if it is valid, an account of which premise is least costly to abandon and what abandoning it commits one to.

The doctrine I am treating is the claim that there is exactly one God, and yet three really distinct persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — each of whom is fully divine. The argument I have been pressing runs as follows.

(1) The Father is God.
(2) The Son is God.
(3) The Father is not identical with the Son.
(4) In (1) and (2), "is God" expresses identity: each person just is the one God, not merely a bearer of a shared divine nature.
(5) Identity is an equivalence relation. If the Father is identical with God and the Son is identical with God, then by symmetry and transitivity the Father is identical with the Son.
(6) (3) and (5) contradict each other. Since the contradiction arises from nothing more than ordinary identity logic applied to the doctrine's own statements, the doctrine is inconsistent. The Holy Spirit could be added to the premises with the same result.

My difficulty is this. My supervisor has pointed out that the argument appears to rest on a general principle — that in true statements of the form "a is F" and "b is F," when the "is" is meant substantively, the "is" expresses identity. But that principle runs wild. Run the same machinery on "Socrates is human" and "Plato is human," and it yields the absurd conclusion that Socrates is identical with Plato; run it on any two things sharing a kind, and ordinary counting collapses. So either the anti-Trinitarian argument is licensed only by a reading of "is God" that the creedal formulas do not by themselves settle, or the defender must reject (4). But rejecting (4) seems to leave only the claim that the Father and the Son share a common nature, and that straightforwardly sounds like the claim that there are at least two gods — just as two humans who share human nature are two humans. The traditional distinction between the one divine nature (ousia) and the three persons (hypostases) is usually introduced at exactly this point, but I have yet to see it stated as a semantic proposal precise enough to evaluate; it often feels like a relabeling of the distinction between identity and predication rather than a resolution of it.

I would most like to keep (3) and (5): the real distinction of the persons, and the ordinary logic of identity. I am prepared to revise my understanding of what "is God" contributes to (1) and (2). But I want to know exactly what that revision costs. In particular: is there a reading of "is God" on which (1), (2), and (3) are all true and the doctrine remains monotheism rather than covert polytheism? If so, what semantic or metaphysical commitments does that reading impose, and how does it avoid the Socrates-and-Plato collapse without special pleading? If not, which premise must go, and what is the price of giving it up?
