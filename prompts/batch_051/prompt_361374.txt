Posting from a rationalist-adjacent corner: I've been chewing on the insect-sentience scale argument and keep hitting the same bump. The argument goes that there about 10^18 insects alive at any given moment, so even a 0.1% credence that insects are sentient gives you an expected 10^15 sentient individuals—dwarfing all the vertebrates everyone fusses over—and therefore insect welfare should swamp our priorities. Fine, the arithmetic is fine. But I can't figure out what the 0.1% is supposed to be tracking. There's a fact of the matter about whether there is something it's like to be a bee; 'sentience' is not a random event we're sampling from a distribution. So the probability is just a name for our ignorance, and the real project is to turn that ignorance into a testable question.

So here's the assignment: you're a philosopher of cognitive science who works on invertebrate consciousness and takes the scale argument seriously. Design the best realistic experiment or battery of experiments you could run to get evidence for the claim 'there is something it's like to be [some model insect]'. Be concrete about the manipulation, the readout, and the pattern of results that would update you toward or away from sentience. I know the usual candidates—motivational trade-offs after injury, learning that isn't hardwired reflex, play-ish behavior, maybe neural signatures—but I want the actual cutoff: what result separates 'felt pain' from 'nociception plus adaptive behavior'. Then do the part everyone skips: say what a positive result would and would not settle for the scale argument. Can any behavioral test ever distinguish raw valence from mere nociception? And even if honeybees pass, does that license multiplying by 10^18 across beetles, flies, and lepidopteran larvae with radically different nervous systems? Think carefully, brainstorm a few candidate designs before picking the strongest, and be honest about the residue that no experiment can reach.
