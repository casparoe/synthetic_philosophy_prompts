I've been spending alot of time this week chewing on graded sentience and moral circle expansion, and I keep coming back to the same question. If you reject the idea that there's one sharp boundary between beings that matter morally and beings that don't, and instead think moral considerability comes in degrees — a fly has less than a dog, and a thermostat has less than a fly, if it has any at all — what determines where a system sits on that continuum? I'm trying to catalog the candidate axes people have proposed. So far I have: (1) nociception in the narrow sense, having receptors and pathways whose activity tracks tissue damage; (2) valence, in the sense of states that are affectively positive or negative rather than just sensory registrations; (3) behavioral flexibility, like learning and novel problem-solving; (4) goal-directedness or preferences, so there's a fact of the matter about outcomes being better or worse for the system; (5) a self-model, some representation of itself over time; and (6) richness of information integration, the degree to which the system binds many signals into a unified state.

I'm starting to suspect this list is nearly exhaustive, or at least that everything else I generate collapses into one of these. Can you propose one additional dimension that a theory could use as a basis for graded moral status — one that isn't just a rewording, special case, or combination of the six on my list — and then be straight with me about whether you think it actually carries moral weight on its own, or whether it just tracks the things already on the list? Epistemic status: genuinely confused, not trying to win an argument. And please write this out in ordinary continuous prose, like an email — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
