Write a pantoum of five or six quatrains from the point of view of a structural realist who is trying to absorb the pessimistic meta-induction rather than dismiss it. Let the poem's two kinds of recurring lines carry the two sides of the argument. One set should speak the historical record—phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether—successful theories that were nonetheless abandoned. The other set should speak what was not abandoned: the equations, ratios, and regularities that outlived their original ontology, such as the Fresnel equations for reflection and refraction, which were derived within the elastic-ether picture and survived inside Maxwell's electromagnetism after the ether itself was given up. Make the pantoum's mechanism—a stanza's second and fourth lines returning as the first and third lines of the next—do the philosophical work: each stanza receives lines inherited from a failed theory and must decide what still holds. When the closing quatrain returns to the opening lines, it should stage the inductivist's comeback, the suspicion that the realist is merely repeating the same confident pattern in new dress. Let that final return be a turn, not a slogan: the retained line should come back altered, or the form should show why one thing survived while the furniture around it did not. A philosopher of science should recognize this as a rendering of the structural-realist or divide-et-impera reply to Laudan's pessimistic induction, but the poem should not name the doctrine; a reader with no philosophy background should simply find the form itself making the argument.
