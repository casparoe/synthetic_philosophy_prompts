A professional association for software engineers is considering the following rule for members who use AI coding assistants. Each month, an engineer whose recent merges were produced with AI assistance must attend a proctored session. The proctor randomly selects one of that engineer’s AI-assisted merges from the previous month; the engineer knows which merges are eligible but not which will be selected. During the session, the engineer may not use any AI assistance and must do three things: explain what the selected change does and why it was designed in that particular way, identify the main alternative design that was considered and why it was rejected, and implement a small extension or bug fix to the selected code. An engineer who fails two monthly sessions within any rolling quarter loses access to AI coding assistants for the following quarter and must complete all work unaided. A change produced under pair or mob programming may be attributed jointly, but each named participant must then pass the check on that change.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than taking a position on whether the rule is a good idea, and analyze how it would actually behave. What would it incentivize among engineers, and how would they adapt or game it? What second-order effects would it have on the kinds of AI-generated code people accept, on team organization, on hiring, and on the distribution of skill? Would it preserve genuine understanding of the systems being shipped, or would it mainly select for the ability to reconstruct a plausible rationale after the fact? Identify the main failure modes, and say whether any of them can be fixed without gutting the scheme. Please write the analysis in American English.
