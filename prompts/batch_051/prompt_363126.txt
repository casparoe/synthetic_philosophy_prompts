So this has been on my mind a lot because my dad got diagnosed with early-stage dementia last year, and I've been losing sleep over the question of whether he's "still there" — then at a dinner the other night two of my friends got into it about the old Parfit-style teletransporter thought experiment, and I realized its basically the same question in science fiction form. I couldn't follow everything, and I want someone to referee it properly.

The argument went roughly like this.

Maya said stepping into the teletransporter — the machine that scans you, destroys your body, and recreates a molecule-for-molecule copy on Mars — is just suicide plus a duplicate. She said identity hangs on being the same living body or brain, not on having the same memories, so the person on Mars is a different person who happens to think they're you. Her big move was the branch-line version: if the machine doesn't destroy the original, everyone agrees the original stays on Earth and the Mars person is a copy. Destroying the Earth body doesn't suddenly promote the copy into being you; it just makes it a copy whose original is dead. "you" dont survive by having a duplicate somewhere else.

Tom pushed back and said this is exactly the thing we shouldn't care about. What matters in survival isn't identity, it's psychological continuity: memories, beliefs, intentions, character, the sense of carrying on your projects. If the person on Mars wakes up with all of that, then from the inside, what you care about when you care about surviving is preserved. He added that this is already all we ever have in ordinary life — our cells turn over, we change constantly, and what ties me to the ten-year-old version of me is psychological connections and continuity, not a soul and not the same matter. He also gave the gradual replacement case: if a surgeon replaced your neurons one at a time with functionally identical artificial ones, and you were awake and talking throughout, at the end nobody would say you had died. But that's just the teletransporter slowed down. So identity can't be what matters.

Maya came back and said slow replacement is completely different, because your consciousness stays online and each step is linked to the last, whereas the teletransporter cuts the thread: there's a gap, and the person on Mars has no living link back to the original's ongoing consciousness. Tom said that can't be the decider, because we don't treat dreamless sleep or general anesthesia as death — consciousness is interrupted all the time and we still survive it. What does the work, he said, is the causal chain of memories and dispositions, and the teletransporter preserves that chain through the scan and the transmission.

Then Maya said he was smuggling in the very thing he was trying to prove: a future person with your memories and plans is only "you" if we assume psychological continuity is what matters. If you start from the body-brain view, that future person is a stranger wearing your memories, and the only person who ever had your experiences is gone. Tom just said that's question-begging in the other direction, and that the reason we care about our bodies in the first place is because they carry our psychology.

I've gone back and forth and honestly can't break the tie. So: you're a professional philosopher who works on personal identity and survival. Please think carefully, and before you give a verdict, brainstorm the strongest version of each side's position. Then adjudicate point by point: who is right about which specific claim, where each argument misfires, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall. I'm less interested in the sci-fi mechanics and more in what this tells me about my dad's case — whether someone who no longer shares many of my memories can still be, in the sense that matters, the same person.
