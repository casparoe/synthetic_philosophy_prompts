In the practice of formal deduction—in logic textbooks, proof checkers, and ordinary mathematical reasoning—inference rules such as modus ponens are applied to premises but are not themselves entered among the premises. No one improves a derivation by adding a further premise of the form “If P, and if P then Q, then Q”; a system that required such additions would betray a confusion rather than provide justification. Yet the conclusion of a valid derivation is still said to be justified by its premises. In Lewis Carroll’s “What the Tortoise Said to Achilles,” the Tortoise accepts A, B, and C, where C is “If A and B are true, Z must be true,” but still declines to accept Z. Achilles tries to close the gap by obtaining one more conditional as a premise:

“If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z.”
“And why must I?”
“Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don’t dispute that, I imagine?”
“If A and B and C are true, Z must be true,” the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. “That’s another Hypothetical, isn’t it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn’t I?”
“You might,” the candid hero admitted; “though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical.”

What theory of inference—and of what it is for premises to justify a conclusion—does the standing practice of keeping inference rules outside the premise set presuppose? Does Carroll’s dialogue show that the practice is forced, because every attempt to do the job with premises merely restarts the regress, or that the practice is a harmless convention, or that the very idea of premises justifying a conclusion is the thing in trouble? Reconstruct the presupposed theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits with, and say what a proof system would have to be like for the Tortoise’s demand to be reasonable rather than obstructive.
