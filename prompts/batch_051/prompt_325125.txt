Ok so I run an undergrad philosophy-ish discussion group, around 14 people, 75 minutes, and I want them to actually feel why there's something weird about assigning probabilities to your own choices while you're still deciding. The idea I'm after is the one people call deliberation crowds out prediction: when a choice is genuinely up to you, you can't simultaneously take the detached-forecaster stance toward yourself and have a real credence about what you'll do — any number you put on it ends up looking like an intention in disguise, a commitment tactic, or a report about how weak-willed you expect to be, rather than a prediction. Like if you announce you're 70% likely to take the gamble, why not just decide to take it and be 100%? And if you announce 50/50, what's the missing information, given that you're the person who's about to make the choice? I want them to run into this in their own skin, not hear it from me.

Design a game or activity with real (small) stakes where participants first put a probability on their own immediate choice and then actually make the choice, with the rules set up so the weirdness shows up in what they do. Two structures I've been toying with, but you should design the actual rules and payoffs: (1) a two-round prediction task where round one is predicting someone else's choice — which feels like normal forecasting — and round two is predicting your own, which doesn't; (2) a game where you get paid for both the accuracy of your self-prediction and the outcome of the choice, so people notice they can guarantee perfect accuracy just by predicting their intention and then following it, which shows the prediction isn't doing any forecasting work. Maybe also a round where mid-range probabilities feel impossible to justify, or a round where another participant bets on you based on your self-probability, and then you can exploit them by deciding differently. Use whatever works; the point is they should arrive at the problem themselves.

Then the debrief: it should map moments in the game onto pieces of the idea — where a 60/40 self-probability collapsed to 0/100, where accuracy payments turned predictions into commitments, where someone tried describing themselves from the outside and stopped actually deciding. Say what will predictably go wrong when this is run (people cynically reporting 50/50 because they haven't decided, people not caring about a few dollars, someone who thinks a self-probability is just self-knowledge about their own habits) and how to handle it. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: part of the philosophical claim is about wether self-prediction is impossible in principle for an ideal agent, and a room of undergrads with snack-money stakes won't settle that — so say where the facilitator has to supply the missing step afterward. No lecture up front, and nobody hears the phrase 'deliberation crowds out prediction' until the debrief. Oh and answer in American English.
