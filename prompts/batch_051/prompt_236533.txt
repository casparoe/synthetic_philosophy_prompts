You are a professional philosopher who works on reasons and the normativity of attitudes, especially the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. Work through the question step by step, and begin by brainstorming two or three candidate diagnoses before settling on one. Where a criterion underdetermines the answer, say so explicitly, and do not force a yes/no verdict the case cannot support.

Philosophers often separate the right kind of reasons (RKRs) for an attitude from the wrong kind of reasons (WKRs). One way to draw the distinction is by the following four marks:

1. Source. An RKR for attitude A toward object O is supplied by features of O that bear on whether A correctly represents or matches O. A WKR is supplied by features of the state of having A — for example, that having A would be beneficial, prudent, or morally good.

2. Fittingness. RKRs determine whether A is fitting, merited, or correct. WKRs determine at most whether having A would be good, useful, or advisable; they leave fittingness untouched.

3. Transmission. RKRs transmit to related attitudes: a feature of O that is an RKR to admire O is, other things equal, also a reason to attend to that feature, to take pleasure in it, and to treat O as admirable. WKRs do not transmit in this way: the demon's threat to punish you unless you admire him is a consideration that makes it prudent to admire him, but it does not make it reasonable to see or treat the demon as admirable.

4. First-personal availability. An RKR can figure directly in first-personal deliberation about whether to hold A as such. A WKR is most naturally paraphrased as a reason to bring it about that one holds A — through practice, selective attention, a pill, or self-manipulation — not as a reason to hold A in that same first-personal way.

Now here is a case built to strain these marks.

Mara is a sculptor deciding whether to admire her teacher Enzo. By any ordinary present measure Enzo is not admirable: his recent work is cautious and derivative. But Enzo has a reliable, well-documented disposition: when he is the object of sustained, genuine admiration from a student he respects, his confidence and standards rise, and within months he begins producing work that is genuinely bold and admirable — admirable by standards Mara already accepts, and recognizably so to critics who knew nothing of the causal history. The disposition is specific: flattery, fear, or a generally supportive environment do not have the effect; only admiration does. A well-informed critic tells Mara: "If you admire him now, he will become admirable."

It seems Mara has a reason of some kind to admire Enzo. Go through the four marks one by one and say, for each, whether it places the critic's consideration on the RKR side, the WKR side, or fails to place it at all. Then answer: does Mara have a right-kind reason to admire Enzo, a wrong-kind reason, a reason of neither kind, or does the case show that the RKR/WKR distinction itself needs revision? If you defend a revision, state it explicitly and check whether it still handles the standard cases, such as the demon's threat and the pragmatic reason to believe. If you defend one of the first three verdicts, say what you are doing with the marks that pointed the other way.
