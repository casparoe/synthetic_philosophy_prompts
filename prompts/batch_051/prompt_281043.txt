I am currently taking a course on early modern philosophy and the history of economic thought, and we have spent several seminars on Berkeley's monetary writings from the 1730s. What has stayed with me is his insistence that the real wealth of a country consists in its industry, labour, and produce, and that money is best understood not as wealth in its own right but as a circulating claim—a ticket or counter whose office is to move goods and remunerate labour. This puts him at odds with the bullionist way of thinking we read against him, according to which the precious metals are the true and durable wealth of a nation and any paper instrument that is not fully anchored in, and ultimately convertible into, silver or gold is a flimsy substitute that will mislead people about the country's real stock.

I would like you to build me a thought experiment, not a historical account, in which Berkeley's account of money and the bullionist account yield opposite verdicts on one and the same policy proposal, even though both parties are stipulated to agree on every ordinary factual matter: the size of the harvest, the goods sitting in the warehouses, the willingness of labourers to work, and the credit of the issuer. The scenario should be set in a plausible 1730s Irish port or market town, should run no longer than a single paragraph, and must not simply assert that one side is correct. The disagreement should be generated by the competing conceptions of what money is, rather than by any disagreement about solvency, inflation, or administrative competence.

After the scenario, walk through what each theory says and why; then identify which feature of the case is doing the real work, explain how a defender of the bullionist view would attack the case, and indicate whether removing that feature collapses the case into an ordinary dispute about creditworthiness rather than about the nature of money. Please write the answer in British English.
