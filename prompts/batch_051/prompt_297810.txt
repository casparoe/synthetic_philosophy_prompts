I'm a PhD student in bioethics and last week my advisior and I got into it about a passage in James Rachels's 1975 New England Journal of Medicine article Active and Passive Euthanasia. Not the famous bathtub case, but a less-cited bit where he argues, roughly, that once you permit letting die, decisions about life and death end up turning on features that are morally irrelevant to whether the patient should live or die. I'm pretty sure he gives the example of an infant with Down syndrome and an intestinal blockage, where doctors will let the baby die by not treating the blockage, but they would never consider directly killing it, and Rachels thinks the distinction between those acts doesn't track anything about the baby's prospects or interests. I might be paraphrasing loosely, but that's the gist.

The thing is, law and medical ethics still very much treat the difference as real. A physician who withholds antibiotics from a dying pneumonia patient under a DNR is practising good medicine; the same physician who injects a lethal dose is a murderer. My advisor thinks there's a defensible line somewhere, maybe in the certainty of the causal chain or in what the doctor is licensed to do, but I'm not seeing it. I want you to act as an expert moral philosopher who works on the doing/allowing distinction and criminal law. Take the hardest version of the case: same patient, same intention, same outcome, same doctor, same duty, except in one version the doctor discontinues the ventilator and in the other the doctor suffocates the patient with a pillow. Is anything left that justifies punishing only one? Before you answer, brainstorm all the candidate justifications that courts and philosophers actually give, test each against that hard case, and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned. Be frank about what you can't settle by argument alone.
