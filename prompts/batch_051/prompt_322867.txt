I have been thinking about a passage in G. E. Moore’s Principia Ethica, and I want to see whether one of his conclusions survives a change he could not have anticipated. Moore distinguishes between asking what things ought to exist for their own sakes and asking what actions we ought to perform. About the first question he writes:

“It becomes plain that, for answers to the first question, no relevant evidence whatever can be adduced: from no other truth, except themselves alone, can it be inferred that they are either true or false.”

Later in the same book, he treats personal affection and the appreciation of beauty as among the greatest goods we can imagine. My understanding of his procedure is that he is not trying to prove this from anything else. He thinks that if you isolate a state of consciousness and ask whether it would be worth having purely for its own sake, the answer has to be seen directly. The only protection against error is to make sure you are really asking that question and not some other one.

I have been wondering whether that view survives a development he could not have foreseen. Suppose a person’s aesthetic and social life now comes mainly through algorithmic feeds. The systems behind those feeds are tuned by A/B testing to produce moments that feel like aesthetic appreciation or personal connection: a striking image, a moving short video, a parasocial bond with someone who seems to know you. These moments can have the outward and inward qualities Moore points to—pleasure in something beautiful, warmth in another person’s company—but they are also designed to be brief, repeatable, and optimized for engagement. If Moore’s method is right, the only evidence I have for calling beauty and friendship good in themselves is the felt answer I give when I attend to those states. But the system has been trained, hour by hour, on what makes me feel that answer. I cannot easily tell whether I am detecting the value Moore was talking about or simply responding to a very good simulation of it.

Does Moore’s argument about the intrinsic value of beauty and friendship survive this development? If it does, what exactly distinguishes the states he valued from the algorithmically optimized look-alikes? If it does not, does the problem lie with his conclusion, with his method, or with the way I have described the development? Please answer by saying what Moore’s argument needs to be true, and whether the development I have described gives it what it needs.
