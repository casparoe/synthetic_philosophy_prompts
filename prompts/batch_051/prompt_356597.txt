I'm a grad student TA'ing a survey course on 19th-century moral philosophy this term, and this week's pairing of Schopenhauer and Nietzsche has landed a little too close to home: my brother has been going through a long depressive stretch, and I keep noticing how much of the sympathy people offer him arrives wrapped in something he experiences as condescending and exhausting. So I'm probably reading the material with more feeling than usual, which also makes me worried that I'm either grading too charitably or too harshly.

The question I set for the first paper was:

Schopenhauer holds that compassion (Mitleid) is the only genuinely moral motive, while Nietzsche repeatedly treats pity as something deeply suspect. Explain as precisely as you can (a) the role Schopenhauer assigns to compassion and what makes it moral on his view, and (b) the main grounds for Nietzsche's suspicion of pity. Then say whether you think the two authors are defending/attacking the same psychological phenomenon, and why.

One student (call them S.) submitted the following answer:

Schopenhauer thinks there are three fundamental incentives behind human action: egoism, malice, and compassion. Egoism wants my own well-being at the expense of others; malice wants another's harm; compassion wants another's well-being. For Schopenhauer, only actions springing from compassion have genuine moral worth, because egoism and malice are always self-directed or harm-directed. Compassion on his account is not a cold recognition that someone is in pain. It is feeling the other's suffering as my own, so that I am moved to act for their sake. The reason this is possible is that behind the principium individuationis all beings are manifestations of the same Will, so the distinction between my suffering and yours is ultimately only phenomenal. To be compassionate is to cognize the noumenal identity of all willing beings, and this is why compassion is the moral bedrock for him.

Nietzsche, by contrast, sees pity as the great danger. He thinks it is depressing and contagious: the pitier takes the sufferer's pain into themselves so the total amount of suffering in the world goes up without any corresponding benefit. It is also a subtle form of condescension, because it puts the pitier in the position of looking down on the suffering person they are supposedly helping. This connects to Nietzsche's slave morality, where the weak band together and turn their suffering into something they can use to guilt the strong. So pity preserves and spreads weakness instead of enabling anyone to overcome suffering.

In my view, these two authors are definitely discussing the same phenomenon — the German word is even the same, Mitleid — and they simply assign opposite values to it. Schopenhauer's compassion is exactly what Nietzsche pillories: a contagious emotional merging in which the boundary between me and the other dissolves. Schopenhauer takes that merging to be the source of all moral worth, while Nietzsche takes it to be a self-abnegating, life-denying affect that should be overcome. Their disagreement is therefore purely evaluative: they see the same thing and disagree about whether it is good.
