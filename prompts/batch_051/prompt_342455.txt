I recently got drawn into a running argument with my brother about whether apparently unselfish behavior in animals — vampire bats sharing blood meals, ground squirrels giving alarm calls, worker bees giving up reproduction — is a problem for an evolutionary view of behavior. To prepare, I read a general-audience article on the gene's-eye view of evolution. It summarized the view like this:

"The gene's-eye view, associated mainly with G. C. Williams and Richard Dawkins, treats the gene, rather than the organism or the group, as the fundamental unit of natural selection. Organisms die, but genes are copied across generations, so Dawkins describes bodies as temporary 'vehicles' or 'survival machines' that genes build to carry themselves into future generations. Calling genes 'selfish' is metaphorical: a gene is selfish in the sense that its effects tend, over many generations, to increase the frequency of copies of itself in the gene pool, not in the sense that it has intentions. The view dissolves the puzzle of altruism, because an organism can lower its own reproductive output and still spread the relevant gene by helping relatives who carry copies of it; Hamilton's rule, rb > c, states when such helping is favored. Dawkins's real conclusion, then, is that organisms are shaped to maximize their inclusive fitness — their own reproduction plus the reproduction of relatives weighted by relatedness. Once we accept this, group-level selection can be set aside: a gene could not spread by undermining its own transmission for the good of the species, so traits do not evolve because they benefit groups. The familiar objection that genes cannot be selfish because they lack intentions is, as the article concedes, just a confusion about the role of the metaphor."

I found this summary very persuasive, perhaps suspiciously so, and I want to know whether I can trust it before I repeat it. I have also come across mentions of "segregation distorters" and "outlaw genes" — cases where genes seem to spread by compromising the reproduction of the organism that carries them — and I cannot see how the article's claim that organisms maximize inclusive fitness leaves room for that.

Please audit the summary. Which parts are faithful to the gene's-eye view as defended by Williams and Dawkins?
