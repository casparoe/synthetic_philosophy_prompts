Me and a couple of friends got into an arguement about tipping and queueing while we were on holiday, and I've been trying to write it up as a tidy numbered argument because I actually think it might be valid, but I keep going back and forth. I'd be curious what you make of it.

(1) In any ordinary restaurant or on any busy pavement, there are things people do simply because everyone expects them: leaving 15–20% in some countries, waiting your turn in others, not crossing until the green man appears in others. Call these local conventions. (2) If a local convention is something nearly everyone in the situation expects everyone else to follow, then someone who knowingly breaks it will cause predictable annoyance, confusion, and a small breakdown in how smoothly the place runs. (3) It is wrong, other things equal, to knowingly bring about that kind of predictable annoyance and confusion for no better reason than "I don't fancy it." (4) So a tourist who knows the local tipping, queueing or crossing convention and breaks it anyway acts wrongly, even if they would be in the right back home.

I can't work out whether this is actually sound, or whether the phrase "other things equal" in (3) is smuggling in the conclusion, because sometimes the convention itself is exploitative or daft and breaking it might be the right thing. I also can't tell whether the move from "causes predictable annoyance" to "it is wrong" goes through, or whether a convention only binds the people who have implicitly accepted it, which a tourist hasn't. Could you go through it step by step and tell me whether the argument is valid, which step fails if any does, and what the sharpest objection to it is? I'd rather you show which reading of each premise the argument needs and whether it still works on that reading, not just point vaguely at one step.
