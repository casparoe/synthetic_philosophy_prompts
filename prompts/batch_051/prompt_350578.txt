Since retiring from a career in atmospheric science, I have been attending a fortnightly reading group at the local university on the philosophical and political questions raised by climate intervention, and I keep returning to the problem of termination shock. If stratospheric aerosol injection were deployed to hold global temperatures down and were then stopped abruptly—because of war, financial collapse, or a change of government—the masking effect would disappear within a year or two and the suppressed warming would arrive as a sharp pulse, plausibly more damaging than the gradual warming it had been offsetting. A governance device discussed in our group, which we have taken to calling a termination escrow, is intended to address precisely this. Before commencing operations, any state or coalition that begins stratospheric aerosol injection would be required to deposit with an independent international trustee funds sufficient to maintain the injection and then to taper it over several decades at the scale initially deployed. The required sum would be set by an expert panel on the basis of projected costs, with mandatory top-ups if those costs rose. The funds would sit outside the operator's control, would be protected from sovereign seizure, and would automatically pay for continued operation and for the taper if the operator stopped, collapsed, or was ordered to stop by whatever body is authorised to issue such an order.

I am not asking whether the escrow is, in the abstract, a good idea. I want to understand how it would behave in practice. Treat it as a mechanism: what incentives does it create for would-be deployers, for the trustee, for states that oppose deployment, and for the insurers or lenders who might be drawn in; where is it likely to be gamed first; what second-order effects does it have on the moral-hazard debate and on the politics of who controls the escrow; and can the obvious failure modes be repaired without gutting the scheme? I would be grateful for an answer written in British English.
