I'm working through some material for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I've convinced myself of something that seems too strong to be right — which usually means I've got a hidden premise somewhere, but I can't find it. We've been studying the objective Bayesian claim that there are uniquely rational priors licensed by evidence alone. The attractive rule on the table is the maximum entropy principle: among all probability assignments compatible with your total evidence, adopt the one with the greatest Shannon entropy, since it is maximally non-committal — it goes beyond the evidence as little as possible. In the finite case this is lovely: if your evidence is only that there are n symmetric possibilities, MaxEnt says assign 1/n to each. But consider the following argument, which seems to show that this rule, and any rule like it, is representation-dependent and therefore cannot deliver a uniquely rational prior.

(1) Suppose your only evidence about a square produced by a calibrated machine is that its area A lies between 1 and 4 square feet. By MaxEnt, your rational prior over A is the uniform density on [1, 4].
(2) The same evidence, stated in terms of side length L, is that L lies between 1 and 2 feet. By MaxEnt, your rational prior over L is the uniform density on [1, 2].
(3) But A = L², and under a change of variables a uniform density over A pushes forward to a density over L proportional to 2L, while a uniform density over L pushes forward to a density over A proportional to 1/(2√A). So the two MaxEnt prescriptions, applied to one and the same body of evidence, contradict each other.
(4) A rational prior should be fixed by the evidence itself; it cannot depend on which mathematically equivalent description of the evidence we happened to use.
(5) So the maximum entropy principle is either inconsistent — it mandates two incompatible distributions for the same state of evidence — or incomplete — it needs an extra, non-evidential rule to pick the parameterization, such as "use the coordinate you happened to measure in" or "use a parameter linear in the measurement scale."
(6) The same difficulty, mutatis mutandis, infects the principle of indifference and any other rule that claims to convert a state of complete or partial ignorance into a unique probability assignment; every such rule is hostage to an arbitrary choice of variables.
(7) Therefore, the core objective Bayesian claim is false: there are no uniquely rational, purely evidence-based priors. What get called "rational priors" are at best conventional modeling choices, not something the evidence commands.

I'm most reluctant to give up the finite-case intuition in the preamble — that a fully symmetric description really does force the uniform distribution — and I'm also attached to step (4), because if a prior flips around under a mere redescription of the same information, calling it "rational" seems to lose its point. My guess is that something goes wrong when I treat the area-based and side-length-based statements as "the same evidence" in (1) and (2), but I can't explain how they could fail to be the same evidence when I know nothing else and the two statements are logically equivalent. Which step do you reject, and what does that rejection cost? If the right repair is to add an invariance or transformation-group requirement on top of MaxEnt, tell me exactly what that constraint would have to look like — and what part of the objective Bayesian ambition it quietly abandons.
