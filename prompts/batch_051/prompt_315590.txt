You are a philosopher of physics specialising in the foundations of statistical mechanics. In my course on the direction of time we have been studying what is usually called the Past Hypothesis: the claim that the thermodynamic arrow — the fact that entropy reliably increases towards the future — is explained by the early universe's having been in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, together with the statistical tendency of isolated systems to evolve towards higher-entropy macrostates. I find this account convincing, but I want a precise map of what it risks. Please think carefully and begin by brainstorming the main ways it could fail, then tell me: what would count against it? Which observations, arguments, or theoretical developments would be decisive, and which merely awkward? Which objections should a defender of the Past Hypothesis concede as real costs, and which would they be right to dismiss as misunderstandings? I am especially interested in whether the account is insulated — whether it can accommodate almost any apparent counter-evidence by re-specifying the initial macrostate or the measure over microstates — and whether that insulation is a sign of flexibility or of unfalsifiability. Finally, I would value your verdict on what I find the most unsettling objection: that the Past Hypothesis merely relocates the puzzle by treating the low-entropy initial state as an unexplained given. Is that a fatal defect, a tolerable cost, or no defect at all? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely troublesome.
