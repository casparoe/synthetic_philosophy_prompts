I'm a science and ideas journalist, currently writing a piece on how people reason about obligations to people they love under pressure. One of my interviewees kept reaching for the phrase 'one thought too many'. The underlying case, as I've now understood it, is Bernard Williams's: a man can save only one of two people in danger, his wife or a stranger; he saves his wife, and that seems fine; but if he has to lean on a principle to do it — that in this sort of case it's permissible to save one's wife — then he's had one thought too many. The phrase is doing a lot of work for this intervewee, and I suspect it means something different each time it shows up. In one remark it seemed to mean that any reflection at all in a crisis is suspect; in another it meant a specific sort of moral self-justification; in another it meant something much larger, about whether we can live by impartial rules at all. Can you lay out the different precise claims 'one thought too many' could be making — what the 'extra' thought is supposed to be, what exactly is wrong with it, and what the phrase would establish about moral theory if true — and then say which of those readings actually supports the job my interviewee was using it to do?
