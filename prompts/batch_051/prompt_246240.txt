A colleague of mine keeps pushing a contextualist line in epistemology by saying that the word 'knows' works just like 'tall' or 'flat'. Her pitch is this: nobody thinks there's a deep metaphysical puzzle about whether a person is tall -- it depends on the comparison class, and the standard can be raised or lowered by the conversation. She thinks 'knows' is like that too. So when the sceptic says we don't know we're not brains in vats, the sceptic isn't revealing a gap in our evidence; she's just cranking the standard for 'knows' up as high as it will go, the way a basketball coach might set the bar for 'tall' absurdly high. Her conclusion is that the sceptical problem dissovles rather than gets answered or refuted.

I want to know how far this analogy actually goes. Can you give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the gradable adjective, the comparison class, the contextual standard, and so on -- and then the places where the mapping breaks down? I'm especially interested in whether 'knows' actually behaves like a gradable adjective in ordinary use (can you say 'very knows', 'known enough', 'he knows it better than she does'?), and whether features like factivity and the role of evidence have any real analogue in the case of 'tall' or 'flat'. And then the question I actually care about: does her dissolution of scepticism survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy doing all the work? If it doesn't survive, is there a better analogy she should be using instead?
