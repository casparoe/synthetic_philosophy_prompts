Here is the setup. A two-person bargaining problem is a pair \((S,d)\), where \(S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2\) is the set of utility pairs achievable by agreement and \(d \in S\) is the disagreement point, the payoffs if no agreement is reached. Assume \(S\) is compact, convex, contains \(d\), and satisfies free disposal: if \(x \in S\) and \(d_i \leq y_i \leq x_i\) for both players, then \(y \in S\). The ideal point \(b(S,d)\) has coordinates \(b_i = \max\{x_i : x \in S, x \geq d\}\), and we assume \(b_i > d_i\).

A solution \(\phi\) assigns to each problem \((S,d)\) a point \(\phi(S,d) \in S\). Two standard properties matter here. Scale invariance says: for any \(a_1, a_2 > 0\) and any \(c_1, c_2\), if we transform \(S\) and \(d\) to \(S' = \{(a_1x_1+c_1, a_2x_2+c_2) : x \in S\}\) and \(d' = (a_1d_1+c_1, a_2d_2+c_2)\), then \(\phi(S',d') = (a_1\phi_1(S,d)+c_1, a_2\phi_2(S,d)+c_2)\). Symmetry says: if \(d_1 = d_2\) and \(S\) is closed under swapping coordinates — i.e., \((x_1,x_2) \in S\) iff \((x_2,x_1) \in S\) — then \(\phi_1(S,d) = \phi_2(S,d)\).

The Nash solution \(N(S,d)\) is the point maximizing \((x_1-d_1)(x_2-d_2)\) over all \(x \in S\) with \(x \geq d\). The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution \(KS(S,d)\) is the maximal point in \(S\) lying on the segment from \(d\) to the ideal point \(b\); equivalently, it is \(d + \lambda(b-d)\) for the largest \(\lambda \leq 1\) with that point in \(S\). Both solutions are scale invariant, and both satisfy symmetry.

Now consider the following argument, which seems to prove too much.

(1) Both the Nash and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions are scale invariant.

(2) Therefore, for any problem \((S,d)\), we may apply independent positive affine transformations to move \(d\) to \((0,0)\) and the ideal point \(b\) to \((1,1)\). Since both solutions merely follow that transformation of coordinates, normalizing in this way is without loss of generality.

(3) In such a normalized problem, the segment from \(d\) to \(b\) is just the diagonal \(\{(t,t) : t \in [0,1]\}\). Hence \(KS(S,d) = (\alpha,\alpha)\), where \(\alpha\) is the largest \(t\) such that \((t,t) \in S\).

(4) But in a normalized problem the two players are in a symmetrical position in every respect that the solution concept is allowed to care about: each starts at 0, each has ideal payoff 1, and any further difference between the two roles would have to reside in the shape of the feasible set. Yet once the disagreement point and the ideal point have both been fixed, a difference in shape is only a difference in the curvature of utility trade-offs, and that is exactly the kind of representation-dependent information that scale invariance was meant to neutralize.

(5) The Nash solution satisfies symmetry. Applied to the normalized problem, symmetry requires \(N(S,d)\) to give equal payoffs, so \(N(S,d) = (\beta,\beta)\) for some \(\beta\).

(6) Among the diagonal points feasible in the normalized problem, the Nash product is \(t \cdot t = t^2\), which is strictly increasing in \(t\). By comprehensiveness, the feasible diagonal points form an interval \([0,\alpha]\). So the Nash solution must choose the largest feasible \(t\), namely \(\alpha\). Thus \(\beta = \alpha\).

(7) Therefore \(N(S,d) = KS(S,d) = (\alpha,\alpha)\). Since the original problem was arbitrary, the Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions coincide on every bargaining problem.
