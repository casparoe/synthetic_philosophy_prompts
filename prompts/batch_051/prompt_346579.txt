I want a working definition of "guilt" and "shame" — a criterion that sorts a painful episode of self-assessment into guilt, shame, both, or neither — and I want it tested against the following cases.

1. A woman lied to her closest friend, was never found out, and months later still feels low and gnawing when she thinks about it.
2. A shoplifter made to stand outside a school cafeteria holding a sign feels, above all, the fact of being seen as a thief rather than the fact of having stolen.
3. A visibly poor adult feels defective and unworthy when well-dressed strangers look at him, though he has done nothing wrong.
4. A soldier whose friend was killed beside him feels, irrationally, that it should have been him.
5. A CEO flees before a fraud trial because he cannot bear facing former colleagues and the press, while insisting he did nothing wrong.
6. A person whose inherited wealth comes partly from an ancestor's slaveholding feels polluted by it, though no one blames them.
7. An unrepentant fraudster is angry at having been caught but feels neither guilt nor shame.

I lean toward classifying (1) as guilt, (5) as shame, and (7) as neither; I have no settled view about (4) and (6). Propose the definitions, run them through all seven cases, and say where they give the wrong answer or no answer, revising or defending any surprising verdicts.

Then use the resulting distinction to take a position on two questions: which of the two emotions is the better moral emotion for a society to rely on, and whether the old anthropological contrast between "guilt cultures" and "shame cultures" names a difference a culture can choose — through child-rearing practices or public shaming — or something too deep in human psychology to select for.
