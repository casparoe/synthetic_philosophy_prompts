I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether factual descriptions of human nature can ever settle moral questions. The piece turns on the is–ought gap: the principle, usually traced to Hume's observation that moral arguments slip from statements about how things are to statements about how they ought to be, and to G. E. Moore's claim that any proposed definition of goodness in natural terms seems to leave an open question. In the course of reporting, I interviewed a philosopher who maintains that the gap is overstated, because of what she calls "thick" evaluative concepts — concepts such as cruel, generous, and cowardly, which appear to be simultaneously descriptive and evaluative. She gave me the following reconstruction of her argument, and I need to know whether to present it in the piece as a genuine refutation of the is–ought gap or as a seductive mistake.

(1) A thick concept has two components: its application is governed by descriptive features of a situation, and applying it also carries a positive or negative evaluation.
(2) For the concept cruel, the descriptive component can be stated in purely non-evaluative terms: an act is cruel just in case the agent intentionally causes pain to a sentient creature, and the act was not needed to prevent a greater amount of pain, death, or serious injury.
(3) There are, therefore, cases — a guard breaking a restrained prisoner's fingers while asking no questions and seeking no information is one — in which the purely descriptive conditions of cruelty are satisfied.
(4) In such a case, one can validly infer that the act is cruel.
(5) It is analytically true, part of what it is to understand the word, that if an act is cruel then, other things equal, it is morally wrong.
(6) Therefore, from a purely descriptive statement (the guard intentionally caused pain of such-and-such a kind, and the act was not needed to prevent a greater pain, death, or injury) one can validly derive a moral conclusion (the act was wrong).
(7) But if the is–ought gap holds as a fully general principle, no purely descriptive statement entails any moral conclusion.
(8) Therefore, the is–ought gap is false.

I can't tell whether the argument is airtight or whether normativity is being smuggled in somewhere. Could you assess it step by step? Is it valid? If it is valid, which premise should a defender of the is–ought gap reject, and what is the strongest version of that premise on which the argument would go through? If it is not valid, say exactly where the inference breaks and why. Please be precise about what "purely descriptive" would have to mean at each occurrence for the argument to work. Keep the response under 900 words.
