I have been going back and forth with a colleague in the neuroscience department about what the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia actually shows. She takes it to be about as clean a dissociation as one could hope for: a patient will say that a pinprick or a hot surface hurts, can rate its intensity and point to the place, yet reports that it is not unpleasant and that she has no urge to withdraw or stop it. My colleague concludes that the sensory character of pain and the badness of pain are separate components, and that pain can therefore occur without being bad. I keep pushing back that the observations only establish a breakdown between the stimulus and the usual verbal, motor, and autonomic expressions of aversion, or a change in how the patient talks about her own state, and that the deeper thesis, namely that pain itself could exist without badness, is not settled by any of this. Neither of us wants to keep trading intuitions, and we are hoping you can referee by proposing something sharper.

As a philosopher of mind who also reads the pain literature, think through this from first principles rather than simply rehearsing the standard dissociation story. Start by distinguishing the interpretations on offer before you defend one, then design the strongest test you can of the claim that pain asymbolia genuinely dissociates the sensory quality of pain from its unpleasantness or badness, as opposed to merely dissociating it from the reporting of unpleasantness, from avoidance behavior, or from autonomic arousal. Be concrete about subjects, measures, controls, and the exact pattern of results that would favor the dissociation reading and the pattern that would favor a deflationary reading. Then be honest about the residue. If the test came out in favor of the dissociation, exactly which claim would have been supported — that unpleasantness is a separable component in these patients, or that pain is not essentially bad — and what would a positive result still leave open, whether for experimental reasons or because it concerns what we mean by pain? Finally, tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail and which consideration or alternative explanation would most likely change your mind.

I am not looking for references or a reading list, and I would like the answer in continuous prose, as in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
