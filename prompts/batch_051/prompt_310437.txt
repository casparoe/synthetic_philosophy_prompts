Randomized controlled trials present a familiar ethical difficulty. A physician who enrolls a patient normally owes that patient the treatment she judges best, but a trial assigns treatment by chance. The standard solution, clinical equipoise, says that randomization is permissible only while the community of competent clinicians is genuinely divided about which arm is better. The weight of the argument rests on locating the uncertainty in the community rather than in the individual physician. An individual doctor's uncertainty is too fragile and too private to license random assignment; an honest, professionally recognized disagreement among experts, by contrast, means no enrolled patient is being denied a treatment that expert opinion has already settled on. The trial is resolving a question the profession has not yet resolved.

Now consider response-adaptive randomization. Under this design the allocation ratio is not fixed in advance. As outcome data accumulate, an algorithm shifts the probability of assignment so that later patients are more likely to receive the arm that is performing better so far, while the trial continues until a prespecified stopping rule is met. The trial therefore acts on its own emerging evidence before the community of experts would regard the question as settled.

Does clinical equipoise survive this development? At first glance the two look incompatible: the algorithm is, in some sense, forming and applying a judgment that one arm is superior even though the expert disagreement that justified randomization remains open. But one might reply that shifting allocation probabilities is not the same as declaring an arm superior, and that community equipoise is a claim about the state of expert opinion, not about the trial's internal statistics. Say precisely which premise of the clinical equipoise argument this design puts under pressure, whether the argument can be repaired to cover it, or whether trials of this kind need a different ethical basis altogether. I am asking what happens to the argument itself, not for a policy verdict on adaptive designs.
