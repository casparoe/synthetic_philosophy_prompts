I'm a novelist, not an economist, so go easy on the jargon. In my next book the protagonist is an acquisitions editor at a midsize publisher, maybe five years in, and the whole second act hinges on her being able to tell the difference between a manuscript that's genuinely bad and one that's only "bad" because everybody is passing on it because everybody else did. I've been reading about information cascades and rational herding—the idea that once a few early people make the same call, later people rationally ignore their own private read and follow, so the later choices stop carrying information for the next person, plus the related thing where people go along because being wrong with the crowd feels safer than being wrong alone. I don't need a lecture on the theory; I need it turned into something my character can run.

So: write a decision procedure my protagonist could actually use when deciding whether to acquire or pass on a submission. She makes maybe eight to ten of these calls a week, the stakes are a low-six-figure advance and, in the story, her career, and she usually has a couple days to decide. She cant run experiments, and she can only see partial information: the first chapters, a reader's report, comp titles, and the fact that other houses have passed or are bidding. The procedure should be a checklist or a set of questions in order, in her voice (smart, a little cynical, not academic), with what counts as a good enough answer to each question, where it stops with a pass, and where it tells her to go against the herd. Mark the places where no checklist can settle it and it's just editorial judgment dressed up as data.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a literary debut that's been passed on by seven editors in a week, and she loves the first chapter; (2) a celebrity memoir that every house suddenly wants after one big offer, but her own reader's report says the manuscript is thin; and (3) a case where the procedure gives her an answer she won't want to hear—you pick the details. Also tell me honestly what the procedure can't do, so I don't write a character who treats it like magic.

Keep it under 1,200 words, and dont make it a textbook; I want to be able to lift most of it into dialogue or a scene.
