I’m an undergraduate currently taking a course on epistemic logic, and a parallel has been nagging at me that I’d like help thinking through. In the standard possible-worlds framework, knowledge is modeled as truth in all epistemically accessible worlds. One consequence is logical omniscience: if an agent knows p, and p entails q, then the agent also knows q; likewise, the agent knows every logical truth. This is usually treated as a defect of the idealization. The standard repairs — awareness structures, impossible worlds, algorithmic knowledge, resource-bounded reasoning — all try to make the modeled agent’s knowledge respect what a finite reasoner could actually derive.

I’m also taking a jurisprudence elective, and the rule that ignorance of the law is no excuse (ignorantia juris non excusat) struck me as having the same shape. A legal system does not claim that citizens have actually read the whole body of valid norms. It holds them answerable as if they knew every norm once it has been validly promulgated. That is normally defended as a normative fiction: knowledge is imputed to the citizen so the system can function, not because anyone thinks the citizen factually possesses it. This sounded oddly close to a “commitment” reading of logical omniscience — the idea that knowledge in epistemic logic isn’t a description of what you have consciously derived, but of what you are committed to once you accept certain premises.

I want to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial. On one side, both look like the same tractability-preserving idealization: rather than modeling the subject’s actual, imperfect epistemic state, the system imputes knowledge of a whole closure class. And the legal system even seems to build in something like resource bounds — promulgation requirements, mistake-of-law carve-outs, and void-for-vagueness doctrines — which might map onto the repairs to logical omniscience. On the other side, the legal rule is prescriptive or ascriptive: it concerns what a citizen is answerable for, not what she does know, whereas epistemic logic appears to be modeling a state of knowledge, even if a highly idealized one.
