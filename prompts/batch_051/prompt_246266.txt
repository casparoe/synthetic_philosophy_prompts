Next month I'm giving a paper at a department I'm visiting, and I'd like to walk in ready for the Q&A. The view I'm defending is roughly this: there is no general moral duty to be legible to others, where by "legible" I mean not sincerity in what you actually say but the broader readability of your reasons, intentions, and likely next moves to the people around you. My line is that we owe others non-deception and sincere avowals, but beyond that legibility is either an instrumental good (it greases coordination and trust) or a role-specific duty (fiduciaries, teachers, public officials), never a freestanding requirement of respect. I add the corollary that we may permissibly be opaque about our reasons even when our oppacity predictably frustrates others, so long as we don't lie or manipulate them. The argument, in a sentence: respect forbids manipulating or deceiving people, not being hard to read; a duty to be legible would be a duty to make other people's practical reasoning easier at the price of one's own authority over self-disclosure, and I don't think any such general duty follows from treating persons as ends. My test cases are deliberately homely: the friend who ends a relationship and won't say why, a colleague who gives a bare yes-no answer without the reasoning behind it, an official acting on a properly non-public reason.

The audience is mostly Kantian and contractualist moral and political philosophers, plus two philosophers of testimony who work on epistemic dependence and second-personal reasons, and one virtue ethicist who writes on honesty and self-presentation. Nobody in the room is a privacy-rights person or an existentialist, who would have been my natural allies, so I'm the odd one out.

What I'm after is a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of their positions. Tell me the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in roughly the order of how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way those people would phrase them, with a two-minute reply to each and a note on where that reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And at the end, flag where your own read is least confident: which objection in your ordering is most questionable, and what consideration about the audience or the argument would change your mind about it.
