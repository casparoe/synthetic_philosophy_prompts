I have spent the last few months trying to generalize the regulative principle of worship into a wider theory of institutions, and I would like an honest assessment of the result rather than encouragement.

The regulative principle, in its original setting, is the Protestant position that corporate worship may include only those elements positively commanded by Scripture: whatever is not commanded is forbidden. The rival "normative principle" says that whatever is not forbidden is permitted. The usual justification for the stricter rule is that worship is not ours to design; its point is fidelity to a received standard, not the expression of our preferences.

I have come to think this inversion of the default, from permissive to prohibitive, is the correct default for a much larger class of practices. Consider practices whose continued identity is precisely what gives them their authority or meaning: legal procedure, scientific protocol, professional oaths, monastic rules, the ceremony around a wedding, a code-review checklist, even the rituals of a small community. In these practices, an uncommanded addition is not a neutral improvement but a change of subject. An oath with an extra clause is a different oath. A liturgy with a new element is a different liturgy. Procedure with an ad-hoc step is different procedure. Each addition looks locally reasonable, but because the changes are cumulative and each one is defensible on its own, the practice can drift into something else with no single blameworthy step.

I have been calling this the closed-enumeration principle: for practices whose point is fidelity rather than optimization, the set of authorized moves is closed, and whatever is not explicitly authorized should be treated as forbidden. On my formulation this is not about consequences (the new step may be harmless or even useful), and it is not about our ignorance of why the old rule exists (I know Chesterton's fence, the principle that one should understand why a fence was built before removing it, and I think this is adjacent but different). It is about the conditions under which a practice remains the same practice.

I want to know three things. First, is the distinction between fidelity-practices and optimization-practices a real joint in nature, or can any practice be redescribed either way, making the principle vacuous? Second, what is the nearest existing idea — I suspect someone has already said this more carefully — and is mine actually distinct from it? Third, where does it break? Give me the strongest cases in which fidelity to a practice requires an uncommanded addition, or in which the closed-enumeration default would freeze a living practice past the point where it serves anyone.
