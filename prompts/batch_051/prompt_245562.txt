I have been trying to decide whether a parallel I noticed between a result in statistical learning and an old problem in epistemology is deep or merely superficial, and I am not sure how to test it.

The learning-theoretic side is the no-free-lunch theorem for supervised learning. Take a finite training set. Consider every possible function from input to output, each assigned equal weight. Averaged over all of them, any two learning algorithms have the same expected error on cases outside the training set. Learning therefore cannot be justified unless one already assumes that some classes of target function — the smooth, the simple, the compressible — are more likely in the relevant domain. The assumption does the work, not the algorithm.

The epistemological side is the problem of induction. Given any finite run of observations, infinitely many regularities fit the data perfectly and then part ways on the next case: green up to now and blue thereafter, and so on. No sound argument seems to establish that the future will resemble the past, and any empirical argument for that principle appears circular. So induction, too, appears to depend on an assumption about the world's structure that cannot itself be proven from the data.

I am tempted to map the two pieces onto one another as follows: target function corresponds to the objective regularity of the world; learning algorithm corresponds to a rule of inductive extrapolation; prior bias toward smooth or simple functions corresponds to the assumption that nature is uniform; the equal weighting of all possible functions corresponds to the skeptical refusal to favor any continuation of the data; and expected error corresponds to expected reliability of a prediction rule.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, or am I pattern-matching? In particular: does the no-free-lunch theorem add anything to the problem of induction, or does it merely restate it in the language of expected loss? Does the theorem's most notable artifact — that no algorithm is better than any other when all target functions are equally weighted — transfer as a claim about induction, namely that, absent a prior favoring regular worlds, every extrapolation rule is exactly as well-justified as counterinduction? And where does the parallel come apart? Say exactly what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer, and name at least one thing the parallel predicts that is false.
