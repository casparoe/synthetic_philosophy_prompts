I cover the social sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a short explainer about why capitalism took of when and where it did. A freelancer sent me the paragraph below to explain Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis for our readers. Something about it feels off, but I dont know the actual argument well enough to fix it, and I suspect the version people remember is not what Weber wrote. Here's the draft, more or less as he sent it:

"Weber's thesis in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism is that the Reformation made modern capitalism possible by teaching people that hard work and making money were signs that you were saved. Before Protestantism, economic life was still bound by the older idea that a person should earn only as much as they needed, and profit was at best grudgingly tolerated. The Calvinists, terrified by the doctrine of predestination, realized they could prove to themselves that they were saved by working successfully in their worldly 'calling,' and so they began to accumulate capital and reinvest it instead of spending it on pleasure. Protestantism therefore caused the spirit of capitalism, and once this work ethic was established it gradually spread to non-Protestants as well. Part of the evidence was that Protestant countries and regions tended to industrialize earlier and were materially richer than Catholic ones. The overall lesson is that ideas, not material conditions, are the engine of economic change."

I want to know how fair this is as a summary, for a publication where the reader may never open the original. Which parts would Weber, or a careful reader of Weber, accept, and which parts are popularized or subtly wrong? I'm especially unsure about the causal claim (I thought he was more cagey than "caused") and about the idea that wealth was a sign of salvation (I remember something about anxiety and proof, but not about wealth). And does the final lesson survive once the accurate parts are restated? If I'm manufacturing a problem and the paragraph is basically fine for a popular explainer, tell me that instead of inventing objections; but if the errors would systematically mislead a reader about what he actually claimed and why it matters, I'd rather fix them before we publish. Write it as continuous prose, like an email, and please don't use headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
