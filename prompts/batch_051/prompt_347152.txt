I'm working on a novel about a guy who runs a small pest control company, and he keeps noticing an asymmetry he doesnt know what to do with. If a kid pulls the legs off a beetle, everyone treats it as a bad sign—deliberate cruelty to a single bug reads as a character flaw. But nobody blinks at spraying a field and killing millions of aphids, or at a summer windshield covered in insects. My character starts thinking in expected-value terms: if each insect has even a small chance of being minimally conscious, the vast numbers seem to make the "accidental" killing enormously significant, maybe more than the single act of torture everyone condemns. He wants to know whether this is real moral progress or a misuse of probability. What is the strongest case that ordinary attitudes here are consistent—that is, that the low probability of insect sentience, or the distinction between direct cruelty and side-effect killing, or something else, genuinely does the justifying work? And what is the best case that the asymmetry is just a psychological habit with bad math behind it? I'd rather have him land on the stronger argument even if it makes him a bit unhinged. If the whole setup is confused—say, probability of sentience is the wrong kind of thing to multiply by population size, or the expected-value framing begs the question—tell me plainly and I'll rewrite the scene.
