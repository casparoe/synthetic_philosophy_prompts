I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about frugality, and my reactions to concrete cases keep pulling me in two directions. On some days frugality looks like a straightforward virtue of character: don't waste, extract the full use from what you already have, and treat unused possessions as a quiet reproach. On other days it looks purely instrumental: resources matter only because they could have gone to someone else or to some better end, so the real question is whether my choices promote overall thrift and well-being rather than whether I personally am frugal. I can't tell which of these readings my own gut is tracking, so I want to test a set of my verdicts against candidate principles.

Here are the verdicts, reported as honestly as I can. Leaving a restaurant meal half-eaten when I am full and no one else would have eaten it strikes me as a small wrong, even though the money is spent either way and no further good is lost. Buying a cheap pan that warps within a year instead of a heavier one that would last twenty years I count as a failure of frugality, a false economy, even though it looked like saving money at the moment. Taking a free branded tote bag I do not need, solely because it is free, seems mildly blameworthy even though no one is harmed and no resource is diverted from anyone. Keeping an expensive coffee machine a relative gave me, still in its box out of politeness, feels genuinely wasteful; I should have declined it or found it a home where it would be used. Driving across town to save three dollars on groceries, spending more in time and fuel than I save, is not frugality in my eyes, just a fixation on not overpaying. Yet spending eighty dollars on a restaurant meal with friends, a meal I could have cooked cheaply at home, bothers me not at all. And throwing away an apple I let rot in the fridge bothers me far more than its price would suggest it should.

The pair I cannot reconcile is this: I am entirely comfortable spending eighty dollars on a meal that uses resources, generates plate waste, and could have been replaced by a cheaper home-cooked dinner, while I judge myself rather sharply for one forgotten apple and for a half-eaten solitary plate whose waste changes nothing for anyone. So my verdicts do not track consequences in any simple way, and they also do not treat every enjoyed use of resources as automatically fine. I would like to know what principle, if any, gets all or most of these verdicts. In particular, is frugality best understood as a duty to be frugal, a requirement on my own conduct that forbids waste regardless of downstream effects, or as an instrumental commitment to promote frugality, understood as getting more total use or well-being out of the same resources, even where that might sometimes demand the opposite of personal thrift? Please state the principle or the small set of candidate principles precisely, identify which of my verdicts each captures and which ones it forces me to abandon, and tell me what the resulting account says about a case I keep going back and forth on: buying food in bulk to save money and then throwing part of it out when it spoils before I can eat it.
