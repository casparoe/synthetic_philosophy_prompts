Please write an argument in defense of the following thesis: if one accepts the principles behind G. E. M. Anscombe's condemnation of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, one is committed to condemning the Allied area bombing of German and Japanese cities as the same kind of moral wrong, and an attempt to exempt one while condemning the other will have to give up at least one premise she actually held. In the course of the argument, address the two strongest lines of resistance. First, the claim that atomic bombs were intended to attack civilian morale directly, whereas area bombing was an imprecise attack on industrial or military targets. Second, the claim that the atomic bombs were chosen to avoid a bloody invasion, so the civilian deaths were an unfortunate side effect rather than the chosen means. Explain how Anscombe's views on intention and noncombatant immunity would treat each of these moves, and specify which commitments would have to be revised or abandoned for the distinction to survive. Then draw out the implication for postwar honors: if Anscombe was right to protest an honorary degree for Truman, what does the same reasoning require with respect to memorials or honors for those who planned or ordered the firebombing campaigns? If you think the thesis as stated is too strong or rests on a misreading of Anscombe, say so plainly and defend the thesis you take to be the correct one instead. Write your answer in continuous prose, as you would in an email or letter, without headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold or italic text, or tables.
