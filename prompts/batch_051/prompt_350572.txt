I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive the clunky vocabulary. I'm drafting a book where an urban planner makes one small change to a neighborhood at a time. Each step, considered on its own, is clearly an improvement — the block gets quieter, then sunnier, then more convenient, and so on — but the end state is plainly worse than the start. While writing I came up with a principle I've been calling the cordon rule.

The rule: "better than" is only transitive within a cordon. Two states can be compared through a chain of small improvements only if the same feature is doing the work at every link. If step one is better because it's quieter and step two is better because it's sunnier, then the chain has switched threads, and the two endpoints fall outside the cordon — they're not comparable at all, rather than one being strictly better or worse. So in my novel, the final neighborhood isn't "worse" in the strict sense; it's just no longer on the same scale as the original.
