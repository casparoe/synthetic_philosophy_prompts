In a seminar on the sources of political legitimacy, the student sitting next to me defended a view he called the performance criterion. A government, he argued, is best understood on the model of a corporation's management. The population is analogous to customers and minority shareholders: they want the institution to be solvent, predictable, safe, and competently administered, and what legitimates management is that it delivers those goods. On this picture, elections are one of several possible auditing devices, not the origin of authority; a firm whose deliveries arrive on time and whose accounts are in order is not rendered illegitimate merely because its customers never voted on the chief executive. He used the analogy to conclude that states of the Singaporean or Chinese sort can be fully legitimate so long as they secure prosperity, public order, and competent rule, and that demands for electoral participation or liberal civil liberties confuse a means of accountability with its end.

I find the analogy doing real argumentative work rather than merely decorating a prior conviction, and I want to know how much weight it can carry. Already I can see some possible mismatches: a customer normally has alternatives and can take her business elsewhere, while a citizen mostly cannot; minority shareholders hold residual claims and some say over the board, while subjects of a one-party state may have no comparable lever; a firm is answerable to an external legal order it did not create, whereas a state is itself the maker and enforcer of rules; and the performance of a firm is measured on a fairly narrow, legible scale, while the performance of a state wraps together security, health, education, dignity, belonging, and justice in a way no balance sheet aggregates.

Please audit the analogy rather than simply rejecting or endorsing it. Draw up the correspondence table: what plays the role of management, the shareholders, the customers, the contract, the bottom line, the takeover bid, the regulator? Then identify the points where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are superficial. Make explicit what the analogy quietly imports: for example, the voluntariness of the relationship, the availability of exit, the externality of the legal frame, and the legibility of the performance metric. Then give a verdict on whether the conclusion, that prosperity and order can by themselves legitimize non-democratic rule, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and say whether some better analogy would do the argument more honest work. Please write your answer in British English.
