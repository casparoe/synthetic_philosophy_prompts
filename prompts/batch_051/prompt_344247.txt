I was reading Kant's Groundwork and got stuck on his example about lying promises, where someone borrows money and promises to pay it back even though he knows he won't. Kant then says: turn the rule behind the act into a law for everyone, so that anyone in trouble may promise whatever they want while intending not to keep it. According to him, the whole practice of promising would collapse, because nobody would believe a promise, and then you could not even use a promise to get what you want. That makes sense as a test. But then I started thinking about Peter Singer's argument about helping people in need. He also asks you to imagine what would happen if everyone in your position acted the same way—if everyone walked past the drowning kid, or if everyone refused to donate because someone else could do it. There the universalized version does not seem to make the act impossible or self-undermining; it just makes the world much worse. So the same what-if-everyone-did-that move gives a contradiction in one case and a pile of bad consequences in the other. Is this a real difference in how universalization works, or are Kant and Singer actually using the same test and I'm just confusing the examples with the logic? I cant tell if the difference is between two kinds of universalization or between deontology and consequentialism slipping in through the back.
