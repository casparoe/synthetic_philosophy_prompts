I keep noticing a structural analogy between an old rule in comparative psychology and a standard move in statistical inference. Morgan's Canon says, roughly, that an animal's behavior must not be attributed to a higher psychological faculty when a lower one would account for it. Model comparison in statistics says something similar about explanations: prefer the model that captures the data without unused complexity, because the richer model overfits noise. Both rules seem to place the burden of proof on the richer explanation, which makes me wonder whether the parallel runs deeper than the word 'simplicity'. I want you to test the mapping rather than merely endorse or dissolve it. Where does it hold, and where does it come apart? For instance: in statistics the complexity penalty can be justified by generalization to unseen data, and its strength can be tuned by cross-validation; does anything in comparative cognition play the role of held-out data? And Morgan's Canon has the form of a standing prohibition rather than a tunable trade-off—it never says how much more explanatory work a 'higher' faculty must do before it is licensed. Does that difference matter for whether the canon is best understood as a disguised prior, a predictive rule of thumb, or something else? And finally: what would have to be true of both sides for a result from model selection to transfer to disputes about animal minds, and is there anything the parallel predicts that actually turns out false?
