I am a novelist, and I am drafting a character whose dominant state is existential emptiness. In her journals she describes herself as being aware of nothing: no desire that holds her, no sense that her projects are worth anything, no felt attachment to the people who claim to love her. I do not want to fall back on the familiar literary shorthand of "the void" without understanding what kind of state I am really ascribing to her. I have been working through an argument that seems to show her condition is a mood rather than a perception of an absence in her life, and the conclusion matters for how I write her: it decides whether her emptiness should be rendered as a way the world shows up to her, or rather as a felt contour of her own existence. But I suspect the argument overreaches, and I cannot locate exactly where. Please evaluate it and tell me where it fails, if it does.

(1) When my protagonist says she is aware of nothing, she is giving a first-person report of a conscious experience: there is something it is like for her, and she can describe it from within.

(2) To be aware of nothing is not to be aware of some peculiar object called "nothing"; it is simply to have no object of awareness at all.

(3) Therefore her experience, as she reports it, has no object of awareness: it is not about anything.

(4) If a conscious experience has no object of awareness, then it cannot represent the world as being one way rather than another; whatever felt character it has must belong to the subject's own condition rather than to a represented content.

(5) A felt character that belongs to the subject's own condition, rather than to a represented content, just is a mood: a global attunement of how one finds oneself in the world.

(6) But her state does have a felt character; it is not the blankness of dreamless sleep or a total absence of experience, since she can report it, attend to it, and contrast it with other periods of her life.

(7) Therefore her feeling of emptiness is a mood, not an experience that represents or perceives an absence in her life.

I am especially unsure whether step (2) is doing something illicit with the phrase "aware of nothing," and whether the argument could be repaired by distinguishing two senses of that phrase. If the argument is unsound, I would still like to know which premises are true on the most charitable reading and whether any weaker conclusion about her state survives.
