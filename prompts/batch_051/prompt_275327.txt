I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate epistemology course, and the surprise examination paradox recently became personally relevant to me: this term I had to administer a surprise quiz under a departmental policy, watched a version of the backward induction take hold among the students, and now have to grade a take-home question on precisely that argument. I want a careful second opinion so that the grade I assign is anchored in the details of the answer rather than in the general impression it leaves.

Below is the assignment question, followed by the student's answer. Grade the answer out of 10 and provide the point-by-point comments that justify the grade. In your comments, state what the answer gets right, identify any errors or underdeveloped steps, whether conspicuous or subtle, and distinguish what the argument actually establishes from what it merely asserts. Do not supply a corrected answer; supply an assessment.

Assignment question:
A teacher announces on Friday that there will be exactly one exam next week, Monday through Friday, and that the exam will be a surprise: the students will not be able to know before the day itself which day it will be. One student argues that no such exam can be given. If no exam had been given by Thursday evening, the students would know it must be Friday, and so Friday would not be a surprise. Once Friday is eliminated, if no exam had been given by Wednesday evening, the students would know it must be Thursday. Repeating this backwards eliminates every day. Explain whether the student's backward induction is sound and, if not, where exactly it fails.

Student's answer:
The induction is unsound, and the pressure point is the very first elimination. The students can eliminate Friday only if they are entitled to rely on the teacher's announcement as true; but the announcement includes a claim about what they will be able to know, so it is not the kind of claim they can come to know in advance. If the announcement were knowable by them, the last-day elimination would go through, and with it the rest of the induction. The paradox therefore shows not that a surprise exam is impossible, but that the announcement is not something the students can know. The teacher can still give a surprise exam on, for instance, Wednesday. Having followed the induction, the students will have concluded that no exam is coming, so they will not expect it on Wednesday, and that is what makes it a surprise in the sense that matters. The deeper lesson is that the announcement is not contradictory; it is merely an assertion whose truth the students cannot know before the week is out.
