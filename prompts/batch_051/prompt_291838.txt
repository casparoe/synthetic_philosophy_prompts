I run a small business-ethics reading group — about a dozen people, mostly folks who work in compliance, sales, or HR, none of them with much philosophy background — and we meet for ninety minutes after work in a borrowed conference room. I want the next session to be on the whole 'can you actually blame a company?' question, but I don't want to stand there lecturing about corporate personhood. I want them to feel the problem from inside: how a genuinely bad outcome can happen even though every single person in the room can truthfully say 'I wasnt the one who decided that.'

So I'm after a structured role-play or staged decision where the group has to act out some decision inside a company — maybe a defect they decide not to recall, or a spill, or quietly selling customer data — with roles like a regional manager, someone from legal, someone from finance, someone from PR, and maybe a low-level worker who first flagged the issue. Everyone should have a defensible reason for doing their small piece, and ideally they're all following rules and targets that predate them. Afterwards we debrief: who, if anyone, should be blamed, and does the blame attach to any person in the room or to the company as a thing of its own. I'd like that realization to come out of what happens in the game, not from me explaining it at the end. So give me rules, roles, a timeline for ninety minutes, a way to set up the decision so it feels real and a little uncomfortable without being traumatic, a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the question, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and an honest note on which part of the idea a roomful of people pretending to be a company cant really reproduce.
