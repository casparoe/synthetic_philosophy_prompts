I'm one of five people on the prize committee for my university's undergraduate geometry competition. This year's rules said solutions may use only straightedge and compass, but also 'or any curve the entrant explicitly defines in the writeup,' which was supposed to cover coordinate descriptions of familiar curves. A finalist solved the angle-trisection problem using a curve generated by a point moving at constant speed along a ray while the ray rotates at constant speed—an Archimedean spiral, basically, specified by a polar equation—and she uses it to trisect any angle in a few steps. Her derivation is locically fine if the spiral is a permissible object. Two of us think it plainly isn't: the spiral is transcendental, can't be produced by straightedge and compass, and since angle trisection is impossible with that tool set, she has effectively changed the rules by adding a new instrument. Two others say that's backwards: she didn't smuggle in an instrument, she gave an exact definition and proved the properties she needed, and deciding which objects are real by asking which drawing tools can copy them is not a modern mathematical standard. Under the dispute is a real philosophical split about what makes a mathematical object legitimate—definability and demonstrable existence, versus constructibility from a fixed set of operations—and whichever way we rule will set the standard for future competitions.

Please draft the short decision memo I should circulate before our Thursday meeting. It should recommend a concrete ruling on this entry—accept, reject, or accept with conditions—with reasons, and it should interpret the phrase 'or any curve the entrant explicitly defines in the writeup' rather than just announcing that the rules are ambiguous. I also need an honest treatment of the strongest reason on the losing side. Don't cite historical results or outside sources; I just need the reasoning, but make clear which criterion for a legitimate mathematical object you are applying and why the spiral meets or fails it.
