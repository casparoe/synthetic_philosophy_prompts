For the past few months I have been reporting a feature on long-range forecasting—climate models, demographic projections, and the technology executives who claim to see the next century. The deeper I get, the more I keep colliding with an old argument of Karl Popper's: that a genuinely predictive science of history is impossible because the growth of knowledge is itself a motor of history, and we cannot now know what we will know next. I find his critique of historicism convincing as a warning against prophecy, but I worry that it proves too much, ruling out any interesting general claims about the shape of human affairs.

While interviewing a demographer, I began playing with a distinction I have been calling laws of the possible versus laws of the actual. A law of the actual would claim that societies pass through determinate stages—feudalism to capitalism, tradition to modernization—and that history has a single discoverable trajectory; Popper, I think, rightly disposes of those. A law of the possible would do something more modest: it would constrain the space of historical trajectories without selecting one. For example, once a cheap, durable, long-distance communication technology exists, no complex society can simply un-invent it while retaining its present level of coordination; and beyond a certain scale, purely face-to-face administration becomes unworkable, so complex states must adopt some form of delegated authority whatever their culture. These are not predictions—they say nothing about what will actually happen—but they look lawlike in a weaker sense: they are universal, exceptionless, and support counterfactuals. My worry is that this is either a genuine middle position between historical inevitability and pure contingency, or a dodge that preserves the word law by draining it of predictive content.

I would like an honest assessment of the distinction: whether it is coherent, whether it already exists under a better name, whether the examples actually behave like laws, and whether it survives the strongest Popperian objections. Please also say how confident you are in your assessment and single out the specific point you are least sure about.
