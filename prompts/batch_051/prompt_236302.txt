A colleague has given me the following compressed defence of Cox's theorem — the claim that any rational system of plausible reasoning must obey the ordinary axioms of probability — and I want it pulled apart before I repeat it any further. Here is the argument exactly as he set it out.

(1) A rational agent must be able to compare the credibility of any two propositions on the same evidence, and these comparisons must be coherent rather than circular. Any such coherent ordering of degrees of confidence can be represented by real numbers, with larger numbers standing for greater confidence and with fixed end points for certainty and impossibility.

(2) Consistency: if the same conclusion can be reached from the same evidence by two different routes of calculation, both routes must assign it the same number; otherwise the choice of route, not the evidence, is doing the work.

(3) The degree of confidence in ‘A and B’, given evidence X, is fixed by two numbers and nothing else: the degree of confidence in A given X, and the degree of confidence in B given X together with A. So there is a single function f that maps that pair of numbers to the number for the conjunction.

(4) In the same way, the degree of confidence in ‘not A’ given X is fixed by the degree of confidence in A given X alone, through a single function g.

(5) Because ‘(A and B) and C’ and ‘A and (B and C)’ are the same proposition, consistency requires f to be associative. Given the usual smoothness and monotonicity conditions, and the requirement that the combination rule reduce to deductive logic at the end points, associativity forces f to be ordinary multiplication after a suitable rescaling. A matching argument for g, using the fact that ‘not not A’ is A and that negation must fit together with conjunction, forces g(x) = 1 − x.

(6) Therefore every rational assignment of degrees of confidence must be a probability assignment. Probability theory is not one useful tool among others; it is the unique logic of plausible reasoning, and departing from it is not merely unwise but incoherent.
