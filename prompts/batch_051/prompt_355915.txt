Been rereading Bentham's Anarchical Fallacies, the piece where he goes through the French Declaration of Rights article by article. The passage that keeps bugging me is his attack on the article that says men are born and remain free and equal in rights: he says it's just not true that anyone is born free, since infants are completely dependent, and not true that anyone remains free, since society always constrains us. So he concludes the whole idea of natural rights only sounds meaningful. I think that's too quick as an argument against natural rights, but it made me want to get my own view straight by going through cases. My gut tells me: a severely disabled adult who can't survive without constant care still has a natural right to life; a prisoner in solitary still has rights; a hunter-gatherer band with no state has a right to its hunting grounds; a legal immigrant facing deportation has a right to be heard even if the majority voted for the law. But I also don't think a late-term fetus has the same right to life as a newborn. And then there's the case I keep going back and forth on: a person with end-stage dementia who has lived her whole life under rights-protecting laws but now can't exercise any of her rights. Does she still have natural rights, or just whatever legal protections she happens to get? Is there a principle that captures all my verdicts, or at least most of them, and what does it force me to give up?
