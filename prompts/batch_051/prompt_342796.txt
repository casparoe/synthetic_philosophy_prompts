I am the copy editor of my high school newspaper, and our faculty advisor has proposed a policy that the editorial board must respond to in writing before the next issue. To stop what he calls semantic decay, every term on a standing list of contested words would have to be used only in its earliest attested sense: 'silly' for blessed or innocent, 'decimate' for the Roman practice of executing one soldier in ten, 'meat' for solid food of any kind, and so on. A committee of two students and one teacher would maintain the list and decide, by majority vote, what counts as the earliest sense of each entry, and its rulings would bind all copy in the paper.

I need to write the board's analysis of how this rule would actually behave over a full school year, not whether it is good in principle. Walk through the incentives and the second-order effects. What would happen to the news section, the opinion pages, and the sports page? How would writers game the rule — by avoiding listed words, quoting sources who use the modern senses, inventing replacement vocabulary, or something else? Where does the system break first, and why? Would the standing committee become a lobbying target, or an authority that gradually legislates meanings instead of recovering them? Is 'earliest attested sense' even a stable target, given that written records need not track how a word was actually first used? Finally, could changes such as permitting the modern sense inside quotation marks or letting words leave the list after a semester fix the failures without abandoning the advisor's aim? Name the failure modes and say which one is likely to be fatal.
