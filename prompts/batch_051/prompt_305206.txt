Act as a philosopher of mathematics with a serious interest in mathematical education. I want a diagnosis of a debate rather than a verdict on which pedagogy is better. Think slowly, and if it helps, begin by steelmanning each side before you diagnose the structure of the disagreement.

The debate is the one about motivation and intuition versus rigor and formality in teaching mathematics, and I have watched a version of it stall inside my own department for years. The motivation-first camp wants early courses to begin with concrete problems, visual and computational explorations, and informal explanations, postponing full definitions and proofs until students have something to hang them on. They say rigor without motivation is empty symbol manipulation: a student can reproduce an epsilon-delta proof and still have no idea what continuity is, and that is not a minor gap but a failure to understand. The rigor-first camp wants precise definitions and proofs early, even when they feel unmotivated, because intuitive pictures lie and are hard to unlearn: a student who has been told that a continuous function is one you can draw without lifting the pen has no resources when she meets a continuous nowhere-differentiable function or a space-filling curve. For them, formalism is what makes reliable intuition possible in the first place; you cannot safely motivate a concept before it has been defined.

Almost everyone claims to want both, but when an actual syllabus has to order the first several weeks and the assessments, the compromise collapses and the two camps vote differently. That is the impasse.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement about which sequence produces better exam scores or better retention in later courses; each side can produce its own studies and its own classroom anecdotes. I suspect the real dispute is about what it is to understand a piece of mathematics: whether understanding consists in definitional and inferential control, or in having correct-enough mental models plus an ability to translate between those models and the formalism. But I can imagine other diagnoses too. Maybe the two camps are silently teaching different populations in their heads, future mathematicians versus future engineers and scientists. Maybe they assign different moral weights to two different failures: the student who cannot state the definition but has a feel for the subject, and the student who proves the theorems but cannot say what anything means. Maybe the question of what comes first is simply ill-posed.

Which diagnosis is most plausible, and what kind of disagreement is this at bottom? Given that diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move it: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, a change in the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected? Please tell me explicitly how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point you are least sure of.
