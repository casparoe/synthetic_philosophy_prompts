I cover AI and data science for a general-audience magazine, and I've settled into a policy about how I report on algorithmic "fairness" claims. When a vendor or a research group says their hiring screen, recidivism predictor, or credit model is "fair," "debiased," or "bias-free," I ask one question before I'll use the claim: which formal fairness criterion did you optimize for, and which standard criteria does the model fail? If they can't answer, or answer with something like "fair by design," I treat the fairness claim as basically meaningless and usually drop it from the story, even when the tool itself is newsworthy for other reasons. The justification I give myself is a half-remembered impossibility result from the fairness literature: under realistic conditions, no risk-scoring model can be simultaneously well-calibrated across groups, have equal false positive rates across groups, and have equal false negative rates across groups, except in knife-edge cases where you've effectively made the problem trivial. So a claim of fairness that doesn't specify the criterion feels like a food labeled "healthy" that doesn't say for what.

An editor pushed back on this. He says the impossibility results are narrow and technical, they only cover a few of the dozens of definitions people call "fairness," and the conditions don't always hold in practice. On his view I'm using a theorem I can't fully reproduce as a blanket reason to ignore alot of vendor claims, which is more a bias than a method. But I can't audit every model myself, and the rule does seem to catch the worst offenders fast. You're an expert in algorithmic fairness and in the epistemology of using formal results as filters for unreliable claims. Think carefully about both the math and the reporting layer: is my policy defensible? If not, is there a restated version that keeps what's useful while avoiding what overreaches? And how would I tell from the inside whether I'm applying a theorem or just wrapping a prejudice in one?
