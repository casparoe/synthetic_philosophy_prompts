I cover science and ideas, and the problem of logical omniscience is one I keep coming back to. Put roughly: the formal accounts of knowledge and belief treat agents as knowing or believing every logical consequence of what they know or believe, and that is plainly too strong. The standard repairs — a distinction between explicit and implicit knowledge, 'awareness' functions, impossible worlds, weaker logics — all strike me as epicycles rather than the right diagnosis.

For several years I have been defending a proposal I call the Single-Step Principle. Inferential knowledge, on my view, is subject to a strict one-step limit: a conclusion counts as known only if it can be reached by a single application of one of the elementary inference rules the agent actually accepts, with every premise of that application already among the agent's explicit standing beliefs. Chaining is not permitted. Whatever a step produces goes into a separate derived register, and items in that register cannot serve as premises for any further step. So if Alice explicitly believes P, if P then Q, and if Q then R, she can infer Q in one step and thereby know it; but she does not know R, even though R follows from what she knows, because reaching R would require her to use Q, and Q is by then a derived line rather than an explicit belief.

I know how that sounds. Yet the principle seems to earn its keep. It explains why someone who knows the axioms of a formal system is not automatically said to know every theorem: any serious derivation is chained far beyond a single step, and the distance between the axioms and the remote theorems is exactly the distance the principle refuses to let knowledge cross. It also blocks the version of the lottery paradox in which the troublesome move is conjoining many individually safe premises into one enormous conjunction; each binary conjunction is within a single step, but the full conjunction is not, because building it requires chained steps.

What I would like is an honest assessment. Is the Single-Step Principle genuinely distinct from the explicit/implicit knowledge distinction, or is it that distinction with a shorter fuse? Is it best understood as a strict form of single-premise closure, or as something else? Give me the strongest ordinary case in which the principle denies knowledge but I should plainly grant it — some everyday two-step inference that everyone would count as knowledge. Then give the three strongest objections, and say which, if any, is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. If the principle fails, tell me what the smallest repair is that preserves its core, or whether the core itself must go. Please lead with your bottom line in your first sentence, with no preamble and no restating of the question, and then give the reasoning.
