I'm a PhD student in political theory and I keep seeing Adam Smith's impartial spectator come up in the stuff I'm reading for my comprehensive exams. A friend in my cohort, who did his undergrad in econ, tried to explain it to me like this:

"Smith's idea of conscience is basically the imagined gaze of a bystander. You step outside yourself and ask how a typical person in your society would judge what you did, and your approval or guilt depends on what you picture them thinking. The problem is that this spectator is built out of whatever attitudes happen to be floating around in your society, so for Smith right and wrong just end up being social convention. Which is why his view can't really criticize the standards of his own society — their imaginary bystander just reflects those standards right back at them."

I have a vauge feeling this is off, or at least not the whole story — I half-remember Smith distinguishing between being praised and being praise-worthy, and something about the spectator being impartial rather than just typical. But I haven't read TMS closely enough to say where my friend's verion goes wrong. Is this a fair summary? Could you go through it and separate what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what Smith would reject, and then say whether the impartial spectator actually collapses into conventionalism or whether it gives Smith a way to criticize the very norms it's built from?
