I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been preparing a historical novel about Johannes Kepler. One question of characterization has troubled me more than any other: how seriously the novel should take his astrology. I asked two friends to help me settle it. They fell into a disagreement I could not referee, and I would like you to adjudicate it point by point.

The first friend, a historian of science, argued that Kepler's astrology was a livelihood rather than a conviction. He observed that Kepler likened astrology to a foolish daughter who keeps her wise mother, astronomy, from starving; that Kepler repeatedly attacked the zodiacal and house-based astrology of his own day as superstitious; and that the horoscopes Kepler actually wrote were produced for patrons who expected and paid for them. The novel, he concluded, should portray a reluctant practitioner who privately thought most astrology was nonsense and said so only in guarded terms.

The second friend, a practicing astrologer, replied that this flattens the man. Kepler did not reject astrology; he reformed it. He discarded signs and houses but kept aspects, the angular relations among the planets, because he believed that geometrical configurations impress the soul at birth much as music does. He subjected his own nativity to the same scrutiny, trying to fix the hour of his birth, and he wrote a substantial defense of a purified astrology against its critics. A man merely earning a living does none of that. The novel, she argued, should show a mind in which astronomy and astrology were continuous.

The historian answered that her position proves too much. If Kepler's astrology was only his harmonic cosmology applied to nativities, then it was not astrology in any ordinary sense, and calling it astrology invites readers to confuse him with the vulgar fortune-tellers he himself despised.

The astrologer answered that the continuity is exactly her point. Kepler's astronomy was itself animated by theological and geometrical convictions, such as the sun as the image of God the Father at the center of the world and the source of planetary motion. One cannot quarantine the astrology as a mere economic concession, she said, without also quarantining the astronomy. To present Kepler as a modern skeptic with a paid sideline is to impose our boundary between science and superstition upon a man who did not draw it.

I can feel the force of both positions, and I honestly cannot decide which should shape the novel. Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move: for each of the four positions above, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating; then say what, if anything, both friends are missing; and finally say which of the two is more right overall and why.
