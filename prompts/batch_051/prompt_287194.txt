I've been arguing with a colleague about whether the symmetric two-sphere universe really refutes the identity of indiscernibles, and I keep noticing that people talk past each other because they mean different things by property, identity, and individual. I'd like to turn that confusion into a teaching device. Please write a decision tree for a philosophy of physics seminar. It should take a student from an initial judgment about a universe containing only two qualitatively identical iron spheres one mile apart in otherwise empty space — for example, whether there are two things there and, if so, what makes them two — to a named position on what individuates particulars. Each internal node must be a concrete question or a judgment about a case that a student can answer without already knowing any theory labels; no node may simply ask whether the student believes in haecceities or bare particulars.

The terminal leaves must include at least these positions: a strict identity-of-indiscernibles position on which no two things can differ merely numerically; a bundle-theoretic position on which a particular is nothing over and above its compresent properties; a substratum theory that admits bare particulars; a haecceitist view that allows two possible worlds to differ only by swapping which sphere is which; a weak-discernibility view on which a symmetric relation, such as being one mile apart, suffices to distinguish the spheres; and an ontic structural realist view on which relations are fundamental and individual objects are not. Your internal questions should draw on at least three of the following cases: the two-sphere universe; two qualitatively identical foam balls swapped while someone is out of the room; two electrons in the singlet state treated as indistinguishable by quantum statistics; and a world described by purely structural equations in which nothing labels the individual nodes.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, locate the actual positions in that space, and diagnose any empty regions as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two leaves cannot be separated by the questions you have chosen, flag that explicitly and say what further case or distinction would separate them. The tree should have no more than seven leaves and at most five internal questions, and the complete instrument, including your diagnosis of empty regions, must stay within 800 words.
