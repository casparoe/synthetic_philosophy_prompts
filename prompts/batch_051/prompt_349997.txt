A student from my moral psychology seminar has been coming to office hours about her relationship, and I've been turning it over without a clean answer. Her boyfriend recently told her, in total sincerity, that he loves her because she's smart, funny, and kind. She said it made her feel great for about ten minutes and then terrible. Her worry is this: if his love is a response to those qualities, then it's not really directed at her in particular—it's directed at whatever happens to bear those qualities. If someone came along who was smarter, funnier, and kinder, wouldn't the reasons for his love just transfer? She said it made her feel like a job candidate who happens to meet the listed qualifications.

Then she caught herself doing the same thing. She could say exactly why she loves him: the way he listens, his curiosity, how patient he is with her anxious family. But she doesn't want to say that's all there is to it. She has this intuition that love doesn't just notice what's already valuable in a person; it somehow makes the person matter to you in a way they wouldn't otherwise, and once that happens the question "who has better qualities?" stops making sense. Her roommate, an econ PhD student, told her this is just sentimental accounting and that love is a preference over a list of attributes like any other.

I definately don't want to give her a vague "both sides have a point" answer. Where would you come down? Is there a way to love someone for their qualities without treating them as an interchangeable list of traits, or does real love have to be grounded in something that isn't a quality at all—something about the history you actually share with them, or the bare person underneath the qualities? What's the most defensible thing to say to her?
