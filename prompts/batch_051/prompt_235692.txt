A friend and I have been arguing about futarchy—the proposal that citizens vote on a measure of overall welfare, and that conditional prediction markets then select the policies expected to maximize that measure. My friend takes the standard view that markets may settle first-order policy choices, but the choice of the welfare metric and the design of the markets themselves remain constitutional matters fixed by voters. I have come to suspect that this is an exemption the view cannot afford, and I wrote up the following argument to make the point. It seems to show that futarchy either leaves its own foundations uncontrolled by the very mechanism it champions, or else collapses into a regress in which market prices lose their epistemic authority. That conclusion strikes me as too strong, but I cannot find the bad step.

(1) Futarchy's core claim is that any policy question should be settled by a conditional prediction market on the chosen welfare metric, because such markets aggregate dispersed information better than deliberation, elections, or expert review. (2) The design of those markets—how contracts are specified, who may trade, how thin markets are subsidized, when trading closes, how the metric is measured, and how manipulation is policed—is itself a policy decision that can change the realized values of the metric. (3) So, by (1), market-design questions should themselves be settled by conditional prediction markets on the metric under the candidate designs. (4) But any market asked to choose among candidate market designs must already be running under some set of trading rules, and the rules under which it runs fix which information its prices can encode; if the winning design also reaches back to govern the resolution of open contracts or the admissibility of the trades that selected it, the market is in part trading on its own institutional conditions. (5) This leaves two options. Either the meta-market runs under rules that are simply stipulated and never themselves opened to market selection—in which case a significant class of policy decisions is exempted from the mechanism, and (1) is false as stated—or the meta-market is asked to determine its own rules, in which case its prices are partly an artifact of the rules being bet on and can be moved and gamed by traders who profit from the rule change itself. (6) Moving to a higher-order market does not help, since the same dilemma reappears at each level. (7) Therefore futarchy cannot give a market-based account of its own institutional foundations: it needs either an ad hoc exemption or an infinite regress, and in the regress the prices cease to be the informationally clean evidence the view depends on.

I would like to keep (1)'s confidence in markets for first-order policy, and I would prefer not to be driven to the conclusion that the prediction-market idea is self-undermining. So my question is: which step or transition is doing the dubious work, and what does it cost to reject it? I have heard it said that market design and the choice of metric are constitutional rather than policy questions; I want to know whether that distinction can be made principled rather than merely convenient, and, if it is accepted, what remains of the original proposal once the line is drawn there.
