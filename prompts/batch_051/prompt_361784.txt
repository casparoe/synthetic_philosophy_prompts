I work on the formal side of social-contract theory and keep returning to the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute over what rationality and ignorance jointly determine. Rawls's original-position argument for the difference principle turns on the claim that, behind a thick veil of ignorance, rational parties would choose by maximin rather than by expected utility; Harsanyi instead treats the parties as facing decision under risk, giving each person's position equal probability and thereby reaching average utilitarianism, while Rawls replies that the thick veil supplies no basis for any single prior. Since that exchange, decision theory has developed a middle route: ignorance can be modeled as a set of admissible priors, with principles evaluated by their worst-case expected utility over that set, and minimax regret as a competing criterion for the same choice. Set aside Rawls's separate worries about a cardinal interpersonal utility index; the issue I want pressed is what the structure of ignorance licenses. Does Rawls's argument survive this development? Specifically, under a multiple-priors construal of the original position, does the thick veil force maximin, as it does when the prior set is unrestricted? Or does Harsanyi's uniform prior re-enter once the admissible priors are required to be symmetric across persons, making the choice among maximin, expected utility, and minimax regret underdetermined by the original position itself? I am interested in which premise of the argument actually bears the weight and whether a repaired version can be stated that still says something important. Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
