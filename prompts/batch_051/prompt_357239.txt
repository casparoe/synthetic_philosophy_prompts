In discussions of moral responsibility I keep meeting the same comparison: resenting a person whose actions were causally determined is like resenting a broken thermostat or a stretch of bad weather—the sensible response to a cause you cannot argue with is to repair it, insulate against it, or step around it, not to hold it to expectations or feel injured by it. Those who reach for this comparison take it to show that if determinism is true, the reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, and the rest—are systematically mistaken, and that we should replace them wholesale with the detached, diagnostic stance we take toward machines and diseases. I doubt the comparison can carry that conclusion. Audit it for me: give the explicit mapping from the thermostat and weather cases to the human case (what corresponds to the thermostat, the faulty part, the repair, the insulation, the stepping around; a table of correspondences is fine), then identify the main disanalogies and separate the ones that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are incidental. In doing so, use Strawson's distinction between the participant or reactive stance and the objective stance, and especially his separation of excuses such as coercion, ignorance, or accident—intelligible only inside the practice of holding people to expectations—from exemptions such as the small child or the severely impaired, which place someone outside that practice altogether. Which of those does the thermostat analogy quietly treat a determined person as? And does the conclusion that we should repair the causes rather than resent the agent survive once that is made explicit, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion all along?
