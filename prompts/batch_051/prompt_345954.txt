I coordinate food procurement and menu planning for a small NGO running school feeding programs and maternal-child nutrition clinics in a rural part of West Africa. We work under tight budget and supply constraints: plant protein is expensive, legumes are seasonal and sometimes unavailable for weeks, fortified products are unreliable, and animal-source foods such as eggs, dried fish, milk powder, and occasional meat are often the cheapest reliable source of complete protein and key micronutrients. My own ethical background is broadly abolitionist about animal agriculture in wealthy countries, and I find our reliance on animal products troubling, but I also suspect that the standard arguments for veganism were built for consumers with abundant alternatives, and that applying them mechanically here goes wrong. I want a decision tool my field officers can actually use without training in philosophy.

Please act as a moral philosopher working on the ethics of diet under scarcity and write me a reusable decision procedure—a checklist or flowchart in words—that a field officer can run on any proposal to introduce, keep, or expand an animal-source food in one of our programs. The procedure should be grounded in the strongest philosophical arguments on both sides: obligations to animals, the moral relevance of necessity and alternative availability, disagreements about whether the principle that ought implies can applies, and the special claims of children and malnourished people. Before laying out the procedure, briefly brainstorm the considerations so I can see what you are drawing on. Then present the procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands off to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact. Please mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Finally, run the procedure on three cases: (a) adding eggs to a preschool breakfast where legume supply is unreliable and stunting is high; (b) procuring a meat-containing ready-to-use therapeutic food for severely malnourished children when a plant-based alternative exists but is not locally approved or stocked; and (c) providing meat at a community celebration where sponsors expect it even though plant foods could adequately feed everyone, at some social cost to refusing. I ask that at least one worked case conclude in a way you suspect I will not like, and that you say clearly what the procedure still cannot settle.
