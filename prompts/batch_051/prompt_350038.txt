I keep noticing a pattern in institutions I know, and I cannot find a precise name for it. A group maintains some practice through voluntary effort and a shared sense of obligation: people show up, contribute, and treat the task as something one simply does. Then a small payment or fine is introduced to encourage the behavior. Instead of strengthening the practice, it weakens: participation drops, people begin treating the task as a service with a price, and those who once contributed out of duty begin asking what the compensation is. What strikes me most is what happens when the payment is later withdrawn. The earlier spirit does not return; the activity seems to have been reclassified as paid work, and the old obligation no longer binds. I have seen this in a volunteer-run community service that began paying a nominal stipend, and in a workplace that started fining late arrivals, after which lateness came to be treated as something one could purchase rather than as a failure of consideration. Is there an established concept for this pattern, in which a price does not simply run alongside an existing norm but appears to displace it, and the displacement persists after the price is removed? I am not looking for a bare label. I want to understand exactly what the mechanism is, how it differs from an ordinary substitution effect or from the claim that the payment was simply too small, and how it bears on the larger question whether markets corrupt or civilize character. If there is no established name, what would you call it, and where would you draw its boundaries?
