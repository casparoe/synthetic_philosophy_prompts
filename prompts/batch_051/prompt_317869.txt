Since retiring from evolutionary ecology, I have been reading the philosophical arguments about whether artificial intelligence will gradually displace humanity, and one parallel has begun to bother me. In my old field, a familiar pattern is competitive replacement: two lineages exploit overlapping niches, one carries a slight behavioural or ecological edge, and the less advantaged lineage declines to extinction — the standard account of the Neanderthals' disappearance as anatomically modern humans expanded. The Darwinian arguments for AI takeover I have been reading seem to have exactly this shape: an overlap between human and machine niches, a differential advantage, and a predicted replacement of the incumbent lineage. If the parallel is sound, AI takeover is simply competitive replacement once more. Yet the differences also strike me as basic: an AI is not a self-reproducing lineage, has no autonomous reproductive interests, and its 'fitness' is mediated by human engineering, markets, and decisions; its replication is something we do to it rather than something it does. Is the hominin-replacement analogy a deep structural match for Darwinian arguments about AI takeover, or a superficial likeness? More precisely: what would AI systems have to be like for the competitive-exclusion model genuinely to transfer, and what does the analogy predict about machine intelligence that we already have reason to think is false?
