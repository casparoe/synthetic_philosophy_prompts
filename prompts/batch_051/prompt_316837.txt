Two coworkers of mine got into a long argument at lunch about some family drama, and they both asked me to referee, which is annoying because I honestly see both sides and couldnt split it.

One coworker's cousin runs a tiny food blog. She gave a new restaurant a 1.5 out of 10, and in the writeup she said the place "won't last the year." The owner is now threatening to sue her for defamation over both the score and that sentence. Sam and Riley went back and forth.

Sam: "The owner has no case, twice over. A 1.5 is just a compressed taste judgment, and taste judgments can't be true or false. And 'won't last the year' is a prediction. Predictions are guesses, which is the opposite of a factual claim. If a theater critic says a show will close in a week and it runs for two years, nobody calls that defamation."

Riley: "I'll grant the score—it's obviously opinion. But 'won't last the year' isn't framed as 'I disliked it.' It's stated flat, as a claim about whether the business will survive, which is either true or false come next year. Meanwhile suppliers read that and tighten their terms. The law doesn't let you publish made-up facts that wreck a business just because you put a star rating on the same page."

Sam: "By that logic every forecast is defamation if it's wrong. Is the weather service getting sued when the picnic gets rained out? The whole point of protecting opinions is that readers know reviewers exaggerate and discount it. Nobody reads a food blog sentence as a balance-sheet audit."

Riley: "The weather service has models, labels uncertainty, and no axe to grind. A food blogger has none of that, and she wrote the closing as if it were fact. There's also a difference between 'the soup was salty' and 'the kitchen gave a dozen people food poisoning.' One is taste, the other is a checkable event. 'Won't last the year' implies she knows something about their cash flow or bookings, which she doesn't."

Sam: "That just shows 'checkable later' can't be the test, because every prediction is checkable later. 'It'll rain on Tuesday' is checkable. The real test is whether a reasonable reader would take the line as factual rather than opinionated trash-talk, and in a review full of rants and ratings, it's clearly trash-talk."

Riley: "You're defending a blanket rule that future-tense sentences are always protected. But I can defame you with a future-tense sentence if it smuggles in a false factual premise: 'you'll get caught for the thefts you've been doing' still asserts you've been stealing. 'Won't last the year' has the same shape—it presents the place as already failing."

Sam: "That's backwards. 'Won't last the year' is a prediction, not an accusation that some hidden fact exists right now. And if the review goes viral, it could itself drive the closure, so the sentence might be self-fulfilling. You can't call a statement false when its own publication helps make it true."

Riley: "A self-fulfilling prophecy isn't a loophole. If I shout 'this bank is insolvent' and the run I cause makes it insolvent, that's still defamation. If she has no basis for the closing claim, the score may be protected but the sentence crosses the line."
