Two friends of mine recently argued, over dinner, about a calendar-reform proposal one of them had read about: re-anchor the common era from its traditional religious epoch to the start of the Holocene, roughly twelve thousand years ago, so that the present year would be roughly twelve thousand rather than its current low two thousands. (1) Elena argued that a civil calendar's epoch should mark something genuinely common to the whole species, and the only serious candidate is the stable climatic interval — the Holocene — in which agriculture, cities, writing, and nearly every polity we know about all sit. Keeping the Gregorian numbering while quietly relabeling its terms as CE/BCE, she said, launders a sectarian anchor rather than replacing it. (2) Marcus replied that an epoch is a coordination device, not a cosmology: its job is to let everyone date things unambiguously, and the entrenched epoch already does that, its historical origins being no more decisive than the pagan origins of weekday names. He added that the Holocene boundary is fuzzy, reconstructed by stratigraphers, and that even the proposal's 'natural' starting point amounts to a conveniently round offset near the geologists' boundary rather than the boundary itself. (3) Elena answered that this would insulate any entrenched convention from criticism; that the AD-to-CE relabeling shows we already feel the problem but stopped halfway; and that switching costs, though real, are one-time and were raised against metrication, which was worth it for a permanent gain in generality. (4) Marcus responded that metrication paid in calculation and trade, whereas a re-epoching pays no functional dividend because the numbers are pure labels. Worse, he said, to treat a 'natural' boundary as neutral is to smuggle in the substantive view that a shared reference point must be natural rather than historical, and it turns the calendar into environmental advocacy — inviting every constituency to demand an epoch that flatters its own values.

I swung one way and then the other, and never reached a stable verdict, so I am bringing it to you. Please adjudicate the argument move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, say who is right about which specific point, identify what, if anything, both of them are missing, and state who is more right overall. Finally, since I want to know how hard to lean on your verdict, tell me which argument or consideration would most change that verdict and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
