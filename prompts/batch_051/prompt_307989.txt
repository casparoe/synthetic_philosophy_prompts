I'm a PhD student working in early modern moral philosophy, and I keep coming back to the second section of the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, where Kant tries to show that the categorical imperative can be applied to his four famous examples. He draws what seems like a structural distinction: some maxims cannot even be thought as universal laws of nature, while others can be thought but not willed as such. I understand the surface contrast, but I want a much more precise account of what the two tests are tracking. In particular, what exactly is the sense of contradiction at work in each case? Is it a formal contradiction in the maxim once it is universalized as a proposition, or is it something more like a practical contradiction in the will's ability to pursue its own ends in a world where the maxim operates as a law of nature? If it is the latter, what makes that failure a specifically rational inconsistency rather than a disguised appeal to bad outcomes or psychological aversion?

Could you explain how the two tests map onto the distinction between perfect and imperfect duties—does the mapping fall out of the tests themselves, or is it supplied by something external? And could you illustrate the difference by walking through one maxim from each class: a maxim of making a false promise when in distress for the first test, and a maxim of neglecting one's talents for the second? I am less interested in whether the tests succeed or fail as a piece of moral argumentation than in getting clear on the interpretive structure: what kind of impossibility each test is meant to identify, and what constraints this places on the kind of maxim an agent can adopt. I would also appreciate some discussion of how the formula of universal law differs from the formula of the law of nature in this respect, since Kant presents them as equivalent formulations but the law-of-nature version seems to import teleological or empirical considerations that the bare universal-law version does not.
