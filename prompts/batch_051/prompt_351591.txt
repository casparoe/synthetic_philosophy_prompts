I've been caught between two ways of talking about frugality, and I'd rather not choose between them by trading examples. One side treats frugality as a virtue or a duty: what matters is that an agent exercises restraint, keeps her desires within bounds, and doesn't waste. The other side treats it as a favorable state of affairs: what matters is that there be more frugality in the world—more saved, less wasted, lower footprint—whether that comes about through individual character, social norms, prices, or public policy. Before I adjudicate that dispute, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory of frugality must do, where "adequate" means good enough to settle that virtue-versus-outcome question and not merely to guide one household. Lay out the desiderata: the cases a theory must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the results from economics and the social sciences it must be consistent with, with the reason each is a requirement. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate the standard positions, and flag which desiderata are negotiable and which are not.

To seed the list rather than exhaust it: the miser and the spendthrift; chosen simplicity versus poverty-imposed thrift; the "poverty premium" by which cheap goods bought repeatedly can cost more than durable goods bought once; the rebound effect, where efficiency gains can raise total consumption rather than lower it; the paradox of thrift in a demand-constrained economy, where a synchronized rise in household saving can lower aggregate demand and leave people worse off; the finding that scarcity narrows attention and makes long-horizon tradeoffs harder; and the mismatch between monetary frugality and material or emissions frugality (the cheap flight, the discount meat). A full specification should also say whether "being frugal" and "promoting frugality" are answers to the same normative question, and whether frugality's value is agent-relative, agent-neutral, or both.
