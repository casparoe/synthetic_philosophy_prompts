You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of collective action and individual responsibility. I have been assembling my considered views about how individuals should think about their contributions to large-scale harms, and I would like a rigorous consistency audit before I circulate the document at a workshop. Please work through the commitments slowly, map the logical relations among them, and distinguish genuine tensions from merely apparent ones. Before giving your final ranking, brainstorm the candidate sources of tension so that the ranking reflects a real comparison rather than a list.

Here is where I have landed.

1. Actualist consequentialism: an act is right just in case no alternative available to the agent would have produced a better total outcome.

2. Inefficacy about ordinary consumption: In large, anonymous market cases — buying a particular piece of factory-farmed meat, taking a particular long-haul flight, ordering a particular item shipped from overseas — my individual choice does not change the number of animals raised, flights scheduled, or units emitted. These systems are insensitive at the margin of one agent, so these acts are not wrong on outcome-based grounds.

3. Voting by expected value: I vote in major elections because there is a real probability that my single ballot is decisive, and a small probability of a very large difference is a large expected difference. That is enough to justify voting on consequential grounds even though my ballot probably will not in fact determine the outcome.

4. Footprint-reduction demand: I also think individuals have reason, and often obligation, to reduce their own carbon footprints — eating less beef, flying less, insulating the house — and I think people who wave away their own slice as negligible are making a moral error.

5. Aggregate-level harm: The moral gravity of climate change and factory farming attaches to the collective outcome, not to any one person's contribution. A collective can do catastrophic harm even when no individual contribution is detectable.

6. Individual-difference constraint: An individual act is not wrong unless some specific being is made worse off by it than they would have been had the agent chosen otherwise. This is the principle behind my ease about the acts in (2).

7. Expected-value procedure: Because actual consequences are unknowable at the moment of choice, I use expected value over possible states of the world as my decision procedure, while keeping it strictly separate from the criterion of rightness in (1).

Please audit this set of commitments. Go through the pairs and say which are in tension and how severely. For each genuine tension, identify what has to give and what the least costly revision would be; for any apparent tension, explain why it dissolves. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to restore coherence overall, and what that revision would cost elsewhere in the set.
