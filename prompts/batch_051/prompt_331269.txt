In the months since a course of medication began producing episodes of depersonalization—episodes in which I have watched my own body speak and move while the felt self seemed to stand outside the causal stream—I have found myself compelled by a view I once dismissed: epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness. The position, as I would now state it, is that there is something it is like to see, to remember, to dread; that these phenomenal properties are real rather than illusory; and that they have no causal powers. The physical world is causally closed, and every piece of behavior, including my own reports of experience, is produced entirely by physical processes. Consciousness, on this view, is the steam from the engine's whistle—real, but doing none of the work.

I am aware of the usual cost of this position, the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment: if epiphenomenalism is true, then my judgment that it is true is itself caused not by my consciously appreciating the arguments but by nonconscious physical machinery, and the belief thereby seems to lose its credentials. I am not asking you to resolve that paradox in the first instance. I am asking what would count against the position, and how much each thing would genuinely threaten it.

Give me an honest account of where the view is exposed and where it is protected. Which observations or experimental findings would count against it—for instance, any evidence that the specific felt character of an experience, rather than its physical substrate alone, makes a downstream difference to behavior or report—and which would break the view rather than merely bruise it? Which arguments, thought experiments, or rival positions, such as illusionism, interactionism, or identity-style physicalism, does the committed epiphenomenalist have to take seriously as live competitors rather than bullet-biting targets? Which concessions do the view's own defenders make—the paradox of phenomenal judgment, the intuition that the awfulness of pain does something, the evolutionary coincidence of consciousness—and do they regard these as damaging or simply as costs already paid? I would personally be most moved by evidence that fine-grained phenomenal content does detectable causal work in shaping what a person can say or do; I would be least moved by the bare intuition that my pain makes me flinch. Finally, state plainly whether the position as I have stated it has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a decisive objection or the ordinary price of a metaphysical view.
