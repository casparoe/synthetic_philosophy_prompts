A friend of mine who works in legal philosophy summarized the causation-by-omission debate as follows: "People say that if you fail to water a plant and it dies, your not-watering caused its death. But omissions can't literally be causes. If my failure to water the plant caused its death, then everyone else's failure to water it caused its death too, since the omission is identical; and by the same logic each of us is causally responsible for every harm we could have prevented but didn't. That's absurd, so omissions must not be genuine causes at all. The real cause is always some positive event or process, like the roots desiccating. When the law treats inaction as grounds for liability, it is attributing legal or moral responsibility, not causation." I'm inclined to think the slide from "everyone who didn't water it caused it" to "everyone is causally responsible for every unprevented harm" is doing a lot of work, but I couldn't specify where it goes wrong. How fair is his summary as an account of the debate? I'd want to know which parts track positions philosophers actually defend, which conflate distinct claims, and whether the argument could be repaired even if the summary is unfair.
