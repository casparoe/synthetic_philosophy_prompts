A colleague keeps pressing me with an argument that seems to show something I refuse to accept, and I cannot find the load-bearing step.

(1) Everything a learning system can output on a given input is fixed by a finite "training package": the data it was trained on, its architecture and learning objective, and whatever priors were built in. (2) Its outputs therefore have no content that does not come, in some form, from that package together with the current input. (3) A finite procedure operating on a finite dataset can only extract, recombine, interpolate within, or extrapolate along structure that is already latent in the data; at no point in the computation does the system consult anything its data has not somehow already gestured at. (4) A genuinely novel idea — an intuitive leap beyond the training distribution — is one whose governing structure is not latent in the learner's prior data in this sense; if it were latent and merely being extracted, the result would be analysis or recombination, not a leap. (5) Therefore no system with a finite training package can make an intuitive leap beyond its training distribution. (6) But a human mind is a finite training package in exactly the same sense: a finite genome and developmental process, a finite stream of experience and enculturation, and no additional information channel. (7) So a human mind can never make an intuitive leap beyond its training distribution. (8) But human minds plainly have — the concept of zero, the principle of natural selection, perspective in painting, a child who invents a game no adult taught her. (9) So one of the steps must fail.

Which premise or step do you reject? Explain why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, whatever diagnosis you give, tell me what it commits me to about the difference between a human child and a large language model trained on ordinary text — and whether that difference can be drawn without redefining novelty so that machines are disqualified from the start.
