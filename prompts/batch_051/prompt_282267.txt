I retired a few years ago from behavioral ecology and have been using the time to get into philosophy of mind, mostly arguments about how much we can say animals are thinking. Something I keep reaching for is the difference between fitting a model and testing it. Morgan's canon, as I read it, says don't interpret an animal's behavior as the product of a higher psychological process when a lower one would do. To me that is a regularization rule, and I have been using a sharper version I call the cross-context rule. The idea is that a single impressive behavior is one data point: a chimp going over to embrace a loser after a fight can be fit by a rich "it understands the other's distress" curve, but it can also be fit by a flat associative-learning line. What disciplines the inference is how the same process handles the animal's behavior outside the original context. So the rule: credit the richer process only when no leaner process handles the animal's behavior across the whole spread of contexts in which it actually lives, not just the one that made the headline. I suspect a lot of the long fight between the killjoys and the romantics in animal cognition is really a fight about how strongly to regularize, and that Morgan was doing early regularization before the word existed.

I would like an honest assessment, not a pat on the head. Is the cross-context rule a real principle or just statistical imagery? Has somebody already worked this out under another name, and does my version add anything? And where does it break down — what does it wrongly license as "lower" when it isn't, and what real cognition does it wrongly forbid? I can already see one worry: some real capacities might be genuinely context-specific, so demanding out-of-context generality could stack the deck against them. But maybe that's a feature rather than a bug. I honestly can't tell.
