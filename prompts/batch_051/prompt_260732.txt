I've been reading about Schrödinger's debt to Advaita Vedanta, and I keep noticing that rationalist-adjacent writers sometimes enlist him in a contemporary debate about personal identity that crystallized only after his death. Schrödinger is often read as holding that the apparent multiplicity of conscious subjects is not metaphysically fundamental: that there is, at bottom, one consciousness which manifests through many minds, and that the boundaries we ordinarily draw between persons are conventions or artifacts rather than divisions in the underlying reality. He also cared, as a working physicist, about what scientific results can and cannot settle about the mind.

Since Schrödinger's death, philosophers and rationalist-adjacent writers have developed a rough three-way taxonomy of views about the metaphysics of persons. Closed individualism holds that each human being is a distinct persisting subject, one subject per biography. Empty individualism holds that there are no persisting subjects at all, only sequences of experiences held together by continuity, memory, and habits of self-modeling. Open individualism holds that there is exactly one subject of experience, numerically identical across all experiences; the appearance of many separate minds is something like the appearance of many whirlpools in one underlying body of water.

It is tempting to file Schrödinger as an open individualist, and some contemporary open individualists treat him as a partial forerunner. But I suspect that filing may flatten distinctions Schrödinger himself would have wanted to preserve — for example, the distinction between the everyday empirical ego and whatever it is that witnesses experience, or the difference between a literal thesis about numerical identity and a mystical formula that was never meant to be cashed out as a metaphysical doctrine. It may also matter that Schrödinger's way of expressing these ideas was more analogical and devotional than the contemporary taxonomy tends to be.
