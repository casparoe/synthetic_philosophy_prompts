I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a teaching hospital, which issues advisory rulings on whether to honor a patient's consent to, or refusal of, treatment when a treating team or family member disputes it. We have no written code beyond our own past rulings, and we try to extend those rather than announce a general theory. The rulings we treat as benchmarks are these. We upheld consent to clipping of an unruptured aneurysm where the surgeon omitted a 0.1% risk of a minor complication that the patient later said would not have mattered to him, on the ground that disclosure need not enumerate every conceivable risk. We set aside consent to spinal fusion where the surgeon called the chance of permanent nerve injury "tiny" and omitted that it was 1 in 50, after the patient had said he would refuse if the risk of paralysis were more than negligible. We set aside consent to living kidney donation where the donor's brother threatened to withdraw contact and financial support if he declined, and the donor said he agreed only to avoid that rupture. We upheld a woman's refusal of further chemotherapy after her adult children wept, pleaded, and offered to take over her household duties, because she affirmed that the decision remained hers. We upheld consent to electroconvulsive therapy from a patient with severe recurrent depression who could state the procedure, name memory loss as a principal risk, and explain how ECT addressed his own symptoms, which we took to show adequate capacity. And we set aside a refusal of a liver transplant by a delirious patient who could accurately repeat what the surgery does but insisted the organ would be used to control him, holding that capacity requires appreciation, not recitation.

The case now before us cuts across all three themes. A 79-year-old woman with mild cognitive impairment—she is oriented, manages her own medications, and lives at home with her daughter—has signed consent for a hip replacement. The orthopedist told her the operation is "one of our most routine procedures; you'll be on your feet in a couple of days," but he did not disclose that, given her age and bone quality, there is roughly a 1 in 20 chance she will need a second operation within a year, or that she carries a somewhat elevated risk of a postoperative blood clot. In the consulting room her daughter, who provides much of her daily care, said, "Mom, if you put this off and you fall again, I can't keep looking after you at home; I can't watch you go through that." The patient can describe the operation and names infection and bleeding as risks, but when we asked what a poor outcome would mean for her, she replied, "Oh, they fix those things now. I'll be fine." She told us she is agreeing mainly so her daughter will not worry.

Please draft the short opinion I would circulate before the meeting. It should state a bottom line—treat the consent as valid, invalid, or valid only with specified conditions—identify which of our rulings is the nearest precedent and whether more than one is genuinely nearest, and be candid about whether our benchmarks determine the answer or instead leave us choosing among live principles. In particular, the opinion should deal explicitly with the choice between a disclosure standard keyed to what a reasonable patient would find material and one keyed only to risks the particular patient asked about; between treating the daughter's statement as a coercive threat to withdraw care and treating it as permissible family entreaty; and between an appreciation standard of capacity and a mere-understanding standard. Where the precedents underdetermine the case, say what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each ruling, and describe the further case that would cleanly separate the competing principles.
