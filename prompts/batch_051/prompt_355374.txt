A couple of people in my reading group got into it last week over Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, book 5 chapter 5 (Rackham translation), and neither of them would let it go, and I honestly couldn't referee it. The passage they were stuck on is this:

"Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food. As therefore a builder is to a shoemaker, so must such and such a number of shoes be to a house, [or to a given quantity of food]; for without this reciprocal proportion, there can be no exchange and no association; and it cannot be secured unless the commodities in question be equal in a sense. It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

Elena said the really striking claim there is that demand is the standard and money is only its conventional representative. So Aristotle is already committed to the idea that unlike goods are genuinely commensurable, through need, before any money shows up; money just makes that commensurability visible. Marcus said she had it backwards: the passage opens with the problem that unlike things can't simply be compared, and the actual solution Aristotle offers is money, a middle term that measures all things. Demand can't be a measure because it varies from person to person and over time, and Aristotle never explains how to quantify it; the only actual unit in the chapter is the one fixed by agreement. Elena shot back that the text says "this standard is in reality demand," and that the last line actually helps her: money can be "rendered useless" only if there is something it is supposed to track, and a pure convention couldn't fail by misrepresenting demand. Marcus said "rendered useless" just means the community can stop accepting the token; it doesn't show the token tracks a pre-existing quantity, and a standard has to be stable enough to compare things against, which demand isn't. Demand is a background condition for exchange happening at all, not a measure inside the exchange. Elena replied that this guts the chapter: if demand is mere background, then equalizing goods is just whatever the market settles on, and Aristotle loses any grip on the difference between a fair exchange and an arbitrary one. Marcus said maybe that's exactly the honesty of the passage: unlike things can't be made commensurate strictly, so all we get is a practical, conventional equalization, and calling demand the standard "in reality" is Aristotle saying what holds a community together, not what performs the measurement.

I'd like to be able to see who's right about what next week. Could you go through their exchange move by move, identify which specific claims each gets right or wrong, and say what both of them are missing?
