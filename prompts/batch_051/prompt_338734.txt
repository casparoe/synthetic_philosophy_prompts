Ok here's the task. You're a philosopher of social ontology who knows Lewis's account of convention cold, and you've also got a decent feel for cultural-evolution takes on norms. Think carefully, and before you settle on a scenario, brainstorm two or three candidate settings and say why all but one fail.

I want you to construct a thought experiment built to a precise spec.

Background, in my own words so no one has to look anything up. On Lewis's picture, a behavioral regularity R in a population P that recurrently faces a situation S counts as a convention only if it's common knowledge in P that (roughly): (1) everyone conforms to R, (2) everyone expects everyone else to conform to R, (3) everyone prefers to conform to R on condition that the others do, and (4) some alternative regularity would have worked just as well if everyone had instead gone along with it from the start. The common-knowledge wrapper is load-bearing here: it means not just that these facts hold, but that everyone knows them, knows that everyone knows them, and so on up the chain.

Now use an evolutionary gloss like this: a regularity is a convention if it's (a) socially copied, (b) stable under everyday payoff-based selection, (c) arbitrary in content, and (d) something people wouldn't independently converge on without social contact. Notice this gloss has no common-knowledge requirement at all.

Here's the spec. Build a case where these two come apart in this direction: the evolutionary gloss says "definitely a convention," but Lewis's account says "not a convention." That's the point of the exercise, since the usual complaint is that Lewis overgenerates; I want a undergeneration-style case instead. I want the culprit to be specifically the common-knowledge failure. So the arbitrariness, conditional-preference, and mutual-expectation material should all be satisfied, either across the whole population or at least within the local subgroups from which the pattern spreads. The thing that should be missing for the population as a whole is the recursive everyone-knows-that-everyone-knows structure.

Constraints: ordinary realistic setting (office building, student co-op, gym, commuter station, whatever — no isolated tribes, no sci-fi, no memory drugs, no hidden game show). One paragraph for the scenario itself, roughly. No one in the story needs to be able to articulate the rule, and preferably most can't. Payoffs should be mundane (avoiding bumping into people, saving time, not looking clueless, etc.). The case is for testing how much work the common-knowledge clause is really doing once conventions can spread through local imitation — so make the verdicts fall out of the mechanics rather than just announcing them.

After you lay out the scenario, do all of the following:
1. State exactly what R, P, and S are.
2. Walk through each Lewis condition. Say which hold and which fail, and explain why the first-order successes don't add up to common knowledge here.
3. Walk through the evolutionary gloss's conditions and say why each of them holds.
4. Identify the single structural feature doing the real work (my guess is limited observational scope plus population size, but if your case does it differntly, say so). Then show what happens if you remove that feature — e.g., if a building-wide memo or some public marker makes the rule common knowledge, does it collapse into a textbook Lewis convention? Walk that through.
5. Finally, give the strongest Lewis-defender counterattack against your case. Would they say P is really several smaller populations each with its own convention? Would they say the practice is a convention in the making, not one yet? Give the best version of that reply, and then tweak the case so it survives it if you can.

Go ahead and write it up, with the brainstorming first.
