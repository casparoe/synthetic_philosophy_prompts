I'm auditing a historiography course at the moment, and after one seminar I got into a long argument with my brother about whether the way we tell the history of colonial education or land reform just inevitably reflects whoever came out on top. I've been trying to turn that argument into a short essay, because I think the slogan "history is written by the victors" is doing a few different things at once and my coursemates won't stop repeating it as if it settles the question. I'm worried realy though that I'm either misreading Mill or just using him as a convenient quote. The essay is built around a passage from John Stuart Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government* (Chapter I):

"All speculations concerning forms of government bear the impress, more or less exclusive, of two conflicting theories respecting political institutions; or, to speak more properly, conflicting conceptions of what political institutions are. By some minds, government is conceived as strictly a practical art, giving rise to no questions but those of means and an end. Forms of government are assimilated to any other expedients for the attainment of human objects. They are regarded as wholly an affair of invention and contrivance. Being made by man, it is assumed that man has the choice either to make them or not, and how or on what pattern they shall be made. Government, according to this conception, is a problem, to be worked like any other question of business."

My basic thesis is that "history is written by the victors" can be split into at least two claims: one where victors deliberately impose a usable past, and another where the social order grows out of the victors' habits and institutions and history-writing follows from that without anyone actively conspiring. Mill's contrast between government as artifact and government as organic growth gives me a way to keep those two apart, but I also want to argue it isn't a clean either/or—that the first can collapse into the second over time, when an originally imposed story becomes simply "how things are." Could you read the passage against my use of it and tell me where I'm stretching him? I'd like comments on whether my two-version distinction is actually doing work or just restating the obvious, whether Mill's passage supports it or undercuts it, and what would make the essay genuinely interesting rather than a "both sides" muddle. Please write your comments as continuous prose, like a letter or an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
