Here's an argument I keep almost posting to a LessWrong thread on automated interpretability, but the conclusion is strong enough that I assume I've made a mistake somewhere and I can't find it. It seems to show that no finite stack of AI interpreters can give you actual understanding of a model, which would make the whole "AI interprets AI" program either trust or just-so stories.

(1) A large model's internals—activations, features, circuits—don't wear their meaning on their sleeve. To understand what the network is doing, you need an interpretation: something that maps those internals to claims a human can assess (this feature fires for legal contracts, this circuit copies the previous token's position, etc.).

(2) Now that we're using one model—call it the interpreter—to read another model's activations and produce these interpretations, the interpreter is itself a large, partly opaque system.

(3) To actually know an interpretation is right, rather than merely plausible, I need to know that the interpreter is latching onto the real structure of the base model and not confabulating a good story. But knowing that about the interpreter is just the original interpretability problem one level up.

(4) So adding an interpreter doesn't buy any ground; it just adds a link. And each link, being itself opaque, needs another interpreter, ad infinitum.

(5) Therefore no finite stack of interpreters terminates in understanding. It terminates either in an uninterpreted human judgment (the very bottleneck we were trying to escape) or in an uninterpreted act of trust in the top model.

(6) So "AI interprets AI" either collapses into "AI produces plausible but ungrounded narratives," or it bottoms out in human judgment after all. The recursive-interpretability project can't deliever understanding in principle.

But (6) proves too much. The same reasoning seems to show that when a human colleague walks me through their buggy code, I can never understand the bug, because the colleague is also an opaque system whose report I can only assess fallibly, and understanding them would require interpreting their brain, and so on. That can't be right—or if it is, I want to know I'm committed to it.

So: where does this go wrong? I want a step-level diagnosis, not a survey of interpretability. Which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd like to keep the idea that a chain of automated interpreters can bottom out somewhere and still count as understanding, but I can't yet point to the step that forces out. If the argument is actually valid, say so and tell me which premise I'm stuck with.

And whatever verdict you land on, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip it, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
