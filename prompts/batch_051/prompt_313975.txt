I'm a cog-sci PhD student, and I've been quietly reverse-engineering my advisor's theory of what counts as an emotion, since she never states it outloud but her case judgments are spookily consistent. From lab meetings and comments on drafts I've collected these so far: a startle response to a door slamming—not an emotion, just a reflex; free-floating anxiety with no object that lasts all afternoon—not an emotion, a mood; fear when a big dog snarls at you—obviously an emotion; moral outrage reading about a company that swindled retirees—an emotion; the quiet little glow after finally solving a hard problem—she hemmed and hawed and then said yes, an emotion; jealousy when a friend gets the fellowship you wanted—yes; and the sinking feeling when you realize you left the oven on—yes, fear-like.

The new case I need a prediction on: a judge reads a brief and concludes, with total equanimity, that a defendant was treated unjustly. She feels no heat, no tension, no affective pang—but later she honestly describes herself as having been indignant. Would my advisor call this an emotion? I'm less interested in the bare yes/no than in the rule behind it: is she running something like a judgment theory, a perceptual theory, a bodily-feeling theory, or some appraisal hybrid? Which of her earlier verdicts are doing the most work pinning the rule down? And if her track record genuinely underdetermines it, what further case should I bring her to break the tie? I genuinely don't know which way her theory cuts here.
