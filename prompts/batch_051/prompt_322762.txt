I have been reading a general introduction to existentialism, and its chapter on Sartre has left me with an account I am not sure I can trust. As the chapter presents it, Sartre's central claim is the slogan that existence precedes essence: whereas an artifact such as a paperknife exists only as the realization of a prior concept or design, a human being arrives in the world with no pregiven nature and only subsequently determines what she is through her choices. The chapter then draws two conclusions from this. First, it argues that the position is ultimately incoherent, since Sartre also maintains that all human beings are free in the same fundamental way, that each person is in every situation responsible for what she is, and that to disown this freedom is bad faith. These are universal claims about human beings, and a universal claim about what all humans share just is an essence; so Sartre either smuggles in a human essence despite denying one, or he forfeits even his claim that freedom is universal. Second, the chapter argues that if we genuinely create our own values through our choices, then at the moment of an original or fundamental choice there are no standards by which to choose, so every such choice is arbitrary; Sartre's language of responsibility and bad faith is therefore merely moralistic pressure with no content, and he has no grounds to criticize anyone. I found this persuasive on a first read, but something about it strikes me as too quick. Is this a fair summary of Sartre's view? I would like to know which parts a careful defender of Sartre would accept, which parts are accurate as far as they go but misleading, and whether the two conclusions actually follow from the accurate parts.
