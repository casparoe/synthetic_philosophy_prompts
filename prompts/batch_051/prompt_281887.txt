I've been looking at a method that shows up repeatedly among people and organizations trying to compare causes and decide where to give. A cause is assigned three scores: importance (roughly, how much good would be done if the problem were solved), tractability (how much progress a given amount of effort would actually produce), and neglectedness (how little attention the problem already receives). Those scores are then combined, often by multiplying them, into a single priority ranking.

I want to know what this procedure presupposes, philosophically, before it ever gets to the numbers. Does it presuppose that good across very different causes is a single thing that can be measured on one common scale, so that a dollar spent on one cause and a dollar spent on another can be straightforwardly compared? Does the step of multiplying the scores presuppose a particular view of how importance, tractability, and neglectedness interact—for example, that they are independent, that enormous importance combined with zero tractability is worth nothing, or that a small improvement in one factor matters more when the other two are already high? Does scoring neglectedness at all presuppose that each additional dollar or hour accomplishes less as a field becomes crowded? Does relying on tractability estimates presuppose that uncertainty should be handled by expected-value reasoning rather than by avoiding interventions whose effects are hard to estimate?

I am less interested in whether these three factors are practically the right ones than in which of these assumptions the scoring rule strictly needs, which ones merely happen to accompany it, and where the practice is underdetermined—where several theories of value could license the same rubric. If one of the presuppositions were rejected, for instance the idea that value across causes sits on a single scale, what would the scoring method have to look like instead?
