I'm designing a session for an advanced undergraduate seminar on moral responsibility, and I want to build the discussion around a single comparative question about Thomas Nagel's challenge of moral luck. The background assumption Nagel presses on is that we hold people morally accountable only for what is within their control, yet that condition appears impossible for anyone fully to meet. He traces the trouble to at least four distinguishable kinds of luck: constitutive luck, or luck in the temperament, dispositions, and capacities one happens to have been formed with; circumstantial luck, or luck in which situations and temptations one encounters; resultant luck, or luck in how one's actions happen to turn out (two equally reckless drivers, one of whom kills a child while the other does not); and causal luck, or luck in the way one's choices themselves are produced by antecedent causes. Rather than discussing these as a flat catalogue, I'd like the session to force an ordering.

Please rank these four kinds of luck from most to least corrosive of our ordinary practice of moral praise and blame, and present the ranking in tiers where two or more of them seem close. For each kind, explain what is distinctive about the challenge it poses, engage with one or two of the standard attempts to neutralize it — for example, that luck affects what we can know but not what is true about a person, that resultant luck concerns outcomes rather than quality of will, or that we can retreat to a reduced conception of responsibility — and state whether those attempts succeed in your view. I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a defended ordering of the four.

So that I can use your answer as a teaching scaffold, please structure it as follows: a section called "Tiered ranking", about a quarter of the response, giving the tiers and a one-sentence justification for each; a section called "Why each kind lands where it does", about half the response, doing the detailed comparative work; a section called "What is left of blame", about a sixth, stating what the ranking implies for whether ordinary blame and praise can survive moral luck; and a final section called "Confidence and weakest link", about a twelfth, in which you say how confident you are in the overall ranking, where the ordering feels most genuinely uncertain, and which single argument you are least sure about.
