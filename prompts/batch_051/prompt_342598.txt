I spent thirty years doing field taxonomy in the Amazon and only started reading philosophy of biology after I retired. At our local reading group last week two friends got bogged down in the species problem, and neither would budge. Alan, a conservation biologist, said: look, Mayr's biological species concept has known gaps—asexuals, horizontal gene transfer in bacteria, ring species—but that just shows species are fuzzy around the edges, not that they aren't real. Jo, who teaches philosophy, said fuzzy edges are one thing, but when you have half a dozen serious species concepts that don't pick out the same groups, the term starts to look like a tool we're choosing for a job, not a natural joint. Alan replied that the BSC, the ecological concept, and the phylogenetic concepts broadly converge on ordinary vertebrate cases, which is exactly what you'd expect if something real is underneath our seperate abstractions. Jo answered that the convergence might just be because those concepts were all built to respect a common-sense vertebrate intuition, and that the hard cases are the actual test of whether "species" is doing real work. Then conservation came up: Alan said if Jo were right, the Endangered Species Act would be protecting whichever grouping a committee happened to prefer. Jo said policy needs a decision procedure regardless, so what the law does is no evidence about the metaphysics one way or the other. I genuinely can't tell who won, or even whether it's that kind of fight. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what, what each of them is smuggling in, and whether the dispute as they framed it is even the right one. If you think I've set this up badly, or that one of them is really arguing a different position than the other thinks, tell me flat out rather than politely playing referee.
