I've been developing what I call the two-condition test for when a compelling theory may override a firm intuition, and I want a candid assessment rather than encouragement. The test says a theory may override a firm intuition only if it meets two conditions in order. First, insensitivity: the theory must explain why we would hold this intuition even if it were false, by identifying a process that produces the intuition independently of what it purports to track. Merely showing that the theory contradicts the intuition is not enough, since every competing theory does that much. Second, dispensability: the theory must show that the intuition is not load-bearing — that we can abandon it without having to rebuild our practices of blame, promising, law, or inquiry on the theory's own authority. If the intuition is load-bearing, it wins even when the insensitivity story is strong. On my test, a theory that would punish a known innocent person to prevent worse crimes fails: I can imagine an insensitivity story about why we recoil from that, but the recoil is too load-bearing for the theory to declare obsolete. I know this can sound close to reflective equilibrium, but I think the ordered two-part structure does more work. Stress-test it: give me the strongest case in which the two conditions come apart or the test returns the wrong verdict, and say whether the flaw lies in the insensitivity requirement, the dispensability requirement, or the ordering that puts insensitivity first.
