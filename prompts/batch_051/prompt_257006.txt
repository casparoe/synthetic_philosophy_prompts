In The Economics of Welfare, A. C. Pigou discusses a habit of mind that he treats as a defect in our perception of the future. As I read him, he says that people look at future satisfactions and troubles through a kind of faulty telescope: things near in time appear large and sharp, while things far in time appear small and dim. He thinks this is not a good reason to count them as less important; it is an illusion we should stand back and correct, the way we correct for an optical instrument we know is out of focus.

I want to test the argument I think he is making. Here it is as I would put it:

(1) If the only reason for discounting future well-being simply because it is future is that it seems less vivid to us from here, then that seeming is not a reason to discount it.
(2) The only reason we actually have for discounting future well-being simply because it is future is that it seems less vivid to us from here.
(3) So there is no good reason to discount future well-being simply because it is future.
(4) If there is no good reason to discount future well-being simply because it is future, then a unit of well-being matters the same whenever it occurs, once we correct for uncertainty about whether it will occur.
(5) Therefore pure time discounting is unjustified.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, which numbered step fails, and why? I am especially unsure about step (2), because it seems possible that people discount future well-being for other reasons—such as a preference for the interests of their current self over their future self, or simply a brute preference for nearer things. But I am also not sure whether step (4) slides from well-being to resources in a way that matters. If you had to save the argument, what is the strongest reading of Pigou's faulty-telescope idea under which the conclusion still follows?
