I'm a novelist, which is exactly the kind of person who shouldn't write an essay on greed, but here I am. I've got a draft for a general-audience magazine and I'd love comments from someone who actually does this stuff before I embarass myself. The essay mainly argues for a distinction, so what I most want to know is whether the distinction does real work or collapses on contact.

Rough version of the argument:

I call it two kinds of wanting more, and I notice it most when I try to write greedy characters. First drawer: the guy who wants more of what he already enjoys. More money, sure, but in the same spirit as wanting a third helping of pasta. The wanting runs in a straight line even when it's compulsive or stupid; it's an appetite that got aimed at the wrong object or allowed to run past its natural stop. Second drawer: the guy who doesn't want more, exactly — he wants more-than-the-other-guy. Money is just a scoreboard. What he's after is being ahead, and the acquiring itself gives him almost nothing unless it's visible or zero-sum.

From outside these two look identical: both accumulate, both lie and cut corners to do it. But the internal shape is different, and I think the old debate about whether greed is a vice or an engine of prosperity trades on conflating them. The old optimism about greed — that one person's private appetite somehow works to everyone else's advantage — seems true only for the first kind. That wanting has no built-in conflict with somebody else having more; it can even make the pie bigger in ways other people get to eat too. The second kind is inherently comparative, so whatever it produces tends to be positional: spending wars, status goods, financial arms races, markets where the whole payoff is beating the other guy. It doesn't grow the pie; it just changes who gets to feel good looking at it.

Then I run a few complications. First, maybe the distinction is just a difference in degree and environment rather than kind: drop a first-drawer appetite into a world where the only measure of more is relative rank and it comes out looking exactly like the second drawer. Second, maybe the first kind is only innocent because I have a novelist's sympathy for it. An appetite pointed at money may not have a natural stopping point, and once there's no finish line, comparing yourself to other people becomes the only score you can keep — in which case the two kinds are stages of one disease and not two diseases.

Comments I actually need:

(a) Is this distinction already a known thing under a name I was never taught? If so I'd rather find out now and either lean into it or revise around it. (b) Does the claim hold that the prosperity argument only requires the first kind, or do the standard defenses of greed quietly depend on something like the second kind too — rivalry, emulation, wanting to do better than the next guy? (c) Am I sneaking a moral verdict in while pretending to give a psychological description? I want the essay to earn whatever verdict it reaches. (d) Where's the weakest step, and is it fixable or fatal?

One last observation to check: a novel built around a first-drawer character tends toward comedy, and a second-drawer character almost always turns the book into melodrama. If that's really true it feels like evidence about which kind of wanting is more entangled with self-deception — but it might just be a confession about my limits as a writer.
