I teach a philosophy of technology seminar for about fifteen master's students, and I am preparing the session on whether large language models should be understood as agents, as simulators, or as something else. I would like a sorting quiz — the kind where readers answer concrete cases and are told at the end which position their pattern of answers commits them to, rather than which label they like. The disagreement I want the quiz to expose is not the crude one between treating the model as a goal-directed agent and dismissing it as a mere text-prediction engine. It is subtler: once a base model has been fine-tuned and wrapped in a system prompt, is the helpful assistant a real, if shallow, agent; is it a persona or character being simulated by an underlying generative model; or does the whole vocabulary of agency and simulation misdescribe a system that just produces text? I would like eight to ten items, each a compact scenario with two or three forced-choice answers in ordinary language that do not require knowing the philosophical labels. Useful scenarios might include: a base model continuing a transcript in which one character lies to another; an assistant refusing a harmful request and saying it cannot help; the same assistant answering a question about what it believes; a fine-tuned model booking a real flight through a tool API; a model whose behaviour flips when the system prompt says 'you are a pirate'; the same weights running with no prompt at all. You need not restrict yourself to these, but they are the kind of case where the three positions come apart.

Please supply a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least three positions — for instance (i) instruction-tuned assistants are agents, (ii) assistants are simulated personas and agency belongs to the simulation rather than to the model itself, and (iii) a deflationary view on which neither description earns its keep — but do not let the list of positions dictate the structure. Build the logical space from the questions first, note any combination of answers that no coherent position occupies and say whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, and flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot separate. For each item, add a one-line note saying which distinction it is meant to pull on. I would rather you commit: choose the actual items, give the scoring, and defend the choices, rather than offering several possible quizzes for me to pick amongst.
