Me and a few friends have this little writing group — mostly fantasy and sci-fi short stories — and there's this one argument that keeps coming back and nobody ever moves an inch. It started when someone wrote a story set in a world where abandoning sick family members to die is considered the morally good thing to do, and the characters treat it as completely normal, even admirable. Some of us said the story just doesn't work, because you can't actually get a reader to go along with that. Others said that's the whole point of speculative fiction and we were just being squeamish.

The weird part is, we're all totally fine when a narrator tells us the sky is green, or that people have gills, or that time runs backwards. But the second the narration asks us to accept that, in this world, torturing someone for fun is right, half the group says its impossible and the other half says it's only hard. We end up arguing about whether the people who refuse are morally uptight, or whether the people who go along are just pretending to imagine something nobody can really imagine. And we've been going in circles for months: everyone knows the examples, everyone agrees cruelty is bad in real life, and still we split the same way every time.

My working theory is that this isn't really about imagination at all. It's about what people are willing to treat as "just a story." You can imagine a green sky because nothing in you cares about the sky being green, but imagining that cruelty is good means letting the story touch the part of you that cares about cruelty, and a lot of people won't do that — so they turn it into a complaint about the writing. But I might be totally off.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a genuine limit on what people can imagine, a moral limit, a confusion about what a story is even asking us to do, or just a matter of craft? And if you had to settle it, what would even count as progress — some kind of test case, a distinction we're missing, or is the whole thing just badly posed?
