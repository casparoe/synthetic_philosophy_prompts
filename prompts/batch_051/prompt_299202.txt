In a philosophy of mathematics seminar we have been pulling on a thread that I now cannot let go of, and I want a diagnosis of where the reasoning breaks, if it breaks. The argument on the table is that arithmetic gives no fact of the matter about whether the number 2 is an element of the number 4, and that this shows the natural numbers are not genuine objects after all. It runs like this.

(1) The natural numbers can be coded as particular pure sets in at least two standard ways, and the two codings disagree on membership facts. On the von Neumann coding, each number is the set of its predecessors: 0 is the empty set, 1 = {0}, 2 = {0,1}, and 4 = {0,1,2,3}, so 2 is an element of 4. On the Zermelo coding, each number is the singleton of its predecessor: 0 is the empty set, 1 = {0}, 2 = {1}, 3 = {2}, and 4 = {3}; since 2 is not 3, 2 is not an element of 4. (2) Both codings sustain exactly the same arithmetic: 2 + 2 = 4, every number has a unique successor, induction holds, and all the usual theorems come out the same. They diverge only on questions that look external to arithmetic, like whether 2 is an element of 4. (3) Nothing in arithmetic itself, and nothing in the practice of counting or computing, privileges one coding over the other; any choice between them would be a convention of bookkeeping, not a fact forced on us by the numbers. (4) So the truth of "2 ∈ 4" is fixed only by a convention, and conventions do not create objective facts. There is therefore no fact of the matter whether 2 is an element of 4. (5) But if 2 and 4 are genuine objects, then "2 ∈ 4" is a determinate claim about a determinate relation between them, and it must be either determinately true or determinately false. (6) Hence the natural numbers are not genuine objects with determinate identities; "the number 2" at most names a position in a structure, and a sentence like "2 ∈ 4" is not really a claim of arithmetic.

This conclusion strikes me as proving too much. I am prepared to grant that the two codings are equally good and that nothing internal to arithmetic picks one out; I am not prepared to grant that "2 ∈ 4" fails to be true or false, or that arithmetic has somehow never been about objects. The structuralist fallback seems to make it worse: if 2 is merely a position in a structure, why could the second position of a progression not be filled by Julius Caesar just as well as by {{∅}} — and if it could, have we not merely shifted the indeterminacy one level up rather than explaining it away? Tell me which step, exactly, I should reject. If the failure is an equivocation or a hidden premise, name it and give the strongest reading of the argument on which it still goes through; if the argument is valid, tell me which premise is the least costly to give up and what it costs a structuralist, a set-theoretic reductionist, and someone determined to keep the natural numbers as genuine objects to give it up.
