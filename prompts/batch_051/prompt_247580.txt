Consider a pattern in state-funded national memory: certain defeats and failed projects, not just victories, are given a central, identity-defining place. The late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century American South built monuments and textbooks around the Lost Cause, recasting the Confederacy's defeat as a noble civilization wronged; Israeli national culture made the ancient siege of Masada, remembered as a mass suicide of Jewish rebels who chose death over capture, a symbol of defiant sacrifice; Serbian memory has for centuries treated the 1389 Battle of Kosovo, a defeat by Ottoman forces, as the founding wound of the nation; and Britain turned the 1940 evacuation of Dunkirk from a military disaster into a story of collective resilience. Treat these four as a single commemorative practice. What view of nationhood or collective identity does the practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that a nation is a trans-generational subject that can be wounded, remember as a single subject, and pass obligations down to members who never experienced the original event? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the four cases share one coherent logic or presuppose incompatible things. Finally, state what a state memory policy would need to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a strictly individualist view, on which nations are not subjects with their own memories and debts but only aggregates of persons.
