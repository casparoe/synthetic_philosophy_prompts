I volunteer with a small charity that redesigns public-health leaflets for people who don't share a first language, and we've been building a system of standardised pictograms so a leaflet can be understood without much text. A colleague who studied information design keeps telling me we are really constructing a picture language, and pointed me to Otto Neurath and the Isotype system he developed in Vienna in the 1920s and 30s. Neurath's lot talked about Isotype as an international picture language for teaching and practical information, but also kept saying it was not a replacement for written language. I've read enough about it now to be genuinely unsure whether it deserves the word "language" or is better thought of as a controlled visual notation.

Here are the criteria for a language I was taught years ago: (1) it has a lexicon of conventionalised symbols with stable meanings, (2) it has rules for combining those symbols so that a symbol's contribution can vary with how it's combined, (3) it is productive, meaning new meanings can be expressed without adding new basic symbols, and (4) it can be used to say things about the non-present: the past, the future, the hypothetical, the merely possible.

Take a mature Isotype-style system of the kind Neurath actually built: a limited stock of standard pictograms (worker, factory, sack of wheat, ship), a convention that repeated identical pictograms stand for quantities, and a set of layout rules governing rows, columns, grouping, and colour so that a chart can express comparisons, trends, and proportions like "unemployment rose faster in the north than the south". New charts can be made, but only by arranging the existing pictograms or by having the system's maintainers design a new pictogram, which then enters the stock.

Does this qualify as a language? Go through the four criteria one at a time and give a definite verdict on each, including the ones where the system only half-qualifies. Then take a stand on the overall question, and say whether the criteria that come out as failures tell us the criteria need changing or that the system genuinely is not a language.
