Been chewing on this argument that you can read the value of knowledge straight off the virtue of curiosity, and it feels like there's a shell game somewhere but I cant pin it to a single step. Go through it and say whether it's valid; if not, which exact step fails and what the corrected version would be.

(1) Curiosity is a virtue: curious people learn more, learning more is good for them, and being good for the person who has it is what makes a trait a virtue.
(2) A virtue is a disposition to want and do the right things for the right reasons — that's the strict sense of the term.
(3) Curiosity is the desire for knowledge for its own sake, not for whatever the knowledge gets you.
(4) So if curiosity is a virtue in the strict sense from (2), then wanting knowledge for its own sake is a right way for a desire to be.
(5) If wanting something for its own sake is right, then that thing is worth having for its own sake.
(6) Therefore knowledge is worth having for its own sake, not just as a means.

I keep half-suspecting (5), since it slides from 'right to want' to 'worth having,' but I also worry the word 'virtue' is doing two jobs early on. Is the argument valid? Is each step true? If it breaks, where exactly does it break, and can it be fixed by choosing one sense of 'virtue' consistently?
