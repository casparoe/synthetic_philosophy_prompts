Earlier this week a friend and I argued late into the evening about whether anti-corruption rules are a parochial Western attempt to impose arm's-length impartiality on societies that run on personal obligation. He insisted that the line between a gift and a bribe can only be drawn locally; I held out for a defensible core that travels. We did not settle it, and the argument made me think again of a clause in my organisation's field-operations code. The code was drafted in 2011 by lawyers in London and reads, in the relevant part:

"Staff shall not solicit or accept any gift, payment, favour, hospitality, or other benefit of any kind, from any person or body that has, or may reasonably be expected to have, dealings with the Organisation, where the benefit might reasonably be thought capable of influencing the impartial discharge of the staff member's duties or of creating a sense of obligation, except for items of nominal value clearly offered as tokens of courtesy."

Now take the following case. We work in a region in which a form of spiritual co-parenthood is a central social institution: to be asked to stand as godparent to a child is an honour, and the relationship creates durable reciprocal expectations of assistance between the two families; to refuse is a serious slight, and it can damage a person's standing and access locally. An official whose department will soon decide a permit for our programme has asked one of our team leaders, in her personal capacity, to be godparent to his child. He presents the request as a matter of personal respect and says nothing about the permit. The role would cost her time and money and would place ongoing obligations to the child upon her; the value to her is intangible—the honour itself and integration into local networks. It is exactly the kind of embedded, reciprocal relationship my friend thought the code was wrong to distrust.

Read solely for its words, does the clause forbid her from accepting? Read for its purpose, what does it require, and what does that purpose tell us about relationships as against transfers? What would the London drafters most likely have said if asked about godparenthood, and how much should that counterfactual matter? What would a reasonable reader in the region concerned take the clause to require, and should that reading count? Which approach ought to win here, and does a rule that catches this protect something universal or overreach into a legitimate institution? I would be grateful for the answer in British English.
