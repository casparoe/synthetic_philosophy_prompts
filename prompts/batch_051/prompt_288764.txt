I teach epistemology, and in my seminars and reading groups I keep running into a specific kind of deadlock that I dont have an established term for. Two people disagree about something ordinary — say whether a certain study is good evidence, or whether a past event happened the way a report says it did. They exchange reasons at first, in good faith. Then, gradually, each side starts saying the other's 'reason' only looks like a reason from inside a whole way of seeing things: 'well, yes, it supports your conclusion, but only if you already trust these methods.' The argument then detaches from the original question and becomes about whether that background itself can be challenged — and at that point neither person can say what would count as settling it, because any proposed evidence is exactly the sort of thing one side treats as already poisoned. It isn't relativism, because they don't both think the two ways are equally good. It isn't talking past each other, because each can accurately restate the other's view. And it isn't an ordinary disagreement over a premise, because they share nearly all their premises; the contested thing behaves more like a rule for what can show up as a premise at all. I know this is somewhere near Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and I have a vague sense there is a label like 'deep disagreement,' but I want the carving, not just the name. What exactly makes a disagreement go this way? How is it different from a merely stubborn disagreement over a premise, and from relativism? Which established concepts come closest, and where are their boundaries? If there is a right term, tell me what it covers and what it excludes; if there isnt, name it and define it.
