A colleague has been defending an anti-aggregationist view about rescue cases, and I have reconstructed the argument as follows. (1) In a forced choice between saving one stranger and saving five strangers, one is required to save the five only if the claims of the five, taken together, outweigh the claim of the one. (2) The strength of a claim is fixed by what is at stake for its bearer: for each person, death is the worst loss there is, and there is no further bearer over and above the individuals who suffers the combined loss of five deaths. (3) So the one's claim is exactly as strong as the claim of any one of the five, and the five, considered as a single competing side, have no stronger claim than the one. (4) When two competing sides have equally strong claims, fairness requires giving each side an equal chance rather than awarding the good outright to one side; a coin toss between them is the natural procedure. (5) Therefore, in one-versus-five, one is not required to save the five, and a coin toss between the one and the five is fair. (6) Nothing in this reasoning depends on the scale of the numbers. By the same steps, in a choice between saving one stranger and saving five million, a coin toss between the one and the five million would be fair. (7) But that cannot be right. A public health authority that must choose between two equally cost-effective programs, one saving a single life and one saving a million lives, is not merely permitted to choose the million-life program; it would be a dereliction of duty not to. So at least one step must go. Which step should be rejected, and what does each option for rejecting it commit one to? I want to keep the thought in (2) that harms are borne only by individuals, but I do not see how to keep it without being carried all the way to the coin toss in (6).
