In my philosophy of language seminar we've been going back and forth on whether grammatical categories could be natural kinds at all — not just handy labels linguists stick on lists of words. I want to work through English prepositions as a test. Say a category is a natural kind if, roughly: (1) knowing something belongs to it lets you predict other properties with confidence; (2) its members share a cluster of properties over and above whatever feature first got them classified; (3) its boundaries are discovered rather than layed down by the linguist; and (4) there is some underlying commonality — shared history, shared function, shared structure — that explains the clustering.

The preposition looks like a proper borderline case. It is a fairly closed class and syntactically coherent: prepositions take noun-phrase complements, they can be stranded in questions and relatives ("who did you talk to?", "the person I was waiting for"), they combine with "right" in spatial uses but not generally in grammatical ones, and new members are hard to coin. Yet semantically the class seems all over the place: "in" is locative, "during" temporal, "with" comitative or instrumental, "of" is a bit of pure grammatical glue, and "than" is doing comparison, which is hardly a spatial relation. Several items double as adverbs or subordinating conjunctions ("after", "before", "since"). And in plenty of languages the same relational jobs are done by case endings, so a preposition can start to look like an analytic case marker rather than a kind of its own.

So does the class of English prepositions count as a natural kind by these criteria? Work through them one at a time and say honestly which pass, which fail, and where the criterion itself is doing too much work. If the answer is no, is that because prepositions aren't a kind, or because "natural kind" is the wrong demand to make of a grammatical category? And is there really a fact of the matter here, or is it all downstream of what a theory needs the category to do?
