I'm taking a course on the privatization of justice, and I want to work through an edge case for the working definition of adjudication we have been using. Our criteria say that a proceeding counts as adjudication — the activity courts perform, as opposed to bargaining, mediation, or administrative decision-making — if it has the following features: (1) a decision-maker who is genuinely independent and impartial; (2) a decision grounded in pre-existing law or rules rather than ad hoc compromise; (3) fair procedure, meaning notice, an opportunity to be heard, and no gross inequality of arms between the parties; (4) a reasoned decision available to the parties and, at least in principle, to public scrutiny; (5) some real avenue for correcting serious error; and (6) a binding outcome backed by the state's coercive power.

Now consider a case that seems to sit right at the edge. A large company inserts a pre-dispute mandatory arbitration clause into the fine print of its consumer and employment contracts. Any dispute is diverted from court into a private arbitration proceeding with these features: the arbitrator is drawn from a roster administered by a private provider and is paid by the case, in practice usually by the company; the arbitrator is required to apply the governing law, and each side may be represented by counsel; the proceedings are confidential; discovery is drastically narrower than in litigation, in ways that generally favor the company; the arbitrator need not issue written reasons; judicial review is available only for rare defects such as fraud or arbitrator misconduct, not for ordinary legal error; the clause waives class arbitration, so each small claim must be brought individually even when the same conduct injured thousands of people; and the company appears before the same arbitration providers in thousands of cases each year, while each consumer or employee appears once. The resulting award is binding and enforceable in court.

Work through this case criterion by criterion and tell me: does mandatory arbitration of this kind qualify as adjudication — is justice actually being administered — or is it a different activity that merely resembles adjudication from the outside? Where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, say so rather than pretending they settle it. Then give your view on whether this case shows that the criteria themselves need revision, and if so, which criterion you would add, drop, or reframe.

Act as the philosopher of law teaching this course. Before you commit to a verdict, take a paragraph to brainstorm the strongest considerations for and against counting the case as adjudication. Then reason carefully through the criteria. Finally, report how confident you are in your overall verdict and single out the one point in your analysis you are least sure of.
