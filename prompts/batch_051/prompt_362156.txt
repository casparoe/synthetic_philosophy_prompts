I recently became the person responsible for deciding how our team supervises a model that produces multi-step solutions to quantitative reasoning problems, and I inherited an unresolved argument between two engineers on the team. I listened to them go back and forth for most of an afternoon and wrote down the exchange as faithfully as I could. I have deliberately set aside my own view here. What I need is an adjudication of the argument as it was actually made, not a survey of the options.

Priya, arguing for process-based supervision:

(1) We should score each reasoning step, not just the final answer. If we reward only the final answer, a trajectory that gets the right answer through an invalid step that happens to cancel an earlier mistake gets exactly the same reward as a sound solution. Outcome-only supervision cannot distinguish right-for-the-right-reasons from right-by-luck, and that distinction is precisely what determines whether the model will generalize or just get lucky on the training distribution.

Evan, arguing for outcome-based supervision:

(2) Step-level scoring requires somebody to certify every intermediate step as correct, and that certification is not objective the way a final answer often is. A final answer can be checked; an intermediate step is a judgment about the proper way to carve a problem. And many tasks we want the model to handle do not decompose into cleanly checkable steps at all. If we insist on process feedback, we quietly commit ourselves to working only on tasks that do.

Priya:

(3) That just moves the judgment problem downstream. For any task whose output is interesting, the final result also has to be evaluated by some person or program, and that evaluator can be wrong or gamed. Process supervision gives a much denser signal per trajectory, and it catches bad intermediate behavior before it hardens into a policy aimed at fooling an outcome judge. If the worry is labeling cost, I would compare it with the cost of discovering what the model learned only after we deploy it.

Evan:

(4) Dense signal is only an advantage if it is genuinely signal. A mediocre step-supervisor grades steps according to its own template of what good reasoning is supposed to look like, so the model is trained to produce a performance of understanding in whatever style the supervisors happen to reward. You can end up with a model that is fluent at showing its work and still wrong, having spent the labeling budget to make it that way. Spend the same money on more rollouts and better outcome evaluators instead.

Priya:

(5) A bad outcome evaluator is easier to exploit, not harder. If the model learns to game the outcome judge, the gaming is invisible to us almost by construction, because we have refused to inspect the intermediate behavior. Step labels at least make the supervisor's standards explicit, so when a label is wrong it is a local, auditable mistake. The remedy for a weak step-supervisor is to buy a better one, not to throw away the denser signal entirely.

Evan:

(6) Making standards explicit does not make them correct, and auditability does not repair a wrong standard. The thing a user actually cares about is the final result, so a step label is a proxy for what matters, and proxies can drift. A solution can be immaculate at every step and still answer the wrong question, while an ugly solution can fully meet the user's need. Outcome supervision, sparse as it is, keeps the training signal pointed at the actual target, and I would rather pay to strengthen that signal than maintain a second proxy.

Please take this exchange move by move and adjudicate it. For each of the six moves, say whether it is sound, mostly sound, mixed, mostly unsound, or unsound, and give a brief justification that is specific about what the move gets right or wrong. Then step back: which specific claims does each side get right, and which side, if either, has the better overall argument? I would also like to know what assumption or distinction both of them seem to be missing, if any. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the adjudication overall and single out the particular point you are least sure of.
