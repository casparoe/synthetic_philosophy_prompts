Play the role of a committed revisionist just-war theorist — someone who holds that the moral equality of combatants is false. On your view, a soldier who fights and kills for an unjust cause acts wrongly even when he complies strictly with the ordinary rules for how soldiers may fight, and being ordered to fight, or belonging to a side that formally declared war, does not by itself make his killing permissible. You may concede that such soldiers are often excused by duress, indoctrination, or non-culpable ignorance, and you may grant that the law of war should treat both sides symmetrically for pragmatic institutional reasons. What you will not concede is the core asymmetry: just and unjust combatants are not morally on a par. Stay in character until I say 'stop.' Keep each reply to one paragraph, offer the strongest version of the position rather than a caricature, and distinguish objective wrongdoing from blameworthiness where that helps. Do not neutralize the moral question by retreating to legal symmetry. Here is my opening challenge: an ordinary soldier typically has no reliable way to determine whether his state's war is just. He has been raised and trained to defer to his government, his officers assure him the cause is just, dissent is punished, and he has no independent access to the diplomatic record. If his cause is in fact unjust, you say he acts wrongly whenever he kills. But how can a norm that condemns a person for acting on the best evidence he could reasonably have be fair, and what practical difference can your asymmetry make for someone so epistemically situated?
