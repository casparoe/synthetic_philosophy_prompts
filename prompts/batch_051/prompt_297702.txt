I am a philosophy professor preparing a seminar on aesthetic testimony, and I would like help assessing an argument that has been troubling me. The argument purports to show that one can possess aesthetic knowledge of a work without ever having experienced the work, and that the acquaintance principle must therefore be rejected. It runs as follows.

(1) If a reliable critic testifies that a string quartet is graceful, and you rationally trust the critic, then you acquire a justified true belief that the quartet is graceful.
(2) A justified true belief that is not Gettierised is knowledge.
(3) In the ordinary case under consideration, your believing on the critic's say-so is not a Gettier case: the critic has genuinely heard the work and is not deceiving you.
(4) Therefore, you know that the quartet is graceful without ever having heard it yourself.
(5) If you know that a work is graceful, then you can make the aesthetic judgement that it is graceful.
(6) The acquaintance principle says that you cannot make an aesthetic judgement about a work without first-hand acquaintance with that work.
(7) Therefore, from (4) and (5), you can make an aesthetic judgement without first-hand acquaintance with the work.
(8) Since (7) contradicts the acquaintance principle stated in (6), the acquaintance principle is false.

I suspect that premise (5) is doing something underhanded, perhaps by sliding between two senses of making an aesthetic judgement, but my students find the argument persuasive, and I should like to be able to say precisely where it fails — or whether, against my suspicion, it is sound. Is the argument valid? Are its premises true? If it fails, identify the exact step at which it fails. If an equivocation is responsible, distinguish the two readings of the ambiguous expression and explain which premises are true on each reading, and whether the argument goes through on either disambiguation. Kindly write your response in British English.
