As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I keep returning to a passage near the end of Alan Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence where he proposes, in effect, a research program: rather than trying to program an adult mind directly, build a machine with a child's ignorance and plasticity, then subject it to an appropriate course of education so that adult abilities emerge. The proposal appeals to me because it converts an impossible-seeming engineering problem into a more tractable one. Instead of anticipating every competence an intelligent system will need, you build a learner and let experience do the rest. It also suggests that intelligence is not a fixed set of representations but something that grows through interaction.

But when I think about what education actually requires, the picture starts to feel incomplete. A human child is not a generic learning substrate. It arrives already oriented toward faces and voices, already sensitive to novelty, already capable of reading intentions, already structured by preferences and aversions that make a teacher's approval or disapproval matter. Education works because teacher and child share a world and share a set of starting points about what is salient, what is rewarding, and what is frightening. If you simply add reward and punishment channels to an initially unstructured system, it is not obvious why the punishments should be avoided or the rewards pursued unless the system already has something like pain and appetite. So the intuition that will not go away is this: education cannot be bolted onto an arbitrary learning algorithm after the fact; a learner must already be the kind of thing for which some signals count as teaching.

I recognize that there are ways to debunk this intuition. The innate structure of a child is itself the product of evolution's own long process of selection, so perhaps Turing's education step should be preceded by an evolutionary or self-supervised phase that builds the necessary prior structure automatically. Or perhaps a human child's special socialization is just one implementation of reinforcement, and an artificial learner with different native capacities could acquire comparable knowledge without sharing our specific cares and vulnerabilities. But I cannot tell whether these responses save Turing's proposal or quietly abandon the very simplicity that made it attractive.

I would like you to present the strongest version of both sides. Is my intuition tracking a real condition on learning, or is it an anthropocentric intuition that should be discarded when we design machines with different native capacities and different ways of being educated? What would justify each move: amending the child-machine proposal to include innate structure, replacing it with a multi-stage learning architecture, or holding to the original proposal and treating the intuition as a limitation of human imagination? Which would you give up, and what would you need to see in an artificial learner before you accepted that Turing's educational route had actually been taken?
