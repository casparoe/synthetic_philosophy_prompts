For most of the past decade I have worked as a machine-learning engineer, and I think almost exclusively in the vocabulary of optimization: objective functions, search procedures, local optima and saddle points, exploration–exploitation trade-offs, gradients and black-box updates, sample complexity, and the view that learning and adaptation are best understood as search over a space of possible policies or configurations. My grasp of evolutionary biology, by contrast, is genuinely thin — I know the slogan that evolution is a kind of search over a fitness landscape, but the apparatus of replicators, lineages, levels of selection, and major transitions is not a language I can think in reliably.

I have been sitting in on a discussion series about whether natural selection favors machine intelligences over biological humans, and I keep suspecting that the argument is being carried by a framework I do not control. The assigned text this week was Samuel Butler's Erewhon (1872), specifically chapter 23, where Butler concludes his discussion of the machines. Here is the passage I keep coming back to:

“May we not fancy that if, in the remotest geological period, some early form of vegetable life had been endowed with the power of reflecting upon the dawning life of animals which was coming into existence alongside of its own, it would have thought itself exceedingly acute if it had surmised that animals would one day become real vegetables? Yet would this be more mistaken than it would be on our part to imagine that because the life of machines is a very different one to our own, there is therefore no higher possible development of life than ours; or that because mechanical life is a very different thing from ours, therefore that it is not life at all?”

As I read it, Butler is advancing several claims at once: that an evolutionary process is not obliged to produce anything resembling the life with which it began; that dismissing machines as a new phase of life because their form of life differs from ours is parochial in the way a plant would be parochial in dismissing animals; and that once machines vary, reproduce, and are selected among, selection has no built-in loyalty to carbon-based agents.

I want you to restate this position, together with its modern descendant — that if machine intelligences become faster, more reliable, and more capable agents of their own improvement than biological humans, selection pressures will favor them over us — systematically in the vocabulary of optimization and search. Please go component by component: for each claim, tell me whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no expression at all in my vocabulary, and mark what my vocabulary forces you to add or suppress. Do not launder Butler's selectionist assumptions into the optimization vocabulary as though they were already there; I want to see where they must be imported. Then give me your honest assessment of the residue: whether it indicates a genuine limitation in the optimization framework or whether it exposes claims that were doing less work than they appeared to do. I care more about an honest ledger of what survives the move between frameworks than about a final verdict on AI takeover.
