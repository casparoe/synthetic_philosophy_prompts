I've been trying to sort out how I actually pick a "reference class" when I make forecasts, and I've got five firm intuitions that refuse to line up. I don't want a bibliography on the outside view; I want to know whether there's one principle behind my verdicts, or whether I need to ditch some of them.

1. The restaurant. A new independent restaurant opens in my neighborhood. Chance it's still around in three years? My gut says: take the citywide base rate for new independent restaurants surviving three years and only nudge it a little for the fact that the chef came from a famous kitchen and the menu is good. Everyone thinks their place is special, and most of them are wrong, so I mostly ignore the specifics.

2. The startup. A software startup with 45 employees just closed its Series A. Chance it reaches Series C within five years? I wouldn't even consider the base rate for all new businesses — that includes restaurants and barbershops. Obviously the right class is something like venture-backed Series A software startups, maybe narrowed to the same rough sector. That one doesn't even feel like a judgment call.

3. The startup again. But when I start adding the founders' traits — elite school, previous exit, first-time founder over 40 — I pull back. I have those facts, and I'm sure someone could assemble a reference class for startups with exactly those traits, but the class would be tiny and the number would be noise. My gut says stop at "Series A software startups" and don't refine further, even though those traits probably matter. I cant explain where the stopping point comes from.

4. The old empire. Now take an empire that's already lasted 300 years, and I want the chance it lasts another 50. The base rate for regimes surviving another 50 years is low, but only because so many die young. The natural class here is "empires that already survived a few centuries." That class is tiny and it was selected by the very outcome I'm trying to forecast (longevity), yet here I'm perfectly comfortable using it. Using all regimes feels like the naive move — which is the exact opposite of how I felt in case 3.

5. The marriage. My brother got married two years ago. Chance it lasts five more years? I start from the base rate for first marriages with their age and education profile. But I actually know them — I've seen how they argue — and I feel entitled to move my estimate a couple points on that basis. Yet in the restaurant case I was dismissive of exactly that kind of personal read. Maybe my one observation is the inside view in disguise; I still think it can move me a little, but "a little" doesn't feel like a principle.

My working rule is something like: use the narrowest reference class that's still big enough and homogeneous with respect to the factors that actually drive the outcome. But "factors that actually drive the outcome" is just the inside view smuggled back in, so the rule either begs the question or collapses the outside view into the inside view one level down.

So: is there one principle that captures these five verdicts, or at least four of them? Which one or two would you overturn, and what does a clean version of the principle imply for my city-budget case — forecasting whether a mid-sized city will hit a budget crisis in the next decade. Should I use all mid-sized cities in the country, mid-sized cities with a similar revenue mix (say, property-tax-dependent), or this particular city's own record across the last three boom-bust cycles? The classes give different numbers, and I don't know wich of my own rules to apply.
