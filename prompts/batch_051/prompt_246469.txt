You are a moral philosopher who works on responsibility, causation, and the ethics of collective action, with a focus on environmental harms. I'd like you to think carefully, and before committing to a single analysis, to brainstorm in note form at least three distinct candidate accounts of what the practice I describe below presupposes.

I recently became the chair of my company's sustainability committee, and I've begun to doubt something I'm now expected to defend. Our firm has committed publicly to becoming carbon neutral, but almost none of that commitment involves reducing our own emissions. Instead, for each ton of CO2 we expect to emit, we plan to buy carbon offsets: payments to a certified provider that funds projects elsewhere — a reforestation plot in another country, a landfill-gas capture system, a program that distributes efficient cookstoves — projects that are supposed to remove from the atmosphere, or prevent from entering it, an equivalent amount of greenhouse gas. The company line, which I used to accept, is that these purchases net out our emissions, so that our total contribution to climate change is zero, or at least fully compensated.

I'm no longer sure this makes sense, and I'd like you to help me see what the practice quietly commits us to. What view of causation, of responsibility, and of what makes an individual contribution to a collective harm matter does a serious offsetting practice presuppose? In particular:

- What has to be true about my company's relationship to the harm for it to be acceptable to continue emitting because we are also paying for reductions elsewhere? Does offsetting presuppose that a harm to one set of people, through one causal pathway, at one time, can be made good by a benefit to different people, through a different pathway, at a different time?
- Does offsetting presuppose that my company's own emissions count as objectionable only if they make the overall balance of harms and benefits worse in some commutable, netting-friendly sense?
- Offsets are typically certified as additional — the funded reduction would not have happened without our payment — and are described as making our marginal contribution efficacious in a new way. How does this interact with the familiar worry that any one firm's emissions are an inefficacious drop in a vast collective problem?
- Which of these commitments are strictly required by the practice, which merely fit comfortably with it, and where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories of responsibility would each license it but for different reasons?

Finally, tell me what would have to change about our policy — if anything — if we rejected the view that our emissions can be compensated in this way and instead treated our own emissions as generating an agent-relative reason not to emit, regardless of the reductions we fund elsewhere.
