I have been defending a view about mathematical proof, and I want an honest map of how exposed it is. The view is that a proof need not be surveyable by a single human mind: the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture are genuine theorems, and their computer-assisted proofs give us mathematical knowledge, even though no mathematician has checked every case by hand — provided the computation is reproducible, the algorithm is specified precisely, and the relevant components have been independently checked or formally verified. I want to know what would count against this view, and what would have to be true for me to abandon it outright rather than merely qualify it.

I am not interested in the tired objection that computers sometimes malfunction; that is a point about engineering, not about the nature of proof. The defeaters I take seriously are these: that surveyability is constitutive of proof, so an unsurveyable proof is a contradiction in terms; that dependence on a computation is epistemically closer to testimony or experiment than to deduction, making the result empirical rather than a priori; that a systematic error in the finite case analysis could reproduce across independent implementations because it is baked into the shared specification; and that a proof that yields no understanding yields no knowledge in the fullest sense, only certified belief. For each, tell me whether it is decisive, whether it is something a sophisticated defender should concede as a genuine cost, and what reformulation would absorb it. I also want a frank verdict on whether the view becomes unfalsifiable once formal verification is allowed, and whether that matters for a claim of this kind. And tell me what a committed defender should themselves count as counting against the view — not what critics merely assert.
