I read and occasionally contribute to forecasting and policy discussion forums, where I keep seeing the phrase "the butterfly effect" invoked as if it settles the matter. Most recently, someone argued that, because of the butterfly effect, a small policy intervention cannot be expected to have predictable large-scale consequences, so there is little point in modeling second-order effects at all. I can hear several distinct claims packed into that phrase: the causal platitude that small causes can have large effects; the technical claim that some deterministic systems show sensitive dependence on initial conditions, making trajectories predictable only up to a finite horizon; the sweeping claim that all interesting real-world systems are like that; the event-specific counterfactual that a given large event traces back to one tiny trigger rather than many jointly sufficient causes; and the strong claim that long-range prediction is impossible in principle rather than merely in practice. I want a menu of the distinct theses the slogan could be making, with verdicts on which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false, followed by an account of which readings would actually support the policy conclusion above — and which reading that conclusion is quietly relying on.
