Two people in a discussion group I belong to got into an argument about a third member's plan to keep up Santa and the tooth fairy for his five- and seven-year-old. Neither would concede, and when they asked me to referee I found I could not separate the strands. The exchange went as follows.

(1) Lina said the practice is wrong in principle because it deliberately makes the parents' testimony unreliable in the very channel a young child depends on for nearly everything else it knows. However the child feels later is beside the point: the parents spend years using the child's one genuine epistemic resource against it.

(2) Adam replied that this proves too much. Parents knowingly teach strictly false simplifications all the time—that a table is solid through and through, that there are no exceptions to the rules of arithmetic—and then let refinements arrive on a schedule. If the objection is "using testimonial authority to install a false belief," early education is full of the same thing. So either Lina's objection condemns ordinary teaching, or Santa is just a scheduled revision of a child's model of the world.

(3) Lina answered that the difference is the direction of travel. In the school-like cases the refinement moves the child closer to the truth, and the parent is waiting for it. In the Santa case the parent does not merely leave out detail; the parent manufactures counter-evidence—bitten carrots, money under the pillow, notes in an unfamiliar hand—specifically to suppress the child's would-be correction. No parent intends the child to approximate the truth about Santa over the years; they intend the whole belief to be discarded. That marks it as deception rather than age-appropriate pedagogy.

(4) Adam said that even granted, the line between staged and scheduled falsehood will not do the work she needs. Parents stage falsehoods routinely and uncontroversially: the park "closes" at dinner, the toy shop "has no more" of the forbidden thing, the medicine "tastes nice." If those are permissible conveniences, Lina owes a principle for why staged falsehood in the service of convenience is fine but staged falsehood in the service of enchantment is not. It cannot simply be "epistemic harm," he added, because a small child is not an adult reasoner, and learning that some stories are held collectively until you are old enough to understand that they are stories is itself a real epistemic skill.

(5) Lina answered that the principle is available. The ordinary convenience lies are discrete, behavioral, and called off quickly. Santa is a standing project: it maintains a false picture across many years, across many adults, with evidence constantly produced to keep it true. And "the child is not an adult reasoner" cuts the other way: because the child cannot audit the claims, the parents' behavior is doing the teaching. A child who is lied to as a project learns that love and authority can include being kept inside a fiction for the adults' own pleasure or nostalgia, even if the adult cannot measure any later damage.

(6) Adam replied that this still treats the child's trust as a fungible instrument and ignores the content of what is actually being transmitted. What the child acquires, on the way out of Santa, is the ability to classify a special sort of utterance—"we all say this because it carries a season, a role, an initiation, not a state of the world"—and that is precisely the ability Lina otherwise wants children to have. The way children are eventually brought in on the secret, often by becoming the deceiver for a younger sibling, shows that the practice partly functions as an initiation into keeping fictions for others, which is not the same as simply being lied to.

I would like a move-by-move adjudication. For each numbered step, say whether it succeeds as a reply to the step before it, which of its claims are right, and where it overreaches. Then say what the strongest point on each side is, what both parties are missing, and who is more right overall, if that question is well-posed.
