I am a software engineer who works on program analysis and formal verification, not a philosopher. I spend my days with transition systems, state-space search, loop invariants, decidability results, and the object-language/meta-language split. I recently came across the MU-puzzle and the idea of JOOTSing (jumping out of the system), and I want to know how much of it survives being restated in the vocabulary I actually use.

Here is the material as I understand it. Take a formal system whose strings are built from the letters M, I, and U, with the single axiom MI and four rules: (1) if a string ends in I, append U; (2) from a string of the form Mx you may obtain Mxx; (3) replace any occurrence of III with U; (4) delete any occurrence of UU. The puzzle asks whether MU can be derived. The standard answer is no, and the standard demonstration does not operate inside the system: it notices that every derivable string has a number of I's not divisible by three, while MU has no I's at all, so MU is unreachable. JOOTSing is the name given to the move this is supposed to illustrate—stepping outside the system and establishing something about it that no amount of rule-following within it seems able to establish.

Restate the position the MU-puzzle and JOOTSing are meant to express—roughly, that there is a principled difference between what can be settled by applying a system's rules and what can be settled only by stepping outside the system—in my vocabulary. Map the pieces as cleanly as you can: the rules to a transition system, derivable strings to reachable states, the question whether MU is derivable to a reachability query, the invariant about the number of I's to an inductive invariant, and JOOTSing itself to whatever corresponds (invariant synthesis, abstract interpretation, the move from exploring executions to proving safety properties, or something else). Do not force a clean mapping where there isn't one. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the position translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in the vocabulary of program analysis. In particular, I want a verdict on the sharp inside/outside distinction. In my world, noticing an invariant is itself an algorithm, and the object language and the metalanguage are both formal systems. Does the distinction between inside and outside survive that fact? Should I conclude that my vocabulary is missing something the position is pointing at, or that the original position is less clean than it looks once it is stated precisely?

End by saying what would change your verdict about the residue—for example, whether the toy system's decidability is doing all the work, whether moving to a system rich enough for Gödel's incompleteness theorems would force you to rebalance, or whether you are overstating how mechanical invariant-finding is—and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write in American English.
