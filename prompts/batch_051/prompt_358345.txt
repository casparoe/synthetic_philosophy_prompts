The purpose of the case is to test whether complicity can be reduced to causal contribution. Construct a thought experiment in which two rival accounts of complicity deliver opposite verdicts about the same agent. The first account, the causal-contribution account, says an agent is complicit in a wrongdoing only if her action made a causal difference to the wrongdoing: to whether it occurred, to how serious it was, or to how likely it was to succeed. The second account, the participatory account, says an agent can be complicit even when her action made no such difference, provided she knowingly took part in the wrongful enterprise in a way that counts as joining or endorsing it. Build a case where the participatory account says the agent is complicit and the causal-contribution account says she is not.

Constraints. The scenario must be realistic and small-scale: a workplace, a professional practice, a community organization, or a family, not a war crime or a criminal syndicate. The agent must be a plausible candidate for complicity on both accounts' own terms: knowledgeable, free, and not acting under duress. Her act must be a positive action of participation, not an omission, a failure to report, or a bare attitude. The causal inertness of her act must be generated by the structure of the scenario rather than stipulated. And the wrongdoing must not be one whose harm consists in being endorsed, acknowledged, or known about, so that the participatory account cannot win by quietly reintroducing a causal effect through expressive harm.

After presenting the scenario in one paragraph, do the following. First, state the wrongdoing, the agent's act, and the precise respect in which that act made no causal difference. Second, walk through what each account says about the case and why, giving each account its strongest version. Third, identify the structural feature that makes the two accounts come apart, and say whether removing that feature collapses the case into one where both accounts agree. Fourth, give the strongest objection a defender of the causal-contribution account would press — for instance, that the case's complicity is really mere endorsement, or that a causally relevant social fact is being overlooked — and say whether it succeeds.
