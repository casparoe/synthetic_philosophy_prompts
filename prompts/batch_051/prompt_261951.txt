I'm giving a paper next month at a law-and-philosophy workshop, defending the view that the resistance to naked statistical evidence is really two different phenomena that get lumped together. In civil cases with a preponderance standard, I want to defend the heterodox claim that bare statistics should be enough: the gatecrasher and the 80% blue bus cases should come out for the plaintiff, because the epistemic discomfort people feel (that the evidence doesn't track what this specific defendant did) isn't a legal reason under a mere probability standard. But in criminal cases I want to defend nearly the opposite: bare statistical evidence should never suffice for proof beyond a reasonable doubt, not because of probability theory but because a conviction is an act of blame, and blame has to be answerable to what this particular person did, which requires causal or trace evidence rather than a distribution over a reference class.

The audience is mostly epistemologists who work on legal proof and who think the right explanation is unified and epistemic (something about knowledge or justification being absent whenever the evidence is bare statistics), plus two evidence law scholars who will care a lot about jury instructions and about weather my civil-case claim is administrable. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by roughly how likely they are and how much damage they do, phrased the way those people would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And include the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
