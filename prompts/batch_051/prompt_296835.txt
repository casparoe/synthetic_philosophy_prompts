I am taking a module on the metaphysics of digital objects this term, and in my essay I have ended up defending a two-part position. The first part is that virtual objects—items, currency, and avatars in an online game—are real abstract artefacts: they exist, persist, and can be destroyed, but their existence depends on the underlying code and on the community of users that sustains them, in roughly the way money or institutions do. The second part is that a virtual harm is a real harm only when it damages a person's actual interests, such as the deletion of an account someone has spent years building, and not merely because something bad happens inside the fiction; harm needs a bearer with real interests, and a fictional character or an unowned item lacks them. I want to work out what would count against this view before I submit. What would have to be true for it to be false, and which developments would be decisive rather than merely awkward for it? In particular, I expect cases involving copying, resetting, or fully reversible in-world damage to be the most serious challenge, but I am worried the view is becoming insulated by its own qualifications. Is it, and would that insulation itself be a problem?
