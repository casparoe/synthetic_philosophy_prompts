I am a practicing financial accountant, fluent in double-entry bookkeeping, consolidation, net-worth accounting, and the treatment of financial instruments as assets of one entity and liabilities of another, but I have only an educated layperson's grasp of monetary economics. I keep encountering two apparently conflicting claims about what happens when a central bank creates money. One side says that creating money only changes nominal units: it rescales prices and outstanding claims but cannot create real wealth, which must ultimately come from producing real goods and services. The other side says that money creation can create real wealth, or at least bring real resources into use, because liquidity and a working payment system are themselves economically productive and a liquid balance sheet is worth more than an illiquid one.

I want you to do a translation exercise rather than adjudicate the debate directly. Restate each position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: balance sheets, ledger entries, sectoral consolidation, net worth, revaluation gains and losses, and the distinction between inside and outside claims. For example, restate the claim that money is a veil over real magnitudes, and the rival claim that liquidity is productive, as claims about what does and does not happen to the consolidated balance sheets of households, firms, banks, and the government-plus-central-bank sector. Then give me a residue report: which parts of each position translate cleanly into accounting statements, which parts you had to distort to force them into balance-sheet terms, and which parts have no accounting expression at all. Where a claim does not survive the translation, tell me whether that is because accounting is simply the wrong instrument for it, or because the claim itself is less precise than it looks once stated economically.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in each main part of the translation and single out the point you are least sure of.
