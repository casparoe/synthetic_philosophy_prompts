I'm working on a novel where a neuroscientist has a small stroke that leaves her able to act but oddly unable to feel like she's the one acting, and in researching it I've fallen into the causal exclusion argument and its been eating at me. The argument, as far as I can tell: every physical event has a complete physical cause. My hand reaching for the coffee is a physical event, and the firing patterns in my motor cortex are already sufficient for it. Now suppose my wanting the coffee is something over and above those neural causes. Then either it causes the hand movement too, in which case the movement is systematically overdetermined by two complete causes, or it doesn't cause the movement at all, in which case the wanting is epiphenomenal — a shadow cast by the brain but doing no work. Both options seem bad, so the clean conclusion is that mental causation is either just physical causation under another description, or it's an illusion. I'll be honest: the argument has its hooks in me. It's tidy, it respects physics, and it avoids spooky dualism.

But I cannot shake the intuition, and it keeps messing with the novel. When I write the scene where she finally decides to call her estranged daughter, I cannot make myself believe that the decision does nothing — that the reason she dials is no more causally relevant than the weather that day. That my wanting something causes me to reach for it is about as certain as anything I've got, arguably more certain than the premises of any argument. If a tidy theory tells me it's an illusion, maybe the theory has a to narrow idea of causation, or maybe the certainty is just user-illusion, the same way my character mistakes the running commentary in her head for the thing that drives her. So here's what I want: give me the strongest case for each possible resolution. One, debunk the intuition and keep the exclusion argument: explain exactly how a convincing illusion of mental causation could arise, and why we should accept it. Two, amend the theory: keep the exclusion argument's respect for physical sufficiency but find room for mental causes by changing something in the setup. Three, reconcile them, most obviously by saying the mental just is the physical so there's only one cause, not two. I want to know what would justify each move, not just which you'd pick.

After that, give me a JSON summary with these fields: "verdict" (one of: theory_yields, intuition_yields, reconciled, amended), "intuition_debunk" (how the illusion would work and why it's survivable, if you went that way), "theory_amendment" (the smallest change to the argument that saves mental causation, if you went that way), "reconciliation" (how both could be kept, if at all), and "least_bad_bullet" (which of overdetermination, epiphenomenalism, identity, or illusion you'd sooner live with, and one sentence why).
