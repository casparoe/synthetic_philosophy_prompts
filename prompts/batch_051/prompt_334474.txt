A colleague of mine in an evolutionary-psychology lab has spent years deciding whether proposed human traits count as evolved adaptations or as something else: byproducts, spandrels, or recently acquired cultural skills. She is unusually consistent, and I would like to reverse-engineer the rule she is running and predict how she will judge a new proposal.

Her past verdicts, with the briefest rationale she has given:

Accepted as adaptations:
1. Language — Accepted. Species-universal, complexly organized, and no byproduct story handles syntax; no other living species has anything close to it.
2. Snake fear — Accepted. People acquire snake and spider aversions faster and more durably than fears of modern hazards, which suggests a prepared learning mechanism shaped by a recurrent ancestral threat.
3. Incest aversion (the Westermarck effect) — Accepted. Childhood co-residence reliably predicts later sexual disinterest, and the fitness cost of close inbreeding is well documented.
4. Menopause — Accepted. The grandmother hypothesis: post-reproductive women who provision grandchildren measurably improve grandchild survival in natural-fertility populations, which points to a life-history adaptation.

Rejected as adaptations:
5. Female orgasm — Rejected. Best explained as a byproduct of developmental and physiological pathways shared with male orgasm; she sees no independent design.
6. Music — Rejected. A byproduct of language and auditory cognition that cultures later elaborate, not a separate adaptation.
7. Reading — Rejected. Too recent and culturally transmitted; there was no ancestral selection environment, so it must recycle older capacities.
8. The human chin — Rejected. A spandrel of facial reduction rather than a functioning trait in its own right.

Next week she must rule on a new proposal: episodic foresight, also called mental time travel — the human ability to imagine specific, personal future events in detail and to use those simulations in long-range planning. It is species-typical and develops on a reliable schedule. Some clinical and developmental data suggest it can be impaired while general intellectual ability is largely preserved, which advocates take as evidence of a specialized mechanism. But no one has measured the fitness consequences of individual differences in natural-fertility populations, and the comparative evidence is disputed: a few nonhuman species show limited forms of future-oriented caching, though none shows anything like open-ended episodic planning. A skeptic could call it a byproduct of general intelligence plus declarative memory, while advocates deny that general intelligence can do the work.

I am less interested in her bottom line than in the reasoning. Predict the verdict she would give if she stays consistent with her track record. State the candidate rule or rules that fit all eight earlier verdicts. Are there several, and do they converge or diverge on episodic foresight? Identify the closest analog among her earlier cases, and say why. Finally, name either a further real case or a well-chosen invented case that would most sharply separate the candidate rules if she were forced to judge it.

Return your answer as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "candidate_rules": an array of objects, each with:
  - "name": a short label for the rule.
  - "statement": a one-sentence statement of the rule.
  - "fit_to_prior_verdicts": an explanation of how it handles all eight earlier verdicts.
  - "prediction_for_episodic_foresight": one of "accept", "reject", "lean accept", or "lean reject".
- "best_guess": an object with:
  - "verdict": one of "accept", "reject", "lean accept", or "lean reject", your best prediction.
  - "rule_relied_on": the name of the rule you rely on.
  - "confidence": a number between 0 and 1.
- "closest_analog": an object with:
  - "case": the earlier case most analogous to episodic foresight.
  - "why": the respects in which it is analogous and the respects in which it is not.
- "discriminating_case": an object with:
  - "case": a description of the further case that would best separate the candidate rules.
  - "how_each_rule_predicts_it": what each candidate rule says about it.
  - "why_it_separates": why this case forces the rules apart.
- "residual_uncertainty": one to three sentences on what would remain ambiguous even after the analysis.
