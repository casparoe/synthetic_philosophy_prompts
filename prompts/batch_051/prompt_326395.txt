I'm a software engineer and currently the maintainer of my company's release-review process for features that touch user data or can otherwise cause harm that won't be obvious from crash reports: feed ranking, automated account flagging, consent flows, new telemetry. Reviews happen about weekly, take roughly 45 minutes, involve two or three engineers and a product owner, and the product owner has final sign-off. None of us has statistical, legal, or formal safety training. The precautionary principle gets invoked in these meetings, but usually as a slogan meaning that absent proof of safety we should not ship, and it gets dismissed just as quickly as a recipe for never releasing anything useful. I want something more disciplined. Can you turn the precautionary principle into a concrete, reusable decision procedure we can actually run during a release review—a checklist or ordered set of tests—that keeps what the principle is getting at while being usable by engineers with no philosophy background? It should say what to do when we cannot put numbers on a risk, and it should handle these hard cases: harms that are serious but only vaguely specified; features that clearly help some users while plausibly harming others; the difference between easy rollbacks and irreversible exposure; and where the burden of proof should sit. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls rather than mechanical tests. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a feed-ranking change that may raise engagement but could amplify content we cannot reliably detect as harmful; a simplified consent prompt that may lead some users to share data they would not have shared under the current design; and an internal tool that flags accounts for review where the cost of false positives falls mainly on people outside the company. Make at least one case come out against what you would expect me to prefer, and end by stating what the procedure cannot do.
