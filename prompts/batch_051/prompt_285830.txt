Suppose someone holds the view associated with Thomas Szasz that the conditions now classified as mental illnesses—recurrent major depression and schizophrenia, say—are not diseases in the way tuberculosis or hyperthyroidism are. On this view they are better described as problems in living: intelligible, value-laden responses to circumstances rather than underlying pathologies. A colleague maintains that this is at bottom an empirical claim: it predicts that, as research accumulates, these categories will not be pinned down by the biological markers, lesions, mechanisms, and natural histories that anchor recognized medical diseases. You think part of the claim is testable but part of it may be normative or semantic, and you suspect no single study can settle the whole dispute.

Design the strongest concrete test of the empirical component of this view. Specify what would be measured, in whom, against what control or comparison, and what pattern of results would constitute evidence for and against it. Then give an honest accounting of what a positive or negative result would and would not settle: about whether the categories are diseases, about whether the original view is best understood as a testable hypothesis, and about the residue—normative, semantic, or otherwise—that no biological finding is likely to touch. Answer in American English.
