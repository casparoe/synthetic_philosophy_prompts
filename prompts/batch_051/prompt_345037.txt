I am an associate editor at a mathematics journal, and our board must decide whether to accept a paper that claims to settle a conjecture that has been open for several decades. The paper reduces the conjecture to a lemma stating that, if the conjecture fails, at least one of well over a million specified configurations must occur. That reduction is short, transparent, and has been checked by hand to the referees' satisfaction. The remaining step is to verify that none of the configurations occurs; the authors do this with custom computational software, made public and run to completion on three different systems, though it is the same program throughout, so a single bug in the case-generation logic would reproduce its error on every run. No referee can survey the case check itself, and no independent implementation of the search exists. Half the board holds that this is simply what modern mathematics looks like, pointing to the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture, both accepted only after similar disputes, and maintaining that code review and reproducibility are the appropriate successors to hand-checking at scale. The other half holds that a computation no human can survey is not a proof in the relevant sense: it is evidence that a proof exists, or a certification issued by a machine, and accepting the paper would quietly replace the journal's epistemic standard of human checkability with something weaker. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next month's board meeting. It should recommend one course of action, whether accept, reject, or accept under specified conditions such as a machine-checked formal certificate of the computation, an independent reimplementation, or publishing the surveyable reduction and the computational claim under different standards, and it should state the strongest version of the losing side before explaining why the recommendation stands.
