I'm a PhD student in political science, and I keep noticing a failure mode in loosely coordinated groups that I don't have a name for. The setup: a bunch of reasonable people each have the ability to act on behalf of the group—sending a correction to a journal, posting an open statement to a community listserv, flagging a problem to a funder, that kind of thing. If you asked each of them privately, most would say the action is probably not worth it. But each person is working from a slightly different slice of the evidence and trusts their own read of it, so each thinks 'if I don't, maybe nobody will, and if somebody does, I'd rather it be me—at least I've been careful.' So the most confident one or two people go ahead, and now the group is committed to an action that a majority of its members privately though was overkill. The part that bugs me is that the outcome tracks the most optimistic member, not the median, and the more people who could act, the more likely it is that someone will. It feels adjacent to tragedy of the commons and maybe groupthink, but neither fits: nobody's depleting a shared resource, and there's no pressured consensus-building beforehand. It also seems like the mirror image of the bystander effect, where everyone waits and nobody acts. Does this have an established name? If so, what exactly separates it from those nearby things; and if not, what should I call it? I'm after the mechanism and its edge cases more than the label.
