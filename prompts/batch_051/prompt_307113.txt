At a public hearing last week about a chemical manufacturer that had pleaded guilty to dumping untreated waste into the local watershed, a company spokesperson repeatedly defended the firm's lobbying against new state regulations by insisting that "corporations are people." A neighbor told me afterward that she found the phrase absurd in the spokesperson's mouth, yet she then used the very same phrase to argue that the company itself—not just the plant manager who signed off on the discharge—is the proper object of blame for the contamination. The exchange stuck with me because the slogan was clearly doing different work for the two speakers, and I suspect it can be read in several incompatible ways. I want to know what, precisely, "corporations are people" could be claiming: from the trivially true or merely legal, to the substantive and defensible, to the almost certainly false. For each reading, I would also like to know whether it actually supports the claim that a company, as distinct from its employees or executives, can be morally blamed—and, relatedly, how it bears on the spokesperson's use of the phrase to ground corporate rights.

Please lay the readings out as a menu rather than picking one and defending it, and for each reading give a verdict and say what would change that verdict: which argument or consideration would flip it from defensible to false, or the reverse, or where your judgment about it is least stable. Format this as a numbered list of exactly five items, one reading per item, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
