I have been reading the luminosity and KK debate long enough to notice that both sides have settled positions, and the same moves now recur without shifting anyone. I want the impasse itself diagnosed, not the underlying question settled.

On one side, the anti-luminosity argument associated with Timothy Williamson is treated as having shown, in its essentials, that no condition that is neither always present nor always absent can satisfy the following requirement: whenever the condition obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. The argument runs a fine series of cases from the condition's clearly obtaining to its clearly failing — the usual example goes from feeling cold at dawn to not feeling cold at noon — and observes that the subject could not reliably discriminate between adjacent cases in the series. It then invokes a margin-for-error requirement: being in a position to know that the condition obtains at one case requires that the condition also obtain at the next, relevantly similar case. Luminosity would let the argument iterate from the first case to the last and derive a contradiction. Since knowing a particular proposition is itself a condition of this kind, the KK principle (if you know that p, you know that you know that p) — or at least the weaker availability version, that if you know that p, you are in a position to know that you know that p — looks like a casualty of the same argument.

On the other side, defenders of luminosity or of KK tend to reply that the argument simply imports a controversial safety-based conception of what it takes to be in a position to know; or that awareness of one's own sensations is not the kind of discriminatory capacity that a series of imperceptible steps tests; or they retreat to more modest claims — that phenomenal states are luminous even if belief- and knowledge-states are not, or that positive introspection holds for suitably idealized subjects even when it fails for finite ones. Both sides are sophisticated, and after reading them I still cannot identify the claim whose acceptance would make either side yield.

My working hypothesis is that this looks like one debate but is really several running under one name: perhaps a disagreement about how much idealization is packed into the phrase 'in a position to know' (one side reading it as bounded by actual, finite sensitivity to adjacent differences; the other as an idealized reflective capacity that abstracts away from the very thresholds the argument exploits), and perhaps also a verbal disagreement about whether a capacity that is only 'available in principle' deserves to be called luminosity. But I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I want my hypothesis checked, not assumed.
