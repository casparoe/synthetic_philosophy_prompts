I have been trying to sort out what I believe about gambles whose expected values misbehave, and I have arrived at a set of views that I would like checked for consistency rather than just graded one by one. My commitments are roughly these.

(1) For any finite or otherwise well-behaved gamble, the correct way to evaluate it is expected value: choose the option with the highest expected monetary value, and pay no more for a gamble than the price that keeps your expected net gain nonnegative.

(2) I do not think the St. Petersburg game refutes expected value reasoning. I take its lesson to be about utilities rather than probabilities: unbounded payoffs need to be passed through a utility function that flattens out at the top (something like bounded utility, or at least strongly diminishing marginal utility), and with that correction the game has a finite value. So the paradox indicts an unrealistic utility assumption, not the expectation framework itself.

(3) For the Pasadena game — keep flipping a fair coin until it lands heads, and if the first heads comes on flip n, you receive (−1)^(n−1) times 2^n/n dollars — I find myself wanting to say that the fair price is the natural-order sum of the expected value series: 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which comes to ln 2, about 69 cents. The large positive and large negative possibilities nearly cancel, and that near-cancellation feels like the right picture.

(4) I hold a representation principle: two gambles with the same probability distribution over monetary payoffs are the same gamble for purposes of evaluation, and the order in which possible states of the world happen to be listed should never change what someone should pay.

(5) I take dominance to be non-negotiable: if one gamble pays at least as much as another in every possible outcome and strictly more in at least one, then the first is better, whether or not either gamble has a well-defined expectation.

(6) Finally, I resist the conclusion that an undefined expected value means there is simply no fact of the matter about a gamble's value. When an expectation is undefined, I think we should look for some principled fallback — limits of truncated versions, dominance comparisons, or a regularity condition that narrows things down — rather than declare the question meaningless.

I cannot shake the feeling that some of these pull against others, but I cannot tell which pairings are the genuine problem, which are only superficially tense, and which single commitment is doing the most damage. Please audit the set in order of severity: for each real tension, say precisely which commitments conflict and what would have to be given up or modified to repair it, and explicitly identify any apparent tension that dissolves once the commitments are read carefully.
