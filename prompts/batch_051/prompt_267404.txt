I'm preparing a handout for an advanced philosophy-of-law course unit on the void-for-vagueness doctrine, and I'd like it to be a decision-tree taxonomy that lets a student locate their own position on exactly how precise the text of a criminal statute must be before the state may enforce it. The handout should sort people by the claims and cases they actually endorse, not by the position they think they hold, so no node may be phrased as a self-description such as whether the student sides with fair notice or with the prevention of arbitrary enforcement; each node must instead present a concrete scenario, or a sharply worded claim, answerable without knowing the theory. Use as raw material specimen statutes on the model of a law punishing annoying conduct in a public space, a corruption statute whose operative term is left undefined, a sentence enhancement triggered by a judge's judgment that a prior crime is of roughly the same type as burglary or arson, and a loitering ordinance police use to clear a handful of people from a park. Label every branch with the claim or judgment it actually tests: for example, whether the decisive defect is that the law fails to tell citizens what is forbidden or that it transfers the real decision to police and prosecutors; whether a court's narrowing construction can cure the defect, and for whom; whether a term with an established common-law meaning can be acceptably open-textured; and whether the required precision must be found in the statutory text or may be supplied by the surrounding legal culture.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers first and locate the actual positions within that space afterwards, rather than working from a received list of labels. I would like six to nine diagnostic questions, with a one-page flowchart as the intended output. The tree should end with at least the following leaves, though you may add others if the space supports them: a plain-meaning view on which precision is measured by what an ordinary reader can extract from the text alone; an anti-arbitrariness view on which the decisive question is whether the statute hands standardless power to police and prosecutors, so that vagueness is judged facially and primarily by the risk of selective enforcement; a common-law-gloss view on which indefinite moral language is acceptable when a stable interpretive tradition supplies content, so that vagueness should usually be assessed as applied rather than facially; and a rule-of-lenity view on which imprecision is handled by resolving construction in the defendant's favor rather than by invalidating the statute. For each leaf, give it a name, or mark it unnamed if it lacks one, and add one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment, the problem it must solve by stipulation or the case it struggles hardest to handle.

Also mark the combinations of answers your tree permits but no one in fact occupies, and say for each empty cell whether it is unoccupied because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive on reflection, or because it is merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, for instance if a plain-meaning position collapses into the common-law-gloss position once its holder is pressed on what the ordinary reader imports into the statutory language. Do not include a bibliography or case citations; each diagnosis should stand on the description of the scenario itself. The finished product should be usable directly as an instructor's key from which a student-facing flowchart can be drawn.
