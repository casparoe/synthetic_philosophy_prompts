After a career of nearly forty years in the natural sciences, I have lately taken up moral philosophy, and I have been trying to give an account of what separates manipulation from legitimate persuasion. My working view is that an influence is manipulative when it meets four conditions: (1) the influencer deliberately aims to bring about a particular decision or behavior; (2) the influence operates mainly through channels other than the target's assessment of evidence and reasons—through fear, social conformity, flattery, priming, or the exploitation of known biases; (3) the influence is less than fully transparent, in the sense that if the target understood in advance how the message had been designed to move them, much of its force would be lost; and (4) the influence obscures options or trade-offs that a fair presentation would reveal, or else runs against the target's own considered interests. I suspect these conditions may need amendment, and a practical matter from my retirement has given me reason to test them.

Since retiring, I have served as an unpaid scientific advisor to a county health board that is designing a campaign to raise shingles vaccination rates among residents over sixty. The communications officer has proposed three elements. First, leaflets would display large photographs of severe shingles rashes alongside short accounts of patients who developed chronic nerve pain. Second, a letter would be sent to unvaccinated residents, identified through pharmacy records, saying that according to county records their vaccination record is incomplete and that the vaccine is due. Third, posters in pharmacies would state that the large majority of people over sixty in the county have already completed the vaccination. The aim is plainly beneficent, and each particular assertion in the materials is, strictly speaking, true. Yet the shape of the campaign—its use of graphic fear, social comparison, and a letter that presses on an administrative incompleteness—leaves me uneasy. Worked through my four conditions, would you count this campaign as manipulation? If your answer turns on only one or two of the conditions, or finds the conditions themselves at fault, what would you revise, and why?
