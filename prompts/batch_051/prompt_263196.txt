Consider the increasingly widespread practice of convening citizens' assemblies, deliberative polls, and other mini-publics to inform or make policy recommendations. These bodies typically bring together a representative sample of ordinary citizens, present them with expert testimony, and then facilitate structured group deliberation in the hope of producing better-grounded public judgements. What view of democratic inquiry does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that political knowledge is something experts already possess and must simply be transmitted to citizens, or does it presuppose that judgement emerges from the citizens' own collaborative activity? To sharpen the question, work from this passage in John Dewey's *Democracy and Education*:

"The problem is to extract the desirable traits of forms of community life which actually exist, and employ them to criticize undesirable features and suggest improvement. Now in any social group whatever, even in a gang of thieves, we find some interest held in common, and we find a certain amount of interaction and cooperative intercourse with other groups. From these two traits we derive our standard. How numerous and varied are the interests which are consciously shared? How full and free is the interplay with other forms of association?"

Using Dewey's two criteria—breadth of shared interests and fullness of free interchange with other groups—evaluate what the mini-public format presupposes about democratic judgement. Does the format implicitly treat the wider public's interests as already fixed and merely awaiting articulation, or as something that must be formed and tested through inquiry? Does it treat the assembly's conclusions as provisional findings in a wider social inquiry, or as a superior substitute for that inquiry? Finally, identify one concrete design feature of such bodies that would have to change if one accepted Dewey's experimentalism—the view that democracy is itself a form of social inquiry whose results are always open to revision—and say why.
