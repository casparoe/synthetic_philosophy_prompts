i've been treating my philosophy of language professor's in-class rulings as a labeling function and trying to reverse-engineer it, like you would with a black-box classifier. every week she gives us a sentence and rules on whether saying it counts as doing the thing the sentence names. so far:

yes (saying it does the thing):
- 'i promise to repay you tomorrow'
- 'i bet you five dollars it rains'
- 'i name this ship the albatross'
- 'i hereby declare the meeting closed'
- 'i warn you that the bridge ahead is out'
- 'i apologize for the delay'

no (saying it doesn't do the thing):
- 'i persuade you that the defendant is guilty'
- 'i convince you that the defendant is guilty'
- 'i frighten you'
- 'i surprise you'
- 'i bore you'

next week she's going to rule on: 'i am sorry for your loss', 'i insult you', 'i understand', 'i am threatening you', 'i forgive you', and 'i dare you to jump'. predict her verdicts on those. more importantly, what rule is she actually running? i can think of at least three that fit the labels so far — something about whether the verb passes the 'hereby' test, something about whether the act is done in the saying rather than by the saying, and something about whether the utterance changes a normative status — and they come apart on the new cases in ways i can't fully straighten out. wich of the new cases would best tell those rules apart, and which are just noise?
