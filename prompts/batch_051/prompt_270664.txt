Heres something I've been turning over. Switzerland votes on referendums constantly, sometimes on pretty technical stuff; California has ballot propositions that are usually drafted by consultants or campaigns and bundle unrelated things into a single yes/no; and Brexit was one binary vote on an enormous question. In all three, the result gets treated as if "the people have spoken," with a kind of democratic authority ordinary legislation doesn't have. But the same institution seems to say two different things at once. On the one hand it treats the popular will as a real, pre-existing thing that the vote simply reveals and that settles the matter. On the other hand it treats the will as something manufactured by the ballot itself: everyone knows the wording of the question, or the order of votes, or how the options are framed can flip the result; the same electorate will vote for lower taxes and then effectively demand more services; and courts or parliaments sometimes override or quietly hollow out a referendum result afterwards. So the will is sovereign, except when it isnt.

What picture of democracy, or of what a public is, does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory and be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then tell me what would actually have to change about how referendums are run if we dropped the "will of the people" idea and treated a referendum as just a device for counting and aggregating individual preferences.

And please write it as continuous prose, like an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
