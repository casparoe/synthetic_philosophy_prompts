I'm in an upper-level seminar on critical thinking as a moral value — the syllabus basically asks whether doubt is a duty — and I've been trying to pin down my own view for the final paper. The course has pulled me in a few directions at once, so I'd love a consistency audit before I committ to any of this.

Here's where I've landed. (1) I'm a doxastic involuntarist: I can't just decide to believe something the way I can decide to raise my hand, and I don't think anyone else can either. (2) I accept ought-implies-can, at least as a rough constraint; it seems unfair to say someone violated a duty if the thing wasn't in their control. (3) But I also think there are genuine epistemic duties. My clearest case is a juror who convicts while deliberately ignoring exculpatory evidence — I want to say they've done something wrong, not merely that their belief is inaccurate or irrational. (4) I'm drawn to something like Clifford's principle: it's wrong to believe on insufficient evidence. (5) I also think the amount of evidence you need before you may believe something goes up with the moral stakes of being wrong. Believing on a casual glance that my train is probably on time is fine; believing on the same casual glance that my coworker has been embezzling is not. (6) But I'm also convinced that being a good friend sometimes requires a kind of epistemic partiality: if a close friend is accused of something and the evidence is mixed but tilts against them, I don't just think it's okay to say I'll stand by them — I think it can be admirable to actually believe them, or at least to let the friendship shape what you believe rather than only what you do. I don't think that's a regrettable bias, and I'm suspicious of any view that makes doubt so central a duty that it would call this irrational.

What I can't tell is which of these are actually in tension and which only look that way. Could you go through the pairs, rank the tensions by how serious they are, and say what the cheapest fix would be in each case? I'd rather not be told to just drop something without hearing what it costs.
