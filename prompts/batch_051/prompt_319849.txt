I'm a TA for an upper-level undergrad course on free will. Here's a midterm question and a student's answer. Give the answer a grade out of 10 — up to 4 points for the reconstruction of the challenge, 3 for the first reply, 3 for the second — and write the point-by-point comments that justify the grade, not just a general impression. The student is fluent and the surface is mostly right, so focus on subtle steps that don't actually follow.

Question: State the strongest version of the challenge that Libet's clock experiments pose to the claim that conscious intentions cause our actions. Then evaluate two common replies: (A) what the experiments measure is an urge or wish, not a decision or intention; (B) subjects' ability to veto the prepared movement preserves free will. How far does each reply get a defender of conscious free will?

Student answer:

Libet's setup had subjects watch a fast-rotating clock and note its position at the moment they first felt the urge to flex their wrist, while EEG recorded the readiness potential (RP). The RP began around 550 ms before the movement; the reported time of first awareness (W) came around 200 ms before. So the brain was already getting ready to move roughly 350 ms before the subject was aware of intending to move. The strongest challenge is: since the relevant brain activity precedes conscious intention, the conscious intention cannot be what initiates the action. The brain triggers the action unconsciously, and the conscious intention is a late side affect, or at best the conscious mind ratifies a process already underway. That threatens the ordinary idea that conscious willing causes action.

Reply A says the W time measures only an urge or wish to move, not a full decision or intention; the real decision could have been made earlier and only the urge surfaces at W. The distinction is real, but the reply is weak: nothing in the data shows an earlier conscious decision, so the defender has to posit an unmeasured conscious event to save the theory, which is basically ad hoc.

Reply B says Libet also found that subjects could still veto the prepared movement in the last 100–200 ms before it fired, and since the veto stops the act, free will survives as the power to refrain rather than to initiate. This is the stronger reply because it gives conscious will a real causal role, and we know the veto is conscious because subjects reported deciding to veto.

Overall, reply A only softens the challenge, but reply B answers it: even if initiation is unconscious, the veto is consciously made and free, so free will is preserved.
