A friend and I spent the afternoon arguing about whether human intentionality could be original rather than derived, and he left me with a version of Dennett’s two-bitser objection to Searle that I cannot get out of my head. Let his reconstruction stand as follows.

1. A coin-slot’s registering of a quarter is clearly derived intentionality: it is about the coin only because its designers intended it to be.
2. If human intentionality were original, then at some point in our ancestry original aboutness would have to appear without being borrowed from any prior intentional state.
3. But our cognitive capacities were produced by natural selection, which has no prior intentional states of its own.
4. Therefore human intentionality is, like the coin-slot’s, derived — yet derived from a process that itself has no original intentionality.
5. So the distinction between original and derived intentionality collapses: either original intentionality is simply posited without explanation, or all intentionality is as-if intentionality, varying only in complexity.
6. This conclusion seems to prove too much, because it treats the difference between a person’s meaning something and a coin-slot’s sensitivity to metal as a difference of degree rather than kind.

I do not accept the conclusion, but I cannot find the misstep without giving up something plausible. Which step or inference should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it commit one to in the original/derived debate?
