I have been working through philosophical accounts of mental content that try to ground a state's meaning in its history — in what the state was selected or learned to do — rather than in an observer's interpretation. The accounts I have in mind say, roughly, that a state means whatever condition it was retained for detecting, where "retained for" is filled in by actual episodes of selection or reinforcement. A recurring obstacle is the following. In the circumstances where a detector was favored, the intuitively correct target condition never occurred in isolation: it was bound together with a particular pattern of proximal stimulation, with various co-present environmental features, and with the downstream uses to which the response was put. Take the familiar frog whose feeding snap is set off by a certain pattern of small, dark, moving shapes; in the settings that shaped the response, that pattern was in practice produced by flies. Or take a rodent that freezes when it hears a dry rustling; in its ancestral habitat, rustling was almost always caused by a predator moving through leaf litter, yet the identical sound could be produced by wind. The history seems unable to decide whether the state means fly or small-dark-moving-shape, predator or rustling-of-a-certain-amplitude, or instead some disjunction of everything that happened to co-occur during selection. I am not asking which answer is right. I want to know whether this precise difficulty — selection history underdetermining content because the past environment fuses several candidate conditions together — has an established name, and whether it is standardly distinguished from the adjacent worry about how a state can misrepresent, for example by firing when its selected condition is absent. If these are treated as distinct problems, or as facets of a single problem, I would like the boundary between them and the terms that attach to each.
