It feels tight, but the conclusion seems to pull the rug out from under the whole reasoning, so I suspect a trick. Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and on what reading?

(1) If a cosmological theory entails that Boltzmann brains — short-lived observers that fluctuate into existence by chance with apparent experiences exactly matching mine — vastly outnumber ordinary biological observers with those same experiences, then, other things being equal, my evidence favors the hypothesis that I am a Boltzmann brain over the hypothesis that I am an ordinary observer.

(2) The cosmological theories under discussion do entail that Boltzmann brains vastly outnumber ordinary observers.

(3) If I were a Boltzmann brain, I would have exactly the evidence I now have: apparent memories of a life, apparent experiments, apparent observations of the universe.

(4) I have no independent, non-question-begging reason to assign the ordinary-observer hypothesis a much higher prior probability.

(5) Therefore, given those cosmological theories, I should think I am probably a Boltzmann brain.

(6) But if I am probably a Boltzmann brain, then my apparent memories and observations — including everything that seemed to support those cosmological theories — are very likely just misleading fluctuations, not reliable records of how the universe is.

(7) So the theories undercut their own support, and I should either reject the cosmology or reject the inference in (1)–(5).
