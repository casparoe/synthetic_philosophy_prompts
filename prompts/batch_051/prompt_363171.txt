I'm in a reading group that has been working through early Christian and modern Protestant ways of reading Scripture, and two of the members have been going at each other for a year. One is basically an Origen person: he thinks a text that is only read in its plain historical sense stays stuck at the level of the letter, and that the point of exegesis is to ascend from the letter to a spiritual sense, otherwise you get fed stones instead of bread. The other is a conservative Protestant shaped by the fundamentalist-modernist debates, and he keeps saying that once you license allegory you can make the text mean whatever you want, and the authority of the actual words collapses. They have finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration, and I got assigned the drafting. They both affirm that Scripture is divinely inspired and authoritative and that it must be read for the life of the church, so that is not where the fight is. The real fight is about what the literal or historical sense is, what a spiritual sense could be, whether a passage needs some edifying meaning beyond its plain sense, and how you tell a legitimate figural reading from an arbitrary allegorical one.

Draft the statement they could both sign after good-faith wrangling. I want: a numbered list of agreed claims, phrased substantively so neither side feels it signed a hostage note; then a disagreements table giving the exact residual disagreements in wording each side would accept for thier own view; then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them; and for each crux, a concrete biblical passage or interpretive test both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please do not manufacture a fake consensus, and if you think part of the dispute is just verbal, say which part and why. Passages worth considering as crux-tests include the near-sacrifice of Isaac, the imprecatory psalms, the conquest narratives in Joshua, and the sun standing still in Joshua 10.
