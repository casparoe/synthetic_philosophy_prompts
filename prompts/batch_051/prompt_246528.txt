I want you to analyze Plimpton 322 as a case study in the question of when counting stops being mere computation and becomes mathematics. Here's the background so you don't have to go look anything up: it's a Babylonian clay tablet from around 1800 BCE, probably from Larsa, now in the Columbia collection. It has four columns and fifteen rows of numbers in sexagesimal (base-60) notation, and the rows encode Pythagorean triples — whole-number triples a, b, c with a² + b² = c² — some of them huge, like (12709, 13500, 18541). There is no statement of the Pythagorean rule on it, no diagram, no proof. What it was for is genuinely disputed: the older reading (Neugebauer and Sachs) saw it as a number-theoretic study of Pythagorean triples, while the currently more common reading (Robson, Friberg, and others) sees it as something like a teacher's aid for setting exercises about reciprocal pairs, with right-triangle problems as the likely application.

Take a reasonably precise version of the view that mathematics starts when people stop just calculating particular answers and begin treating the relationships themselves as the object of study. Run the analysis both ways. First, by the lights of that view, is Plimpton 322 doing mathematics, doing arithmetic, or sitting somewhere in between? Be disciplined about the mapping: say exactly which features of the tablet (the size of the triples, the ordering of the columns, the missing first-column digits, the absence of a theorem statement, the probable classroom use) push toward which classification, and where the theory has nothing to say. Second, turn it around: what does the tablet do to the distinction itself? Does it survive contact, or does the tablet straddle the line in a way that suggests the line between calculation and mathematics was never very sharp in the first place?

And throughout, seperate what's settled from what's actually contested. The physical facts — date, structure, the fact that the rows encode Pythagorean triples — are basically settled. The purpose of the tablet and what the scribe thought they were doing are not, and the number-theoretic reading is now widely called anachronistic. So dont present the teacher's-aid reading as consensus, but don't pretend the Neugebauer reading is dead either. Flag explicitly which parts of your analysis would change depending on which interpretation you accept.
