I teach an introduction to American literature course and am preparing a unit on Nathaniel Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter, and I want to bring the novel into contact with the philosophical literature on guilt and shame rather than teaching it only as a historical artifact. The novel looks to me like a deliberately arranged natural experiment: Hester Prynne's transgression is made public and answered with communal shame — the letter, the scaffold, the crowd's gaze — while Arthur Dimmesdale's parallel transgression is kept private and internalized, where it becomes self-laceration, illness, and eventually a confession that kills him. But the moral arithmetic is not obvious. Hester survives her shame, partly converts it into a public identity and a form of service, and earns a wary kind of respect, while Dimmesdale's hidden guilt devours him. At the same time, Hawthorne never lets the town's shaming look like anything other than cruel and self-righteous, and the letter continues to mark Hester to the end.

I would like a reading of the novel through the distinction between guilt and shame, and through the older anthropological thought that a culture might be organized primarily around one or the other — shame as external and social, a matter of one's standing before others; guilt as internal and private, tied to the wrong done rather than to the whole self. What, on your reading, is Hawthorne claiming about which of these is the better moral emotion, and about whether a culture can choose between them? Does the novel bear out the familiar modern verdict that guilt is the more mature, more moral emotion and shame the crude instrument of the crowd, or does it complicate that verdict by showing that Hester's public shame, precisely because it is finite and externally discharged, is more survivable than Dimmesdale's endless private guilt? Or is the real operative distinction something else — confession versus concealment, or the difference between a self that can withstand exposure and one that cannot? I would also like to know which scenes you would send a student to, where the guilt-shame lens genuinely illuminates the book, and where an honest critic has to admit that the novel resists it and shows the costs of both emotions instead of ranking them. End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the standard contrast tends to leave out.
