Ok so in my school philosophy club we have been going back and forth about the knowledge account of assertion, which is the idea that you should only flat-out assert something if you know it. My friend gave an argument against it that seems to prove way too much, and now I cant figure out which step to blame.

(1) If the knowledge account is right, then you shouldn't flat-out assert that p when you don't know that p.
(2) You don't know that your lottery ticket lost. Say the drawing already happened but you haven't checked, and your only reason is that your ticket is one of 14 million and only one wins, so there is a 1-in-14-million chance it won.
(3) So by (1), you shouldn't flat-out assert that your ticket lost.
(4) But that is absurd. If a friend asks whether they should even bother checking their ticket, saying no, your ticket lost is a totally normal and fine thing to say. Someone who answered actually you don't know that would just be annoying, not deep.
(5) If (2) is right, the same thing applies all over the place: the doctor with a 99.9 percent accurate test doesn't know the patient is negative, the forecasters don't know it will rain tomorrow, nobody knows the plane won't crash. So the knowledge account would rule out most ordinary assertion.
(6) If you try to fix that by saying knowledge doesn't require a zero chance of error, then the only difference between the lottery case and those other cases is how tiny the chance is. And any line between 1-in-100, 1-in-a-million, and 1-in-14-million looks arbitrary.
(7) So since (4) is the thing I am least willing to give up, something earlier has to go. But I can't tell whether the real problem is (1), (2), or (6), and I don't know what each option would cost.
