I keep tripping over the same inconsistency in how we talk about work, and I want someone to either dissolve it or show me the principle. Take the 35-hour week: when French unions defend it, chunks of the English-language press treat it as laziness with a legal stamp, as if those workers are cheating at a game everyone else is forced to play. Take karoshi, death from overwork in Japan: the same outlets will run a somber piece about how no one should be worked to death, that it's a structural pathology. So too little work is a moral failing, and too much work is a tragedy. But a founder or a novelist doing 80-hour weeks all summer is called passionate, devoted, doing their life's work. And a trust-fund kid who turns his hobby into a three-day-a-week career is called a freelancer, while a single parent on benefits working the same three days is called dependent. Same hours either way; the only things that change are who's choosing the schedule and who's signing the paychecks. There's a leftover Protestant-work-ethic voice in my head saying work is virtuous and idleness is sin, and a modern voice saying the whole point of rising productivity is that we should all work less, and neither one explains why the judgments cut the way they do. I've written versions of all these stories, and I honestly can't tell wether I'm applying a principle or just a class reflex. Give me the strongest justification you can find for judging identical hours differently across those cases — autonomy, consent, ownership, contribution, meaning, whatever works — run it through the 35-hour week, karoshi, the founder's all-nighters, and the benefit claimant's three days, and tell me which part of the asymmetry, if any, I should actually keep. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question.
