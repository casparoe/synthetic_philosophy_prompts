I have come to treat a generalized version of the Principal Principle as close to the whole truth about how objective chance should constrain rational credence. In the form I accept: suppose a rational agent learns that the objective chance of a proposition A at time t is x, where that chance assignment is lawlike, and suppose further that the agent has no evidence that lets them outpredict the announced chance—no foresight of the outcome, no information about how the chance assignment was produced, no correlation with a better predictor. Then the agent's rational credence in A, conditional on everything else they know, should be x. On this view, every apparent case in which a rational agent deviates from a known chance is either a case of ordinary misinformation or a case of what I will call inadmissible information.

I want a clear-eyed map of what would count against this position, not just what critics would say. What would a committed defender of the principle have to concede counts against it, and which concessions would be fatal, merely awkward, or merely logically possible? In particular, please work through the following stress tests and say for each whether it is a live challenge, a misdescribed version of inadmissible information, or a case the principle can absorb without real cost:

(a) A chance setup that assigns a positive probability to futures in which the agent rationally concludes that the setup was biased, so that the assignment, when fully spelled out, authorizes beliefs that conflict with the assignment itself.

(b) A forecasting system that is well calibrated and consistently beats the known chance for the event in question, even though the agent has no information about its mechanism. If rationality requires the agent to favor the forecaster's distribution, does that refute the principle, or does it only show that the known chance was not the operative one?

(c) A deterministic world in which there are no nontrivial objective chances, yet rational credence built from partial information obeys the same formal rules. If the principle holds in such a world merely as a theorem about coherent updating, what is left of its claim to be a substantive constraint involving chance?

(d) A world in which objective chance is not one natural quantity but several—single-case quantum chance, statistical-mechanical probability, evolutionary frequency—each with its own update behavior. If the principle survives only by switching among these as convenience demands, is that a defeat or a clarification?

End with an overall verdict: how exposed does the principle actually turn out to be, and has the standard strategy of classifying recalcitrant information as inadmissible insulated it to the point of unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a reasonable price for the principle's generality, or a reason to stop treating it as the whole story about chance and credence?
