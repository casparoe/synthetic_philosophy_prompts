I keep coming back to the Senate nuclear option as a Nomic problem, and I want someone to actually work the analogy instead of just comparing things. I know the basic history — in 2013 the Democrats, tired of Obama's nominees being filibustered, used a ruling from the chair to reinterpret what the cloture rule required, so that a simple majority could confirm most nominees; in 2017 the Republicans did the same thing for Supreme Court nominees. So don't rehash the story. What I want is a careful mapping: treat the Senate as a Nomic-like game, the kind where the rules are themselves moveable pieces, where changing a rule is a legal move, and where the interesting patology is when someone changes the very rule that says how rules change. What counts as the "constitutional" level rule here (the one that's not supposed to be amendable by ordinary moves), what actually got changed in 2013 and 2017, and was the change a move made inside the old rules or a change of the old rules by something outside them? Then run it the other way and be honest about where the Nomic model fails: there was no neutral umpire, the minority's incentives shifted, and party discipline did a lot of the work the formal rules allegedly did. And tell me what the episode teaches about self-amending rule systems generally. Does the Senate show that a self-amending ruleset always collapses into "whatever the current majority can get away with," or does it show the opposite — that self-amendment survives because the extra-rule costs of using the amendment machinery are what actually restrain people? I'd rather you flag the Senate procedure details you're unsure about than pretend.
