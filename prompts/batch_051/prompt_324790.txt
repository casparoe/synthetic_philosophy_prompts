I'm a novelist, and I've gone down a rabbit hole over the old Clifford/James fight about whether you're ever entitled to believe without sufficient evidence, because I'm writing a character who keeps betting her life on gut conviction and I wanted the philosophy right. What strikes me is how stuck the debate is: everyone can rehearse Clifford's shipowner, everyone can rehearse James's point that some life-deciding choices you dont get to make twice, and still neither side moves an inch. Cliffordians say belief is never private and insufficient evidence always corrupts your habits and your community; Jamesians say that rule quietly favors caution, because for live, forced, momentous choices the evidence never arrives in time and refusing to choose is itself a risky choice. Its not that either side is ignorant of the other's examples. My working theory is that they're doing two different jobs: Clifford is giving a backward-looking ethics of belief for claims you could have checked, James is giving a forward-looking ethics of commitment for decisions you can't wait on. But maybe that's too tidy, and underneath there's a real disagreement about whether belief is voluntary or whether practical stakes can lower an epistemic bar. I want a diagnosis, not a survey or a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, competing bedrock intuitions? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, an empirical finding about belief control, a formal result, or is the thing just stuck?
