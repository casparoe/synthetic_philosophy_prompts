I serve on the planning committee for a joint evening of remembrance in a city with large Armenian and Jewish communities. For the first time, the two communities' historical societies have agreed to hold one event rather than two, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks. The room will include descendants of survivors of the Armenian genocide and of the Holocaust, along with a number of historians; some of the families agreed to attend only on the condition that nothing in the program would compare the two histories or imply that they are equivalent. I am discovering that this condition is much harder to meet than it sounds. Nearly every available sentence either folds both histories into a single general lesson about human cruelty, or quietly ranks them by scale, duration, or intention; even saying that both were terrible can begin to feel like a comparison. We are not claiming the two histories are the same, and we are not claiming one was worse.

Please write the opening remarks, no more than 400 words. The speech should honor each history in its particularity without leaning on dates or statistics, and the refusal to compare should do the actual structural work — determining what the speech says, what it declines to say, and how it ends — rather than being announced once and then set aside.
