I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I read Bertrand Russell's "On Denoting" on a writers' forum recommendation and this passage keeps hooking me: "Suppose now we wish to interpret the proposition, 'I met a man'. If this is true, I met some definite man; but that is not what I affirm." That is exactly the deal I make with a reader three paragraphs into a story. "A man walked through the gate" records an existential—there was a man, I'm not promising to produce which one, and the narrative may abandon him; "the man walked through the gate" tells the reader a particular is already on the ledger and their attention should hold me to it. Is this a real structural mapping to Russell's analysis, or just a novelist's metaphor dressed in quantifier talk? If it's real, what has to be true about how readers track referents for the distinction to carry over, and what would Russell's account predict about prose that turns out false—for instance, why do readers accept "the man" when no man has been introduced, and is that fiction breaking the machinery rather than using it?
