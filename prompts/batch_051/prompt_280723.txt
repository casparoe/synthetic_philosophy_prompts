Last night I had a long argument with a friend, and I have been turning it over ever since. He ended up defending a general claim: that no rule setting out the procedure for changing rules can itself be validly changed. His reasoning was as follows. Suppose a rule states how the rules under its authority may be altered. If that rule is applied to itself, then the proposed change is circular, because the very rule being changed is being used to authorize its own alteration. But if the rule is not applied to itself, then changing it would require some prior rule specifying how amendment rules may themselves be amended; and that prior rule raises the same question all over again, and so on without end. So, he concluded, we are left with either circularity or an infinite regress, and neither can make the change legitimate.

The concrete case that set this off is not hypothetical. I help run a membership organization whose bylaws say that any bylaw may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members, and we now need to amend that very provision. My friend insists this cannot validly be done. I have a strong sense that he is treating every regress as though it were fatal, when only some regresses actually undermine the view that generates them, but I could not articulate the distinction. Is he right about the bylaw? More generally, how should one think about when an infinite regress of this sort genuinely destroys a rule or practice, rather than merely being something a coherent system can absorb?
