Take Mary's room, but give Mary, while she is still confined, a complete mathematical model of her own brain and body — a set of equations she fully understands, not a conscious duplicate — and enough computing power to run it forward in time. She uses it to predict every physical detail of the day she will leave the room and first see a red rose: the retinal events, the neural cascades, the exact words she will say, and the high probability she will assign to the thought that she has just learned something new about what red looks like. She trusts the model completely, memorizes the prediction, and writes a note to her future self saying that this feeling of novelty will be an illusion and should be discounted. On the day she leaves, the prediction is correct down to the last detail. What should the knowledge argument, the ability hypothesis, and the view that phenomenal knowledge is just old facts under new concepts each say about this case: does Mary know something new when she sees red, and if so when did she come to know it? Which of those standard positions is most damaged by the case, and should the released Mary trust her own sense of having learned something new, given that she predicted that very sense in advance? Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
