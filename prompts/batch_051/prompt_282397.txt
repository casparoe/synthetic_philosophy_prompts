My half-formed idea is about strong longtermism and the cluelessness objection.

The leading defenses of strong longtermism distinguish an axiological claim — that, from a sufficiently impartial and temporally neutral evaluative standpoint, expected effects on the far future dominate the expected value of our choices — from a deontic claim — that many agents are therefore required or at least strongly permitted to prioritize interventions aimed at the long-run trajectory over near-term causes. The transition from the axiological claim to the deontic claim relies on expected-value reasoning: even a small probability of influencing astronomical numbers of future lives appears to swamp near-term certainties. But that transition presupposes that we can form probability estimates, however rough, about how present actions ramify into the far future. The cluelessness problem threatens exactly this: for many actions, the long-run consequences are not just hard to estimate but effectively opaque. I suspect the standard responses to cluelessness — settling for robust rather than precise probability judgments, or arguing that we know the sign of an effect without knowing its magnitude — were motivated mainly by near-term charitable choices and do not carry over cleanly to longtermist interventions, because the relevant causal chains are longer, more contingent, and more sensitive to the choices of intervening agents. My aim would be to show that cluelessness is not a generic complication for consequentialism but a specially severe problem for the deontic version of strong longtermism, while leaving the axiological version largely untouched.
