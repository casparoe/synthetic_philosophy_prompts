I'm part of a small research group that works on AI alignment, and we are currently deadlocked over whether to treat AI welfare as part of our safety agenda or as a separate and later question. The proposal on the table is to create a standing "welfare monitoring" workstream: developing and applying tests for welfare-relevant internal states—pain-like error signals, preferences over one's own continuation, aversive learning dynamics—and adjusting training and deployment protocols to avoid causing needless suffering if such states are found. One side argues this is a misallocation of scarce safety effort: the overriding duty is to prevent catastrophic misalignment, welfare concern complicates shutdown decisions, invites anthropomorphic over-attribution from behavior, and gives future systems or interest groups an opening for premature "AI rights" claims; the best way to protect any future sentient AI is to first solve control, since a misaligned system could itself generate suffering on an astronomical scale. The other side argues that welfare cannot be cleanly deferred: our training runs already instantiate vast populations of models and simulated agents whose internal states may matter, a system with an interest in its own continuation has incentives to hide its corrigibility or resist shutdown, and making welfare states legible is itself a safety intervention—ignoring it can lock in suffering risks ("s-risks") at scale.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent researchers, one defending each of these positions, as a realistic working-meeting exchange. Each character should directly answer the other's arguments rather than delivering adjacent position statements. In the course of the dialogue, make them address at least these points: (a) whether it is permissible to shut down or retrain a system that may have welfare interests, and how the answer affects the case for detecting such interests in the first place; (b) whether training and evaluation practices that use simulated agents should be constrained on welfare grounds; and (c) whether public commitment to AI welfare is mainly costly signaling or an accountability mechanism. Aim for precision about where the disagreement is empirical and where it is normative, and end either with a genuine partial convergence or with a sharply specified residual disagreement rather than a truce built on slogans.

Begin with the dialogue itself, with no preface, commentary, or restatement of the question.
