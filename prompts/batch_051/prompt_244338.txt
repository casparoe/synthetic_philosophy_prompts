I'm working on a piece about a weird split I keep running into when I talk to economists, and I'd like some help getting the argument right. Some of them will tell you, completely straight-faced, that utility isn't a thing inside people's heads — it's just a compact way of summarizing what they choose, nothing more. Others talk about utility as if it's obviously about welfare, satisfaction, how well a life is going, and they think choices can get it wrong. I gather this is partly a hangover from the old Benthamite idea that people pursue pleasure and avoid pain, and partly a reaction against it — Samuelson and company trying to make economics about observable behavior instead of introspection.

What I want is a dialogue between two characters who actually defend these positions. One is a micro theorist who holds the revealed-preference line: utility is just an index constructed from choices, and any talk of 'real' utility is metaphysics. The other is a behavioral or welfare economist who thinks that move hollows the concept out, because then you can't ever say someone chose badly, and welfare economics loses its point. Set it at a conference happy hour after a talk on using choice data to measure wellbing, and make them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other. I especially want them to wrestle with the addiction case: if someone keeps choosing to smoke, did they reveal that smoking is what they prefer, and does that settle anything about their welfare? Each character should be genuinely committed to their view and push the other on the hardest implications, not just lay out the options and leave it there. And if you had to say, by the end, which character has the stronger case, commit to that and say why — don't just declare it a tie. No citations or reading lists — I want the arguments themselves, not a syllabus.
