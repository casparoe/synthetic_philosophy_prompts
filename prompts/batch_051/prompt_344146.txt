My roommate and I stayed up way too late last night arguing about whether our student society should use ranked ballots to pick next year's campaign issue, and he ended up trying to convince me that the whole problem of group decision-making is basicly hopeless. I can't shake the argument but the conclusion seems way too strong, so I want to know where it comes apart. Here's roughly how he laid it out:

(1) Suppose a group has to pick one winner from three or more options, and each member has a strict ranking of the options. A voting rule is any rule that takes those rankings and returns a single winner, provided it's not rigged to pick some fixed option regardless of what everybody says (so it's onto: every option could win under some profile).
(2) The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says any such rule is either dictatorial (some fixed member's top choice always wins) or manipulable: there is at least one profile where at least one voter can get a better outcome by reporting a ranking other than their true one.
(3) But a manipulable rule doesn't really aggregate what people want. If voters have reason to lie, the winner ends up reflecting their lies, or their guesses about each other, not there actual preferences, so the outcome has no claim to be the group's choice.
(4) Therefore the only rules that honestly track people's preferences are dictatorial ones.
(5) So any fair, non-dictatorial group decision among three or more options—majority rule with a plurality winner, Borda, instant runoff, whatever—is either secretly strategic or not really fair.
(6) But that's absurd: committees and juries and our little student society do choose among three-plus options all the time, and it isn't all a sham. Some of those choices are pretty reasonable.

By the end he was saying that choice by majority is basically a myth, and that we either have to accept real-world voting as theater or stop pretending groups have preferences at all. I'd most like to keep (1) and (6); other than that I genuinely can't tell whether the problem is a mistaken premise, a bad inference, or a misreading of the theorem, and I don't know what I'd have to give up either way. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
