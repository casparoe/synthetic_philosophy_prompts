I'm trying to write something about *Children of Men* and antinatalism for a small film club, and I keep going back and forth on whether the movie is basically a 2-hour refutation of Benatar or an accidental case study for him. The bit from *Better Never to Have Been* that's stuck in my head is about prospective beneficence: "it is strange to mention the interests of a potential child as a reason why we decide to create them, and it is not strange to mention the interests of a potential child as a reason why we decide not to create them." That line seems to cut pretty hard against the film's whole emotional register—Kee's baby is treated as a reason to create more, as if the absent happy children of the future are a loss. But Benatar would say their absence isn't bad because there's no one to be deprived.

I want a committed reading, not a list of possible interpretations. Read *Children of Men* through the prospective beneficence asymmetry: where does Cuarón's visual style actually track Benatar's point (e.g., the pre-birth world as grim but not morally worse for the unborn, the emphasis on suffering among the living), and where does the film make a genuine counterargument (e.g., the baby as a public good, the role of hope among existing people) that Benatar's framework mishandles? Does the film, read closely, end up testifying for or against the asymmetry? I'd rather you defend one take and flag its weak spots than give me a 50/50.
