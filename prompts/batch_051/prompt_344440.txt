I am a retired natural scientist — my training was in experimental physics — and I have spent the past few years reading philosophy of science. A question keeps bothering me about chaos theory, specifically about strange attractors and the butterfly effect, and I would like a careful treatment.

A colleague of mine insists on two things. First, that when a deterministic system is chaotic, its unpredictability is a property of the system itself and not merely a limitation of our instruments or models. Second, that one can in principle decide empirically, from a recorded time series alone, whether an irregular process is generated by a low-dimensional deterministic attractor rather than by a genuinely stochastic process.

I would like you to set out the strongest empirical test of those two claims. I want a concrete protocol: what you would measure or compute from the data — a Lyapunov exponent estimated from delay-coordinate embeddings, a surrogate-data comparison, a dimension estimate, forecasting performance, or something better — and I want you to say what result would favor the deterministic-chaos reading and what result would favor the stochastic reading. Then, just as important, I want an honest account of what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, does any such test separate unpredictability from indeterminism? And can any signature in a finite, noisy record license the inference from "this sequence is unpredictable for us, because small initial errors are amplified" to "this sequence is intrinsically unpredictable"? If the operational tests take us only part of the way, mark the exact point at which the empirical evidence gives out and interpretation takes over.
