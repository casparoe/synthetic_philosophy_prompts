I'm putting together a little philosophy of math reading group, and two of us have been arguing for months about whether the sentence "2 is an element of 4" has a determinate truth value. One camp is structuralist: numbers are just positions in a structure, so membership questions that depend on a particular set-theoretic coding are like asking whether the knight in chess weighs more than the bishop—the question doesn't make structural sense. The other camp is a hardline von Neumann reductionist: the finite von Neumann ordinals aren't just one coding among others, they're what the naturals are, so 2 = {∅,{∅}} and 4 = {0,1,2,3}, and 2 ∈ 4 is plainly true. We've finally agreed to try an adversarial collaboration rather than another year of talking past each other, and I want you to write the joint statement the two of them could actually sign.

Don't make it vacuous. Start with a numbered agreed-claims section: the things both camps accept about the Peano axioms, about the von Neumann vs Zermelo codings, about what ordinary arithmetic does and doesn't settle, and about what "false" vs "ungrounded" amounts to. Then do a disagreements table. In it, the residual disagreement has to be stated precisely, in wording each side would accept for it's own view—no strawmanning. Then give the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one camp. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, plus what each side predicts in advance. If part of the dispute is just verbal, say which part and why, but I suspect part of it is real.

And at the very end, add a short note on where your drafting is most likely to go wrong—which formulation is probably smuggling in one side's framing—and what argument or consideration would make you redraw the crux table.
