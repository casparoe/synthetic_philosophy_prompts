I'm a med student in a research-ethics seminar, and the instructor keeps leaning on "when in doubt, randomize." It comes up every time somebody asks why it's okay to flip a coin between two treatments instead of just giving patients whatever the doctor privately thinks is best. Last week she used the same phrase to explain why a trial could keep randomizing people after the data saftey board had already seen an interim result trending in favor of the new drug. Her move was, roughly: the board looked at the numbers and decided there was still enough doubt, so randomization stays. That bugged me, because at the start of the trial "doubt" seemed to mean something like "we honestly can't say which arm is better," but halfway through it seemed to mean something closer to "the trend wasn't convincing enough to stop." Those feel like different states wearing the same word, and I don't think she noticed the slide.

I'm not after a reading list or a history of the phrase. I want to know what precise claims "when in doubt, randomize" could actually be making. Doubt on whose part — the individual clinician treating the patient, the community of experts as a whole, the safety board, or all three? Doubt about what, exactly — which arm is better on average, which arm is better for this particular patient sitting in front of the doctor, whether the evidence has crossed some predetermined threshold?

And do the different readings have different implications for whether the mid-trial call to keep randomizing was actually justified? Lay the readings out like a menu, with verdicts on which are trivial, which are substantive but false, and which one, if any, is doing the real justificatory work. If the phrase turns out to mean half a dozen different things, I'd rather have the menu than a single gloss.
