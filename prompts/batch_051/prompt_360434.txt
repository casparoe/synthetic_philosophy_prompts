I'm a PhD student working at the messy border between Buddhist ethics and theories of well-being, and I've been trying to pin down my own view — roughly, tranquilism — before I commit it to a chapter. At its core it's just: only the absence of craving and suffering has final value; everything else matters only insofar as it conduces to that. But when I list out the things I actualy believe around it, I'm not sure they all fit. Here's the package:

1. Only the absence of craving and suffering has final value; nothing else is good in itself.
2. The craving to be rid of craving is still craving, so it isn't some innocent exception — it's inside the problem, not outside it.
3. But some cravings are self-eliminating, like the impulse to sit down and let a thought pass, and those are fine as long as they reliably remove more craving than they produce.
4. The absence has to be a felt condition of a subject. I don't think a rock is in the best possible state, even thogh it has no craving and no suffering; the good state is an experiencing subject's quiet, alert equanimity, not blankness.
5. Since only the final state matters, how someone gets there doesn't matter in itself: thirty years of practice and a well-aimed pill are equally good if they issue in the same durable felt state.
6. Yet I also think equanimity that rests on a false picture — someone calm because they believe a loving deity is literally holding everything together — is less good than the same felt calm with an accurate view, even if the feeling is identical and equally stable.
7. And I can't bring myself to say the right response to a child drowning is to feel nothing. The ache that makes you jump in seems like part of what should be there, even though it hurts.

I'd love an audit of this set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and how bad is each? Order them by severity and say what each conflict would push me to revise; then give me the cheapest fix that keeps the core intact.
