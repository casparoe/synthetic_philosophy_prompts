In my historiography seminar everyone keeps reaching for the same image when the Terror comes up: a revolution is like Saturn, the parent who devours his own children, and the French Revolution is supposed to be the perfect case — the Girondins go first, then Danton, then the Hébertists, then Robespierre and the Committee themselves, all swallowed by a process they helped set in motion. The person reaching for it is usually trying to close down the contingency reading, the one where 1792–94 is an improvised response to invasion, civil war and factional panic; but I've also heard it used against the class-struggle reading, on the grounds that the saturnine dynamic is a general law of revolutions rather than something specific to any one class.

I want to pressure-test the image. Give me a correspondence table: what maps onto the parent, the children, the act of devouring, and the detail that Saturn did it pre-emptively to stop his offspring from overthrowing him. Then go after the places where the analogy breaks, and sort the disanalogies that matter for the inevitability claim from the ones that are just cosmetic. Tell me honestly wether the conclusion — that the revolution devouring its own leadership was structuraly inevitable rather than contingent — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work.
