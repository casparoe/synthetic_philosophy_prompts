I have been trying to think through a proposal that would take the who-decides question out of democratic backsliding diagnoses. The immediate background is the familiar set of contested cases—Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States—where rival observers describe the same institutional changes either as democratic decay or as ordinary political competition that the incumbent's opponents are losing. The proposal is that a regional association of democracies would create an independent Backsliding Review Board. The Board would score every member state each quarter on a fixed, public checklist of twenty-five procedural indicators: whether constitutional courts can review executive decrees, whether public-broadcaster boards are appointed without a partisan majority, whether electoral commissions are staffed in a balanced way, whether emergency powers are time-limited, whether corruption investigations track officeholders and opposition figures at similar rates, and similar items that can be scored from statutes and administrative records rather than impressionistic judgment. Each indicator would be coded from zero to four by a professional staff of statisticians and retired judges; the weighted total would be published as a 0–100 score, along with the coding rules and the underlying records. If a state's score fell below 60 for three consecutive quarters, consequences would trigger automatically: the state would lose its vote in the association's council and development bank, its officials would become ineligible for association offices, and aid that would have gone to the national executive would be rerouted to municipal governments and civil-society organizations. The indicator list could be amended only by a four-fifths majority of member states, excluding any state currently below the 60 threshold, and states could appeal only factual coding errors, not the choice of indicators or weights.

I am not asking whether this is a good idea; I am asking how it would actually behave. What would it incentivize among incumbent governments, opposition parties, civil-society monitors, and the Board's own staff and data sources? How would a determined government game the index without changing the underlying reality—by legislating formal compliance while moving power into informal networks, by starving or pressuring the institutions that produce the underlying records, by contesting borderline codings, by capturing the appeals process, or by campaigning to rewrite the indicator list? What do the 60-point threshold and the three-quarter clock do to the politics around the score: cliff effects, timing games, an incentive to hover just above 60? Does codifying backsliding in this way settle the worry that the concept is just a partisan label, or does it simply relocate the contestation to indicator selection, weighting, and coding? And what happens when a state below the threshold is excluded from voting on the very rules that classify it—can that be justified, and can any of these failure modes be fixed without gutting the automaticity that was the point of the scheme?
