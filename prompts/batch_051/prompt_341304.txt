Malthus's *An Essay on the Principle of Population* went through six editions, and his statement of the checks on population shifted in ways that are easy to pass over. Here is one passage from the first edition, published in 1798, chapter VII:

“The power of population is so superior to the power of the earth to produce subsistence for man, that premature death must in some shape or other visit the human race. The vices of mankind are active and able ministers of depopulation. They are the precursors in the great army of destruction, and often finish the dreadful work themselves. But should they fail in this war of extermination, sickly seasons, epidemics, pestilence, and plague advance in terrific array, and sweep off their thousands and tens of thousands. Should success be still incomplete, gigantic inevitable famine stalks in the rear, and with one mighty blow levels the population with the food of the world.”

In his later, revised presentation of the principle, Malthus names the checks on population as moral restraint, vice, and misery, with moral restraint placed first.

These two passages seem to pull in different directions. The 1798 passage makes the check on population sound like something that happens to people from outside: vice, epidemic, plague, famine. The later statement includes moral restraint, something a person undertakes voluntarily before the fact, as a check alongside the others. Is this a genuine contradiction, or a change in the theory, or only a change in emphasis? Can the two be reconciled by distinguishing between different kinds of check, for example preventive and positive checks, or by reading the first passage as a description of what happens when moral restraint fails? What did the insertion of moral restraint actually do to the theory: did it make the account more humane without changing the underlying mechanism, or did it introduce an elastic category that made the theory harder to falsify, because any postponement of catastrophe could be attributed to restraint? Give the candidate reconciliations and then say which one is most faithful to the whole structure of Malthus's argument across the editions, rather than special pleading.

If you think the apparent conflict is an artifact of asking two passages to do two different jobs—for instance, because the 1798 passage is deliberately isolating the positive checks while the later statement is giving the full taxonomy—say so plainly, and then address the substantive question behind the reconciliation: whether the addition of moral restraint in the later editions was a real theoretical change or merely a clarification of what the first edition already implied.
