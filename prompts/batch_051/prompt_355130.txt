I work as a backend engineer who spends most of my time on distributed systems and reliability, and I have no formal background in philosophy. I recently came across the outline of Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, and I cannot tell how much of it is a substantive claim about knowledge and how much is stage-setting for a famous example. As I understand it, Nozick requires that a true belief track the truth in order to count as knowledge: if the proposition had been false, the person would not have believed it, and if it had been true in slightly different circumstances, the person would still have believed it. The notorious consequence is the failure of epistemic closure: a person can know one thing, know that it entails a second, and still fail to know the second. The standard case is that you know you have hands and know that having hands entails you are not a handless brain in a vat, yet you do not know that you are not a handless brain in a vat, because in the vat world your belief-forming process would produce exactly the same verdict as it does now.

Restate this view, and especially the closure failure, in the vocabulary I actually use: health checks and synthetic monitors, false positives and false negatives, a node that cannot distinguish a crashed peer from a network partition, alerts computed over local state rather than ground truth, and test suites that only cover the code paths they actually exercise. Push the translation as far as it will honestly go, then give me the residue report. Which parts of Nozick's picture map cleanly onto the difference between the service being fine and the monitor reporting it as fine? Which parts translate only under strain, such as possible-worlds talk versus the actual, after-the-fact failure modes of a deterministic system? Which parts have no counterpart in my terminology at all, such as privileged ground truth, the subject's first-person point of view, or the normative thought that a lucky true belief is not knowledge? And in the end, is the residue a limitation of my vocabulary, or does it show that the philosophical picture is quietly assuming the external observability that real distributed systems never have?
