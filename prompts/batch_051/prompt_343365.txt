I am a journalist who covers logic and the philosophy of language, and I recently sat in on a workshop on truth and vagueness. During the discussion period, two speakers clashed over whether every statement must be either true or false. Neither was obviously the winner, and I have been turning the exchange over since. I reproduce it from my notes as faithfully as I can. Could you adjudicate it move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say which specific points each person has in hand and what, if anything, both are missing?

1. The first speaker, a semanticist, opened by saying that bivalence is a structural feature of any coherent account of assertion. His claim was that if a sentence is not true, then it is false, because to deny a sentence is simply to assert its negation.

2. The second speaker, a philosophical logician, replied that this confuses a convenient idealization with a fact about natural language. She offered two kinds of case. First, presupposition failure: a sentence about the present king of France being bald is not false in the ordinary way, because there is no present king of France for the sentence to be about. Second, borderline predication: a sentence calling a borderline-height person tall is neither true nor false, because the predicate is not sharp enough to determine a truth value.

3. The semanticist responded that the presupposition case can be handled without admitting truth-value gaps. On his view, once the sentence's hidden quantificational structure is analyzed, it expresses a false proposition; the felt defect is a matter of pragmatic infelicity, not semantic neutrality. He added that the vagueness case is only epistemic: there is in fact some sharp cutoff for tallness, even if speakers cannot locate it.

4. The logician came back that the quantificational analysis gets a false truth value only by replacing the original sentence with a different one. She said speakers can distinguish ordinary falsity from the kind of failure involved when a presupposition is missing, and that the original sentence is better described as defective rather than false. As for the epistemic treatment of vagueness, she said it simply stipulates sharp boundaries and then treats our inability to locate them as the entire phenomenon. That, she argued, does not explain why competent speakers would reject both polar verdicts rather than merely express uncertainty about which polar verdict is correct.

5. The semanticist then shifted to logic. He said that if borderline sentences are gappy, then negation, conjunction, and disjunction require a non-classical semantics, and this carries a severe cost: on the most natural three-valued treatment, a sentence of the form P or not-P will itself be gappy when P is borderline, which means abandoning the law of excluded middle as a logical truth. The logician replied that this is a feature rather than a bug: the law of excluded middle is exactly what should fail as a logical truth for vague discourse, even if it remains valid for precise mathematical discourse.
