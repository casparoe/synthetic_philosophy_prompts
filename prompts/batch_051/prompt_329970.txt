I'm a philosophy professor putting together a module on the metaphysics of computation, mostly for students who've done a fair bit of programming but no philosophy. I've been kicking around a theory about mutable state and I'd like an honest assessment before I teach it.

Here's the theory, or maybe the metaphor that got out of hand. A mutable variable is not a name for a thing; it's a name for an office, in the sense that people talk about "the president" as an office that survives while occupants come and go. When you bind x to 5, you're not baptizing an object, you're creating an office and installing 5 as its occupant. Assignment is eviction: the office stays, the occupant is replaced. Identity over time belongs to the office, not to the value. So x doesn't refer to 5 and then later refer to 6; x refers to one office throughout, which is occupied first by 5 and then by 6.

The part I want assessed is my explanation of why mutation is hard to think about. I want to say: its not change that's hard — we handle the seasons, the presidency, a growing child, fine. What's hard is that mainstream notation renders the office invisible. In `x = x + 1`, the two x's are doing different jobs: the one on the left names the office, the one on the right names its current occupant. But the syntax uses the same mark for both, so the reader has to keep an unmarked, invisible object (the office) in their head while simultaneously tracking what occupies it. Natural language usually flags this distinction in all sorts of small ways — the difference between being the president and holding the office, definite descriptions, tense, whatever. Code strips all that away, and then leaves you with one symbol that is somehow both. So on my view the difficulty of mutable state is mostly a notation-induced blindness to offices, not a deep limitation of the mind.

I know this brushes up against lvalue/rvalue, and Aristotle's point that change needs something that stays put, and probably some philosophical logic about offices and occupants. But I think the thesis is stronger than those: the cognitive difficulty of mutable state just is the difficulty of tracking an invisible office. Before I call it a theory, tell me whether it's actually explanatory or just a metaphor with a syntax example. Does it survive aliasing, object identity versus equality, pass-by-reference, and values that are themselves office-like? Where does it break? And I'd particularly like you to say what would change your mind about it — which argument or example would make you reject it rather than patch it, and where you think your own reasoning here is least secure.
