I'm a PhD student working on the governance of solar geoengineering, and I've ended up as the person who drafts briefing notes for a small independent panel that vets proposed outdoor experiments before funders will touch them. The panel has only handled a handful of cases, and its reasoning has never been written down in a way that makes its rule obvious. We now have a new application to prepare a recommendation on, and the disagreement among the panel members is getting a bit unhelpful, so I'd like a careful read of whether our own precedents actually force an answer.

Here's the track record. (1) The panel approved a marine cloud brightening trial that sprayed ordinary sea-salt aerosol over a few square kilometres of coastal ocean for two weeks, provided local inshore fishing groups were consulted and the trial could be stopped within days; it accepted that the forcing was negligible and fully reversible. (2) It rejected a lower-stratosphere injection experiment that would have released enough sulphate to produce a small but measurable regional cooling for about six weeks — and the rejection was not really on safety grounds, since the physics was treated as low-risk and reversible; most of the discussion turned on whether doing it would make actual deployment more thinkable. (3) It approved, with conditions, a high-altitude balloon test of injection hardware that released only water vapour, on the basis that the environmental effect was no worse than ordinary aircraft emissions; the conditions concerned data transparency and public notice, not the release itself. (4) It rejected a proposed multi-year programme to inject sulphate over the Arctic to slow summer sea-ice loss, on two main grounds: if the programme were interrupted, the suppressed warming would rebound sharply and, the panel thought, do more harm than no intervention; and most states and communities in the affected region had never seriously been consulted.

The new application is for a one-off release of a very small quantity of sulphate aerosol in the lower stratosphere, as a plume-tracking and microphysics measurement exercise. The release would not produce a detectable change in regional or global temperature, and there is no meaningful termination-shock risk because there is no ongoing suppression of warming to interrupt. The launching state's scientific body has approved it, and communities near the launch site have been consulted. But it would be the panel's first approval of an actual stratospheric sulphate release, and the plume will drift across a couple of international borders in trace concentrations, with no consultation of those other states.

What should the panel decide if it wants to be consistent with its own track record? Which of the four precedents is the closest analogue, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle would we be adopting with either choice? Could you structure your reply as: (1) the precedents restated, briefly; (2) candidate principles these decisions could express, at least two and ideally three; (3) what each principle says about the new application; (4) the closest precedent and why; (5) your recommended decision, with an honest note on what principle the panel is endorsing either way. Keep section 1 to a sentence or two per case; no need to be exhaustive beyond what's neccessary.
