Explain what Scanlon means by the individualist restriction in his contractualism. How does this lead him to resist aggregating the small burdens of many people to outweigh the serious burden of one person? Use the transmitter room example. The student wrote:

Scanlon’s contractualism says an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject. The individualist part is that we have to attend to the reasons of each person separately, not to the total amount of harm or benefit. In the transmitter room case, a technician is trapped and injured, and if we stop the broadcast to help him, millions of people will miss part of a game. Utilitarian reasoning would let the sum of small disappointments outweight the technician’s serious pain, treating the viewers as if they were one big person. Scanlon thinks that’s a mistake. The technician could reasonably reject a principle that says his serious pain should count for less than the aggregate frustration of a huge audience. Each viewer’s complaint is small, and Scanlon’s point is that small complaints cannot be added up to outweigh a serious individual complaint. So Scanlon’s view implies that numbers can never count. If we are choosing between saving one person and saving five from equal danger, the fact that there are five is just an aggregate fact. Since no one of the five has a stronger claim than the one, contractualism cannot say we should save the five, and a fair procedure like a coin flip would be required. That is the price of the individualist restriction.
