So I've been running a little reading group on the evolution of cooperation, and we just spent two weeks on the prisoners dilemma — the one-shot logic where defecting strictly dominates no matter what the other person does, and then the iterated version where reciprocity and the shadow of the future change everything. I want to close the last session with a poem, and I have a specific form in mind.

Write a villanelle, 19 lines, where the two refrains are (1) the defector's temptation, something in the voice of "take the payoff while the other's back is turned," and (2) the reciprocator's rule, something in the voice of "I'll match whatever move you make." Don't use those exact lines, make them better. But the form has to do real work: each refrain should return like a move in an iterated game, so the alternation enacts tit-for-tat — defect answers defect, cooperate answers cooperate — and after a single betrayal the poem should let it go rather than spiral into endless mutual punishment, which is the whole point of the forgiving strategies that survive noisy repeated games. The closing couplet, where the two refrains finally meet, should hit the reason those two lines keep encountering each other: the shadow of the future. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows Axelrod's tournament can see why tit-for-tat did well — it starts nice, retaliates in kind, forgives once the other side returns to cooperating, and is simple enough to be read — without the poem ever naming any of that. And someone who doesnt know the material should still get a good poem about the difference between meeting a stranger once and having to live with them.
