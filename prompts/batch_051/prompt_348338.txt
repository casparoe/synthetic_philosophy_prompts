I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the old problem of how evil could show up in a universe made by a good god. One angle I keep coming back too is the fall of Satan, because it seems to dodge the usual excuses: there's no body, no hunger, no trauma, no ignorance — he's an angel, he knows god, and he still rebels.

A theologian I interviewed keeps using a privation analogy. He says evil isnt a positive thing that got created; its more like how darkness isnt a substance, just a lack of light, or how a hole isnt a thing but an absence of dirt. Same with cold — an absence of heat. On his telling, god creates beings and goods, and evil enters as a falling-short, not as something god had to make. He uses this to explain why a good creator isnt responsible for evil, including Satan's fall: what god made was good (the angel, the will, the freedom), and the fall is a defect in that good thing, not a positive thing that needed creating.

I can feel the analogy doing a lot of work, but I'm not sure how far it goes. Could you audit it? In particular: what maps onto what in the darkness/cold/hole picture when you apply it to Satan's rebellion; where the mapping breaks down; whether the bits that do break matter for his conclusion (that god didn't create the evil) or are just cosmetic; and whether the analogy quietly imports something the target lacks — for instance, in physics the absence of light is just... expected, not a choice. And can an act of will, a deliberate rejection with full knowledge, really be like a mere absence? If the analogy fails, is there a better one, or should we just drop the picture and talk about it differently?
