Longtime reader of the rationalist blogs here. For the past few weeks I have been playing a classification game with a friend who works in evolutionary biology, partly to see where she stands on the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique. I describe a trait, and she files it as adaptation, spandrel/byproduct, or constraint—labels she uses confidently, though she has never said exactly how she draws the lines between them. Her record so far: the vertebrate eye, adaptation; the panda's enlarged wrist-bone 'thumb' used to strip bamboo, adaptation; the human chin, spandrel; male nipples, spandrel; the loop of the giraffe's recurrent laryngeal nerve from the head into the chest and back, constraint; feathers as flight surfaces in modern birds, adaptation; the human appendix, spandrel.

I am about to give her three new cases. First, the female orgasm, described neutrally as an arousal response whose reproductive function, if any, is disputed. Second, the mammalian middle-ear ossicles, which began as part of the jaw in our distant ancestors and now transmit sound. Third, the blind spot in the vertebrate retina, where the optic nerve passes through the photoreceptor layer.

Predict how she will file each of the three. More importantly, lay out the candidate rules that would make her existing record coherent, say which of the three new cases would cleanly separate those rules, give the best-guess verdicts that follow from your preferred rule, and name a further case that would settle whatever ambiguity remains.
