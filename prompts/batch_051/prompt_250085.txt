I’m a software engineer who builds systems for representing legal rules, and I keep hitting the problem of legal fictions—things like “the corporation is a person” or “the law presumes everyone knows the law”—which courts treat as true even though everyone involved knows they are not literally true. I want to evaluate theories of what legal fictions are, what they do, and how they fit into the rest of law, but I don’t have a formal philosophy background, so I need the criteria spelled out first.

A passage in Henry Sumner Maine’s *Ancient Law* keeps bothering me. Maine says that early law is “a habit.” If law is, at bottom, a social habit rather than a deliberate command, then a legal fiction looks like a different kind of thing: an artificial, willed device inserted into a habit. Before I read the competing accounts, I want a spec.

Give me exactly five numbered requirements that any adequate theory of legal fictions must satisfy, each one or two sentences. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp. Do not include an introduction or conclusion; just the five numbered items. Use American English.
