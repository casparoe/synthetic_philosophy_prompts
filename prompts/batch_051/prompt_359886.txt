I'm putting together a discussion guide for a working seminar of scientists and statisticians who use probability constantly but have never been forced to say what they take probability claims to be about. Please write a decision tree that sorts a respondent into whichever interpretation of probability is actually doing the work in their reasoning. The leaves must include the frequentist, the subjectivist (Bayesian/personalist), and the propensity interpretations, each carrying one sentence about its most awkward commitment. If you find a coherent position that falls outside those three — an objective, single-case, but evidential rather than physical reading of probability is the obvious candidate — add it as its own leaf and name it, rather than stretching one of the three to cover it.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names. Each internal node should be a concrete yes/no question that a thoughtful non-philosopher can answer without knowing any labels. The branches should be labeled with the claim that splits them, not with the positions they lead to. Keep the tree to at most six questions and at most six leaves, and tell me if any of the questions you tried turned out not to earn its keep.

The distinctions I care about are these, and each should do genuine separating work in the tree:
— whether probability is a feature of the world independent of anyone's beliefs, or a feature of an agent's epistemic state;
— whether a single non-repeatable event (rain tomorrow, this patient's relapse) can bear a probability, or probability attaches fundamentally to sequences or repeatable setups;
— whether hypotheses and theories can bear probabilities, or only events;
— whether two rational people with identical evidence can legitimately assign different probabilities to the same proposition, or one of them is simply wrong.

Use at least these four cases as diagnostic material: a coin whose next toss is just about to happen; a named scientific hypothesis assessed against a body of evidence; a deterministic mechanism — say, a pseudorandom number generator — whose long-run output frequencies are stable; and two trained forecasters who, given the same data, announce different probabilities for the same election result. The questions should force respondents to answer about the cases, not ask them to pick between -isms.

There are at least two defensible root splits for this space: objective-versus-subjective, and events-versus-propositions. Commit to one, and say briefly why you did not choose the other. Where your tree leaves a coherent cell unoccupied, diagnose it in one sentence: is the view there incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? If two positions end up indistinguishable by their answer pattern, flag that explicitly rather than papering it over with a question that merely rephrases the issue.

Finally, run yourself through the finished instrument. Say which leaf you land on, give your reasons, and identify the single commitment of that leaf you find hardest to live with and why you keep it anyway. I want a committed answer: take the test and stand somewhere, rather than concluding that the interpretation of probability is just a matter of taste or problem context.
