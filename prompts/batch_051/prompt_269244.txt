I cover the social science/ideas beat for a general audience, and I keep running into a version of the following argument at panels on campus culture, and I genuinely can't tell whether I'm resisting it because its bad or because I just don't like its conclusion. I'd like a surgical take on it.

(1) A moral culture should be judged by how well it lets people who have been wronged get a hearing and some kind of redress from third parties. (2) Dignity culture does this through impartial institutions, but in practice those institutions track the interests of the powerful, so a lot of wronged people get ignored. (3) Honor culture does it through reputation and self-help, but that leaves anyone without status or a credible capacity for retaliation out in the cold. (4) Victimhood culture does it by treating a claim of victimization as itself a source of moral authority, so even low-status people can mobilize public support and get a hearing. (5) And whatever gets the least powerful wronged people heard and helped is the morally best culture, full stop. (6) So victimhood culture is morally superior to honor and dignity culture.

My gut says (4) is just a highlight reel and ignores what victimhood culture does badly, but when I try to say that it comes out as 'yes, but look at these downsides,' which doesn't feel like it identifies a broken inferential step. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what would the strongest repaired version of that step be?
