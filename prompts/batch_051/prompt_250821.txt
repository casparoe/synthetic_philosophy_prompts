In my course on rational choice under uncertainty, I have become convinced that repeated multiplicative gambles should be evaluated by maximizing expected log wealth — the Kelly criterion — rather than expected wealth, and that this is not just one risk preference among others but a privileged norm: it tracks the long-run growth rate, avoids staking everything on favorable gambles that would almost surely lead to ruin, and gives a clean answer where expected-value reasoning goes wrong. I want you to test this position rather than restate it. What would have to be true for it to be false? Consider the strongest objections: that log is simply a utility function, so Kelly is just expected utility with logarithmic utility rather than a distinct kind of geometric rationality; that maximizing long-run growth can come apart from what a finite-lived agent actually has reason to care about; that the result depends on restrictive assumptions such as known probabilities, identical repeated bets, and multiplicative payoffs; and that Kelly sometimes recommends stakes that seem intuitively too large or too small. Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed Kelly or geometric-rationality theorist would themselves concede as counting against the view, and give an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a problem for a norm of this kind. Please answer in American English.
