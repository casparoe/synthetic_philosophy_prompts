The question I set was: "Define first-order and higher-order evidence, giving one example of each. Then explain what epistemic akrasia is, and evaluate the claim that an agent could be rational in believing P while simultaneously believing that her total evidence does not support P. Work through at least one concrete case in which an agent receives evidence about her own cognitive performance."

The student wrote: "First-order evidence bears directly on the truth of a proposition, while higher-order evidence bears on how likely one is to have assessed the first-order evidence correctly. For example, if a pharmacist computes a dosage, the arithmetic and the data she uses are first-order evidence about the correct dosage; the information that she has been awake for thirty hours is higher-order evidence. It does not itself say anything about the pharmacology, but it bears on how much the calculation should be trusted. Epistemic akrasia is believing P while also believing that this very belief is not supported by the evidence. It is the epistemic analogue of practical akrasia, in which one judges that one ought to act a certain way but acts otherwise. Most epistemologists hold that such a state is irrational, because rational belief requires responding to what you take your evidence to support; so a person cannot rationally believe P while believing that her evidence does not support P. Consider the pharmacist again. When she learns she is sleep-deprived, she acquires a reason to distrust her calculation, so she should lower her confidence in the dosage. But once the fact about sleep deprivation is added to her total evidence, it is itself evidence about the dosage: someone who is sleep-deprived is more likely to make arithmetic mistakes, so the probability that the calculated dosage is correct is lower than it would otherwise be. Higher-order evidence, properly understood, is therefore just a special case of first-order evidence, to be weighed with everything else in the usual way. This makes the irrationality of akrasia trivial: if the agent takes the higher-order evidence seriously, she adjusts her first-order belief, and if she does not, she is ignoring relevant evidence. Still, one restricted kind of case seems to permit akrasia. An agent may know that human beings are fallible in general and be aware that she might be mistaken, yet continue to believe P. No one thinks such awareness should force her to abandon every belief. This shows that epistemic akrasia, at least in a limited sense, is sometimes rational."
