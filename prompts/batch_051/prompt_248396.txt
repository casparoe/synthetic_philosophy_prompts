Yesterday evening a friend and I ended up in a protracted argument about what it means to understand a proof, after our seminar on the epistemology of mathematics took up the familiar claim that a student can reproduce a proof perfectly and still not understand it. My friend resisted that claim and, as we walked home, she laid out the following argument.

(1) A proof is a finite sequence of steps, each of which is an axiom, an assumption, or follows from earlier steps by an explicitly permitted rule of inference.  
(2) To understand a proof is to be able to recognize, for each step, whether that step is permitted.  
(3) Whether a step is permitted is fully determined by the axioms, assumptions, and rules of inference of the system in which the proof is given.  
(4) A student equipped with a reliable mechanical checking procedure can recognize, for each step of any proof so given, whether that step is permitted.  
(5) Therefore, such a student can recognize, for each step, whether it is permitted, and so, by (2), understands the proof.  
(6) Hence the ability to state what the proof is doing as a whole, or to explain its central idea, is not necessary for understanding it.

I have the sense that step (2) is quietly importing a procedural account of understanding, but I cannot make the objection precise, and steps (1), (3), and (4) all seem right. Is the argument valid? If it is valid but unsound, which premise fails, and what is the most defensible revision of that premise? If it is valid and sound, say why the conclusion should be accepted despite its counterintuitive sound.
