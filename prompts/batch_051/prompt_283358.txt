I'm in an ethics course and we've been doing deontology, which I liked until I realized my own verdicts on the cases don't obviously add up to anything. I started writing them down to see if there's a rule lurking underneath. They go like this.

Switching the trolley away from five people and onto one: permissible, though I'd feel awful about the one. Pushing the large man off the footbridge to stop the same trolley: wrong, and I don't even consider it close. A surgeon carving up one healthy unconsenting patient to save five patients who need organs: obviously wrong, even if the match is perfect. Bombing a munitions factory in a just war, foreseeing that some civilians in the next building will die: permissible, as long as the civilian deaths aren't wildly out of proportion to the military gain. Lying to the murderer at the door about whether my friend is inside: completely fine, I'd do it without hesitating. Breaking a promise to meet a friend for coffee because I stopped to help a stranger who collapsed: also fine. Torturing a captured terrorist to find a bomb set to kill dozens: I keep going back and forth, but if I'm honest my settled view is that torture is wrong, even if I'd probably cave and do it and then think I'd done wrong. Framing an innocent person to stop a mob that's about to kill several other innocent people: wrong, and I don't feel tempted.

The case that has me stuck is this one. I'm a prisoner, and the enemy officer says he will shoot two of my fellow prisoners unless I shoot one of them myself. I think the right answer is to refuse. But if I refuse, two people get shot instead of one, and the only difference is who's pulling the trigger. If I think killing is wrong, shouldn't I want less killing rather than more? And yet refusing feels like the only answer I can live with. I can't tell whether that's a real principle or just me refusing to get my hands dirty.

Is there one principle, or a small set, that gets all of these? I have a vague sense that it's something about intending vs. foreseeing plus something about the difference between my act and someone else's, but when I try to combine them it gets becomeing ad hoc. Please tell me what principle or principles you'd extract, how they handle each case, which of my verdicts they force me to revise, and what they say about a case I haven't listed: a self-driving car that has to choose between swerving into one pedestrian and running over five. And since I want the reasoning to be honest rather than just tidy, tell me what argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about the principle, and where your own reasoning is shakiest.
