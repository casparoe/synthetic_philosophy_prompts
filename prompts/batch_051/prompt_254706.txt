I am preparing a talk for a philosophy department colloquium and need a Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I will defend is the following.

Aumann's agreement theorem is best understood as a diagnostic consistency theorem, not as a result showing that rational agents cannot agree to disagree. The theorem says that if two agents share a prior over a common probability space, have finite information partitions, and their posterior probabilities for a given event are common knowledge, then those posteriors are equal. The common-prior assumption does all the normative work. It is not an idealization of rationality; it is a substantive empirical claim about what the agents believed before receiving their private evidence, and in real disagreements it is usually the least plausible assumption. The popular slogan that rational agents cannot agree to disagree is therefore a misreading. I argue that the theorem's legitimate function is diagnostic: when two agents have common knowledge of each other's posterior values and still differ, the correct conclusion is that at least one structural condition has failed, and the common-prior condition is normally the best candidate. The talk concludes that the theorem does not by itself favor either conciliationism or steadfastness in the epistemology of disagreement; it instead shows why the disagreement debate must be reframed around the question of whether and how shared priors are established.

My argument proceeds in four moves. First, I state the theorem precisely over a common probability space with finite information partitions. Second, I use the contrapositive to show that it says only that divergent mutually known posteriors imply the failure of at least one condition. Third, I argue that the common-prior condition is neither required by rationality nor empirically innocent, and is the assumption most likely to fail between real peers with different histories and evidence. Fourth, I conclude that appeals to the theorem in the disagreement literature typically bypass the hard question — how common priors are established — and that this bypass is what I call prior laundering.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium at a research university. Most of the room are epistemologists; roughly half work on the epistemology of disagreement and divide between conciliationist (equal-weight) views, which say discovering disagreement among competent peers should make you move substantially toward their view, and steadfast views, which say you may largely retain your view when you have reasons to trust your own reasoning. There are also two formal epistemologists who know the technical results in detail, one microeconomist who treats common-prior and no-trade arguments as standard tools, and one philosopher of artificial intelligence who wants to know whether multi-agent systems should be designed to force convergence. The conciliationists will likely hear the talk as an attack on the epistemic significance of peer disagreement; the economist may find the diagnostic reading either obvious or a misuse of the theorem; the formal epistemologists will press on the mathematics; and the AI person will ask what follows for design.

For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, what I should concede rather than defend, and what I should hold.
