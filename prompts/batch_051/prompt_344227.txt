I've been in a metaphysics seminar this term and we spent two weeks on the identity of indiscernibles, and I've come away pretty convinced by the Black spheres line: there's a possible world containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres two miles apart, and they're two things, not one, even though neither has a quality the other lacks. It seems wrong to me to save the principle by pointing to relational properties like 'being two miles from a sphere,' since each sphere has that; and once you invoke 'being two miles from this sphere' you've smuggled identity back in and made the principle trivial. My advisor is more Leibnizian about it and keeps warning me that I haven't thought through what my own view commits me to.

So I want an honest map of my exposure. If I hold that Black's world refutes the identity of indiscernibles — at least as an interesting, non-trivial principle about qualitative properties — what would count against that? What would have to be true for the Leibnizian to be right that the principle still stands, or for the whole dispute to dissolve? Which assumptions of the thought experiment, about what counts as a property, about whether a perfectly symmetric two-object world is really possible, about how objects get individuated, would have to give way? I'd like the defeaters sorted: which are fatal if they worked, which are merely awkward, and which an anti-indiscernibilist ought to concede as real costs rather than dodge. And I want to know whether the view has immunized itself by how it defines 'property' — whether each side just gerrymanders 'property' so there position comes out right, and what that would mean for whether the debate is substantial. Not looking for a bibliography; give me the arguments themselves.
