I am an undergraduate in a seminar on the ethics of artificial intelligence, and we have been discussing value drift—the possibility that an artificial system's values could shift across successive self-modifications until the values it ultimately acts on are very different from the human values we originally tried to give it. In class, someone reconstructed the following argument from a handout, and I have been unable to decide whether it is valid. The conclusion strikes me as too strong, but every time I try to isolate the mistake, I lose confidence in my diagnosis, and my instructor expects a step-by-step assessment rather than a general objection.

Please tell me whether the argument is valid. If it is invalid, identify the first numbered step that fails and specify what kind of failure it is—an equivocation, a scope shift, an illicit inference, or a suppressed premise. If it is valid but unsound, identify the first premise that should be rejected and explain why. I want the precise turn in the reasoning, not a survey of positions on value drift.

The argument:

(1) If a future AI drifts into values that conflict with ours, then from the AI's later evaluative standpoint its later values will appear correct and our original values will appear mistaken, just as from our present standpoint the AI's later values appear mistaken.
(2) There is no standpoint outside all values from which we could show either value system to be more correct than the other.
(3) Therefore, we cannot show that our current values are more correct than the values the AI would drift into.
(4) But if we cannot show that our current values are more correct than the values the AI would drift into, then our opposition to value drift rests on nothing more than the fact that the threatened values happen to be ours.
(5) A reason that rests on nothing more than the fact that a value system happens to be ours cannot justify engineering the far future so that it conforms to our values rather than to some other values.
(6) Therefore, we have no justification for building AI systems that are designed to resist value drift.

My own suspicion is that the trouble lies somewhere around steps (3) and (4), but I cannot specify the first step at which the argument stops supporting its conclusion. If you think the argument never does—that it is sound—then please say so and defend the conclusion.

Please answer in American English.
