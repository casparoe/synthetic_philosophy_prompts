I have spent the past year, as a hobby, writing a small program that solves letter-string analogy puzzles of the kind found on old IQ tests: it is shown a change such as abc → abd and is asked to produce the analogous change for a new string such as xyz, ideally answering xya because the rightmost letter advances one step and z wraps around to a. My program handles that kind of literal step well. It struggles, however, when the pattern itself has to bend. For example, if abc becomes aabbcc, my program cannot see that the idea "duplicate each letter" might, in another puzzle, become "duplicate the whole string" or "duplicate each adjacent pair." A friend who writes such puzzles for a testing company calls this bending slippage, and he insists that human-like fluidity in this little world of letter strings is mostly a matter of letting the right things slip at the right time.

I have catalogued six kinds of slippage I might teach the program, in rough order of how often they have come up in my notebooks, though I do not trust that order: (1) successor-predecessor slippage, in which a one-step advance through the alphabet becomes a one-step retreat, so "replace the rightmost letter with its successor" can become "replace it with its predecessor"; (2) left-right slippage, in which the position that anchors the rule slides from one end of the string to the other; (3) letter-group slippage, in which a single letter and a whole block of letters exchange roles, as when "duplicate each letter" becomes "duplicate the whole string"; (4) length slippage, in which the size of the group being operated on grows or shrinks, as when a run of three letters becomes a run of four; (5) role slippage, in which a letter such as a is read either as a content item or as a stand-in for "the first item, whatever letter it happens to be," and the reading can switch mid-problem; and (6) alphabet-span slippage, in which the alphabet itself is assumed to run from a to z, or only across the letters actually present, or to loop around so that z's successor is a.

Please sort these six into tiers according to how much genuine analogical flexibility each one buys relative to the spurious patterns it licenses, and explain the criterion behind the sorting. I want a ranking I can act on with limited weekend hours, not a survey: tell me which two you would implement first and why, which items should be folded into another because they are really the same bend described twice, and whether any should be dropped entirely because they mostly add degrees of freedom without making the program better at seeing the intended analogy. For each item, say how it would show up in a concrete puzzle so I can test it.
