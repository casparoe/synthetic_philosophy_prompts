I work for a county land conservancy in the upper Midwest, and I keep getting into it with my brother-in-law, who's doing a PhD in something adjacent to animal ethics. His claim is that our restoration work might be net negative for wild animals: healthy habitat just means more sentient creatures living short, painful lives full of starvation, disease, and predation, so maybe a drained, degraded site is doing the local wildlife a favor. I think he's confused, but I've realized I can't state the principle that makes him wrong, and its started to infect how I think about our projects. The only defensible version of his point, I think, is that if anything matters here it's the welfare of the individual animals, and everything we call ecosystem health is just a proxy — sometimes a bad one. But I can't just throw away the proxy, because it's often all we can measure cheaply.

Here's the actual job. Several times a year we decide whether to restore, drain, burn, or otherwise "improve" a patch of ground. The official criteria are all biodiversity, native-species composition, ecosystem services — nobody asks whether the individual animals are better off. I want a decision procedure that takes the animal-welfare angle seriously without pretending I can count every vole. It has to be something I can run in an afternoon per site, and something our board (mostly conservation biologists plus one county commissioner) will sign off on, which means it can't just take "suffering matters" as a premise — it has to make the biodiversity criteria and the welfare criteria speak to each other, and flag exactly where they come apart. Please give me: a checklist or flowchart in words; the inputs I need and which ones are make-or-break; the hard cases it has to handle (a wetland whose restoration would multiply slow, disease-prone amphibians; a site where "restoring" means putting native predators back into a prey population at carrying capacity; maybe a feral-herbivore removal); and the points where judgment can't be eliminated, marked as such. Then run it on three hypothetical sites, one of them a case where the procedure tells me the restoration is bad for animal welfare but I'd probably still do it for other reasons. And be honest about what the procedure can't tell me.
