im taking a pragmatism course and we've been reading Peirce on how belief gets fixed and the pragmatic maxim, and i've started using a version of it as a personal rule. When someone asserts something in a debate, I ask them to say what I should expect to be different in experience or practice if the claim is true rather than false. If they can't specify any difference, I stop treating it as a real claim and set it aside—more like a figure of speech than a beleif I have to update on. For example, a friend said the university's new grading policy was structurally unjust, and when I asked what I should expect to observe or do differently if it was unjust rather than just badly designed, they gave me something vague about systems and power, so I dropped the point. Another time someone told me qualia might be an illusion, and I asked what I'd expect to see or feel differently, and they couldn't say, so I wrote it off. In a debate about whether rent control helps tenants it worked great, because I asked what I should expect in vacancy rates and rents, and we could actually argue.

I like the rule because it saves time and forces arguments to be concrete. But I suspect I'm using the pragmatic maxim as a shield against claims that are true but don't cash out easily in predictions, or against the idea that some real differences are only visible from inside a theory. Is my rule rationally defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle? If it isn't defensible as stated, is there a version that keeps the time-saving without the dogmatism? And where exactly does the pragmatic maxim stop being a good filter—what kinds of claims does it wrongly exclude?
