The first is Bostrom's simulation argument — the trilemma where if posthuman civilizations could and would run tons of ancestor simulations, we should think we're probably in one. She says the simulation argument mostly deflates this far-future fanaticism: if we're in a simulation, our actions probably don't reach the actual far future of base reality, and the best lever we have is trying to influence the simulators or not get the sim shut off. I worry the fanaticism just relocates: maybe the far future of our simulated world matters just as much, or the relevant stakes become all far futures across the simulation tree, or shutdown risk is just existential risk wearing a different hat.

Give me your credence in this claim, defined so it's not a fudge: taking the simulation argument seriously substantially dampens, rather than merely relocates, far-future fanaticism — where far-future fanaticism is the view that present altruistic choices should be made by their effects on the far future, and 'relocates' covers cases where the far future in question just becomes the sim tree or the simulators' world. Decompose the number, don't just give it. I want at least: (a) your probability we're in a simulation; (b) conditional on being in one, the probability our actions can't substantially influence base reality's long-run expected value; (c) conditional on that, the probability the fanatical conclusion about what to do now actually weakens rather than just getting a new object; and whatever other terms you think are needed, like a term for the not-in-a-simulation branch or for whether simulated far futures count the same. Give each term a rough number and a line of justification, show how they combine into the final credence, tell me which term you're least confident in, and say what evidence would move your final number most.
