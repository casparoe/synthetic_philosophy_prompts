I am a third-year undergraduate reading philosophy and cognitive science, currently taking a course on animal minds, and I sit as the student member on our university's Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Board. Next week the board must decide on a research licence application from a zoology group that proposes to test whether Eurasian jays can plan for future needs by caching perishable and non-perishable foods in chambers they have learnt will later be inaccessible. In the application's expected-benefits section, the investigators defend the study by claiming that flexible caching of perishable items is best interpreted as future planning rather than as a direct response to the animal's current motivational state.

Two board members have raised a Morgan's canon objection. They invoke C. Lloyd Morgan's principle in An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894), which I shall paraphrase rather than quote: we should not interpret a piece of animal behaviour as the outcome of a higher psychical faculty when it can be interpreted as the outcome of one lower on the psychological scale. They argue that the jays' behaviour can be adequately explained by reinforcement of site-specific caching choices and learned differences in food decay rates, so the proposal's framing overstates the capacities it is meant to investigate. The other board members reply that Morgan's principle is only a rule of thumb against unnecessary mentalism, not a testable hypothesis, and that the point of the study is precisely to make that distinction empirically. They also worry that the board's job is to assess welfare and the 3Rs, not to police investigators' theoretical vocabulary.

I have been asked to draft the board's decision memo. It must give one concrete recommendation: approve the application, reject it, or approve it with specified conditions. It should state the conditions or refinements precisely enough for the investigators to respond to, and it should engage seriously with the strongest points on the losing side—especially the worry that the board would be smuggling a philosophical doctrine into a regulatory decision. I would also like the memo to explain how Morgan's principle, read in its original context, should actually bear on this case: as an epistemic caution, as a claim about what explanations are scientifically respectable, or as something else. Please write the memo.
