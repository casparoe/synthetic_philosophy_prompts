Is there a name for the following pattern? I keep noticing that philosophers treat whichever kind of input they reached first as if it were the data and the other as if it were the theory to be saved. One philosopher starts from a simple principle — a ranking relation must be transitive, one principle should govern all of morality — and takes the principle's simplicity itself as a reason to trust it; when a case conflicts, they diagnose the case away as too exotic, a framing effect, or an unreliable feeling. Another starts from the case, vivid and stable under reflection; when a principle conflicts, they complicate the principle rather than surrender the case, adding clauses, exceptions, and auxiliary distinctions. Each accuses the other of ad hocness and cooking the evidence, and each thinks the other's input should bear the burden of proof. I know there is a standard picture that says one should keep adjusting principles and particular judgments until they fit together; but that picture is symmetrical, and what I keep noticing is the opposite — an asymmetry about which side gets fixed and which side is allowed to bend, together with the fact that the same philosopher can reverse the asymmetry from one debate to the next. Does this configuration have an established name? If so, how does it differ from the balancing picture and from the familiar divide between trusting general principles over particular cases and trusting particular cases over general principles? If not, what would you call it, and where are its edges — can the asymmetry ever be principled, or is it always just bias?
