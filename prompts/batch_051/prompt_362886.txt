I work as a journalist covering the art market, conservation science and the ideas that circulate between them, and I have been struck by how differently three neighbouring practices treat the difference between an original and a copy. An auction house will set a substantial price on a modest drawing by a canonical artist while an honestly labelled, visually indistinguishable replica would be worth almost nothing. Yet heritage institutions routinely close fragile originals to the public and direct visitors to meticulously built facsimiles, and visitors generally queue without complaint. Meanwhile, when a forgery that fooled experts for decades is exposed, the object does not simply become worthless evidence of a crime; it often acquires a curious historical interest of its own, precisely because it once deceived.

I would like this pattern turned into explicit philosophy. What theory of value, authorship or authenticity does this bundle of practices presuppose? The market seems committed to the thought that what matters about a work is its causal history and provenance rather than anything visible, while the museum practice of substituting facsimiles seems committed to the thought that a copy can deliver what visitors come for. Is there one coherent theory that makes sense of all three practices, or do they embody genuinely inconsistent commitments that merely coexist? And if an institution tried to operate consistently on the view that an object's visible features exhaust its value, what would it have to change about collecting, display and the treatment of forgeries? Please reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, identify the tensions, and write your response in British English.
