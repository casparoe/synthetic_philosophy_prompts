You are a professional philosopher of mathematics who regularly designs active-learning exercises for undergraduates and who is careful about the gap between a felt intuition and an argument. I teach a 20-person introductory philosophy seminar, one 50-minute session, on platonism versus nominalism about mathematical objects. I want an in-class game that makes students feel the pull of the indispensability argument before I name it, rather than hearing it from me. The thing I want them to run into themselves is this: nominalist paraphrase of talk about numbers, sets, and functions works tolerably for small fixed cases (using token-talk in place of type-talk), but it becomes forced, clumsy, or self-defeating once a task requires general instructions, precision, or reasoning about arbitrary cases — at which point either the paraphrase quietly imports the very entities it was meant to avoid, or the team gets stuck. Please design the activity: teams write and/or execute instructions for a concrete manipulation task (assembling blocks, distributing supplies, scheduling, laying out a grid) under a stated nominalism rule you choose — for example, no number words, no set/function/relation vocabulary, no variables — with a points payoff scheme that does not use real money, rewards both successful execution and successful detection of smuggled mathematical talk, and includes a mechanism that forces at least one team into the general case where the paraphrase becomes painful. Think carefully about why a naive version of this game (a simple vocabulary taboo) would fail, and harden the design against that failure. Then give me the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto platonist claims about quantification and reference, onto one-to-one correspondence as a limited substitute for counting, and onto the distinction between numerals (concrete marks or sounds) and numbers (abstract objects). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, students treating the exercise only as a word game, a team dodging the forced general case, or students concluding that plain-language paraphrase proves no such entities exist — and how I should handle each. Finally, be frank about which step of the philosophical argument the activity cannot supply on its own, where I will have to supply it in discussion.
