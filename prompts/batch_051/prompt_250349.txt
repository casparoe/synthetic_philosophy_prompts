I have been developing a theory of musical sadness that I call terminal irresolution. The core claim is that a passage sounds sad not because it resembles weeping or because we merely project a mood onto it, but because it presents a goal-directed structure whose completion is repeatedly made salient and then withheld: a melodic line keeps descending toward the tonic without landing, a phrase rises toward a peak it never quite reaches, a cadence is begun and then swerved away from. Sadness, on this view, is the felt recognition that this withholding is not a defect but the content of the music, and the pleasure of sad music is the safe rehearsal of the shape of an intention that cannot complete. The theory predicts that dark timbre or slow tempo alone will not make music sound sad, and that structurally complete music will not sound sad no matter how somber its surface. I know this sounds close to expectation-based accounts of musical emotion. Is it genuinely distinct from them, or just a metaphor? Give me the strongest version of the view, say what it gets right, name the nearest existing ideas, and then show me where it breaks: what sad music does it fail to explain, and what does it wrongly predict should sound sad?
