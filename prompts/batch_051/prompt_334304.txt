I run a reading group on the epistemology of introspection and we've been stuck for months on the reliability question. One half of the room thinks introspection is basically confabulation all the way down: people make up reasons for their choices, miss things sitting right in front of them, rationalize split-brain decisions, and are systematically overconfident about the accuracy of their reports. The other half thinks those studies are just bad introspection — mostly retrospective or off-the-cuff reports from people who were never really attending to their own experience in the first place, and that with better methods (concurrent reporting, trained subjects, or beeper-style experience sampling that catches people in the moment) introspection turns out to be pretty serviceable. Same papers, same people, no movement in months.

My working theory is that this is not really one disagreement. The two sides are using the word introspection for two different jobs. One side is asking whether people can report the causes of their own behavior; the answer to that is probably no, or at least only by theorizing after the fact. The other side is asking whether people can report the contents and character of their ongoing conscious experience; the answer to that is a lot closer to yes. If that's right, the debate is stuck because it's been posed as a single question with a single reliability score, when it's really two questions with different answers.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is my theory roughly right, or is there still a substantive disagreement left over — for instance, about how much inference and interpretation leak into even concurrent, first-person reports? What kind of disagreement is this, exactly, and what would count as progress: a cleaner distinction, a set of experiments, a methodological fix, or just an agreement to track the two questions seperately? Also, be honest about where your diagnosis is least stable: what argument or finding would make you change it, and which part of my picture would you most expect to be wrong.
