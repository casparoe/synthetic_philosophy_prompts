Give me your actual credence that Leibniz was right in the literal sense: that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds, where 'best' means that no metaphysically possible world ranks higher in overall value by whatever the correct axiology turns out to be. I don't want the usual maneuver around the question, where the answer is left to depend on whether God exists; I want a number and its derivation. Decompose it into at least these terms: (a) the probability that a perfect-being god exists; (b) the probability that cross-world comparisons of total value are well defined enough for there to be a best world at all — no infinite ascent of better and better worlds, no pervasive incomparability; (c) the probability that, if such a god exists, its goodness requires actualizing the best world rather than merely some world containing no gratuitous evil; (d) the probability that, conditional on all of that, the actual world is the maximum-value world despite its suffering and other frictions; and (e) an honest residual term for the possibility that no such god exists but the actual world is nevertheless maximal by luck. Give each term a number and a one-line justification, show how you combine them, and then tell me which term your final credence is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move that term most.
