I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been drafting a novel set in a small coastal community whose last four fluent speakers of a language are deciding whether to teach it to the next generation. In trying to get the ethical stakes right, I have been reading around the value of languages and cultural forms, and last month I hosted a dinner at which two friends who have thought about these questions far longer than I have fell into an argument I still cannot referee.

Marcus opened by saying that if the last speakers themselves are content to let the language go—if they judge that their children's lives will be better in the majority language and feel no grief at the loss—then there is no harm to speak of. A language, he said, is valuable because it is valuable to the people who use it; when the users no longer value its continuation, mourning it is sentimental tourism by outsiders. Elena replied that this treats a language as though its value were exhausted by current preferences. A language, she argued, is a store of accumulated human achievement—ways of dividing experience, names for places, poetic forms, concepts of kinship and obligation—and we do not treat other such stores that way. If a congregation voted to demolish an old church because upkeep was inconvenient, she said, we would recognize that something beyond their preferences was being lost; languages deserve the same recognition, only more so. Marcus answered that the analogy fails because a language lives only in the minds and mouths of its speakers, while the church is a public object others can visit and study; and that preserving a language against its speakers' own preferences would make living people custodians of a museum piece for the aesthetic pleasure of outsiders. Elena countered that the question is whether the loss carries weight even when the speakers consent, and that Marcus's principle proves too much: if the value of a cultural form is just the sum of its holders' current preferences, then a population voluntarily abandoning literacy or its own artistic traditions would also be no loss, a conclusion almost nobody accepts. Marcus said there is still a difference. Literacy and a living art tradition benefit the people themselves in real time, whereas a dying language, by hypothesis, no longer benefits its remaining speakers enough to outweigh its costs, and future generations cannot miss what they never knew. To insist otherwise, he said, is Platonism about culture—values floating free of any valuer. Elena finished by saying that future people can be deprived without knowing it, the way a person born blind is deprived of sight without being able to miss it; the loss to the human repertoire—what future people could have spoken, sung, or known—is real even when no one grieves for it in the moment.

I have tried to report the exchange faithfully, and I honestly could not say who has the better of it. I would like a point-by-point adjudication: which of Marcus's moves are sound and which fail, which of Elena's moves are sound and which fail, where an argument proves too much or does real work, and what either side is missing.
