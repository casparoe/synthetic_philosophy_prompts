Contract law has a weird pair of habits. When a judge has to decide what a clause in a written contract means, she'll usually say she's trying to give effect to the intent of the parties. But then she'll refuse to look at almost all the direct evidence of what they actually intended: no prior drafts, no negotiating emails, no testimony from the person who wrote the clause about what he though it meant. Instead she'll stay within the four corners of the signed document, maybe allow some evidence of trade usage, and ask what a reasonable person in the parties' position would understand the words to mean. Even stranger, many courts say outside evidence can only come in once the text is found to be ambiguous—but deciding whether a text is ambiguous usually requires looking at exactly the outside evidence you're not supposed to look at yet. What view of textual meaning, and of authorship, does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, say whether it's coherent, and spell out what contract litigation would have to look like if courts instead really believed that a text means what its author intended.
