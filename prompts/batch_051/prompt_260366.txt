After a long career in the natural sciences, I have recently begun working through moral philosophy, and one problem has come to occupy me: the paradox of deontology. The deontologist holds that certain act-types—torturing a prisoner, killing one innocent person as a means to saving several, breaking a serious promise—are wrong or to be avoided even when they would bring about the best overall consequences. But the justification for such constraints seems to me to be caught in a dilemma. Either the constraint is justified by the value of what it protects, such as the inviolability of persons or the dignity of rational agents, in which case it should give way when violating it protects that value to a greater degree, and deontology collapses into a refined consequentialism. Or the constraint is not justified by any value to be promoted but is simply posited as a basic moral fact—a brute prohibition of the sort I would not have been willing to accept in science without some deeper theory.

I have come to think this dilemma is genuine: deontology cannot escape it, and every escape route is either disguised value-promotion or an appeal to the unexplained. What I want to know is what would count against that position. Please give me a map of its exposure, not just the standard replies. Which arguments, cases, intuitions, or formal results should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward? Are there stable third options I have dismissed too quickly—for example, constraints grounded in agent-relative value, or a distinction between honoring value and promoting it? Which considerations would a sophisticated deontologist concede count against the simple dilemma, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject? Finally, I would like an honest assessment of whether my view has been insulated by the way I have framed the paradox, and whether that insulation is a defect of my formulation or a legitimate demand that a moral theory explain rather than posit its constraints.
