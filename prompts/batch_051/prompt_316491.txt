I am giving a paper next month to a joint workshop of philosophers of law, constitutional theorists, and anti-discrimination scholars, and I would like question-period preparation rather than a sympathetic restatement of my position. The thesis is that what makes a ground of discrimination suspect in equal-protection doctrine is not immutability but that classification along that ground is a socially read signal that the state is ranking persons by a historically entrenched hierarchy of civic worth rather than acting on a legitimate regulatory predicate. I argue that immutability is at best evidence: religion is suspect though mutable, while age and left-handedness are immutable without being suspect. On the proposed test, sexual orientation should receive heightened scrutiny, age should not, and disability deserves more demanding judicial review than it currently gets. The audience includes two comparative constitutional lawyers who regard suspectness as a family-resemblance judgment no single criterion captures; a critical race theorist who thinks any list of formal criteria lets courts treat subordination as a problem of category misuse rather than a material structure of power; an originalist-leaning legal philosopher who doubts that a freestanding test of suspectness is legitimate constitutional interpretation at all; and a disability-rights scholar who will press the worry that a history-of-hierarchy criterion quietly excludes disability, since its salience lies partly in formal equality's denial of it. Prepare me, as a numbered list of exactly five items with no introduction or conclusion, the objections I will actually get, ordered by likely damage, each phrased in the questioner's own idiom. Use at most two sentences per item: one for the objection, one for my best reply and the point where that reply is most likely to give way. Make the fifth item the question I will be hoping nobody asks.
