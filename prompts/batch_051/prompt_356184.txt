Me and my friend Dana have been going back and forth about Buridan's ass ever since it came up in my philosophy elective—the donkey that starves because it's standing exactly halfway between two identical piles of hay and has no reason to go left instead of right. Dana thinks the whole thing is overblown. I've been throwing cases at her for weeks to see if she's consistent. So far she's said: two identical Gatorades in the fridge, just grab one, and someone who freezes there is being weird, not rational; a guy who can't decide between two identical split-level apartments, flip a coin (and if you catch yourself hoping for heads mid-flip, that's a real preference trying to show itself); a woman who can save only one of two identical twin kids from drowning, there's no correct left-or-right answer but you absolutely have to pick, and hesitating is worse than any actual pick; two job offers that are exactly the same except one has a slightly nicer chair, you take the nicer-chair job, and if you still can't decide then it's anxiety, not rationality; and when I said I genuinely have zero preference between the blue and the black phone case, she said I'm probably fooling myself and whichever one I eventually pick will show which I actually wanted all along. But then she also says Buridan's donkey doesn't prove that choice needs a reason—it's just a broken donkey, since real agents can just pick.

New case: someone gets two sealed envelopes that are identical in every way, and she's told (correctly) that each has exactly twenty dollars in it. She has to open one. She doesn't feel the tiniest lean toward either, rolls a die to decide, opens the left one, and afterward says the die made the choice and she still has no preference at all—not even a little. What would Dana say about her? Was opening the left envelope rational? Does the fact that she opened it mean she secretly preferred it after all? I'm less interested in Dana's exact words than in the rule she's actually running. I can think of at least a few rules that sort of fit what she's said: that in a tie you just pick and no reason is needed; that ties reveal hidden preferences as soon as you act; or maybe that a perfectly rational agent really would get stuck the way the donkey does. Which of these (or what other) best fits her track record, what would each say about the envelope case, and what extra case should I hit her with next to tell the surviving rules apart?
