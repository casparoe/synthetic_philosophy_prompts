I am a PhD student in philosophy of physics, working on whether physics could have a final theory and, if it did, how we would be in a position to know that we had reached one. Before I engage with any more optimistic or pessimistic pronouncements on this question, I want the success conditions written out explicitly.

Write the desiderata that a candidate final theory would need to satisfy for us to be warranted in calling it final, with the consideration or motivating case behind each requirement. Please organize the list so that it separates at least the following senses of final, since I suspect much of the disagreement comes from sliding among them:

- final as formally complete: well defined at all energy scales, no true divergences, no regime where the description simply breaks down;
- final as explanatory complete: it either answers or shows to be ill posed questions such as why this gauge group, this spectrum of particles, these symmetries, and this low-entropy initial state rather than some other;
- final as empirically adequate in principle: it contains everything future experiment could observe, combined with empirically discriminable in practice: it can be supported by evidence we could actually obtain;
- final as unique or nearly unique: there is no continuum of equally consistent rivals, the way an effective field theory is compatible with indefinitely many ultraviolet completions.

For each desideratum, mark whether it is non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference; naturalness, background independence understood as the demand that the theory not presuppose a fixed background spacetime, determinism, and the requirement that the theory be constructible rather than selected from a string-theoretic landscape of low-energy vacua are the sorts of candidates I have in mind. Then address the part I most want: which subsets of these desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable, or at least in deep tension. In particular, say whether explanatory completeness and empirical discriminability can be jointly satisfied, given that any finite body of evidence is consistent with infinitely many rival theories, so that a demand for a proof of uniqueness may outrun any possible confirmation. Say also whether a requirement of Planck-scale ultraviolet completeness can be reconciled with the fact that all our actual knowledge is captured by effective field theories valid only below some cutoff; a final theory claim is, after all, a claim about a regime no experiment currently probes. And identify the single requirement on your list that does the most work in excluding currently popular candidates, with reasons.

Finally, give a brief scorecard, without citations, of how the standard model plus general relativity understood as effective field theory, string theory, and the asymptotic safety hypothesis in quantum gravity, understood as the claim that gravity is non-perturbatively renormalizable through an ultraviolet fixed point, fare against your list. Keep the weight on the desiderata themselves; the scorecard is only a check on the list.
