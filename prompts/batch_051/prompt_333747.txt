I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been volunteered to lead a session at our company's machine-learning reading group on what I have since learned is called the reference class problem. As I understand it, the problem is that the probability assigned to a single case depends on which class of similar cases one compares it to, and there is no obviously principled rule for choosing that class. The audience is roughly twenty practice-minded engineers who are comfortable with statistics and model evaluation but have never taken a philosophy course. I have about 45 minutes and a whiteboard.

The occasion is a concrete dispute. Our team maintains a churn model, and someone recently asked whether an output like "this account has a 23% chance of churning this quarter" is even a coherent claim about that account, given that the number comes out differently depending on which segment of the training data the account is placed in. I believe the real issue is conceptual rather than technical, and my job is to get that conceptual issue across.

I do not want the talk itself. I want a teaching plan for it.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on the foundations of probability and statistical inference. Think through the dependencies carefully, and before committing to a structure, brainstorm two or three candidate running examples and explicitly weigh them against the constraints before settling on one.

Your plan should cover:

1. The order in which the pieces must be introduced and why, in terms of what depends on what. I can name some of the pieces involved — frequentist probability, single-case probability, conditional base rates, the act of choosing a conditioning set — but I cannot yet see their dependency structure.

2. The single carrying example that will tie the session together, with a precise statement of what it must have and what it must avoid. I want an example in which different defensible groupings yield plainly different probabilities and in which no grouping is obviously the right one, but I suspect there are subtler criteria as well, and I want them made explicit.

3. The misconceptions I should expect from ML-literate, philosophy-naive engineers, and the point in the teaching sequence where each should be headed off. I already expect someone to say this is just the usual bias-variance tradeoff, or that the fix is to condition on every feature and let the data decide; tell me how to keep those from swallowing the session.

4. A final check for understanding that would distinguish "the idea landed" from "they can repeat the phrase."

5. What I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how to signal that the omission is deliberate rather than a gap in my own understanding.
