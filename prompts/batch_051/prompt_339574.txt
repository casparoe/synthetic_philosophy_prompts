I facilitate meetings for housing co-ops and consensus-based nonprofits, a couple times a month, in three-hour blocks, with people who have zero background in philosophy. The decisions carry real stakes — budgets, maintenance, hiring, sometimes whether a member has to leave — and the group signs off by consensus, so whatever I use has to be runnable aloud in a meeting without homework. The recurring failure is that everyone says "I'm in," the minutes record a decision, and then nothing happens — not laziness, but because each person had a private intention to do their bit and the group never formed the kind of intention you can attribute to "us." I know enough philosophy to know there's a real distinction between a mere pile of aligned personal intentions and a shared plan or joint commitment, where each person's part is built partly on what they expect the others to do, everyone is in a position to know the parts mesh, and no one can quietly drop their part without owing the others an account. I want you to turn that distinction into a reusable meeting procedure: a checklist of checks or questions, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, that tells me whether "we, as a group, decided" or just "everyone seperately intends to do something." It should catch people using group language while only holding private intentions, and also catch the inverse case where a group has created a joint commitment but its members dont realize they're bound by anything. Include the places where the procedure has to hand the decision back to me as a judgment call, and say what the procedure can't see. Then run it on three cases from my work: (1) a co-op votes unanimously to raise dues but no one can say what the finance committee's part is or when it happens; (2) a neighborhood group where the same four people always say "fine by us" but nobody would dream of asking them to actually do anything afterwards; and (3) a board that passed a motion to launch a training program, then a month later tried to treat it as never decided because three members were "quietly against it the whole time." Make at least one come out as "no decision here" despite a clean vote in the minutes, and at least one come out as "the group is committed" despite members insisting they never individually agreed. I'm expecting at least one of those to be an answer I won't like.
