I retired a few years ago from a career in molecular biology and have since been working my way through the philosophical literature on procreative ethics. I keep returning to procreative beneficence—roughly, the principle that prospective parents ought to select, from among the possible children they could have, the one expected to have the best life, or at least none worse than the others, given the available information—and to the contrast between liberal eugenics, which prizes parental choice under state-secured conditions, and the older, state-directed eugenics. I would like help thinking through a concrete proposal in that area, treated as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a moral position to be endorsed or condemned.

Suppose a country where IVF with preimplantation genetic testing is already common introduces the following scheme. Every viable embryo in a licensed clinic is assigned an Expected Flourishing Index score based on polygenic predictors of early mortality, severe morbidity, and cognitive or educational outcome, along with genomic indicators for a short list of severe monogenic disorders and a modest penalty for predicted serious disability. The index is statutorily forbidden from incorporating ancestry, race, ethnicity, or sex. Clinics must transfer the highest-scoring viable embryo in a cycle unless the prospective parents file a written renunciation explaining why they prefer a lower-scoring embryo; renunciations are anonymized and published only in aggregate. Cycles that follow the selection rule receive a substantial state subsidy, while cycles that deviate do not. Genetic counselors are paid a flat fee per cycle rather than per test or per embryo. The index itself is revised every five years by a standing expert commission whose proposed revisions must be ratified by a sortitioned citizen jury.

I am less interested in whether this scheme is just in the abstract than in how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize among prospective parents, clinics, counselors, the firms that produce the polygenic scores, and the citizen jury? Where is it likely to be gamed first, and what would the gaming look like? What second-order effects should we expect over a decade or two—in the distribution of genotypes, in the IVF market, in the boundary between subsidized and private reproduction, in the political coalition around the index, and in how people regard children who score poorly? What happens to traits the index does not or cannot measure? And which of the failure modes could be fixed by modifying the mechanism without abandoning it, and which could not?
