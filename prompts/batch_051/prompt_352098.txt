I have been trying to make sense of the scorekeeping picture of conversational presupposition, and I suspect I am misreading the rule of accommodation. As I understand it, when an utterance requires a presupposition that is not yet part of the common ground, the conversational score is adjusted so that the missing presupposition is added, so long as nothing in the context blocks that adjustment. But then presupposing seems to give a speaker a way to introduce contentious claims without ever asserting or defending them. Someone can say, 'I regret that the committee buried the audit findings', and — provided nobody objects at that moment — the proposition that the committee buried the findings apparently becomes part of the common ground, as if the audience had granted it. Yet hearers plainly do challenge this sort of move: 'Wait, the committee never buried anything.' So either the standard picture wrongly rules out a familiar conversational act, or I have confused accommodation with something stronger, such as belief or endorsement. Could you clarify what is actually added to the scoreboard when a presupposition is accommodated — shared belief, shared acceptance for the purposes of conversation, or merely a record of what the speaker is treating as settled — and whether the theory really licences using presupposition as a device for smuggling claims past an audience? I would also like to know what distinguishes the presuppositions that hearers may legitimately refuse from those they are normally expected to accommodate. Please write your answer in British English.
