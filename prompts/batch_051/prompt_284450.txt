I teach an undergrad course on social theory, about 18 students, and next week were doing the machinery and automation section of Capital volume one. I want an activity that makes them feel the tension Marx is poking at, rather than have me lecture it: an individual capitalist who adopts a labor-saving machine can undercut the others and win; but if everyone adopts it, the living labor that (on Marx's account) is the only source of surplus value shrinks away, and the basis of profit starts to undercut itself. I want them to walk into that contradiction themselves.

Can you design a 90-minute in-class simulation with roles and simple materials? Something like a few owners and a bunch of workers, with cards or chips standing in for labor, tools, machines, wages, raw materials, and finished goods. Payoffs in points or candy, not real money. Set the rules up so the first owner who buys a machine expands output and does visibly better, but by the later rounds, once everyone's got the machine, the workers are underemployed, unsold goods pile up, and profits sag across the table—even though each owner made the locally rational decision. The workers should also feel the difference between being needed as laborers and becoming disposable 'hands' to be hired and fired.

Then write a 20-minute debrief that maps what happened in the game onto the concepts—constant vs. variable capital, relative surplus value, the reserve army of labor, and the falling rate of profit—without using those terms during the game itself. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (owners hoarding points instead of investing, workers colluding and going on strike, somebody wanting to break the machine, point inflation) and how you'd handle each. And be straight about what a points-economy game can't capture: for instance, that on Marx's view machines transfer the value of their own wear and tear to the product but don't create surplus value, and a clean classroom token system will probably fudge that.

Write it in American English, casual is fine, and keep the whole thing under 900 words—tight enough that another instructor could run it straight from your description.
