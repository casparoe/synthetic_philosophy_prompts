I have been reading the second edition of Locke’s *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, and in Book II, Chapter IX he reports a problem put to him by William Molyneux. As Locke presents it, a man born blind has learned by touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal and roughly the same size, and he can reliably tell by feeling them which is the cube and which is the sphere. Suppose the man is then made to see, and the two objects are set before him on a table. Molyneux asks whether, before touching them, he could now tell by sight which is the cube and which is the sphere, and Locke reports that he and Molyneux both think he could not. The reason supplied is that although the man has had experience of how the two shapes affect his touch, he has not yet had experience that the shape which affects his touch in one way must affect his sight in a corresponding way.

Now consider a variation in which the blind man’s tactile training has not been limited to naming and sorting the two shapes. Instead, before gaining sight he has become highly proficient at handling a larger set of objects—cubes, spheres, rectangular blocks, and cones—and at performing purely tactile reasoning about their spatial relations: which objects can be stacked stably on which, which will roll if placed on a slope, which can pass through a round opening, and so on. When he gains sight, he is shown a cube and a sphere but is not told which names belong to them; he must first answer, before touching them, which of the two would roll if the table-top were inclined, and which would fit through a round hole cut in a screen. How does this change the dialectical force of the original problem? In particular, does the original negative answer still follow if the man’s prior tactile knowledge is structural rather than merely associative, and what does each side of the rationalist/empiricist disagreement make of the variation? Please write your answer in American English.
