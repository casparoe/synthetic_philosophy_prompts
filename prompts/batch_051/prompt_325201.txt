I just came from a three-hour argument with my friend Dana about weakness of will and what hyperbolic discounting is supposed to show about it. Her line is that weakness of will is a folk illusion: each of us is really a sequence of time-slices, later slices discount the future hyperbolically, and what we call akrasia is just the later slice choosing what is genuinely best from its own time-position. I dont agree, but I couldnt make the disagreement precise, so I'm trying to collect my own verdicts on a bunch of cases and see whether there is one principle behind them, or whether Dana is right that my distinctions are ad hoc.

I'd like you to answer as a philosopher who works on action theory, weakness of will, and rational choice over time. Please think it through carefully and start by brainstorming a few candidate principles before settling on the best one (or a small set of competitors).

Here are my cases and my gut verdicts:

1. Last week I planned to wake up at six and run. I went to bed genuinely thinking running was the thing to do, all things considered. When the alarm went off I turned it off and went back to sleep, while still thinking at that moment that getting up to run was the better choice. I count that as clear weakness of will.

2. My brother is asked in March whether he'd rather have $100 on April 30 or $120 on May 1. He picks the $120. When April 30 arrives he takes the $100, and while doing it he says, "I know the $120 tomorrow is the better deal overall, but the hundred in my hand is too much." I want to call this weakness of will too, not just ordinary hyperbolic discounting. The fact that the flip is predictable doesn't make it any less weak, to my ear.

3. A friend of mine has a strict eating plan. At her sister's wedding she decides, before anyone even offers her cake, that the exception is worth it: she'll eat the cake, enjoy it, and adjust the week. She eats it and doesn't regret it afterward. I do not count that as weakness of will. She changed her all-in judgment for that occasion.

4. My cousin takes "the future self is a different person" thing literally. When he orders the third drink or watches one more episode at 2 a.m., and I point out that tomorrow he'll wish he hadn't, he says: "Tomorrow-me is nobody I owe anything special to, and right now this is what I want, so I'm doing it." He isn't deceiving himself, and in the morning he says the same thing in reverse. My gut says this is not weakness of will either, even though it wrecks his plans exactly the way weakness of will does. I can't say why this is not weak while cases 1 and 2 are.

5. I had planned to skip a party because I didn't want to run into my ex. A friend texted me that my ex had already left town, so I went. My judgment about what to do changed on the basis of new information. Obviously, not weakness of will.

6. I actually don't have a settled verdict on this one. I know I'll flake on a morning run, so I pay a friend $50 in advance to knock on my door at 6 a.m. The night before, I find myself wanting to text her to call it off and promising myself I'll still run even without the fee. I don't send the text, but I came close. If I had sent it, would the sending be the weak-willed act, or is the weak-willed act only the later sleeping in?

So my questions:
- Is there a single principle that captures my verdicts in cases 1 through 5, and if so, what is it, stated precisely?
- What distinguishes case 2 (which I call weakness of will) from case 4 (which I don't), if anything? Or is that pair just inconsistent, and one of my verdicts needs to go?
- What does the principle say about case 6, and about this further case: a person pays a clinic in advance to dull his future desires, explicitly so that his future self won't even be tempted to deviate? Is that preempting weakness of will, or is it a different thing altogether?
- Finally, how much of Dana's time-slice view survives once the principle is on the table?

At the very end of your answer, after you've explained, please add a line in this exact format: \answer{...} with a one-sentence statement of the principle you settle on (or a one-sentence summary of your best candidate if no single principle survives).
