Two friends of mine got into it the other night about Max Black's two-sphere universe: a world that contains nothing but two iron spheres, identical in size, composition, and temperature, two miles apart in otherwise empty space. One said the case refutes Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, since the spheres share every property and yet are plainly two objects, not one. The other said they don't share every property, since each one is at a different position. The first shot back that in that world positions can only be defined relative to the spheres, so being at point A and being at point B are just relational properties that both spheres have equally. The second then said that if that's right the whole description is self-defeating, because you can't even say there are two spheres rather than one unless something makes them distinct, so the setup can't get off the ground as a counterexample. The first said distinctness doesn't need a distinguishing property, and if you demand one you've built the principle into your description instead of testing it. Then they drifted into whether adding a single scratch to one sphere is a new case or just changing the subject, and I lost the thread a bit. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move: who's right about which point, where each overreaches, and what both of them are missing?
