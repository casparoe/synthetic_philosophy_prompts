I have been reading an account of C. S. Peirce's pragmatism for a school essay, and one point about the pragmatic maxim has been troubling me. Peirce seems to say that the entire meaning of a concept consists in the sensible, practical effects we would expect its object to have: if two hypotheses imply no difference in anything we could experience or do, the distinction between them is empty. He also argues, in his discussion of the methods by which people fix their beliefs, that tenacity, authority, and a priori reasoning are inferior to the method of science because they can each make it impossible for experience to correct the resulting belief. But if meaning is exhausted by practical consequences, I do not see why the method of tenacity is not on equal footing with science. A person who clings to a comforting conviction, ignores contrary evidence, and organizes her life around it will derive enormous practical consequences from that conviction, and from her perspective those consequences will seem entirely satisfactory. If the content of a belief is fully fixed by the practical difference it makes, does not Peirce's pragmatic maxim imply that a belief is true or acceptable whenever it practically works for the person who holds it — which would make truth relative and would undercut his own preference for scientific inquiry over all rival methods of fixing belief?
