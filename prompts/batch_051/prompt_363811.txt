You are a professional philosopher who works on population ethics and moral uncertainty, and who regularly translates abstract arguments into practical guidance for cause-prioritization teams. I am writing the methodology section of a working paper on insect welfare, and I am stuck on how to move from the scale argument to an actual policy. The scale argument, as I am using it, runs as follows. We may assign insects a low probability of sentience—assume for the sake of the paper between 1 and 10 percent—but the number of insects alive at any one time is on the order of 10^18. If we simply multiply probability by population, the expected number of sentient lives at stake in insect-relevant decisions dwarfs almost every other cause. What is unclear to me is which decision rule should actually govern how an organization weighs insects once it accepts that the probability is low but the numbers are enormous.

Below are seven candidate rules that different members of my team have defended. Sort them into four tiers: top tier (defensible enough to endorse in the paper), second tier (respectable but flawed), third tier (defensible only under special conditions), and bottom tier (you would reject outright). For each rule, give one sentence explaining why it lands where it does. If two rules land in the same tier, indicate which you would lean toward and why. Finally, for the rule in your top tier, give a concrete number between 0 and 1 for the weight an individual insect should receive relative to a mammal whose sentience is not in doubt, and explain what in the rule fixes that number.

The seven rules:

1. Threshold rule: assign zero moral weight to insects unless the probability of sentience reaches 0.5; above that, treat insect interests as equal to mammal interests.
2. Linear rule: weight each insect exactly by p, the probability that a randomly selected insect has valenced experience—experience that feels good or bad to the subject.
3. Concave rule: weight each insect by the square of p, on the grounds that uncertainty about the reliability of the evidence itself should be reflected by shrinking already-small probabilities.
4. Default-sentience rule: once p clears a trivial floor such as 0.01, act for high-stakes decisions as if insects are sentient rather than betting on aggregated expected value.
5. Split-decision rule: avoid combining probability and population at all; first compare interventions under the hypothesis that insects are sentient and under the hypothesis that they are not, then choose only policies that are acceptable under both, using a maximin-style criterion when they conflict.
6. Burden-of-proof rule: give insects no weight until there is positive evidence of valenced experience of the kind invertebrates are often claimed to lack; a low probability is by itself not enough.
7. Stakes-sensitive rule: use a linear or concave weight for cheap, reversible interventions, but switch to something closer to default sentience for irreversible or very large-scale actions such as pesticide or land-use policy.
