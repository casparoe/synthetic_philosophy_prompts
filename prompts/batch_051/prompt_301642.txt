So I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been assigned a piece that's basically: if Keynes came back and read the current AI-and-the-end-of-work debate, what would he actualy say. The obvious hook is his 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, where he predicted his grandchildren would need to work maybe fifteen hours a week because the economic problem would be more or less solved by now. Everyone reaches for that essay when UBI or the four-day week comes up, and I don't want to write another lazy "Keynes was right" or "Keynes was wrong" column.

I'd like you to model this carefully. Put yourself in the role of an expert historian of economic thought who has read Keynes not just as the macroeconomist but as the Bloomsbury moralist—the guy who thought the love of money was close to a pathology, who wrote with real nostalgia about his early beliefs, and who also had a strong elitist and technocratic streak about who should manage society. Start by brainstorming, for yourself, what his actual commitments were about work, leisure, wealth, and the good life, and where they sat in tension. Then give me your best predictions for what he'd make of: (a) the argument that a universal basic income should replace most work as AI automates knowledge work; (b) the FIRE movement, where people grind in jobs they dislike so they can retire at forty; (c) hustle culture and the way wellness and self-optimization have become status games; and (d) the claim that AI will produce a permanent class of economically superfluous people instead of a leisure class. Where would he say we've misread his 1930 essay? Where would he be surprised by what actually happened, and where would he double down? Rank your predictions by how confident you are, and write the whole thing in American English.
