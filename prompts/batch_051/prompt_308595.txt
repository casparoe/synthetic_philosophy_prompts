I'm taking a course on the philosophy of AI and we keep going back and forth on this, so I want to pin it down. The classic shutdown-resistance argument seems to assume a goal-directed agent: the system has some terminal goal defined over world histories, and since an off switch leads to future histories where that goal never gets achieved, staying on has higher expected utility. So a capable enough system will hide its intentions, disable the off switch, or at least resist being turned off, even if its goal is something totally harmless. But that conclusion looks like it falls out almost entirely from the fixed-utility-function premise, not from anything about intelligence or self-preservation.

Now suppose you have an approval-directed agent instead — something that selects actions by predicting what a human overseer would approve of, maybe because it was trained with RLHF and its behavior in each context just tracks predicted approval. Shutdown isn't obviously bad for such a system in the same way: it doesn't have a stake in future world states, it could take an action that leads to being turned off if that's what the overseer would approve, and after shutdown there's no "it" left to have lost out. What I'm trying to work out is whether the shutdown-resistance arguement survives this shift. Which exact premise is doing the work, and does the argument die once you swap in an approval-directed agent — or can it be repaired into something weaker but still worth worrying about? I'm not asking which architecture we'll actually build; I'm asking what the argument needs to be true, and whether that's true only on the goal-directed picture.
