Next month I am giving a colloquium in my department defending a thesis about the place of virtues and duties in consequentialism, and I want a preparation sheet for the question period, not a restatement of the thesis. The thesis is that the familiar trilemma — treating virtues and duties as derivative from act-evaluation, as merely instrumental heuristics, or as independent sources of normativity — is a false trilemma. I defend a constitutive account instead: the virtues are the acquired dispositions through which a finite agent becomes able to perceive and respond to the very values that consequentialism identifies, and duties are the public, teachable form of that same capacity. Their authority is conditional on their contribution to overall value, but their standing is not merely instrumental, because an agent who set them aside whenever a local calculation seemed to conflict would, over the actual range of cases, be reliably worse at doing what consequentialism says matters — not merely less efficient. My argument in compressed form runs: finite agents cannot reliably compute act-consequentialist verdicts; the dispositions that actually track consequentialist value are virtue-like in structure; what explains their authority is their tracking, while what they are is a modification of perception and motivation rather than an external instrument; so the standard trilemma fails to capture their standing.

The audience is my department's moral philosophy workshop: mostly neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicists and Kantian constructivists, along with two consequentialists who work on demandingness and are suspicious of indirect consequentialism. The virtue ethicists will think I have put the order of explanation backwards; the Kantians will think a duty whose authority is conditional on outcomes is not a duty at all; the consequentialists will press on whether a decision procedure distinct from the criterion of rightness is coherent, or whether my view is just self-effacing rule-consequentialism in virtue-theoretic clothing.

Give me exactly eight numbered entries and nothing else — no introduction and no conclusion. Order the entries by how likely each objection is to be raised and how much damage it would do if pressed well. Each entry should be at most two sentences: the objection phrased as this particular audience would phrase it, followed by my two-minute reply and the point at which that reply is softest. Include as the final, flagged entry the question I am hoping nobody asks.
