I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and am preparing a feature on self-reference, paradox, and the thought that some systems contain loops no single level of description can fully explain. I want to use Jorge Luis Borges's short story 'The Circular Ruins' as the central example: a magician retreats to an abandoned temple and spends years dreaming another man into physical existence, trains him and sends him out into the world, and in the final scene walks into a fire and realises that he himself is a dream. Read that story through Douglas Hofstadter's notions of the tangled hierarchy and the inviolate level. A tangled hierarchy is a loop of levels in which none is highest, and the inviolate level is whatever is kept outside the loop and stops it collapsing into paradox — for Escher's Drawing Hands, the artist's real hand outside the picture, which drew both hands. I would like a reading that says precisely where the story instantiates these concepts, where it complicates or resists them (does Borges give an endless regress of dreamers rather than a closed loop, and what, if anything, plays the role of the inviolate level in the story?), and what the story adds to the conceptual point that the bare vocabulary of levels and loops misses. Treat both the story and the concepts with precision rather than using either as decoration for the other.
