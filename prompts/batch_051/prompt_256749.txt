I'm the registrar at a maritime museum and we keep having the same fight about whether our 19th-century schooner is still "the same ship" after a restoration. We make calls like this all the time — for insurance, for the display label, for a donor agreement that says we won't "substantially replace" the vessel, and for grants that only apply to original fabric. I'd like a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run whenever a restoration or rebuild is proposed: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because its a values call rather than a factual one. The team is curators, conservators, and a lawyer; none of them have a philosophy background, and we usually have about an hour. Ground the procedure in whatever the Ship of Theseus discussion actually teaches, but translate it so nobody has to know the jargon. Then run it on three cases: (a) over forty years of continuous maintenance we've replaced around 70% of the hull planks one at a time; (b) a proposal to replace the keel, stem, and every frame while reusing only the original fastenings; and (c) the one I'm dreading — a sister museum reassembled the original planks we sold off in the 1960s into a hull, and now claims they have the real ship while we have a continuously maintained but mostly new hull. Don't soften any of the three answers; if the procedure tells me something I won't like, say so. And be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure can't settle.
