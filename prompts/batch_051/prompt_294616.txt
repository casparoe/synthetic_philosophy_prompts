Take the three-prisoners problem in the following exact form. A, B, and C are in separate cells; exactly one of them will be freed and the other two executed, with each initially equally likely to be the one freed. A privately asks the guard to name one of B or C who will be executed. The guard is truthful: if B is the one to be freed he must name C; if C is the one to be freed he must name B; and if A is the one to be freed he chooses B or C by a fair coin. In the standard version of the puzzle, when the guard names B, A's chance of being freed stays at 1/3 while C's rises to 2/3.

Now change the case. A does not hear the guard cleanly. After hearing the garbled sound, his credence that the guard named B is 0.8 and his credence that the guard named C is 0.2, and he is certain the guard named one of the two. He updates by Jeffrey conditionalization on the partition {guard named B, guard named C}, holding fixed the conditional probabilities of each prisoner's being freed given each possible utterance.

Work through the exact posteriors, and give the general formulas as functions of p, where p is A's post-auditory credence that the guard named B. In this uncertain-evidence version, does A's own probability of being freed still remain fixed at 1/3? For which p does the 2/3 advantage switch from C to B, and does A's own probability ever depart from 1/3 as p moves through [0,1]? Then replace the fair coin with a biased guard who, when A is the one to be freed, names B with probability r and C with probability 1-r. Compute the three prisoners' posteriors as functions of both p and r. Discuss which features of the standard answer survive this two-parameter generalization and which are artifacts of certainty plus tie-breaking symmetry.
