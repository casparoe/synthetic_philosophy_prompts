I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about advance directives, and going case by case has made more of a mess than I expected. I'd rather not start from any theory; here are my gut calls.

R is bipolar. When she was stable, she signed an advance directive saying that if she's ever in a manic episode and refusing treatment, she authorizes the hospital to admit and medicate her anyway, and she definitely doesn't want the refusal she gives during the episode to count. Last month she went manic—hasn't slept in days, grandiose, says she's never felt better and refuses to go in. I'd have her committed without a second thought. The stable R should win.

M is the one that keeps me up. At 55, sharp as ever, she wrote a directive saying that if she developed severe dementia she'd refuse antibiotics, feeding tubes, everything—that a life without her memories and her work was one she'd rather not live. She's 62 now, with advanced Alzheimer's. She doesn't recognize her kids, but she's gentle, laughs when someone puts on the old songs, and grins through her chocolate pudding. She gets pneumonia. Without antibiotics she'll die; with them she goes back to that same contented routine. My gut says give the antibiotics. But I can feel that I'm overriding exactly the person who tried to tell me what she wanted, and I'm not sure I can defend the distinction between her and R.

T was raised a Jehovah's Witness and at 25 signed the card refusing blood. He left the faith in his forties and told his family and his doctor, more than once, that he'd accept blood now. Before he got around to updating the paperwork, a car crash left him unconscious and bleeding out with the old card still in his wallet. I'd transfuse him. The card describes a person who no longer exists, as far as I'm concerned.

Q got an ALS diagnosis and, while completely competent, wrote a directive refusing a ventilator once her breathing failed. Now she can't speak or move her limbs but is cognitively all there; her breathing is failing. I'd honor the directive. The earlier self and the later self feel like the same person in a way that M's don't, but I honestly couldn't state the principle behind that feeling.

So: is there one principle—about when the earlier self gets to bind the later one—that gets all four? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what's the least embarassing theory that keeps as many as possible? And I'd like to know what the principle says about a fifth case: someone signs a directive refusing treatment if he's ever in a locked-in state after a stroke, and now he's exactly in that state and his family says he seems, as far as they can tell, to be doing okay. I don't have a firm view, and I suspect my answer on that one will force me to change one of the first four.
