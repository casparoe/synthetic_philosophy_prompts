Question:

In Posterior Analytics I.3, Aristotle argues that if every item of knowledge rests on demonstration from prior premises, then either the premises go back forever, or the reasoning turns in a circle; in neither case, he thinks, do we genuinely know, so there must be non-demonstrated first principles. Reconstruct the regress argument for epistemic justification that this passage suggests. Then explain how foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism each propose to stop or accept the regress. Finally, assess which proposal is strongest and why, paying attention to the difference between a belief's being caused and its being justified.

Student's answer:

The regress problem: if a belief is justified only by inferring it from another belief, then that supporting belief must itself be justified, and so on. This yields three possibilities: the chain stops at a basic or non-inferentially justified belief, goes in a circle, or continues infinitely.

Foundationalism takes the first option. Its basic beliefs are self-justifying: they are justified without receiving their justification from other beliefs. Because they are self-justifying, they must be infallible; otherwise, fallibility would introduce the possibility of error and then they would need further support, restarting the regress. Foundationalists therefore must restrict basic beliefs to a small set of self-evident truths, and infallibility is a necessary feature of any foundationalist position.

Coherentism takes the second option and says circular chains are not vicious. Justification is holistic: a belief is justified by its coherence with the rest of one's belief system, and this coherence is what ties the circle together. The main objection is that coherentism cannot distinguish a coherent false system from a coherent true one, because coherence is purely internal. That's correct as far as it goes, but coherentists reply that truth is itself coherence, so the objection is question-begging.

Infinitism takes the third option: the regress continues infinitely and each belief is justified by the next one in the chain. Peter Klein has defended this by saying that an infinite chain of reasons can support justification, provided the chain is non-repeating. The obvious objection is that finite minds cannot actually have an infinite number of beliefs, so infinitism is automatically false because it demands something psychologically impossible.

On balance, foundationalism is strongest because it blocks the regress and grounds knowledge in self-evident truths. The problem with the other two is that they don't actually provide a stopping point for justification: coherentism collapses into accepting that any sufficiently coherent system is justified, and infinitism collapses into saying that justification is never completed.
