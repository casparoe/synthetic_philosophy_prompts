I'm a PhD student working on reinforcement learning, and over coffee my officemate (who does cognitive science) and I got into it about whether a trained network could be said to 'want' anything. I wrote up my side as an argument and I half-suspect one of the steps is a trick, but I cant say which or why. Here it is:

(1) Training a policy with policy-gradient RL is an optimization problem: you're searching over policies for the one with highest expected reward on the training distribution.

(2) So the trained policy is, on the training distribution, a function that picks the action that would maximize expected reward — at least up to whatever the optimizer managed to find.

(3) Being a system that picks actions so as to maximize expected reward for an objective is exactly what having a goal amounts to.

(4) So the trained policy has a goal (roughly: score reward) purely in virtue of being the output of an optimizer. No evolution, no designer intending it to want anything, no reinforcement over generations.

(5) If a pure optimizer can produce a wanter just by gradient descent, then wanting is the kind of thing that falls out of any sufficiently good search — natural selection isn't required, and neither is consciousness.

I think the move from (2) to (3) is where it's slippery: (2) says the policy is selected for high reward, but (3) says the policy selects actions in order to get reward. But when I try to say what more my own wanting could be than a disposition to select among options, I end up agreeing with the argument, which feels wrong. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest reading of that step on which its true — does the argument then still go through? And if the conclusion is right, is it a substantive claim about minds or just a stipulation about the word 'want'? Please answer in British English.
