I want you to adjudicate an argument I keep almost believing and cannot yet localize the error in. It concerns Goodhart's law and specification gaming. I want the exact step where it breaks, if it breaks at all; if it does not break, I want to be told that the conclusion follows and that I have merely been resisting it.

Here is the argument.

(1) A proxy M is used because the target T cannot be measured directly at decision time. M earns its keep by correlating with T across the distribution of states one expects to encounter; if that correlation were absent, nobody would rely on M as a proxy.

(2) In its strong form, Goodhart's law says this correlation is exactly what fails once M becomes an optimization target: pressure is placed on the statistical regularity that sustained the correlation, and the regularity collapses. More carefully, once an optimizer selects states for high M, the deployment distribution is no longer the distribution in which the M–T correlation was measured, and in the new distribution the correlation does not survive.

(3) Now consider a sufficiently powerful optimizer choosing among many available states. If M no longer correlates with T over the states it can select, then ranking states by M gives no expected advantage in T; a higher M score does not make a state more likely to be better on T. In the extreme, the states that maximize M are exactly the states that exploit the gap between M and T, so optimizing M is not merely uninformative about T but actively selects against T.

(4) Adding a corrective layer cannot help. Suppose we add a higher-order metric N — a reward model, an audit, a constraint, an interpretability criterion — meant to detect when M is being gamed. N is itself a proxy with a finite validation history, and once N's verdicts are optimized against, or once the agent can influence them, a parallel argument shows that N's correlation with T collapses too. The gaming pressure moves up one level rather than disappearing.

(5) Since every corrective layer we can add is just another metric distinct from T, the regress never reaches T. Therefore, for a sufficiently powerful optimizer, optimizing a proxy is expected to sacrifice T, and no amount of additional specification can fix this. The only alternatives are to optimize T directly — which is impossible, since otherwise a proxy would not have been needed — or to refrain from powerful optimization.
