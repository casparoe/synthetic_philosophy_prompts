I am a novelist working on a book set among medieval disputants, and in the secondary literature I keep meeting the same image: God's attributes are like the rays of the sun, or like white light passed through a prism — one undivided light at the source, many colors only as it reaches the human mind. One commentary uses this image to explain how God can be omniscient, omnipotent, and perfectly good while having no parts: the distinctions among the attributes, it says, lie on our side of the prism rather than in God. From this it concludes that divine simplicity comes at almost no cost — we may go on speaking of many genuinely different divine attributes, since the differences are only differences in our way of knowing. I suspect the analogy is doing real argumentative work, but I do not know how far to trust it. Could you lay out what maps onto what in the prism-and-rays analogy; where the mapping breaks down; what the picture quietly imports — for instance, whether the attributes are being treated as effects of God rather than as God's own essence, or whether attributing distinct perfections to God becomes merely a projection of the human mind; and whether the conclusion that simplicity is compatible with a full plurality of real divine attributes survives once the disanalogies are on the table?
