I'm a new attending on a trial that randomizes patients between standard chemo and a newer regimen, and I've made myself a rule about enrollment: whenever I catch myself leaning one way about an individual patient — for example, thinking a frail older patient would probably do better staying on the standard arm — I hand the consent conversation off to a colleague who doesn't share the hunch. I was taught that you can only ethically enroll someone if you're genuinely uncertain which arm is better, so I treat any private lean as disqualifying. My supervisor says I'm over-reading it. She says trials only need clinical equipoise at the level of the field: honest, unresolved disagreement among reasonable experts. A doctor's hunches about particular patients, she says, are unavoidable and maybe even part of good judgment. But the thing that bothers me is this: if nobody actually caring for the patient is on the fence, who or what is "the field," and why should its aggregate disagreement make it okay for me to enroll someone I privately think would do better on the other arm? Is my rule defensible, or am I holding trials to a standard of individual uncertainty that was never the real requirement?
