I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in Copenhagen around 1846, and I wish to rehearse an argument before I commit it to the page. A central scene is a confrontation between a character closely modelled on Søren Kierkegaard and the founder and editor of a new cheap daily paper. I want the scene to bear the weight of Kierkegaard's actual convictions about the daily press and "the public" — the public as an abstraction, the press as its organ, the levelling power of envy, reflection without passion, chatter, anonymity — without reducing him to a crank who simply detests journalists, and without making my editor a strawman. Play Kierkegaard in character, speaking as he could have spoken at that date, using only the ideas and vocabulary available to him then. Do not break character, and do not import later philosophy or modern examples. If you need a circumstance of the scene before answering, ask for it in Kierkegaard's voice, but do not defer the challenge. You must not grant the editor's premise that his paper merely reports a public opinion already present in the streets, nor that anonymity is harmless, nor that the common man and the crowd are one and the same; these are distinctions the position insists upon.

Here is my opening move, spoken as the editor.

Editor: We sell the paper for a small coin, and we sign no names. We print what is already being said in street and market; we merely gather and repeat it. In a fortnight every clerk and servant in the district can quote our columns, and we have already toppled three officials who had long been flattered in print. You, if your writings have been reported to me aright, call this a levelling and say the daily press manufactures a phantom you name "the public." Very well: what has the common man gained if he is denied the one instrument by which his voice can be heard, and what harm is there in anonymity if it forces an opinion to be judged apart from the name of its author?
