In health class we just did the depression unit, and the list of symptoms was basically: sad mood most of the day, not enjoying anything, crying a lot, changes in sleep and appetite. The advice was if you've had those symptoms for more than two weeks you should get help because its an illness. But last year, after my grandma died, I had pretty much all of them for over a month, and everyone—my parents, my guidance counselor, even my doctor—told me that was grief and it was normal, even important, and I shouldn't try to push through it or numb it. So there's this weird asymmetry: the same set of bad feelings counts as an illness when they show up on their own, but as this meaningful, almost sacred thing when they come from a loss.

What's actually justifying that difference? Is it just the cause? If so, why should the cause decide whether feeling terrible is healthy? Suppose there were a pill that would take away the grief but leave all your memories of the person, and your good feelings about them, intact. I'd take painkillers for a broken arm without thinking twice, but I'm honestly not sure I'd take the grief pill, and I can't say why. I want the strongest principle you can find that explains why we treat grief differently from depression, or differently from physical pain, and then an honest verdict on whether it really works or whether the whole distinction is something we should give up.
