I work as a recording and live-sound engineer, and I think the way my tools think: waveforms, spectral energy, envelopes, transients, compression, reverb, phase. Last month a composer asked me to make a cue sadder, and I did it without hesitating—rolled off the top end, stretched the tempo map, lengthened the attacks, pulled out the reverb tail—and only afterward realized I could not say which of those moves was the sadness and which was decoration. That has been gnawing at me. A slow descending phrase, a dark mix with heavy low-mids, a vocal that breaks at the peak: these can sound sad, and not just cause sadness. I can recognize a passage as sad while I am in a good mood, working on it for hours without catching the feeling, so the sadness is not simply the sound's effect on me. But if it is not the effect, what in the sound carries it?

In conversations and online forums I have picked up, in a rough way, a few standing answers. One view says sad music sounds sad because its contours resemble the way sad people speak and move: falling pitch like a sigh, slow attack like a body moving heavily, narrow and drooping intonation. Another says that when we hear sad music we are really hearing a persona—not the composer, not the performer—whose voice or gesture the music is; the sound is sad because it sounds like someone expressing sadness. Another answer says the sadness just is the music's tendency to produce sadness in listeners, and that my staying dry-eyed while recognizing the sadness is the thing that needs explaining, or explaining away. And there is a fourth view I have only half-absorbed, which says sadness is a real, irreducible perceptual quality of the sound, the way pitch or density is, and that each of the reductive stories above fails in a different way.

What I would like is a systematic translation of each of these positions into the vocabulary I actually use: fundamentals and harmonics, spectral envelope and centroid, formants and vocal-tract filtering, attack/decay/sustain/release shapes, transients, tempo, loudness contours, articulation, spatial cues like reverb and distance, compression and dynamic range—the whole toolkit. Be honest about the translation: say which claims carry over cleanly, which had to be distorted or forced to fit, and which have no counterpart at all in signal-processing terms. Then give me the residue report. My working hunch is that the entire disagreement collapses into a handful of measurable acoustic features—slow falling formants, low spectral centroid, long attacks, compressed dynamic range—and that words like "expression" and "aboutness" are just loose ways of pointing at those features. I want to know whether the residue proves that hunch right, or whether it shows that my vocabulary is missing something load-bearing. In particular, if the persona view has a remainder that no combination of frequency, time, and amplitude can state, tell me whether that remainder is a real feature of the music or a shadow cast by the philosophers' vocabulary.
