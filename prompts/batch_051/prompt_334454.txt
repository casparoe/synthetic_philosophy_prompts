So I've been stuck moderating a year-long argument between two friends, one of whom has gone deep down the ergodicity economics rabbit hole and the other of whom is a fairly orthodox expected-utility person. The ergodicity guy (time averages, Ole Peters style) thinks almost all the trouble in economics and ordinary decision theory comes from people computing ensemble averages across parallel worlds when what a decision-maker actually lives through is a single time path, so the right objective is the time-average growth rate of wealth; he'll point to the standard multiplicative coin-flip, even odds, where a win multiplies your wealth by 1.5 and a loss by 0.6 — expected wealth grows five percent per round, but the rate a single player actually cumulates is about minus five percent per round, so you get ruined by a bet that's "good in expectation." The expected-utility guy says this is just the Kelly criterion or log utility wearing a costume, and that once you specify a utility function over wealth, the apparent paradox dissolves because risk aversion was never about maximizing expected money. I finally got them to agree to an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the document they shoudl have produced.

For the document itself: start with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about the mathematics of non-ergodic multiplicative processes, about what a theory of rational choice under risk and repetition has to explain, about what the plain expected-value criterion gets wrong, and about what standard expected-utility theory already has room for. Then make a disagreements table whose rows are the cruxes. I have my own suspicions about the real cruxes, so work these in unless you find better ones: (a) whether utility is a preference-elicited object or an objective pinned down by the dynamics of the decision problem; (b) whether the repeated multiplicative gamble is a fair model of real economic choices or a toy that makes the critique look good; (c) whether time-average growth maximization is a normative rule, a descriptive account of what people do, or both; and (d) whether "ergodicity economics" adds anything beyond known results like log utility and Kelly betting. For each row, give the exact disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own view, one concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction about that case written down in advance. Finally, flag whatever part of the fight you think is merely verbal. Don't let them sign anything vacuous, and don't slant the agreed claims toward either side — the whole point is that both of them could put their name to the agreed part.
