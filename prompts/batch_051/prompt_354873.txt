So we start from the same text, here is Peter Suber in The Paradox of Self-Amendment: A Study of Law, Logic, Omnipotence, and Change (1990) setting out the dilemma:

"The dilemma is sharp. (1) If the rules of change cannot authorize their own change, then they are either immutable, or they may only be changed by a second set of such rules, which in turn must either be immutable or mutable only by a third set, and so on to an arbitrary halt or an infinite regress. (2) But if the rules of change can permit and structure the process of their own change, then they must permit their own limitation and repeal. But at first sight, at least, paradox attaches to any rule that authorizes the limitation or extinguishment of its own power. For it seems that such rules would supply the premises for a transfer of authority in which the conclusion contradicted at least one premise, or an action much like that of an omnipotent being limiting its own power to act."

My opening move: I dont see why horn (2) is a paradox. If an amendment clause is used to repeal itself, the old clause and the post-amendment absence-of-a-clause are never valid at the same time. The old clause governs the amendment procedure, the amendment happens, then the clause is gone. That's a temporal sequence, not a syllogism, so the phrase "the conclusion contradicted at least one premise" seems to assume the old clause stays around after being repealed in order to contradict the result. Where is the actual contradiction, and why shouldn't I say the system simply had a self-repealing rule and then no longer had it?
