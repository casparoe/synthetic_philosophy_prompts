I'm not a philosopher, just someone who ends up in a lot of group decisions, and I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for. The cleanest version I've come up with is this. Two housemates have one ticket to a film that one of them, Alice, finds genuinely objectionable. Three options: Alice goes, Ben goes, nobody goes. Alice's ranking is nobody-goes > Alice-goes > Ben-goes: she'd rather nobody see it, but if someone has to, she'd rather be the one, because she'd at least watch it critically, whereas Ben would enjoy it and that bugs her more. Ben's ranking is Alice-goes > Ben-goes > nobody-goes: he thinks Alice would actually benefit from being exposed to it, but if she won't, he'd enjoy it himself rather than waste the ticket.

So I try to build a fair decision rule with two parts. Part one: each person gets a veto over whether they personally go. Alice, choosing just between Alice-goes and nobody-goes, prefers nobody, so the rule says nobody beats Alice. Ben, choosing just between Ben-goes and nobody-goes, prefers Ben going, so the rule says Ben beats nobody. Chain those: Ben beats nobody beats Alice, so Ben beats Alice. Part two: if both people prefer one option to another, the decision respects that. But both of them prefer Alice-goes to Ben-goes — Alice would rather go herself than let Ben enjoy it, and Ben would rather Alice go than go himself. So part two says Alice beats Ben. Same two facts, opposite answer.

I get that this is some kind of voting paradox, but it doesn't feel like the usual one where a majority cycles A beats B, B beats C, C beats A. There's no majority voting anywhere — just unanimity plus personal vetoes — and you still get a flat contradiction. Does this exact thing have a name? And what's doing the work: the vetoes, the chaining step, the particular preferences, or the fact that all three options are about who 'personally' does the thing? Would it survive with three people or four options, or does it need this exact two-person, three-option shape? If its got no name, what would you call it, and how would you define it tightly enough that it isn't just 'any old voting mess'? I'm not just after the label — I want the carving: what this pattern is, what it isn't, and which nearby ideas it's adjacent to but distinct from.
