After a working life in a physics department I've been spending my retirement reading philosophy, and I keep getting stuck on a problem about probability and decisions that I would once have thought was settled. Suppose I am offered a gamble on a fair coin: heads multiplies my total wealth by 1.5, tails multiplies it by 0.6. If I think of it as a one-off wager, the expected multiplier is 1.05 — a 5% average gain — and the physicist in me says that is a good deal, so I would take it. But if I think of the same gamble as a standing policy — every week, re-staking my entire net worth each time — my judgement flips. The typical week multiplies wealth by only about 0.949, becuase the relevant per-round growth is the geometric mean, the square root of (1.5 × 0.6), not the arithmetic mean. Over a lifetime of flips the typical trajectories are crushed toward zero, while a tiny lucky minority become so rich that they drag the average across many parallel gamblers upward. So the time average is negative and the ensemble average is positive, and my attitudes track the frame: I would take the single flip but would refuse the repeated policy, even though every individual flip in the series is identical and each has the same positive expected value.

I see the same asymmetry in ordinary economic practice: people will call a trade or a bet good value when offered once, and reckless when offered as a permanent strategy, though the arithmetic of each individual exposure is the same. I want to know whether this asymmetry is defensible, or whether one of my two reactions is simply a mistake. Please give me a committed answer rather than a survey. Either defend the distinction between the one-off and the repeated case on principle — saying exactly why a difference in time horizon, or in what gets averaged, justifies treating identical gambles differently — or tell me which of my reactions (take it once versus refuse the policy) I should give up, and why. If you think both have a kernel of truth, explain what the correct unified principle is and what it says I should actually do when offered the next flip.
