Before I retired I ran a small isotope geochemistry lab, and probability was mostly something we did to our data; now that I have time I've gotten into philosophy and joined a reading group on the foundations of probability. I keep noticing that the same word 'probability' gets made to do very different jobs in the room. The ex-engineer says the probability of a single event is meaningless because there's no long-run frequency to measure. The Bayesian says a probability is just a degree of belief and there is nothing more to it. Last week we spent an hour on whether a single event can have a probability and got precisely nowhere, and I suspect people are mostly arguing past one another.

Could you write a 'which interpretation of probability do you actually hold?' quiz for this group, eight to ten questions, with a scoring key at the end? Each question should be built around a concrete case: a fair coin that will be tossed exactly once tomorrow at noon, a 30 percent chance of rain in tomorrow's forecast, the chance a particular patient survives surgery, a radioactive atom decaying within the hour, the probability that a mathematical conjecture you are currently thinking about is true, the probability that a slightly bent coin lands heads, a one-off historical claim such as whether Caesar crossed the Rubicon, and anything else you find useful. Each question should have two or three answer options that pull apart frequentism (finite, limiting, or hypothetical), subjectivism, propensity, and objective Bayesian or logical probability; phrase the questions so someone can answer without already knowing those labels, and don't just ask whether probability is frequency, belief, or physical. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto those positions, and it should also tell me which positions the quiz cannot really tell apart (I suspect propensity and hypothetical frequentism will be hard to seperate on cases alone, but I'd like to know for sure). If some pattern of answers has no natural label, say so, and say whether that seems to be because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unoccupied. Please write the whole answer in British English.
