A friend and I just spent a long evening arguing about whether "national character" is a coherent notion or merely respectable stereotyping. She argued that claims like "Germans are orderly" or "Brazilians are warm" are unfalsifiable, because any counterexample gets dismissed as an exception and the supposed average is never specified. I replied that persistent, culturally transmitted differences in average dispositions are in principle detectable, and that an effect can be real even when it is small relative to within-country variation. But the measurement problems seem severe: people rate themselves against a local reference group, translations shift item meanings, and a disposition may surface as one behavior in one place and a different behavior elsewhere. I want to know how far evidence can actually push on this. Design the strongest empirical test you can for whether there is such a thing as national character — understood as something more than individual variation plus a widely shared stereotype. State what would count as a positive result, what would count as a negative result, and be explicit about what even a positive result would not settle: in particular, whether stable cross-national differences in average self- or peer-rated traits are enough to recover the word "character," or whether the interesting claim thins out under operationalization. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning — no preamble and no restating of the question.
