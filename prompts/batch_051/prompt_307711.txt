I'm designing a course module on ancient Greek philosophy of mathematics, and I've hit a snag in Euclid's treatment of a concept that seems both foundational and unstable. In the Elements, Euclid defines the unit as that in virtue of which each existing thing is said to be one, and a number as a multitude assembled out of units. That makes the unit conceptually prior to number and apparently not itself a number, which sits awkwardly with our habit of saying "the number one"; it also looks suspiciously close to circular, since the definition leans on the word "one." I want a working definition of "unit" precise enough to do real classificatory work, not a dictionary gloss.

Define "unit" so that it correctly classifies all of the following: (a) a single apple resting on a table; (b) the number 1, considered as an object of arithmetic; (c) a meter, as a unit of length; (d) a heap of sand, considered in relation to its grains; (e) a phoneme within a spoken word; (f) a corporation treated as a single legal person; (g) a "family unit" in a housing-assistance program; (h) an hour of time, a continuous stretch with no natural boundaries; and (i) an arbitrary conventional unit chosen by a scientist when setting up a measurement scale. For each case, say whether Euclid's definition, as paraphrased above, handles it correctly, and where it forces you toward a surprising verdict. I suspect "unit" does two different jobs — an indivisible element in a count and a conventional standard of measure — and that Euclid's definition really captures only the first of them. If you agree, give the strongest version of each notion and say how they relate; if you disagree, defend a single unified definition against all nine cases. Finally, indicate which of your verdicts you take to be the least secure.
