I'm a high school student in a civics class, and we have been arguing about civil disobedience: whether sit-ins, rent strikes, or student walkouts can be justified even when they break a law, and whether a citizen ever has an obligation to obey a law she regards as unjust. In our last discussion, a classmate kept settling arguments with the line "an unjust law is no law at all," and our teacher endorsed it. The move was always the same: from "this law is unjust" straight to "therefore no one is bound to obey it — indeed, it is not really a law at all."

I am fairly sure that one sentence is doing more work than it can carry, but I cannot yet say exactly what the work is or where it fails. Please take the slogan apart into the distinct precise claims it could be expressing. For each candidate claim, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or actually false; specify what it would have to mean by "law" (a statute, a valid legal norm, a morally binding directive, something else) and by "unjust" (procedurally flawed, substantively wrong, gravely so, partly so) for the claim to come out true; and say whether that reading actually supports the move my classmate and teacher were making. Then, rather than leaving the options open for me to choose among, commit to a position: say which reading, if any, is the strongest defensible version of the slogan, and give a definite verdict on whether that version entitles someone to conclude that an unjust law imposes no obligation at all. Where the underlying issue is genuinely unresolved, say so plainly, and then defend the position you take anyway.
