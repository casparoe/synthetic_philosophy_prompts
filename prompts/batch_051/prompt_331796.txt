Compare the Eric Williams thesis about why Britain abolished slavery with Appiah's honor-based account of how moral revolutions happen. Just to be clear, I mean Eric Williams the historian, not Bernard Williams the philosopher. As I understand it, Williams argued that the British didn't end the slave trade and slavery mainly out of moral conviction; the West Indian sugar economy was declining and British capitalism had its own reasons to want a free labor system, so the humanitarian campaign was at most a pretext or a sideshow. Appiah, by contrast, argues that big moral changes—he uses dueling, footbinding, and Atlantic slavery as cases—turn on shifts in what a society considers honorable or shameful, which is a different kind of thing from either rational argument or economic interest. I'm wondering whether these two accounts actually conflict or whether they're operating at different levels: maybe Williams is explaining the political and economic timing of abolition while Appiah is explaining the change in moral sentiment and status. Which account handles the slavery case better, and where does each one strain? Also, I'd like you to keep straight what's fairly settled versus what's genuinely contested here—for instance, I gather the empirical claim that Caribbean slavery was in decline when Britain abolished it is itself disputed—and not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
