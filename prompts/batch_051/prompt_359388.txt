Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether petitionary prayer to an omniscient and immutable God makes sense, and we ended further apart than we began. Since I suspect our deadlock is not personal but structural, I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself.

His case was the classical one. An omniscient God cannot be informed by a request, because he already knows every need and desire before it is spoken. An immutable God cannot be moved by a request, because his will and plan are already fixed. So petitionary prayer is either redundant, because it asks God to do what he was already going to do, or incoherent, because it asks him to do what an unchangeable will cannot now be caused to do. The only legitimate leftovers he allowed were thanksgiving, confession, and the expressive value of saying things aloud.

He also rejected the standard theological reply before I could make it: that God has eternally decreed that some goods will be granted only in response to petition, so that prayer is a real but subordinate cause within an unchanging plan. He called this evasive, and his objection was not weak. If the decree already fixes exactly what will happen, then the prayer is as fixed as the outcome, and saying that the prayer causes the outcome within the plan seems to add nothing; if, on the other hand, the prayer genuinely introduces something not already fixed, then immutability has been given up. I felt the force of this, and it pushed me toward a different defense.

The defense I found myself giving was that petition is not primarily a mechanism for transmitting information or moving God at all. It is a practice by which the one who prays is ordered differently: one's dependence and desires are made explicit, one's will is brought into contact with God's will, and one takes up the stance of a child asking a parent rather than a client filing a request. His reply was immediate: then cease calling it petition. If nothing about the asking can make a difference to what God does, it is self-directed meditation dressed in the language of request, and the request is at best a piece of theater.

By then I suspected we were not answering the same question. He was evaluating a speech act by its causal output; I was evaluating a practice by its formation of the agent. And I noticed that this is also why the larger theological and philosophical debate has never settled: each side has a well-known set of moves, the moves do not touch each other, and the same standoff reappears. My working hypothesis is that the debate is really two debates sharing a name, one about whether a petition can play a causal role in what an immutable God does and another about what petitioning is for, and that the impasse comes from treating an answer to the second as an answer to the first. But I am not sure; it may instead be a substantive theological dispute about whether a request has a point only if it can affect the outcome, or a verbal dispute about what counts as a petition.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Engage the actual positions: what exactly must each side be committed to, and which commitment is secretly doing the work? Is the disagreement verbal, metaphysical, normative, or a disagreement about what kind of practice prayer is? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves it, an argument that one of the questions is idle, or a reason to expect the debate simply to continue? I am less interested in winning the argument than in knowing what kind of argument we were in.
