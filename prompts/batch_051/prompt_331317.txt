Im prepping for an episode of my philosophy of biology podcast, and I need you to act as an experienced interviewer who knows the gene's-eye view literature cold. My guest defends a modernized version of the gene's-eye view of evolution — the replicator/vehicle picture you get from Dawkins and George Williams, with genes as the stable units that persist across generations and organisms as the throwaway vehicles they build — but updated to survive decades of criticism. She's very sharp, and she has a stock move for every objection, so I want eight to ten interview questions that don't let her hide in them. Brainstorm her most likely replies before you finalize each question, and write the follow-up that anticipates her answer.

Cover at least these, each with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply:

1. The 'selfish gene' metaphor. Her line will be that it was never a claim about agency, just a way of tracking which level's fitness you count. Ask where a bookkeeping choice stops being innocent and becomes a causal claim, and how an outsider would catch it happening.
2. The levels-of-selection objection. Her standard reply is that gene-level and multilevel-selection accounts are intertranslatable, so you may as well use the cleaner one. Press on whether translatability at equilibrium is enough when the actual causal competition could be between groups, organisms, or genes — and ask what observation would make one description more than a convention.
3. The averaging worry. Critics have argued that treating the gene as the unit averages over contexts in a way that can misrepresent the causal structure of selection, for instance when a trait is favored between groups but disfavored within them. Ask her what makes gene-level fitness more than a weighted average, and when the averaging is legitimate.
4. Intragenomic conflict. If an organism is a coalition of genes whose interests mostly overlap but dont fully coincide — genes that bias their own transmission, genes expressed differently depending on which parent they came from — does the gene's-eye view explain the organism as a negotiated truce, or does it presuppose the very organism it's supposed to explain? Follow up on what the 'parliament of genes' picture predicts that a purely organism-level view wouldn't.
5. Plasticity and niche construction. Her obvious reply is that norms of reaction and constructed niches are themselves phenotypes, so all of this is still gene-led. Ask whether that move is falsifiable: is there any developmental or ecological result that would force her to demote the gene from its privileged spot, or is the view now compatible with everything?

Then end with a question about what evidence, if anything, would change her mind — and dont let her get away with a methodology lecture instead. If at any point she says a rival finding is 'just a re-description of the same facts,' make the follow-up force her to say what the re-description actually buys us.
