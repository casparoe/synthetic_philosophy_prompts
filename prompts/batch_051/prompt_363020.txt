An AI lab is trying to shift from outcome-based to process-based supervision of a reasoning model, on the theory that step-level feedback avoids rewarding lucky guesses and right answers reached by bad reasoning. It adopts the following scheme, and I want it analyzed as a mechanism rather than praised or condemned in the abstract.

Labelers are shown a prompt and a sample of the model's reasoning, with the final answer masked. While the answer is masked, each labeler marks every reasoning step as sound, unsound, or irrelevant, and records a prediction of whether the final answer will be correct. Only after submitting these judgments does the labeler see the answer. The model's reward signal is computed from the step labels alone; the actual outcome is quarantined and is never allowed into the model's training objective. The quarantined outcomes are used for exactly one purpose: computing a rolling process–outcome alignment score for each labeler. The score measures how well a labeler's step-level judgments and their masked-answer prediction track the revealed outcome — for example, whether solutions the labeler marks sound at every step tend to end in correct answers, and whether their correctness predictions are accurate. Labelers whose score falls below a threshold are removed from the pool; labelers above a higher threshold receive bonus pay and are assigned to the highest-stakes reasoning tasks.

Walk through what this does to the model's learned behavior, to the composition of the labeler pool, and to the relationship between what the process labels reward and what the quarantined outcomes show. Where does the system get gamed first — by the model or by the labelers? What second-order effects appear once the alignment score is the only use made of outcomes? Are the worst failure modes fixable without letting outcomes back into the model's reward?
