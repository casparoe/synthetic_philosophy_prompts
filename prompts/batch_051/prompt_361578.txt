I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I keep hearing assembly theory pitched as a way to spot life in weird places. The setup is this: pick a small alphabet of basic parts. To build an object, you join two parts at a time, and you may reuse any chunk you've already made. The assembly index of an object is the fewest joining steps needed to build it. A crystal scores low, since once you can make one unit cell you just repeat it. A long random string scores high, since nothing is worth reusing. A folded protein or a cell-like system scores very high. The subtle version of the claim is that the real tell of selection is finding many copies of a high-index object; the blunt version, that the index is simply what complexity is, I'm not entierly sold on. Write an essay weighing whether assembly index really captures complexity or mostly captures how hard an ideal tinkerer would have to work, and whether it trips on cases like a shuffled deck's exact order (high index, no selection) or a city built by dumb local rules. Reach a verdict and defend it; don't end on the fence.
