I'm a novelist, and I'm writing an essay for a small literary magazine about H. G. Wells's The Invisible Man, reading it against the ring of Gyges story in Plato's Republic — the old claim that if you could make yourself invisible and get away with anything, you'd be a fool not to, because morality is only fear of consequences. My working thesis is that Wells is actually closer to the Socratic answer than to Glaucon: Griffin's invisibility doesn't liberate him, it hollows him out; he becomes a voice without a face, and by the end the novel seems to say that the man who can act with impunity has lost the very thing that made his goals worth having. But I can already see two ways that reading is to clean. First, Griffin is cruel and grandiose before the experiment — he steals from his father and doesn't seem to care that the man kills himself — so maybe Wells is saying the ring doesn't corrupt, it reveals. Second, the novel spends so much of its energy on the comedy and panic in Iping that the moral spine might be weaker in practice than I want it to be. What I'd like is a reading that takes both the book and the Republic seriously: where does The Invisible Man genuinely dramatize Glaucon's challenge, where does it answer it, and where does it resist both readings? Point me to the scenes that carry the weight — the long confessional scene where Griffin explains his backstory, the final chase, whatever else you think matters — and tell me what Wells gets that Plato's thought experiment misses about seeing and being seen. I'm especially interested in whether being visible to others is doing moral work in the novel that the Republic can't quite express, and whether the book actually dramatizes that or just sneaks it in by making Griffin unsympathetic from the start. I don't need any secondary literature, just a close reading a novelist could use.
