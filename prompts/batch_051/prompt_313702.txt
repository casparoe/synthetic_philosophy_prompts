I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been made responsible for drafting the content-moderation taxonomy for a mid-sized social platform. I want to reason from Mill's harm principle, not from received industry practices. The passage I am anchoring on is from John Stuart Mill, On Liberty, Chapter II:

"But the peculiar evil of silencing the expression of an opinion, is, that it is robbing the human race; posterity as well as the existing generation; those who dissent from the opinion, still more than those who hold it. If the opinion is right, they are deprived of the opportunity of exchanging error for truth: if wrong, they lose, what is almost as great a benefit, the clearer perception and livelier impression of truth, produced by its collision with error."

Assuming it is reasonable to treat my platform's power over speech as a form of social coercion at scale, please describe the most important implications of this passage for how I should design the moderation taxonomy. I am less interested in whether the law permits removal than in whether Mill's argument gives a principled reason to prefer one removal policy over another.

Return your answer as a single JSON object with these fields:

"implications": an array of exactly six objects, sorted from most to least important. Each object must have:
- "implication": a concrete implication for a moderation rule, category, or default
- "textual_basis": the phrase or clause from the passage it most directly follows from
- "implementation_tension": the hardest part of encoding that implication in a real system
- "removal_direction": one of "more", "less", or "mixed" indicating whether following it tends toward more removal, less removal, or depends on context

"edge_cases": an array of exactly four objects applying the reasoning to specific speech categories. Each must have:
- "category": the name of the speech category, such as medical misinformation, coordinated disinformation, hateful abuse, or obvious falsehoods
- "millian_action": one of "remove", "downrank", "keep with context", or "no confident recommendation"
- "justification": two or three sentences applying the passage to that category

"limits": an array of exactly three objects identifying where the platform should hesitate to import Mill's argument because of disanalogies between nineteenth-century public debate and algorithmic distribution. Each must have:
- "disanalogy": the specific difference
- "effect_on_policy": how that difference should weaken, reverse, or complicate the Millian implication

"verdict": a one-paragraph summary of the most important implication of this passage for a software engineer designing a moderation taxonomy, stated in terms I could defend in a design review.
