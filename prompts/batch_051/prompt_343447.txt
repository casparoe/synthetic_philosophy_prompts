Recently my housemates and I started a stupid little accountability game. Each week I announce something I intend to do — gym three times, no phone after 11, finish the reading — and I have to put a probablity on whether I'll actually do it, then we settle up Sunday. The thing I keep running into is that the number seems to change the very thing it's predicting. Say 80 and I feel locked in; say 30 and I've basically given myself permission to skip. Worse, if I'm genuinely still deciding on Wednesday night, what would it even mean for me to be 70% on a choice that's still up to me? So I want the requirements straight before I read any more about this. Act as a professional decision theorist and philosopher of action. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming a bit before you commit to a final list. Then write the desiderata: what does an adequate theory of assigning probabilities to your own future voluntary choices actually have to get right? For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it — cover cases where the probability feeds back into the action, cases where your choice and a prediction about your choice are correlated without one causing the other (the boxes-puzzle kind of setup), cases of temptation and weakness of will, and cases where you're still deliberating. Also mark which distinctions such a theory can't collapse: predicting something you'll observe versus choosing something under your control, evidence versus cause, your view as the agent versus an outsider's view. Flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste, say which single requirement you think knocks out the most existing ways of handling this, and tell me if any subset of the list can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I care most about. And please skip citations and reading lists; I want the demands, not the sources.
