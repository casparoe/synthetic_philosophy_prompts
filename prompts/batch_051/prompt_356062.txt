I spent my working life as a research chemist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy in a small seminar of former scientists and physicians organized by our local university's continuing-education program. Next month it falls to me to lead a forty-minute session on epiphenomenalism about consciousness, and I want to build the session around what philosophers call the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if conscious experience makes no causal difference to anything physical, then our acts of judging and reporting that we have conscious experience — themselves physical events, and so, on this view, fully caused by physical antecedents — seem to be caused without the very experiences they are about playing any role in producing them. I am not asking for an explanation of the view; I have read enough to understand it. What I need is a plan for teaching it to this particular audience.

The group is scientifically literate and impatient with terminology, and its members will arrive with strong default assumptions: that of course the felt pain causes the hand to withdraw, that natural selection would not preserve a trait that does no causal work, and that any view with so counterintuitive a consequence must rest on a verbal trick. Please give me the sequence of ideas for the session, including what must be in place before the paradox can be made to bite; the single concrete example or case that should carry the discussion, and what it must have and what it must lack; the misconceptions the group will bring or form, with the precise points in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than argued down; what I should openly decline to resolve at this level; and a final question whose answer would tell me whether they have grasped the paradox rather than merely found it disagreeable.
