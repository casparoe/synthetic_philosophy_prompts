I've been going back and forth on LessWrong about the Machamer/Darden/Craver mechanistic explanation stuff, and I keep feeling like Leibniz's mill argument is a better stress test than most of the modern counterexamples people throw at it. Here's the passage from the Monadology, in the Latta translation, that I keep returning to:

"Moreover, it must be confessed that perception and that which depends upon it are inexplicable on mechanical grounds, that is to say, by means of figures and motions. And supposing there were a machine, so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, it might be conceived as increased in size, while keeping the same proportions, so that one might go into it as into a mill. That being so, we should, on examining its interior, find only parts which work one upon another, and never anything by which to explain a perception."

What I want is a concrete worked example, not another gloss of the debate. Pick a real biological mechanism—long-term potentiation, a circadian clock, bacterial chemotaxis, whatever—where the standard MDC parts-and-activities story is genuinely good, and then restate Leibniz's worry as a challenge to that story. Specifically: if someone says "when I look at the parts and their activities I never see anything by which to explain the target phenomenon," is that just a level-confusion, or does it expose a spot where the MDC account has to quietly change topic? Give me the parts/activities mapping for your mechanism, then test the mill argument against it, and tell me whether the right MDC-style reply is to reject Leibniz's demand, domesticate it as a demand for more organization, or admit the mechanism leaves something out. Also say how confident you are in your read and single out the point you're least sure about.
