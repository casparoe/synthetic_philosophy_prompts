I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I get the labels wrong. My next book has a civil suit and a public-health subplot running side by side, and I keep noticing that the word "cause" seems to be doing different work in the two settings, which is messing with my head.

In the courtroom chapters, everyone keeps asking the but-for question: would the plaintiff's injury have happened if the defendant hadnt done the thing — say, left a rotten stair rail up instead of replacing it? If the injury would not have happened without that act, the act counts as a cause. If it would have happened anyway, it doesn't. That's the default test for cause-in-fact. But there's an awkward patch lawyers bolt on: when two fires merge and burn down a barn, or two factories dump waste into the same river, each fire or factory can say "the harm would have happened anyway because of the other one," and then courts often fall back on a "substantial factor" test instead.

Meanwhile, the public-health people in my other subplot are much more finicky. They talk about causation in terms of interventions — they'll say smoking causes lung cancer not just because a world without smoking looks different, but because you can intervene on smoking (get people to quit, ideally in a randomized trial) and change the outcome. They dont like the bare delete-the-factor comparison; they want the comparison to be a surgical manipulation of one factor while holding the rest fixed.

So on one side we have a practice that defines cause by comparing to a world where the act is simply removed, and on the other side a practice that defines cause by comparing to a world where you actively intervene on a variable. Are these the same theory of causation wearing different hats, or do the two practices quietly presuppose different things? What does the but-for test actually commit a judge or jury to — a full counterfactual metaphysics of causation, or just a rough practical rule that could sit on top of several rival theories? And is the "substantial factor" escape hatch a sign that the legal practice is incoherent, or a sign that the but-for test was never meant as a theory of causation in the first place?

Reconstruct in prose the implicit theory or theories each practice presupposes, say which parts are strictly presupposed by the practice and which are merely compatible with it, and tell me what would have to change in a courtroom if we replaced the legal picture with the interventionist one the epidemiologists use.
