I ended up down a Wikipedia hole after a friend mentioned the American 'designated survivor' arrangement, and now I can't work out what the practice is actually committed to. The setup, as I understand it, is this: before a State of the Union or an inauguration, when the President and nearly the entire line of succession are gathered in one building, one Cabinet secretary is taken to a secure, undisclosed location, so that if the building is destroyed and everyone ahead of them is killed there will still be someone under the Presidential Succession Act who can be sworn in and keep the government going. This person has never been elected to the presidency, and in ordinary times the same system seems to treat election and inauguration as the entire basis of presidential authority. Yet the continuity plan seems to assume that, when it really matters, the thing that counts is that a constitutionally designated officeholder has survived, and that the military, civil service and courts will treat them as fully legitimate even though the electorate never had any say in it.

I keep swinging between two readings: either the practice presupposes that the continuity of the state is a value in its own right, one that overrides ordinary democratic authorisation in the extreme case, or it is just a bit of emergency risk management that almost any political theory could accept because the alternative — a total vacancy at the top — is worse under every view worth having. Which is it? Reconstruct the implicit view of legitimate authority that the practice rests on, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and then tell me what a continuity-of-government policy would have to look like if it were rebuilt on a strict view that only election by the living citizenry can confer legitimate power.
