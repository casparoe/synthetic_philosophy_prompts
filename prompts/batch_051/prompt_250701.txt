Here's something I've been turning over. The old liberal argument for mental privacy usually runs on a practical assumption: your mental life just isn't available to other people, so the state has no occasion to punish bare thoughts, and the law can keep its act requirement without ever having to face the question of whether it would punish thinking if it could. The standard line is that you don't get punished for thoughts alone; intentions matter only when they can be shown to accompany an act.

Now suppose non-invasive neural decoding gets genuinely decent: a portable EEG or near-infrared setup that can reconstruct the gist of a sentence you're silently rehearsing, or tell whether you've just pictured a particular face or object, without your cooperation. Not perfect, but usable enough that an employer or a police station could buy one and get a noisy readout of some of the thoughts passing through your head.

Does the old argument for mental privacy survive that? In particular: which premise does the development actually bear on? Is it the factual premise that thoughts are inaccessible, or the normative premise that the state shouldn't police thought even when it can? If the argument was quietly doing all its real work on the factual premise, then it dies the moment decoding arrives. If it can be repaired, give a repaired version that doesn't just reply that the tech isn't there yet and that still protects something once thoughts are readable — maybe in terms of cognitive liberty, mental self-determination, or the act/thought distinction itself. And tell me how confident you are in the verdict, and which claim in your answer you're least sure about.
