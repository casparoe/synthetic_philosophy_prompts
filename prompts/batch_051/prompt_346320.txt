I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and much of my recent work sits at the boundary of art, technology, and cultural value. I have been collecting my own judgments about originals, forgeries, and reproductions. Each verdict below feels right to me on its own, but I cannot find a single principle that captures them all, and I expect that some of my verdicts may need to be revised. I would like you to work out what principle, or small set of principles, best captures the pattern; to tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; and to apply the result to the case that most resists my intuitions.

First: a forger paints a picture in the manner of an eighteenth-century master and passes it off as a newly discovered work. For a decade it is exhibited, praised, and studied as genuine. Then the fraud is exposed. My judgment is that the painted surface is unchanged, but I no longer value the object in the same way. I would not travel to see it. I want it described not as part of the master's work but as part of the history of forgery. The exposure seems to have changed the object without changing its appearance.

Second: an artist, during her lifetime and without any deception, paints a second version of one of her own compositions for a later patron. The two are nearly indistinguishable to the unaided eye. My judgment is that I count the second as a genuine work by her and would hang it without embarrassment, but I find myself calling it weaker and more mechanical than the first, even though I could not defend that ranking by looking at the two surfaces side by side.

Third: a museum produces a physical facsimile of a famous drawing, matching its paper, pigments, and signs of age so closely that I cannot tell it from the original without instruments. My judgment is that the facsimile is useful and informative, but I still have not seen the drawing, and I would travel far to see the original. The original has something the facsimile lacks, but that something is not additional visual information, because the facsimile already supplies that.

Fourth: after a sculptor's death, her estate authorizes a foundry to cast a bronze from a plaster she made, according to processes she specified. Another cast of the same composition was made while she lived and was finished under her supervision. My judgment is that both are authentic in some practical sense, and both derive from her plaster, but I value the lifetime cast more and regard it as more fully the work, without being able to point to any visible difference that explains the ranking.

Fifth: yet I read a novel in an ordinary paperback and never feel that I have missed the novel, even though the book is a machine-made copy, set in a different typeface and printed long after the author's death. I feel no need to see the manuscript. The words seem to be the work in a way that the surface of a painting, apparently, is not.

Sixth: a beloved building is destroyed by fire and rebuilt with entirely new materials according to the architect's exact plans. My judgment is that the rebuilt structure is a reconstruction, not the same building, however faithful it is. I am glad it stands, but I do not think I have stood in the old building. This sits very uneasily with the fourth case, where I was willing to treat an object made after the artist's death from the artist's original as the work; the architect's plans play the role that the sculptor's plaster played, yet I reach opposite verdicts.

Seventh, the case I cannot settle: a model is trained on a living illustrator's published work and can produce, on request, new images in her style that she did not make and does not endorse. Some of these images are, to my eye, genuinely good. I do not think any particular element has been copied, and yet I share the illustrator's sense that something of hers has been taken. I also do not think the outputs are worthless. I am unable to say whether this is a claim about labor, about control over one's style, or the same underlying principle as my other verdicts, and I do not know which of my verdicts it should force me to reconsider.

Please propose the principle or principles that best unify these judgments, state precisely which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and apply it to the seventh case and to the comparison between the posthumous cast and the rebuilt building. A bare name for the phenomenon will not help; I am after the carving, including where its edges are.
