I am a science journalist, not a philosopher. I write for a serious general-interest outlet about empirical work in psychology, medicine, and the social sciences, and several times a month I have to decide whether a body of work or a source is reliable enough to report as likely true. The stakes are real but not symmetrical: if I overtrust, I dress up a false claim in my outlet's credibility; if I undertrust, I keep sound work from readers who would have been better off knowing about it.

The problem that keeps recurring is circularity. To check whether a researcher's method is sound, I read other researchers who use the same method, teach it, and have built careers on it. To check whether my own reading of the methods literature is right, I rely on the very background understanding I should be double-checking. To check whether a source is an authority, I usually have to accept that source's own account of its track record. Epistemologists describe something close to this as the bootstrapping problem, with the related worry about easy knowledge: a source can seem to license the conclusion that the source is reliable just by producing outputs that, taken at face value, say the source is reliable. I want to know which of these circular moves are harmless and which are vicious, and I want that in a form I can actually use on deadline.

Please write me a decision procedure I could run in a day or two, without formal statistical or philosophical training, for deciding when to treat a source or method as reliable enough to report. It should be a reusable procedure I could show an assigning editor who cares about consistency across stories, not about my private sense of which findings smell right. Give the questions in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say where the procedure stops with "no"; say where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of editorial values rather than evidence; and at each step be honest about where the procedure is doing real epistemic work and where it is just a judgment call made to look procedural. In particular, make explicit when the outputs of a source are allowed to count as evidence of that source's own reliability, and when they are not.

Then run the procedure on these three cases.

1. A respected lab posts a preprint reporting that a brief online intervention substantially reduced anxiety in a large sample. The effect estimate comes from an analytic method the lab developed itself, and the only evidence that the method is valid comes from the lab's own earlier papers applying the method to similar data. There is no independent replication. My instinct is to cover it with cautious enthusiasm because the lab is reputable and the effect size is large.

2. A group of economists reports, using a standard technique in the field, that a policy caused a large drop in unemployment. Statisticians from outside economics reply that the technique rests on assumptions that are seldom testable and are largely enforced by peer pressure within the discipline. The economists respond that the technique's long record of use inside the field is itself evidence that it works. I need to know whether that reply is a vicious circle.

3. A researcher reports a negative finding from an underpowered pilot study on a rare disease intervention. Standard checklists would say the study is too weak to report. But patient groups say it is currently the only relevant data in existence, and reporting it might change what researchers count as a live option. My instinct is to leave the study alone.

I expect the procedure to return answers I will not like, and that is part of the point. Tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: where even a correct procedure leaves the uncertainty in my lap.

If this request rests on a confused premise — for instance, I have misdescribed the bootstrapping problem or easy knowledge, or the demand for a procedure that cleanly separates harmless from vicious circularity is itself confused — say so outright instead of complying politely, and then do the closest thing that is defensible.
