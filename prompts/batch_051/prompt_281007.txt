I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist before retiring, and I have only lately begun reading philosophy and theology with the care they require. I keep returning to a question on which my training and my recent reading pull in different directions. I would like you to answer it as a reasoned projection, not as a report of settled scholarship.

Augustine of Hippo held that the original creation was good and that death and corruption entered the human condition through the first human sin. He never had to confront the modern natural record: predation, parasitism, disease, injury, and extinction are ancient features of the living world, present for hundreds of millions of years before any human being existed. The fossil record preserves dinosaurs with healed fractures, bone infections, and tumors; mass extinctions such as the end-Permian event eliminated most of the species then alive; and in many living species the majority of offspring die before reproducing, sometimes slowly consumed from within by parasitoid larvae. These are not fringe findings; they are part of the ordinary furniture of evolutionary biology.

I want you to predict how Augustine would most plausibly respond if this record were laid before him accurately and in detail. Take as fixed the commitments he is standardly read as holding: a good and non-deceiving Creator, a real Fall, and the entry of death into human life through sin. Do not assume he would simply abandon those commitments, and do not assume he would simply deny the evidence. Given his habits of thought—his drive to distinguish, to reconcile Scripture with what careful study of nature shows, and to preserve both divine goodness and human responsibility—where would he most likely draw his lines, and which line would cost him the most? Consider in particular whether he could separate human death from the mortality of other creatures, whether he could treat postlapsarian corruption as distinct from the ordinary fragility of finite created life, and whether he could regard animal suffering as a permitted feature of an ordered creation rather than an evil God must have excluded at the outset. Then state as concretely as you can the position you think he would actually defend, and indicate how much, if anything, it would force him to revise.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Also say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
