I'm on my city's data protection commission and we're trying to reverse-engineer the rule we've actually been applying, because our decisions have been all over the place. The city charter just says residents have a right to "privacy and informational self-determination," but also that the city has to provide public safety and efficient services, which is not exactly a algorithm. The commission is split in a way that doesn't help: one member keeps invoking German-style informational self-determination and data minimization; another is more American about it, saying what happens in public is public and the important thing is that the government's own use of data be transparent and contestable; a third keeps hinting that well-publicized scoring systems for nuisance behavior, of the kind you see in Chinese social credit pilots, have made the city more orderly and should be extended.

Our precedents, then: we allowed the transit agency to release aggregated, de-identified fare-card data to a private developer for a trip-planning app after a re-identification audit; we refused to let the police get a live, warrantless feed of apartment-lobby cameras, on the grounds that continuously watching a building's comings and goings is exactly what the charter exists to prevent, while saying we had no objection to the landlord keeping the recordings for building security; we approved a school district's software that scans student emails and documents for self-harm keywords, so long as alerts go to counselors rather than police and the raw logs are deleted after 30 days; we rejected a mall's plan for facial-recognition screens in common areas to target ads, since shoppers can't realistically avoid the mall and so can't meaningfully consent; and we let the housing department run a 'good tenant' points pilot in public housing, where on-time rent and no noise complaints earn points toward small rent discounts, provided the formula is public, tenants can appeal entries, and individual records are deleted after a year.

Next month we have to rule on whether the city can require all taxis and ride-share vehicles to run inward-facing cameras whenever a passenger is in the car, storing footage for 72 hours, with police able to get access after the fact with a warrant, or immediately in an emergency. The city says it's about driver and passenger safety; drivers say it will be used to discipline them; some riders say a cab is a private conversation on wheels. The chair wants me to draft the memo we'll debate: what should we decide if we're trying to be consistent with our own track record, which of our past rulings is the closest analog and why, and if our precedents don't force a single answer, what principle would we be adopting with each option — and what further case would most cleanly separate the candidate rules?
