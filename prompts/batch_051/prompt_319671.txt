The exam question was: 'Frege notes that identity statements of the form a=a are trivial and knowable a priori, whereas true statements of the form a=b can be informative and a posteriori. Explain the puzzle this raises, and explain how Frege's distinction between sense and reference is meant to solve it, using the morning star/evening star example.'

Here's the student's answer (lightly cleaned up for spelling):

'Frege's puzzle starts from the fact that a=a is knowable a priori and trivial, while a true identity of the form a=b can be a posteriori and informative. If a=b is true, then a and b refer to the very same object, so the sentence seems to be saying only that the object is itself—which should make it no more informative than a=a. Frege's example brings this out: "the morning star = the morning star" is empty, but "the morning star = the evening star" recorded a genuine astronomical discovery, even though both terms pick out Venus.

Frege's solution is that expressions have both a reference and a sense. The reference is the object, while the sense is a mode of presentation, a way the object is given to a speaker. "The morning star" presents Venus as the bright body visible in the morning, and "the evening star" presents Venus as the bright body visible in the evening. The reason the identity is informative is that the two names differ in sense even though they agree in reference. Senses are subjective ideas in the mind of each speaker, which explains why the statement can be new to one person and old news to another. So when someone discovers that the morning star is the evening star, the thought they grasp is just the trivial thought that Venus is Venus; the real discovery concerns the signs, not the world.'
