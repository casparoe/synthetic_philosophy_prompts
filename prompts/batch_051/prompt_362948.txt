I'm teaching a course on comparative religion this term, and I've hit a problem I can't seem to settle: every definition of religion I try either quietly assumes something like a creator god or an organized clergy, or it turns out to include things I'm confident aren't religions and exclude things I'm confident are. I'd like a working definition of religion, but I want it tested, not just stated. Would you propose a definition and then run it through the cases below, telling me which ones it classifies cleanly, which ones it gets wrong, and whether the wrong answers should push me to revise the definition or to abandon my initial verdict about the case?

The cases I keep going back to are these. (1) A lay Buddhist in the Theravada tradition who keeps precepts, venerates the Buddha, and neither affirms nor denies a creator god; I would call this a religion. (2) A Confucian household that maintains an ancestral altar, performs seasonal rites, and studies the classics, but has no doctrine of gods; I lean toward calling this a religion, but a colleague insists it is an ethical tradition, and I can't say what the difference consists in. (3) A secular congregation that meets on Sunday mornings for music, a talk about living meaningfully, shared meals, and a code of mutual care, with no supernatural commitments of any kind; I would say this is not a religion. (4) The civic rituals of a modern nation-state—flag ceremonies, national holidays, war memorials, oaths of office; I would also say these are not a religion, though I can't deny they resemble one. (5) A twelve-step recovery group whose members are asked to turn their will over to a higher power, which many of them take to be the group itself rather than anything divine; this one genuinely stumps me. (6) A solitary person who meditates each morning and keeps a small altar to deceased relatives, but belongs to no organized community and makes no claims about how the universe is put together; I have no settled view. (7) The public rites of ancient Rome, in which holding office required venerating the city's gods and the deified emperor as a civic duty; I want to call this a religion, but it sits uncomfortably close to case 4.

If the right answer is that the word religion does two different jobs—say, one marking something about the sacred or supernatural and another marking something about community, ritual, and meaning-making—then give me both definitions and tell me which of the seven cases stay put, which move, and which refuse to be classified either way. I'm not after a taxonomy; I'm after a definition that can survive contact with the cases I actually have to teach.
