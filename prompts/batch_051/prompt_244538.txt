I have been teaching a course on African American intellectual history, and I keep returning to W. E. B. Du Bois's mid-1920s claim that all art is propaganda and that Black artists should willingly direct their art toward the struggle for racial justice. The argument feels compelling to me as a systematic position: every artwork, even one that presents itself as apolitical, is produced under historical conditions that shape what it can say and to whom; the demand to keep art pure is itself a political demand, usually made by people who can afford not to notice the politics. So Du Bois's conclusion seems basically sound: the relevant question is not whether art is propaganda, but whose propaganda it is.

And yet I have a firm intuition I cannot shake. When I read a poem whose only evident aim is to render a private grief precisely, or look at a quilt whose power comes from its formal invention rather than its message, it feels wrong to judge it primarily by whether it advances a collective cause. I do not just mean that such work has some pre-political aesthetic merit on the side. I mean that treating it as an instrument for uplift seems to miss what the work is, in a way that is not merely a prejudice left over from the art-for-art's-sake tradition Du Bois was arguing against. Maybe my intuition is exactly the thing his argument should debunk; maybe it tracks a real distinction between art that openly serves a cause and art whose politics lie precisely in its refusal to be useful. I do not want a summary of Du Bois. I want a negotiated verdict: what would it take for my intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for Du Bois's instrumentalism to be amended or abandoned, and is there a reading on which both survive?
