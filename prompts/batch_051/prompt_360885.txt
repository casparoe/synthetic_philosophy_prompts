I am a PhD student in political theory, writing a dissertation chapter on how local governments govern the procurement of automated decision-making systems. I have been working through Langdon Winner's essay Do Artifacts Have Politics, and I should like to test one of its arguments against a current practice.

The passage I am thinking about comes from the part where Winner distinguishes two ways in which technical things can be political. He first describes cases in which a specific technical arrangement was chosen in order to produce a particular social outcome, such as an agricultural machine whose design favoured large growers over smallholders, or a public facility arranged to admit some groups and not others. He then turns to the stronger claim that some technologies are political in themselves: their successful operation requires, or is at least overwhelmingly compatible with, a particular kind of social order. He offers large-scale, high-risk technical systems such as nuclear power as examples, arguing that they cannot be run without centralised, hierarchical, security-minded management, quite apart from anyone's intentions or ownership.

The practice I want to examine is the increasingly common requirement that municipalities carry out an algorithmic impact assessment before purchasing or deploying an automated decision-support system for housing allocation, welfare administration, or policing. The assessment typically requires the authority to specify the system's purpose, the affected groups, the expected social consequences, and the safeguards that will be put in place. The practice is often defended publicly as a neutral, procedural check on bad design.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about the politics of artefacts? Specifically, does it presuppose Winner's stronger thesis that some technologies are intrinsically political, or does it remain compatible with a view on which technology is neutral until human agents inject political values into it? If the practice presupposes the strong thesis, which part of the assessment commits it to that? If it does not, then exactly where would Winner object that the practice is philosophically underdescribed? And in practice, would a public authority that took Winner's strong thesis seriously have to change the design of an impact assessment, or only the rhetoric around it?

I should be grateful for an answer in British English.
