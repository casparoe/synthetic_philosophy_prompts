I facilitate a monthly discussion group for adults at a public library. The members are thoughtful and argumentative, but mostly without formal philosophical training, and I have found that the moment I start lecturing I lose the room. Lately I have been circling an idea I want them to experience rather than hear about: some hard choices are hard not because we lack information, and not because the options are nearly equal, but because the options draw on values that will not line up on a single scale. I want to stage the phenomenon so that the group runs into it themselves before anyone names it.

Please design a ninety-minute activity for about sixteen adults in a room with chairs, tables, and a whiteboard. The deliverable should be something participants actually do: a structured forced-choice exercise, a ranking task, or a deliberation under constraints. I am open to the exact case, but it should be one where each option serves a different value and no option is obviously worse than the others on all counts — for example, choosing the single use of a publicly owned vacant building as a clinic, a rehearsal space for young musicians, a food pantry, or a shelter, though feel free to use a better case if one occurs to you. No one should need to read anything beforehand, and I should have to say very little while the exercise runs.

The rules should be arranged so that participants first make a series of choices comfortably, then hit the phenomenon from the inside. In particular, build in a round where participants compare options in pairs after having committed to an earlier overall ranking, and then are asked to add a small amount of one value to the option they did not choose. They should be able to discover that the added increment does not reliably change what they choose, even though they are unwilling to call the original options equal. Then have them try to assign numerical weights to the values at stake and use those weights to predict their own earlier choices; the scoring system should fail to reproduce what they actually chose, in ways that feel revealing rather than merely confusing. Specify the materials (cards, handouts, markers, or whatever is needed), the exact rules for each round, and the short prompts I give between rounds.

Then write the debrief as a sequence of moves: which moments in the activity I should point back to, in what order, and how to name the distinction in plain language without burying the group in jargon — something like the difference between one option being better than another, the options being equal, and a third possibility in which neither is better and yet they are not equal. The debrief should get participants to see that the discomfort they felt is information about the structure of the choice, not evidence that they were being irrational or indecisive.

I also want a short section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: which instructions people will misread, where the discussion will wander, which conclusions they will jump to (for example, that they were simply ignorant of the facts, that all choices are subjective, or that the exercise shows no options can ever be compared), and how I should respond in the room.

Finally, be explicit about what the activity cannot get participants to feel and what I will have to supply by speaking directly — for instance, whether the exercise can distinguish between values themselves being unrankable and merely the limits of the particular decision procedure they used. I would rather overstate that gap than have the group walk away thinking the exercise proved more than it did.
