Just spent an hour arguing with a friend about the wrong kind of reasons problem, and he left me with an argument I can't find my way out of. Its one of those where every step sounds fine and the conclusion is clearly insane:

(1) Suppose a demon threatens to break my legs unless I admire him. Then the demon has the property being such that, if I admire him, my legs stay intact. That's a property of the demon, not a property of my mental state.

(2) That property counts in favor of admiring him. If someone had the property being such that admiring them would save your life, you'd obviously have a reason to admire them.

(3) So the threat gives me a reason that is about the demon himself, not just a reason to manipulate myself into an admiring state.

(4) A standard move in the wrong kind of reasons literature is to distinguish object-given reasons, which bear on the object, from state-given reasons, which bear on the attitude or its consequences.

(5) By that test, the demon's threat is object-given. So it is a right-kind reason.

(6) Right-kind reasons are exactly the ones that make an attitude fitting. So admiring the demon is fitting, which is to say the demon is genuinely admirable.

(7) But that's absurd: a threat cannot make someone admirable. So the object-given/state-given distinction collapses, and maybe there's no real difference between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons at all.

I keep trying to say the slide is somewhere between (3) and (5), but he just dares me to name the property that does the real work if not the one in (1). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it force me to take on?
