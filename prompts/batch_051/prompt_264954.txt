I'm trying to work out what I actually think about achievement and excellence, going by my gut on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know what principle gets them, or which of my verdicts I should drop.

Here are the cases. A mathematician spends years on a proof, finishes it, and then dies; her notebooks are destroyed and nobody ever reads it. I think she achieved something real, and that it was good that she did it, even though no one ever got anything out of it. A man memorizes every phone number in the Philadelphia directory and can recite them without error; I don't think that's an achievement in the same way, even though it's harder than basically anything I admire. An amateur golfer hits a hole-in-one on a blind shot with terrible form; I don't count it as an achievement, the result just happened to him. A chess player works out a beautiful novelty in a private game that nobody else ever sees; I think it's excelent anyway. A surgeon performs a new procedure brilliantly and saves a life, but she does it only for the fee and actually despises the patient; I still think the operation was excellent, and it was good that it happened. But a pianist with perfect technique who feels nothing for the music and only plays to win a bet: I don't think that's an achievement, or at least not an excellent one, even though the playing is flawless.

The pair that bothers me is the mathematician and the phone-book guy. Both are hard, both succeed at what they set out to do, and neither helps anyone else, but I want to call one an achievement and not the other. Is there a principled line between them? And can one principle get all my verdicts, or do some of them have to go? What does your best account say about a case I keep flip-flopping on: someone who becomes genuinely world-class at a craft as a kid, purely to beat their sibling, then gives it up forever and never uses or enjoys it again -- was it good that they became that good?
