I chair the terminology comittee of a standards development organization that publishes safety and performance standards for consumer equipment. Five or six naming proposals reach us each month, and once a term is printed in a standard it is almost impossible to change, so we'll be living with the choice for decades. Most of us are engineers, not linguists, and a term usually gets about 45 minutes of debate before we have to vote; I'm the one who has to write up the committee's recommendation for the full board afterwards. We keep going in circles about whether new technical concepts should get names coined from Greek or Latin roots or plain-English phrases. Some members want the classical compounds because they feel precise, stable, and international; others think that kind of jargon just shuts people out. I'd like you to turn whatever principle is doing the work in that disagreement into a decision procedure we can actually run. Please write it as a checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a good-enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a 'no,' and where it hands the decision to a person because what's left is a values call rather than a factual question. Then run the procedure on these three proposals: (1) a term for a failure mode in a high-pressure valve that will appear only in inspection manuals and engineering standards; (2) a term for a fee charged when a customer cancels a service contract early, which will appear on consumer bills; (3) a term for when two prescribed drugs interact in a way that makes one of them ineffective, which will appear in both a professional guideline and a patient medication guide. At least one of the three should produce an answer my committee won't like — dont smooth it over. And say plainly what the procedure cannot decide for us no matter how carefully we use it.
