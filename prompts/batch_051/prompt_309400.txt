You're a professional philosopher who works on exploitation and the ethics of markets. I want you to think carefully about a bunch of gut reactions I have, and tell me whether there's a clean principle behind them. Start by brainstorming a few candidate principles and stress-testing each one against my cases, then settle on whichever principle—or small set of principles—you think is best. If some of my verdicts have to be dropped or revised, say so honestly. I care more about getting a precise principle than about preserving every one of my gut calls.

Here are my cases and verdicts.

1. The garment factory. A factory in Dhaka pays the local equivalent of $2.50 a day for 11-hour shifts, six days a week, and the fire exits are chained shut. The workers are women who took the job voluntarily because their alternatives—scavenging, going back to their villages—are worse by their own lights. If the factory closed, they'd be worse off. The owners could raise wages substantially and still make a profit, but they don't. I think the owners are exploiting the workers, and that what they do is wrong, even though the workers benefit relative to their next-best option.

2. The hurricane water. After a hurricane cuts off the water supply, a store sells bottled water at $18 a bottle, up from $1. Everyone who buys is better off buying than not buying. I think this is wrong and exploitative.

3. The tourist and the bracelet. A tourist in a poor beach town haggles a souvenir seller down from $12 to $4. The seller still makes about a dollar on the deal and is glad to sell at $4 rather than not sell. My gut says the bargaining is fine—not exploitation—even though the tourist is clearly using the fact that the seller can't easily find another buyer today.

4. The clinical trial. A drug company runs a trial in a low-income country. Participants enroll because otherwise they'd get no treatment for their illness at all. The trial gives them the experimental drug and basic monitoring, but no post-trial access, and much less ancillary care than they'd get in a US trial. I think it's exploitative, and I lean toward it being impermissible, but I'm less confident than I am about the factory.

5. The kidney sale. A desperately poor man sells a kidney to a rich foreigner through a broker. The seller gets $3,000, which changes his family's life; the buyer gets a kidney; the broker takes a big cut. The seller would do it again and says he was better off after. I think it was exploitative, and that the broker at least did something seriously wrong—probably the buyer too. But I can't fully say why, since everyone ended up better off and consented.

6. The decent factory. Another garment factory in the same country pays slightly above the local average but far below what it could afford, caps shifts at nine hours, and runs a clinic and a school for workers' kids. Workers are much better off there than they would be anywhere else. I don't think this company is exploiting its workers—or if it is, it's not the kind of exploitation I object to.

The pair that keeps bugging me is the tourist haggling versus the first factory, and maybe the water seller too. All those deals are voluntary, both parties know the seller or worker is in a tight spot, and the person on the weak side still ends up better off than their next-best option. But I want to say the tourist is fine and the factory owner and water seller are wrong. That tension is exactly what I need a principle to sort out.

My own rough guess is something like: exploitation is wrong when you use someone's bad fallback options to get them to accept terms that are unfair, where unfair means something like worse than what a minimally decent version of the relationship would give them. But that seems to just move the question to what counts as a decent baseline, and I suspect it would end up condemning the tourist, and maybe even the decent factory.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State the principle precisely, explain which clause or feature handles which case, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up if any, and then apply it to a case I haven't listed: a labor broker in Vietnam who recruits women for jobs like the first factory and charges each of them a month's wages for the placement. Is he exploiting them?
