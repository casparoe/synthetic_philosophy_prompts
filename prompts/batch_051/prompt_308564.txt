I work on probabilistic forecasting and machine learning systems, and I think in terms of model classes, likelihoods and posterior predictives, calibration and proper scoring rules, epistemic versus aleatoric uncertainty, distribution shift, and the difference between a fitted model's probabilities and whatever process generates the data. I want to understand a debate in the philosophy of probability from inside that vocabulary, not have it explained away in it.

Here is the material I want translated. In the philosophy of probability there is a principle, usually called the Principal Principle, which says that a rational agent's degree of belief in a proposition, conditional on the proposition that the objective chance of that proposition is x, and on any further evidence that is admissible, should be x. Admissible evidence is roughly evidence that bears on the proposition only by bearing on its chance: a record of how chancy the proposition already is counts, while the testimony of a reliable crystal ball does not. The principle is meant to capture the idea that rational credence defers to objective chance. Around it there is a long-running dispute with at least these moving parts.

First, there is the question of how much work the admissibility clause is doing, and whether the principle is a substantive constraint on rational belief or merely a consistency condition on two ways of quantifying uncertainty. Second, there is the undermining problem. If chances are not primitive but are fixed by the total pattern of actual outcomes — the chance of an event is whatever the best systematization of the whole history says it is, in something like the way a model is selected to compress a data stream — then the proposition that the chance of A is x is itself evidence about how the future turns out. A future in which the outcomes are arranged in a way that the chance function, together with the principle, assigned a very low probability is an undermining future. If an agent has independent evidence that such a future is coming, then conditionalizing on that evidence should drive her credence away from the chance, and the principle and ordinary updating come apart. This pressure has produced proposed repairs to the principle; I care less about reconstructing any specific repair than about why the problem arises and what the repairs are trying to preserve. Third, the dispute sits on a deeper divide: on one side, chance is treated as a primitive, mind-independent propensity that the world simply has; on the other, it is treated as a compact summary of the actual pattern of events, with no further modal force. The undermining problem bites mainly for the second view.

I would like the following. First, restate the principle and each of these moves in my vocabulary: treat degrees of belief as the outputs of a probabilistic model, objective chance as the true data-generating process or the best model of the environment, admissible evidence as conditioning only on information already captured by that process, and the undermining problem as whatever happens when the process you defer to is itself an estimate drawn from the data you are trying to predict. Be explicit about where the mapping is doing real work and where it is just renaming one kind of uncertainty as another. Second, give me a residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts translate only with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Third, deliver a verdict on the residue. Where the translation breaks down, is that breakdown a limitation of the forecasting vocabulary — something the philosophers can say that my tools make it hard to see — or is it a sign that the original claims were doing their work through ambiguity, and that once they are forced to say exactly which feature of a prediction problem is being constrained, the philosophical debate resolves into several distinct engineering questions with different answers?

Finally, tell me how confident you are in what you have said, and pick out the specific point you are least sure of.
