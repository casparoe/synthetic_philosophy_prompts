I am a PhD student in economics, and next month I have to lead a single eighty-minute session on the Allais paradox and the independence axiom for an interdisciplinary reading group — mostly philosophers, political theorists, and legal scholars, with two economics students. They have already worked through expected utility theory well enough to state the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and compute expected utilities, but they still treat the independence axiom as a technical condition with no intuitive moral, and I suspect half of them think the whole subject is normatively overengineered. I want the session to make the paradox do real philosophical work: by the end they should see the Allais choices not as evidence that people are irrational or that expected utility is empirically false, but as a precise challenge to one axiom — the independence axiom — and thereby to the idea that a single separable preference relation should govern choice over gambles at all.

Do not explain expected utility or the Allais paradox to me; plan how I should teach it. I need the dependency structure: which distinctions and principles must be in place before the paradox can even register as a paradox (I assume something like acts versus outcomes, the common-consequence or sure-thing reading of independence, and the difference between an axiom being violated by a subject and an axiom being put under normative pressure), and why each must precede the next. Then give me the one carrying example. I would rather not lead with the standard Allais numbers — the certain $1m versus an 89/10/1 gamble over $1m/$5m/$0, and the matched pair that replaces the common 89% chance of $1m with nothing — if a different pair does the teaching better; if the standard pair really is the right carrier, defend using it anyway. Specify what the example must have and must lack: the magnitudes, the probabilities, the number of branches, and how the common consequence should be presented so that “cancelling” it feels like a legitimate move rather than a trick. Then give me the misconception map, in the order these will actually arise, and where in the sequence each should be headed off: I expect “it is just diminishing marginal utility or risk aversion,” “independence means the lotteries are probabilistically independent, and in Allais they are not,” “this only shows people are inconsistent and a moment of reflection would fix it,” and the opposite, “the axioms are normative, so the choice data is beside the point.” For each, say how the sequence should absorb it instead of dropping it. End with a single check-for-understanding question I can pose in the last ten minutes that would distinguish “can recite the paradox” from “sees how it puts real pressure on independence,” and tell me explicitly what I should refuse to cover at this level (I am guessing the full representation theorem, rank-dependent and prospect-theoretic repairs, and the finer structure of the common-ratio effect). The session must be self-contained from the material I present; do not assign readings and do not mention literature.
