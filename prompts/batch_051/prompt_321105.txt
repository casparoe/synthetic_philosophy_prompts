I have been working with a distinction I call incapacity-ineffability versus impropriety-ineffability, and I want an honest assessment of whether it can carry the weight I put on it. Incapacity-ineffability is the standard reading of 'unsayable': language cannot represent the object, because any predication splits what is unified or any description misses what is essential. Impropriety-ineffability is different: language can do the representing, but the practice rules out certain uses of the resulting sentences — producing a description substitutes labeling for the attention the practice requires, and treating it as a report is itself a misstep within the discipline. When a tradition says nothing true can be said of the ultimate, I read it as forbidding a use of language rather than confessing a limit of language. My claim is that most familiar cases of mystical ineffability are impropriety cases misread as incapacity cases, and this bears directly on the question whether what cannot be said can be known: what is improper to say can still be known, even propositionally, but cannot be turned into an instruction or a report without corrupting the practice. I realize this runs together semantics, pragmatics, and the sociology of religious language. Is the distinction a real one, or have I merely redescribed an existing contrast? What are the strongest cases for the incapacity reading, and do they defeat the view or only a flattened version of it?
