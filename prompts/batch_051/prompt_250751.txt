I'm giving the five-minute tribute at the retirement dinner of the longtime director of a neighborhood settlement house that is about to be absorbed into a citywide human-services agency over her objection. For thirty years she argued — with funders, with social workers, with me — that belonging is not a delivery mechanism for health, safety, or social mobility: a community is a final good, and when one dissolves something is lost that cannot be replaced by a better-funded program or by staff staying in touch. She also drew a line she cared about even more: what is lost is not just a set of one-to-one friendships, which could in principle survive dispersal, but the community itself — the shared "we" that planned, forgave, kept obligations, and had standing to call on its members. The room will include her staff and the families who used the house (many of whom speak in exactly the outcomes language she spent her life resisting), her funders, and two moral philosophers who will notice whether the toast keeps final and instrumental value apart. Please write the toast. It must honor her without once praising the house for its measurable results — that would be the one way to dishonor her. It must be moving and intelligible to people who have never heard the word "instrumental." And it must not pretend that the community survives its dissolution just because the members keep in touch. Begin with the toast itself, with no preface, no title, and no commentary before or after.
