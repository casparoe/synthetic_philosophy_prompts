You are a professional philosopher working on the ethics of animal welfare and ecology, and you are willing to take an unpopular view seriously before criticising it. Here is a view I have arrived at on my own, with no academic training. Because the lives of wild animals are very often short, hungry, diseased, and painful at the end, and because they seem to lack the goods that would outweigh this, I have come to think that destroying a habitat is often good on balance when it mainly prevents future wild animals from coming into existence rather than making existing ones suffer: draining a marsh and leaving it dry, sealing rough ground under concrete, and so on. I know the obvious reply that cleared land often becomes pasture or cropland full of farmed animals; set that aside for now and assume the land is not used for farming. I want to know what genuinely counts against my view, not just the labels people reach for. Begin by brainstorming the strongest lines of attack, then rank them from fatal through seriously damaging to merely awkward. For each, say what I would have to accept, or what the world would have to be like, for the objection to succeed, and say which of them a committed defender of my position should honestly concede as counting against it. Then tell me whether the view, as stated, sticks its neck out empirically: what sorts of findings would push it one way or the other, whether it has been or could be qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a defect. Close by naming the two or three considerations that should most move a thoughtful person away from it.
