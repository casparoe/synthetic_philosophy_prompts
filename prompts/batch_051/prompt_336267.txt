I teach a logic course where I use the MU-puzzle, and I've caught myself generalizing its lesson into a personal policy about arguments, and I want to know if the generalization is sound. The puzzle: start with the string MI and try to reach MU using only four rules—append a U to any string ending in I; double whatever follows an M; replace III with U; delete UU. After some fiddling inside those rules, the move that actually solves it is to jump out of the system and look for an invariant: the number of I's is never divisible by 3, so MU, with no I's at all, is unreachable. Jumping out is the right move there, and I can prove it after the fact.

Here is the policy as I actually use it: when a disagreement is looping inside a shared vocabulary, I stop engaging with the moves and start diagnosing the vocabulary instead—what can and cannot be said in it, what counts as a move, what hidden invariant keeps everyone stuck. Last week I did it in a long argument about whether a colleague's grading scheme was fair: after an hour of going in circles, I announced that the word fair was doing the trapping and refused to keep arguing in those terms. I treat the judgment that someone's vocabulary cannot even state the problem as a strong reason to stop arguing inside that vocabulary.

My worry is that in the MU-puzzle the jump is verifiable because I can prove the target string is unreachable, whereas in a live argument I cannot prove the other side's vocabulary is a trap; and I have noticed I reach for the move mostly when I am frustrated or loosing. So: is this habit defensible as a standing epistemic policy, or is it a bias wearing a systems-theoretic costume? Under what conditions is jumping out genuinely truth-tracking, and under what conditions is it just exit with an intellectual pedigree? And if the policy needs fixing, give me the fixed version rather than just telling me to be more careful.

Answer in five sections, in this order: (1) The policy, stated cleanly; (2) The case that it's defensible; (3) The case that it's self-serving; (4) How to tell the two apart from the inside; (5) Bottom line. Aim for roughly 100 words for sections 1 and 5, 200 for 2 and 3, and 150 for 4.
