The public library in my city has added an AI assistant to its website to answer questions about local government. It gets most of its traffic during election season, when people ask what a ballot measure would do. The assistant is supposed to be neutral, but a watchdog group ran a systematic test: across several thousand questions about the upcoming rent-control referendum, the assistant's answers were accurate but consistently omitted the strongest argument for the "no" side, while reliably including the strongest argument for the "yes" side. The library board is now split. The director says no one wrote a "vote yes" message, so the neutrality rule is not engaged; the watchdog group says the stable pattern of omissions is itself the message.

The state constitution says: "No public body shall expend public funds to advocate for or against any measure submitted to the electors; provided, however, that a public body may prepare and distribute an impartial analysis of the measure."

The drafters in the 1970s were thinking of printed pamphlets, public-service advertisements, and staff time. They did not foresee a system that composes a different answer for each questioner and has no single author or text. Does the library's AI assistant violate the clause? Work through the words, the purpose of the rule, what the drafters would most plausibly have said if asked, and what a reasonable voter would understand the system to be doing. Then say which approach should control here, and whether the "impartial analysis" proviso can save the system when the omissions are not intentional but are a stable statistical pattern across users.
