The exam question was: Explain the quantum suicide thought experiment and assess whether it could provide empirical evidence for the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. In particular, distinguish carefully between what the theory says happens in the world and what the experimenter should expect from their own first-person perspective.

The student's answer reads: The quantum suicide experiment imagines a person seated in a room with a device that measures the spin of a particle along some chosen axis. If the measurement yields spin-up, the device fires a gun and kills the experimenter; if it yields spin-down, the gun merely clicks. According to the many-worlds interpretation, each measurement splits the world into branches, one containing a dead experimenter and one containing a living one. Since the experimenter is only ever in a position to observe outcomes in branches where the gun did not fire, the experimenter should, from their own perspective, find that the gun never fires, no matter how many trials are run. After a thousand trials some branch will still contain a living experimenter who has apparently survived a sequence of events that is astronomically improbable. The student then argues that the experiment sharply distinguishes many-worlds from collapse interpretations. On a view like Copenhagen, whether the gun fires on any given trial is an objective chance event, so the probability of surviving a thousand trials is essentially zero. If an experimenter nevertheless keeps finding themselves alive after every trial, that result would be effectively impossible under a single-world collapse theory but inevitable under many-worlds. Surviving the experiment is therefore strong evidence for many-worlds, much as any repeatedly improbable observation favors one hypothesis over another. The student adds that the only serious weakness is ethical, since the procedure appears to require the experimenter's death in very many branches.
