I've spent the last few years working in my uncle's seafood wholesale business, and before that I did a summer at a salmon processing plant, so I've ended up thinking a great deal about how farmed fish and crustaceans are treated—not from any philosophy or animal science course, just from watching the work and reading trade news. I have strong reactions to particular practices, but I can't tell whether there is a single principle behind them. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on animal ethics, think carefully about what I report, and help me see whether my verdicts add up to a coherent view. Here are the cases. Leaving salmon to suffocate slowly in air strikes me as wrong. Killing them with a sharp blow to the head that knocks them unconscious instantly before bleeding them out doesn't bother me much, even though the fish is just as dead. Boiling lobsters alive feels indefensible to me, but killing a lobster by splitting it straight through the head with a heavy knife, when it is done competently and kills at once, seems acceptable. I find the routine practice of cutting or crushing off one eyestalk of a female shrimp to make her spawn cruel—perhaps crueler in my gut than a clean kill—even though the animal survives and the wound is small compared with slaughter. I think it is clearly wrong to pack fish so densely that they bite each other and their fins erode. I am also uneasy about putting cleaner fish such as wrasse or lumpfish into salmon cages to eat sea lice when many of those cleaner fish simply waste away and die, even though it lets the farm avoid chemical treatments. But I accept that a farm hit by a disease outbreak may have to cull a whole stock quickly, and if the cull is done with an anesthetic overdose or another genuinely fast method, I don't object to the mass killing itself. I am torn about ice slurry, which is meant to stun fish by cold: part of me says the fish go quiet and it is less awful than suffocation, part of me suspects it is just slow suffocation in disguise. And I think electric stunning is fine when it works properly, but unacceptable when the settings are wrong and fish are cut while they can still feel it.

What I want to know is whether there is one principle that captures all or most of these reactions—something about felt suffering, about the capacity to suffer, about a difference between killing and hurting, about necessity, about naturalness, or about something else I haven't named. Please start by brainstorming a few candidate principles, test each one against my cases as I've described them, and then say which principle fits best. Be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would force me to revise. Finally, tell me what your proposed principle predicts about two cases I keep going back and forth on: killing a healthy farmed fish with a gradual anesthetic like clove oil as a routine slaughter method, and transporting live fish for many hours in crowded tanks before they are killed. I'm not looking for references or reading suggestions; I want the reasoning laid out clearly enough that I can see whether my gut reactions hold together.
