I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep coming back to an asymmetry in the way we treat different kinds of property after the owner disappears. Think of two cases. A tenant vanishes from a furnished apartment, and once the statutory waiting period has run, the landlord clears the unit and disposes of the sofa; almost no one expects the tenant to reclaim it years later, and a passerby who salvages a desk left on the curb isn't thought to have stolen anything. Now consider the sole developer of a niche graphics utility who dies intestate with no locatable heirs, leaving a program that has been out of commerce for decades. Thousands of people download it from an abandonware archive each year, and many of them would happily carry off that curb desk without a twinge; but almost none of them believe the software has become anyone's property, and most would accept that a resurfacing heir could force copies offline. Time and the owner's absence seem to dissolve claims to the tangible object while freezing the intangible work inside someone's absent grip. The same divide shows up in archives: a decaying 1930s pamphlet can be rebound, sold, or discarded by whoever owns the paper, while a 1930s photograph whose rights holder cannot be found can't be digitized without legal anxiety. Why should "no one is there to claim it" release one kind of property and tighten the other?

The obvious candidate justifications are easy to list: physical goods are rival and wear out, while intellectual works are non-rival and don't decay; leaving a chair on the curb is a clearer signal of abandonment than not updating a copyright; long copyright terms exist partly to protect heirs and intermediaries who might emerge later. I want you to go past that list and build the strongest principle you can that actually generates both halves of the ordinary judgment, then stress-test it against nearby cases: a well-maintained bicycle locked in a public rack for one week versus software untouched for thirty years; a painting auctioned from an unclaimed storage unit to a collector who will preserve it versus an orphaned program needed to open a public archive's file format; a trademark that lapses through non-use while copyright does not. For each candidate justification, say whether it survives the stress test, and end with a verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or softened at the edges.

I want reasoning throughout, not a legal memorandum: if you invoke the law, describe its operation in general terms rather than citing cases or statutes, and don't turn this into a reading list.
