Two people in my reading group got into it this morning about a passage in Hobbes's De Corpore on the ship of Theseus. The passage, as one of them read it, says that if the ship is repaired over time by replacing each plank as it decays, the vessel remains the same numerical ship even once no original plank remains. What they actually fought over is what follows if a bystander has been collecting the discarded planks all along and then rebuilds them into the original shape.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) He said the rebuilt planks are also the same ship, because every discipline from the law courts to the navy would treat the reassembled original materials as the original if the other ship had sunk. So Hobbes's "same numerical ship" can't rule out the second ship, and the criterion collapses. (2) She said no, Hobbes's claim is that the ship is a unitary body whose identity rides on continuous functional organization, not on the material; the reassembled planks are just a replica made of old bits. (3) He came back: if functional organization is what matters, then an exact modern reproduction with new materials is also the same ship, which means identity becomes a free-floating form and the planks were never relevent. (4) She replied that maybe it's not about any single feature but about causal continuity: the repaired hull is the same object because nothing broke the chain of repair and use, whereas the rebuilt pile has a gappy history, so it isn't the same. (5) He said the gappy history is just an accident of where you point; if you followed the planks themselves, they have a continuous history too, and she is arbitrarily privileging the hull's career over the material's career.

Adjudicate this move by move, please. For each of the five moves say whether it's right, wrong, or partly right, and whether it actually bears on the Hobbes passage. At the end, say whether Hobbes's criterion handles the old-planks rebuild or leaves it open, and if both sides are partly right, where you would draw the line between them.
