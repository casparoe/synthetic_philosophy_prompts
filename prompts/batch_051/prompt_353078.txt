I keep going back and forth on this and I want someone to actually work the costs instead of giving the standard lecture. I am genuinely attracted to the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, the idea that if two things have exactly the same properties they are not really two things at all—they are one thing under two labels. In its strong form it says even intrinsic features suffice: if two objects share every intrinsic feature, they are identical. And I find that almost logical; if there is literally no way in which A differs from B, then saying there are two looks like double counting.

But then there is Black's universe. Imagine a universe containing only two iron spheres, each exactly one mile across, made of the same alloy, at the same temperature, same color, same mass, same polish, sitting two miles apart. Nothing else exists, no observer, no other furniture of the world. Each sphere is, so far as the description goes, a perfect duplicate of the other; any qualitative feature you can point to in one, the other has. The principle seems to force me to say there is really only one sphere there, or at least that the pair is not two distinct objects. But that feels plainly wrong. The description of a universe with just two identical iron spheres seems perfectly coherent. If the creator of that universe told me afterward that there had only ever been one sphere there, I would think he had misunderstood which universe he built, not that I had learned something deep.

So I have a compelling principle and a firm intuition, and they cannot both come out intact. I do not want the cheap answer. Walk me through what each move costs. If I keep the principle and deny that there could be two such spheres, what else do I have to give up, and is that too high a price? If I keep the two spheres, how should I weaken the principle, and does the weakened version still do any real work? Is the relational-properties move any good—saying the spheres differ because one has the property of being two miles from sphere B while the other has the property of being two miles from sphere A? Does that mark a real difference, or does it just smuggle in labels that the symmetric universe itself does not come with? Tell me which way you would lean and what would change your mind. And please just write me a continuous answer, like an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
