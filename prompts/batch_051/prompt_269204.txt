In the circles I move in, gratitude keeps being treated as a kind of debt. People say we owe our parents a debt for raising us, and that an adult child who never repays it is morally in arrears rather than merely distant. The same picture is then stretched further: someone will say we owe our good fortune to luck, as if luck were a creditor, and public rhetoric often insists we owe a debt to past generations that can never be repaid. The debt model seems to be doing real argumentative work here. It turns gratitude from something nice into something owed, with a creditor, a sum, and a due date, and it licenses the judgment that failing to return the benefit is not just ungenerous but a failure of justice.

I want you to audit the debt model of gratitude. Give me the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences would be fine. What in gratitude is supposed to play the role of the creditor, the debtor, the principal, the interest, the repayment, and the discharge of the debt? Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusions people draw from the ones that are merely incidental.

In particular, tell me what the debt picture quietly imports that the target may lack: a determinate and identifiable creditor, a quantifiable amount owed, a clear point at which the obligation is cancelled, voluntariness in taking on the obligation, and enforceability. Do those imports cause problems when the supposed creditor is luck or a dead generation? Does the debt model actually support an obligation of gratitude to benefactors, to fortune, and to the past — considered separately — or does it collapse in some of those cases while surviving in others? And if it fails as the master analogy, say what a better analogy would be, what the debt metaphor gets right that any replacement should preserve, and which of the original conclusions can be kept once the disanalogies are on the table.
