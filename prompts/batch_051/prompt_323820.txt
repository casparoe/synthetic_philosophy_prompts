I'm a novelist plotting a near-future book, and I need help thinking through a premise. Suppose that around 2045 a series of longitudinal neuroimaging and behavioral genetics studies converge on a result that gets popularized as psychological egoism being true: every voluntary action, when you trace its underlying motivational structure, is ultimately initiated and sustained by the agent's own self-models, expected self-related affect, and felt reasons. There's no motivationally clean altruism pathway independent of the agent's self. But the same studies also show that some self-models are other-inclusive—they represent other people's welfare as part of the agent's own good—and when those representations are active, people behave prosocially at real cost, even when no one is watching. So it's not the cartoon version; it's more like everything is self-referential, but not everything is selfish in the ordinary evaluative sense.

My question: if this became widely accepted, how would ordinary moral discourse, child-rearing, criminal law, and close relationships change over the next thirty years? Would people become more sympathetic to normative ethical egoism, or would they start treating moral norms as useful shared fictions? Which groups or institutions would adapt fastest? I'm less interested in whether the science is plausible than in the social mechanics given that many people believe it. Give me the chain of reasoning: which effects are robust, which are guesses, and where do you think your reasoning is most likely to fail? Also, what sort of evidence or argument would change your mind about the main prediction?
