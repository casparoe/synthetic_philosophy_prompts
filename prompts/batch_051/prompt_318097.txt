I am a PhD student in practical philosophy working on the ethics of consumption, and I would like a worked test case for a chapter on whether the precept ‘be frugal’ is best read as a constraint on one’s own conduct or as an instruction to bring about less resource use overall. My hope is that the case will separate a deontological reading, on which the requirement attaches to the character of one’s own acts, from a consequentialist reading, on which it attaches to what one’s acts cause.

As a working account, take the following four criteria for an action’s qualifying as an exercise of the virtue of frugality. (i) Restraint: the act declines a level of resource use that the agent’s income and social position would make unremarkable. (ii) Sufficiency: the agent’s governing reason is to secure enough while avoiding waste, rather than to hoard, to punish the body, or to impress others through thrift. (iii) Disposition: the act flows from a stable pattern of restraint rather than a one-off bout of economising. (iv) No false economy: the saving is genuine over the whole relevant horizon, not merely deferred or displaced onto others.

Now take the following case. R is a salaried policy analyst who lives personally frugally — no car, mended clothes, a small rented flat — and who wants to shift consumption norms in her city. Each year R spends roughly a quarter of her income on a ‘Festival of Enough’: renting a large central venue, paying tradespeople to teach repair, batch-cooking, and low-energy living, buying durable equipment, and providing free meals. The event is far larger than the same content delivered through a series of modest evening classes, and R’s name appears on the publicity; R says the scale is the point, because sufficiency has to be made visible and glamorous to be copied. R also concedes that the festival’s own setup consumes significant materials and energy, though her rough accounting estimates that the skills and habits it spreads prevent several times the consumption it creates over the following decade.

Please work through criteria (i)–(iv) and say whether R’s running of the festival qualifies as an exercise of the virtue of frugality. Present the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1–4 should address criteria (i)–(iv) in turn; item 5 should give your overall verdict and say whether the criteria should be revised so that promoting frugality in others can itself count as frugal. Where a criterion returns no or an indeterminate answer, say briefly whether the problem lies in the specification of the criterion or in an unresolved question about whether ‘being frugal’ tracks the agent’s own acts or the sum of acts she causes.
