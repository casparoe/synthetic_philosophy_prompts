A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about a mutual acquaintance who has been living in a shared house for months without ever contributing: he eats the communal food, uses the heat and the internet, and lets others replace the things he consumes. His defense has always been that he is not taking anything from anyone, since the room would otherwise stand empty and the food was already purchased. My friend ended the argument by saying that there is no such thing as a free lunch — someone always pays, and if you are not paying, you are taking from whoever does. I ended up agreeing with his conclusion, but I am no longer sure the slogan actually earns it, and I want to know whether it is doing real argumentative work or just functioning as a way of ending the discussion.

I would like you to unpick the sentence "there is no such thing as a free lunch" and lay out the distinct claims it could be used to make — from a banal economic observation that every benefit traces back, somewhere, to a cost borne by someone, to a much stronger moral claim that receiving a benefit without contributing toward its cost is a form of wrongful taking or exploitation. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something that is actually false, and say which reading or readings would genuinely support the verdict that our acquaintance is a free rider in the morally objectionable sense. In particular, I want to know whether the metaphor of "no free lunch" smuggles in the very conclusion that is at issue — namely, that someone has been wronged — or whether some non-metaphorical version of it can carry that conclusion on its own.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and social scientists who study free riding and collective action, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Since I plan to bring a diagnosis rather than an essay to my next conversation with my friend, please lay the readings out as a numbered menu with a short verdict on each, and end your answer with a final line of the form \answer{...} whose content is the number of the reading the argument must rely on, or \answer{none} if no reading does the necessary work.
