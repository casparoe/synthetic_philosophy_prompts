I'm drafting a position statement for a seminar on the ethics of everyday commitments—lateness, RSVPs, promises to send a file "by Friday," that sort of thing—and I've come to a set of views that keep pulling in different directions. Before I present, I'd like an outside audit of whether the package hangs together. Here is approximately where I've landed:

1. Promising, even in small matters, is obligation-generating in its own right. When I promise to be somewhere at three or to send a reference by Friday, what I owe is to do the thing promised—not merely to bring about a good outcome, and not merely to compensate afterwards if I don't.

2. But the stringency of such an obligation seems to me to track invited reliance rather than the content of the promise. The same promise to send a recipe is weightier if the other person tells me they urgently need it, and close to negligible if they are entirely indifferent to whether I perform.

3. The distinctive wrong of breaking a small promise lies mostly in misleading the other person—leaving them to arrange their plans around a false expectation. This makes timely notice central: cancelling a lunch with a friend an hour before is a much lesser wrong than simply not showing up, even when the final state of affairs is the same.

4. I also think an appointment is a promise to be there at the time agreed. Yet I don't think ordinary lateness is a breach of that promise. Within some conventional margin it is a failure of consideration rather than of fidelity; only beyond that margin—which I can't precisely locate—does it become a genuine breach.

5. One case seems clear to me: if I realize at 2:50 that I'll be twenty-five minutes late to a three o'clock meeting and I immediately text, and the other person is genuinely indifferent—perfectly content to read in a café—then I do them no wrong at all, even though I don't arrive when I said I would.

6. In the opposite direction: if I simply forget a small promise to a colleague, and it turns out they never needed the thing and never noticed it was missing, I still owe them an apology. Something small remains wrong, though I'd struggle to say what it consists in if no one was inconvenienced or misled.

7. I also distinguish the ethics of particular acts from the assessment of character. Chronic lateness or the serial breaking of small promises is a serious vice of unreliability, but that judgment about character is largely independent of the question which particular acts were wrong and how wrong. And I believe that breaking a small promise to a friend is worse, other things equal, than the identical failure toward a stranger—and that this is a difference in the obligation's content, not merely in consequences.

I'd like the audit to be committed rather than open-ended. Which of these commitments are in real tension, and which pairs only appear to be in tension once the content of a promise—or the difference between performance, warning, and release—is made precise? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. Finish by committing to the single revision you think would leave me with the most defensible overall package, and say why you'd choose it over the alternatives.
