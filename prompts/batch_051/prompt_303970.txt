I am writing about when a sub-state national group has a moral right to secede unilaterally, rather than a right merely to seek a negotiated independence settlement. I have been working with five criteria:

(a) Distinct peoplehood: the group is a historically continuous community whose language, institutions, or culture distinguish it from the majority society, and this distinctness is not something manufactured recently for political purposes.

(b) Territorial base: the group forms a stable local majority in a contiguous territory to which it has deep roots, rather than being dispersed or a recent settler implant.

(c) Remedial grievance: the group has suffered, or continues to suffer, a serious injustice at the hands of the state — subjugation, systematic discrimination, denial of basic rights or autonomy, or the abrogation of self-government that it once had.

(d) Democratic authorization: a clear and sustained majority of the group's members, not merely a plurality of a mobilized slice of the electorate, supports independence in free and fair votes held under conditions opponents can reasonably accept.

(e) Externalities: the seceding unit would be economically viable, would protect the rights of those who voted no and of minorities inside the seceding territory, and would not seriously destabilize the wider region.

Take Catalonia in the period around the 2017 independence referendum as the test case. The salient facts, as I understand them: Catalan is a distinct language, and Catalonia has long-standing institutions and its own parliament and government, the Generalitat, with devolved powers under the 1978 Spanish Constitution; a 2006 autonomy statute, which would have expanded those powers, was partly struck down or reinterpreted by Spain's Constitutional Court in 2010; the region is comparatively wealthy rather than oppressed in any ordinary sense; the October 2017 referendum was ruled illegal, was boycotted by unionist parties, was partly suppressed by Spanish police, and returned roughly 90 percent yes on roughly 43 percent turnout; in regional elections the pro-independence parties have tended to win a slim majority of seats on roughly 47–48 percent of the popular vote; and after a short-lived declaration of independence, Spain invoked Article 155 of the Constitution, imposed direct rule, and prosecuted or exiled Catalan leaders.

I am asking about the moral right to secede, not about what Spanish law permits, though legality can matter insofar as it bears on a criterion. Go through the five criteria one by one and say how Catalonia scores on each, being explicit where the evidence is mixed or where a criterion is vague enough to be applied in more than one defensible way. Then say whether, on the strongest defensible reading of the criteria, Catalonia has a unilateral right to secede. Finally, step back: does this case show that the list needs revising — for instance, that a purely remedial criterion such as (c) rules out secession for a thriving, self-governing people that simply wants out, or that (d) lets a disciplined minority manufacture the look of a mandate? If you think the criteria need revision, state the smallest revision you would make and say whether Catalonia qualifies under your revised list.

End with a single last line that gives your overall verdict in this form: "Verdict: qualifies", "Verdict: does not qualify", or "Verdict: underdetermined". Put nothing after that line.
