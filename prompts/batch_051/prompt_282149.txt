A manufacturing cooperative adopts a charter meant to eliminate, in one stroke, both of the failures Marx diagnosed in wage labor. On the economic side, it abolishes any class of owners who live off the surplus: each month the firm takes its sales revenue, subtracts the costs of materials, energy, facilities, and machine depreciation, and distributes the entire remainder to its workers in proportion to the hours they worked. Nobody receives a return merely for owning capital, and the equipment is owned in common by the workforce. On the workplace side, the firm eliminates fixed job descriptions: workers rotate weekly through design, machining, inspection, and customer contact, and they can be reassigned by majority vote. No production decision is made by anyone who does not also perform rotating production work. The firm sells into the same competitive market as ordinary firms, which it does not control.

Analyze how this arrangement would actually play out rather than how it is intended to work. In particular: (a) Does it eliminate Marxian exploitation, understood as the appropriation of unpaid surplus labor, or does it merely relocate the problem into risk-bearing, reinvestment decisions, and the treatment of past labor stored up in machines? (b) Does rotation and collective control address what Marx called alienation, understood as estrangement from the product of one's labor, from the labor activity itself, from one's own creative powers, and from other people? (c) What incentives does the scheme create around hiring, investment, shirking, and competition in the product market, and how would workers or the surrounding market game or undermine it? (d) Are the likely failure modes fixable with small amendments, or do they reveal something deeper about Marx's diagnosis? Please write the answer in American English.
