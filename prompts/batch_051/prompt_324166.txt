You are a philosopher of mind with special expertise in panpsychism and an interest in metaphilosophy — specifically, in what it takes for a philosophical debate to move forward. Think carefully and do not rush to a verdict. Before you settle on a diagnosis, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate diagnoses of the impasse described below, and then explain which one you endorse and why you reject the alternatives.

In the modern philosophy of mind, the combination problem has been treated as the central difficulty for panpsychism, and the resulting debate has been oddly static for years. The contours are familiar. Critics argue that no number of distinct micro-subjects — each a small center of experience attached to some microphysical item — can compose a single unified subject like the one reading this sentence: the unity of consciousness is not the sort of thing that admits of summation, so panpsychism merely moves the explanatory gap down to the micro-to-macro transition and leaves it there. Panpsychists have replied in several different ways. Some hold that micro-subjects constitute the macro-subject, and that the 'combination' is mediated by a primitive experiential bonding relation, one they take to be no more mysterious than physical composition itself. Some give up on constitution and posit that macro-subjects strongly emerge from micro-subjects, accepting the transition as a brute fact. Others invert the priority altogether, making the cosmos — rather than its microphysical parts — the fundamental subject, so that the task is not to combine many small subjects into one big one but to show how a single fundamental subject can have partial or local perspectives. Every one of these moves now has standard objections and standard replies, and the argument seems to have stopped moving.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about whether subjects can combine. It is about what an explanation of consciousness has to do. One side treats a subject of experience as the kind of thing that could in principle be built up or decomposed, and therefore demands a compositional story connecting micro-subjects to the macro-subject; the other side treats subjects as fundamental relata that can stand in primitive, unanalyzable relations, and regards the demand for a compositional story as question-begging. If that hypothesis is right, the two sides are not disagreeing about a single well-defined question; they are evaluating panpsychism against different standards of explanatory adequacy. But I am not sure it is right. The impasse might instead hide a genuine empirical question — for instance, about the relation between the philosophical combination problem and the neuroscience of binding — or a conceptual confusion about what 'subject' means across these positions.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a verbal disagreement, a substantive disagreement, or two research programs passing each other under one heading? Do the sides differ about facts, about explanatory standards, about the meaning of 'subject,' or about where the burden of proof sits? Engage with what the actual positions claim, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an empirical result, a formal point about composition, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question as posed is ill-posed. Say also whether the different panpsychist replies — constitutive, bonding-relation, emergentist, and cosmopsychist — are affected differently by whatever is really driving the stalemate.
