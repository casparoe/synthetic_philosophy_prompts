I write about science and ideas, and I keep running into a puzzle about rational choice that I want to think through before using it in a piece. It grows out of Buridan's ass, but I want to press it as a problem about rationality rather than free will. Here is the argument as I understand it:

(1) A rational agent selects one option rather than another only if something favors the selected option over the alternative.
(2) In a fully symmetric choice — two identical bottles of water, equally distant, equally priced, nothing to distinguish them — nothing favors either bottle over the other.
(3) A rational agent would not leave herself worse off when she could be better off at no cost.
(4) In the symmetric case, the only way not to leave herself worse off is to select one of the two bottles.
(5) So rationality seems to forbid both selecting and not selecting: such cases look rationally impossible, which means one of the premises must be given up.

I half-suspect the trouble is in (1), because the thirsty agent does have a reason to take one bottle — namely, thirst — though she has no reason to take this bottle rather than that one. But I have also seen it argued that the only coherent response is to allow a distinction between picking and choosing, where picking is an intentional selection among alternatives none of which is favored. And I have seen that dismissed as relabeling rather than solving the problem.

Is the argument valid as written? If not, which step fails, and on which precise reading of it? And which parts of this debate are actually settled? Please separate what people who work on rational choice would mostly accept from what is still genuinely contested, and don't present one side's view as the consensus.
