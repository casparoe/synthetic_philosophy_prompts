A friend of mine is an ICU nurse, and after a long argument about a news story we realized we couldn't settle a basic thing: what are we actually saying when we say someone is dead? The standard line — basically the law where I live, and I think in most U.S. states — is that you're dead when either your circulatory and respiratory functions have irreversably stopped, or all functions of your entire brain have irreversibly stopped. But those two don't look like two routes to the same thing; they look like two different things that happen to get called the same word. A person declared brain-dead and left on a ventilator is warm, has a heartbeat (because the machine breathes for them), can heal wounds, and there are cases of pregnant women declared brain-dead being kept on ventilators until delivery. If a body is doing all that, calling it dead has to be leaning on some specific idea of what death is — maybe "the organism as a whole has stopped functioning" or "the person is gone." But the cardiac route seems to lean on a different idea: the body as an integrated system has shut down.

I want to get clear on the philosophy hiding inside the practice, not just the dictionary. What does this two-route standard strictly presuppose about what death is, as opposed to what it merely fits well with? Where is the protocol actually committed to a particular theory, and where is it underdetermined, so that more than one theory of death would end up licensing exactly the same hospital protocol? And if you swapped the implicit theory for either a higher-brain personhood view or a strict "the whole organism's life must have ended" view, what would have to change, concretely, in how death gets determined in law or in an ICU?
