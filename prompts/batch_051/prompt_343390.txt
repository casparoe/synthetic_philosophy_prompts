Earlier this year my business partner embezzled from our firm, and I found that I could not stop resenting him even after I came to understand the financial and personal pressures behind what he did. A friend pointed me to Strawson's work on the reactive attitudes, and I have been troubled by the claim that their psychological inescapability is enough to show that determinism does not really threaten moral responsibility. I am collecting objections to that inference. So far I have: (1) inescapability is a psychological fact, not a normative one, and from our inability to abandon an attitude nothing follows about whether we should hold people responsible; (2) the view cannot say which excusing and exempting conditions we accept without quietly relying on a theory of responsibility, so it borrows what it is meant to replace; (3) the scope and intensity of the attitudes vary across cultures and eras, so their inescapability is less invariant than the argument needs; (4) the view gives no account of which instances of resentment or gratitude are fitting or proportionate, and so cannot distinguish justified resentment from mere vindictiveness; (5) the appeal to inescapability would equally cover other recalcitrant responses such as spite, schadenfreude, or group prejudice, which most people are not willing to place beyond rational criticism. I think these cover most of the standard ground. I need an objection that is not on this list and not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them—one that attacks the move from unavoidable to justified from a genuinely different direction. What is it, and is it actually distinct from (1)? I ask because most of my own attempts have collapsed back into that basic gap.
