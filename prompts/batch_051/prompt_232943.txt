You are a professional philosopher with expertise in moral psychology and political epistemology. Please work through this carefully; begin by steelmanning both the theory and the intuition in your own words, and only then adjudicate between them.

I find myself pulled in two directions by Moral Foundations Theory and the social-intuitionist model of moral judgment that usually travels with it. On that picture, moral judgments are produced by fast, affectively laden intuitions, while the reasoning we do afterwards is mostly post-hoc justification—the rider constructing arguments for a direction the elephant has already taken. Political differences, on this view, are largely differences in which of a small set of evolved moral foundations—care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity—people's cultural and political contexts have taught them to weight most heavily. I understand why this theory is attractive. It has real systematic virtues: it unifies a huge amount of moral variation, across cultures and across the left-right divide, under a small set of common mechanisms. It explains moral dumbfounding, the way people reach firm verdicts they cannot articulate grounds for, and it makes sense of why the other side's convictions feel visceral rather than merely incorrect. It also gives me a tidy interpretive procedure: when I meet a view I find alien, I can ask which foundations it protects before I ask whether it is right.

The problem is an intuition I cannot shake: at least some of my own moral-political changes appear to have run the other way. I did not first feel that a policy was wrong and then hunt for a rationale; I absorbed arguments and empirical information over a long period, and the affect followed the reasoning. For instance, my views about criminal punishment and about which inequalities demand correction changed mainly because I learned specific empirical things, not merely because I acquired new feelings. And I cannot shake the further conviction that some foundation-weightings are not just different but less defensible: a politics built principally around loyalty or sanctity can be more than merely alien to one built around care and fairness—it can be worse justified. If the psychological story is right, I worry the distinction between explaining a moral conviction and justifying one starts to dissolve.

I am not asking you to tell me which side wins. I want to know what would have to be true—or what evidence I would need—for the intuition to be the thing I give up: that is, on what grounds could I reasonably conclude that my memories of reasoning-first change are introspection errors, and that my sense that some foundation-weightings are better justified is itself just another intuition with a debunking explanation? And I want to know what it would take for the theory to be amended instead: how much genuine causal power can it grant to reasoning, and how much of a normative filter can it admit, before it stops being the parsimonious, unifying theory that attracted me? Finally, say whether there is a reading on which both survive—something like a division of labor between the descriptive psychology of why people on the left and right differ, and the normative question of who is right—and if so, what the psychology of left and right would still tell us, if anything, about who is actually right once that division is drawn.
