Imagine William James somehow got a look at today's higher-order theories of consciousness — the view that a mental state only counts as conscious if there's a further, distinct thought about it. He died long before those theories got formalized, so he never replied to them directly. Your job is to predict, as confidently as you reasonably can, whether James would have signed on, rejected them, or modified them into something of his own — and defend that prediction.

Please don't hedge into the can't-know-what-a-dead-guy-would-think rutine. Take a side. Use what we do know about him: the stream of thought, his repeated complaint that introspection never turns up a little inner observer standing outside the thought, his later radical-empiricist attempt to dissolve consciousness as a seperate entity into pure experience, and his idea that a passing thought can be aware of itself directly rather than needing a second thought about it. The sharp question: would James see the higher-order approach as a helpful formalization of the self-involving character of thought, or as a relapse into exactly the inner-observer picture he spent decades arguing against?

After you've staked out the prediction, spend a final paragraph saying where the extrapolation is shakiest — what would have to be true about James for him to land on the other side.
