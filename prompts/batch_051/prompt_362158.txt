I'm in a philosophy of law course and got into a long argument with a friend about whether omissions can be causes, and after going back and forth I tried to write out the argument that's doing the work on my side. It goes like this.

(1) A cause has to be a real item in the world—an event, a process, a state—something that produces or brings about its effect.
(2) An omission isn't a real item in the world; it is precisely the absence of an item, namely the act that didn't happen.
(3) An absence can't produce or bring anything about; only positive items can.
(4) So no omission ever causes an outcome.
(5) But a nurse who deliberately skips a patient's prescribed antibiotics has caused the patient's deterioration—courts don't treat that as a mere metaphor, and we pick out that nurse from the thousands of people who also didn't give the drug.
(6) So at least one of (1)–(3) must be false, and causation by omission has to be legitimate.

I have a nagging feeling the word 'cause' is sliding between two different ideas in (1)–(3) and (5), but I can't make the two readings crisp, and I'm not even sure weather that's where the problem is rather than in (2). Is this argument valid? If it fails, which step exactly fails, and under what reading? If the solution is to distinguish a 'production' sense from a 'difference-making' or 'responsibility-tracking' sense of cause, spell out both readings and re-run the numbered argument under each to show where it stands or falls. And if the argument is actually fine, say straight out that the conclusion follows and why (4) and (5) only look like they conflict.
