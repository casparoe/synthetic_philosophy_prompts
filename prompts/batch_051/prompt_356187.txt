During the first year of the pandemic I followed the legal fights over lockdowns and border closures unusually closely, and a pattern has stayed with me since. A government would keep people at home or close a border by relying on an old public-health statute or an emergency decree. Months later a court would find that the statute did not actually authorize what had been done—the measure had been unlawful from the day it was issued—yet the court would not punish anyone, would not compensate the people who had been fined, and would often leave the measure's effects in place. In some cases the legislature then passed a law making the original measure lawful after the fact. The same basic move shows up outside pandemics, after an attack or a border surge: officials act first, courts later say the action exceeded the law, and a ratification or amnesty for the officials follows. I found that most people, including me, wanted to say two things at once. The officials were not criminals for acting in a hurry, but this kind of after-the-fact cleaning-up of an unlawful act is not something we would tolerate so casually if it concerned ordinary citizens rather than a government's own emergency response.

I keep turning over what this practice commits us to about the relationship between legality and legitimacy. The practice seems to run on the idea that whether an act is legal and whether it is legitimate are two different questions, and that legitimacy can later be converted into legality, as if the law always arrives late, after necessity has already settled the question. Can you reconstruct the implicit theory of law and legitimacy that this practice presupposes? In particular, does the practice strictly require that legality and legitimacy can come apart in this way, or could it still make sense if a society insisted that an unlawful act is never legitimate and that legality can never be granted backward? And are the three pieces of the pattern—courts announcing that a measure was unlawful while leaving its effects in place, legislatures ratifying the measure afterward, and a public that treats the officials as having acted responsibly—consistent with one another, or do they pull in different directions? Finally, if a society really did give up the separation that the practice seems to assume, what would its emergency procedures have to look like instead: how would border controls and lockdowns have to be ordered, reviewed, and repaired?
