Consider a qualified version of Stigler's law of eponymy, which I have come to accept. As Stigler states it, "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer." Read literally that is absurd; read as a serious thesis about scientific credit, it says something like this: among the laws, constants, theorems, diseases, and effects that carry a person's name, the name is systematically a poor guide to priority. The mismatches are not random noise but the product of recognizable mechanisms—a result being ignored and later rediscovered, credit flowing to the person who makes an idea canonical rather than the person who first arrived at it, credit flowing to the already eminent, and names being attached late by third parties for pedagogical or rhetorical convenience rather than by anyone checking the historical record.

I want an honest map of what would count against this thesis, not a list of entertaining exceptions. Spell out the observable difference between a world in which eponymy is systematically misattributive and one in which eponymy is mostly accurate but our attention is drawn to a memorable minority of failures. What patterns in the timing of names, the distribution of errors across fields, the ratio of correct to incorrect eponyms, or the identity of the namers would discriminate between those two worlds? Which candidate counterexamples would be decisive? Take Halley's comet or the Gaussian distribution: what historical facts would have to be established about such a case before it counts as a genuine counterexample, and what can a defender of the thesis say to absorb it without giving up the view? Which mechanisms, if shown to be weak or absent, would do the most damage—for example, if the misattributions turned out to be concentrated in a few famous cases rather than spread widely across named objects, or if naming in a field were usually controlled by informed near-contemporaries? Distinguish the evidence a committed defender would concede counts against the position from the evidence only a critic takes to count. Does the self-referential fact that Stigler credited the observation to Robert K. Merton strengthen the thesis, or does it expose the thesis as partly an artifact of its own presentation? Is the view in danger of being insulated, treating every apparent counterexample as another instance of the very mechanisms it invokes—and if so, is that insulation a defect, and what evidence could in principle break it? Rank the would-be defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and conclude with a judgment about how exposed the qualified thesis actually is and whether that exposure is a problem.
