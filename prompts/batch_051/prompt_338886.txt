I spend part of my week maintaining a university database and the rest working through the philosophy of language, and I keep noticing a structural parallel between two otherwise unrelated debates. On one side there is the familiar divide over proper names. The descriptivist says a name's reference is fixed by a cluster of associated descriptions: the bearer is whatever best satisfies them, and the name carries content that can in principle be revised when the facts change. The causal-historical picture says the opposite: a name is more like an opaque tag, fixed once at an initial act of naming and then passed along a chain of uses, no matter how many of the associated descriptions later turn out false. On the other side, database design seems to face the same fork. A record can be identified by a natural key built from descriptive attributes, such as a person's given name, family name and date of birth, in which case the identifying attributes must be kept correct and every reference to the record revised when they change; or it can be identified by a surrogate key, an arbitrary UUID or auto-incrementing integer assigned at creation, in which case identity is stipulated and survives the replacement of every descriptive column. The two strategies even appear to generate the same difficulties: natural keys break under duplicate descriptions or stale data, while surrogate keys invite the question of what, beyond the system's continuity, that bare token picks out once every meaningful field has changed.

What I want to know is whether this is a genuine identity of structure rather than a loose analogy or a confusion. If it is genuine, what would have to be true on each side for results to transfer, and which way does the illumination run? Does the database designer's preference for surrogate keys, on the grounds that natural keys are unstable as the world changes, amount to an unnoticed practical argument against descriptivism? Or does the descriptivist have a ready retort, namely that a surrogate key is just a hidden description of the form "the record assigned token K by system S at time t"? And what does the mapping predict that turns out false on one of the two sides? A further wrinkle is that natural languages already mix the two mechanisms inside a single person's name: a given name is often chosen partly for its content, while a family name is inherited along a lineage and its descriptive content can go stale, as with a present-day solicitor whose surname is Baker. Is that division of labour a clue that the two philosophical theories are answering two different questions, or is it merely a convention with no bearing on the semantics of names?
