I keep coming back to this bit from Bertrand Russell's *The Problems of Philosophy* (the first chapter, "Appearance and Reality"):

"Thus colour is not something which is inherent in the table, but something depending upon the table and the spectator and the way the light falls on the table. When, in ordinary life, we speak of the colour of the table, we only mean the sort of colour which it will seem to have to a normal spectator from an ordinary point of view under usual conditions of light. But the other colours which appear under other conditions have just as good a right to be considered real; and therefore, to avoid favouritism, we are compelled to deny that, in itself, the table has any one particular colour."

I'm writing a paper on disjunctivism and the argument from illusion, and i want to use this passage as my central exhibit. Write an essay that does this: first explain how Russell's reasoning is meant to support the claim that we don't directly perceive the table but only sense-data. Then lay out how a disjunctivist would reject that last step — the idea that because a table looks different in different conditions, what we're aware of must be something other than the table itself. Does disjunctivism give a better account of what's going on when, say, a white wall looks pink under red lighting? Make sure you weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, but don't end with "it's complicated" — defend a verdict about whether disjunctivism actually escapes the argument from illusion.
