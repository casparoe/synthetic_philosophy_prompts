The standard worry, as I understand it, is the countermajoritarian difficulty: judicial review lets a small number of judges who are not accountable to voters strike down decisions made by the people's representatives, which seems in tension with democratic self-government.

I would like you to analyse a specific episode through that lens. Take Shelby County v. Holder (2013), where the U.S. Supreme Court, by a 5–4 vote, invalidated the Section 4(b) coverage formula of the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Congress had reauthorized that formula as recently as 2006 with large bipartisan majorities, and it determined which jurisdictions had to obtain federal preclearance before changing their voting rules. The majority held that the formula rested on outdated data and violated equal state sovereignty; the dissent replied that the Fifteenth Amendment entrusts enforcement of voting rights to Congress and that the legislative record supported reauthorization. I know the broad facts; what I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a summary. Who or what occupies the 'majoritarian' and 'countermajoritarian' roles here, and how does the federalism element change that picture? What would a theorist who takes the countermajoritarian difficulty seriously say the Court should have done, and how do the majority and the dissent line up with that prescription?

Then run the analysis the other way. Which features of the episode does the countermajoritarian difficulty explain well, and which does it miss or even misdescribe? In particular, does the standard framing assume a simple conflict between judge and majority that breaks down when the Court strikes down a civil-rights statute rather than protecting an individual right, and when the 'majority' spans different Congresses and different generations? Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
