Act as an expert on the philosophy of software copyright, especially copyleft licensing. I am an undergraduate currently taking a course on the philosophy of intellectual property, and I sit as the student representative on my university's open-source release committee. The committee must rule on a case, and I am responsible for drafting the decision memo.

The case is this. A research lab has written an application that contains no copied GPL source code. The application can do its work only by sending structured messages through a clean inter-process interface to a small separate service that wraps a modified GPL-licensed graph-analysis library. The lab will release its modifications to that library under the GPL, but it wants to release the application under an MIT-style permissive license. The lab argues that the process boundary and the absence of copied code make the application an independent work that merely interoperates with GPL code. Our committee's policy says copyleft obligations attach to derivative works but not to independent works, so the decision turns on where one program ends and another begins.

I need a short memo the committee can act on. Before drafting, think carefully about what would make the derivative-work boundary principled rather than a line drawn by engineering convenience: work through candidate principles such as authorship and adaptation, functional dependence, the user-visible boundary between processes, and the purpose of copyleft itself, and test each against the fact pattern.

Then draft the memo as exactly five numbered sections, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion beyond the sections. Section 1 should state a concrete recommendation. Section 5 should say how confident the committee should be in that recommendation and identify the single point in the analysis you are least sure of. Do not rely on case citations; argue from the concept of a derivative work and from what copyleft is meant to protect.
