In Part V of Spinoza’s Ethics, “Of the Power of the Understanding, or of Human Freedom” (R. H. M. Elwes translation), Spinoza writes:

> Here, therefore, I repeat, I shall treat only of the power of the mind, or of reason ; and I shall mainly show the extent and nature of its dominion over the emotions, for their control and moderation. That we do not possess absolute dominion over them, I have already shown. Yet the Stoics have thought, that the emotions depended absolutely on our will, and that we could absolutely govern them. But these philosophers were compelled, by the protest of experience, not from their own principles, to confess, that no slight practice and zeal is needed to control and moderate them : and this someone endeavoured to illustrate by the example (if I remember rightly) of two dogs, the one a house-dog and the other a hunting-dog. For by long training it could be brought about, that the house-dog should become accustomed to hunt, and the hunting-dog to cease from running after hares.

I want to test an argument this passage suggests to me about Spinoza’s necessitarianism and his doctrine of the intellectual love of God. Here is the argument:

(1) In Part I, Definition VII, Spinoza says a thing is free only if its existence and action are determined by its own nature alone, not by something external.

(2) In Part V, the intellectual love of God arises necessarily in the mind from the third kind of knowledge and is an affect: a pleasure accompanied by the idea of God as its cause.

(3) When the intellectual love of God arises from the mind’s own nature insofar as the mind has adequate ideas, the mind is determined by its own nature alone rather than by external causes.

(4) Therefore, by Definition VII, in having the intellectual love of God the mind acts freely in the strict sense.

(5) But the quoted passage says the mind does not have absolute dominion over the emotions, and Spinoza criticizes the Stoics for thinking otherwise.

(6) So either the intellectual love of God is not an emotion, or Spinoza’s Definition VII concept of freedom is not the concept at work when he calls the mind free in Part V, or Spinoza contradicts himself.
