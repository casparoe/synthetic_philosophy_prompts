I've spent the last few weeks going down a rabbit hole about whether the internet has any coherent answer to "whose law governs this post?", and I've started to suspect my day job is doing half my thinking for me. I work as an SRE/infrastructure engineer, so I'm on solid ground with distributed systems—request routing, CDNs and edge caches, regional replication, data residency, failover, config-as-code, single sources of truth—but I've never read legal theory. The setup I keep coming back to: someone in country A posts something; the first durable copy lives in a datacenter in country B; a reader in country C gets it from a cached copy on an edge server in country D; the post is legal in A and B but unlawful in C, or is legal in C but the platform's home country D restricts it. The positions I've seen float around are: (1) the speaker's country's law should govern; (2) the law of the place where the content is stored should govern; (3) the reader's country's law should govern; (4) the law of the platform's legal seat should govern. And then there's the practice, which a friend in trust & safety calls "compliance by lowest common denominator," where platforms build their content rules around the most restrictive big jurisdiction and apply them everywhere so they don't have to maintain a zillion seperate policies.

Now restate the whole debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully and precisely as you can. Think origin server versus edge cache; whether the law of the reader's country is like a policy enforced at the client's local point of presence; what "the platform's legal seat" corresponds to—control plane, authoritative nameserver, something else; and what happens when two regions' rules conflict and the system has to choose between global consistency and local availability. After the translation, tell me what carried over cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what couldn't be stated in my vocabulary at all. The intuition I want tested is that this is at bottom a routing-and-policy problem: each jurisdiction's law is just middleware applied at a different layer of the request path, and platforms take the union of all restrictions because that's the only configuration that won't get them sued. Is that right, or is the vocabulary doing real work behind my back?
