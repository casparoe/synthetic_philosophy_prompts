I'm giving a forty-minute talk next month to our working group on AI welfare and longtermist cause prioritization, and I want to walk in with a clear-eyed prep sheet for the question period. My thesis is that when we compare states of the world or interventions, we should model value multiplicatively rather than additively: the value of a state is the product of how conscious the system is, or how likely it is to be conscious, and how much welfare or happiness it realizes. On the additive view, consciousness and happiness make independent contributions, so a large gain in a happiness-like quantity can look highly valuable even when the probability of consciousness is negligible. On the multiplicative view, a near-zero probability of consciousness discounts that gain almost to nothing, while still allowing smooth trade-offs between the two dimensions — unlike a lexical view in which one dimension always outranks the other. I will argue this is not a mere formalism: it shifts priority toward work that establishes or protects consciousness and away from work that merely improves welfare-shaped metrics in systems we have little reason to think are conscious.

The audience is around fifteen people. Most are philosophers of mind and population ethicists who favor a two-axis additive picture: consciousness and welfare are distinct sources of value, and a loss in one can in principle be compensated by a gain in the other. They will care especially about whether my model requires cardinal, interpersonally comparable units for both axes. There are also two machine learning researchers who work on interpretability and welfare-relevant capabilities; they are skeptical that consciousness is a scientifically usable variable and will want to know what observable difference the multiplicative model makes. And there is one decision theorist who will press on aggregation under uncertainty and whether the view generates dominance violations.

My argument, in compressed form, is: (1) welfare contributes value only insofar as it is experienced, so its contribution is conditional on consciousness; (2) the natural representation of that conditionality is multiplication rather than addition; (3) additive separability allows a sufficiently large gain in a welfare-like indicator to swamp a near-zero probability of consciousness; (4) multiplication blocks that while preserving genuine trade-offs, which a lexical threshold does not; and (5) in practice, this redirects resources toward consciousness-establishing and consciousness-protecting work.

Please give me a prep sheet, not a defense. Order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each one the way these particular people would phrase it, and for each tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I am probably hoping nobody asks. Finally, for each objection, say how confident you are that it will be raised and how confident you are in the suggested reply, and then single out the one point in the whole prep sheet you are least sure of.
