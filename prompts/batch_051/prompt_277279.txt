You are an expert in criminal law theory and moral responsibility, and a skilled philosophical debate coach. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the strongest possible objections before you craft the prep sheet.

A philosopher is presenting a paper at a criminal law theory workshop. The paper defends this thesis: accomplice liability is not fundamentally a causal relation. A person is complicit in another's wrongdoing, the speaker argues, when her conduct plays a certain deliberative role in the principal's wrongdoing — for example, when she gives assurance, authorization, shared planning, or a putatively reason-giving endorsement that the principal actually takes up in deciding and acting — regardless of whether her conduct makes any counterfactual difference to the wrong. The speaker calls this the participatory-deliberation account. She argues that it gives the cleanest explanation of why inert helpers are complicit (a second lookout either of whom would have sufficed; a getaway driver whose car is never used; an accomplice whose encouragement the principal did not need), while mere encouragement that never reaches the principal is not complicity. She also claims the account captures the distinctive wrong of being in on it without collapsing into guilt by association.

The audience is a longstanding workshop of criminal law theorists and moral responsibility philosophers at a public law school. Most accept broadly retributivist foundations and believe accomplice liability requires the accomplice to causally contribute to the principal's conduct or to its known risks; a minority are attracted to inchoate-crime and joint-intent accounts. They care about doctrinal fit, over-criminalization, the difference between complicity and attempt or bare agreement, and whether a theory can generate workable jury instructions. They will press every point on concrete cases.

Produce a Q&A prep sheet for the speaker. It should list the objections this particular audience will actually raise — not generic ones — ordered by a combination of how likely each is to arise and how damaging it would be if unanswered. Phrase each objection as a member of this workshop would, in their vocabulary. For each objection, give a short reply (what the speaker could say in one to two minutes) and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Throughout, mark what the speaker should concede up front, what she must hold for the paper to survive, and include the one question she most hopes nobody asks, with an explanation of why it is the dangerous one.
