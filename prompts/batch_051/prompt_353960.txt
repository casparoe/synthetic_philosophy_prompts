I am organising a panel discussion for a student philosophy of cognitive science society. One of the panellists defends the depictive side of the mental imagery debate: she holds that at least some visual mental representations are depictive rather than propositional, and that the chronometric and neural data support her view. I would like you to draft eight to ten probing questions that go beyond having her restate the position or recite the familiar image-scanning findings. Each question should come with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, so that the moderator can push past the stock answer.

Please include at least one question on each of the following points, though you may reorder them or add further questions where they cut deeper. First, the tacit-knowledge objection: ask what behavioural result would cleanly separate a depictive buffer from a propositional system that encodes metric information and can mimic spatial effects, and what follows if no such result is realistically obtainable. Second, the reinterpretation problem: if images are depictive, one might expect a person to reinterpret a mental image as they would an ambiguous picture, yet subjects are often poor at this; ask whether this is an anomaly for the view, and press the likely reply that reinterpreting a mental image differs from reinterpreting a picture. Third, demand characteristics: ask how much of the chronometric evidence she would concede once the instructions are treated as invitations to behave as if inspecting a picture, and what residue she thinks survives. Fourth, the charge that the depictive-propositional dispute is merely notational: any depictive representation can be recoded propositionally and any propositional system can represent spatial relations, so ask what kind of behavioural, computational, or neural fact could break the tie. Fifth, the neural evidence: ask what pattern of activation would count against the depictive account rather than merely failing to support it, given that activation of visual areas might be epiphenomenal, preparatory, or downstream of a propositional read-out.

You may add further questions where they are sharper, for instance on imagery in congenitally blind people, on aphantasia, on whether images have a fixed grain or resolution, or on the homunculus worry about who inspects the picture. Avoid generic questions about how she entered the field, and do not ask for reading lists, citations, or survey articles. Please write the questions in British English.
