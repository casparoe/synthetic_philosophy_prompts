A colleague compressed the debate over the structure of human values into the following chain, which he attributed to value pluralists: liberty, equality, friendship, and knowledge are irreducibly many; because they are many, there is no single scale of value on which to weigh them; because there is no single scale, conflicts among them have no determinate resolution; because conflicts have no determinate resolution, any resolution is ultimately arbitrary or an ungrounded commitment; and therefore pluralism turns out to be a form of relativism about the good. He contrasted this with monism, which he said escapes the whole chain by tracing all value to one fundamental source whose quantities can always be compared. I suspect the middle steps do more work than the pluralist's starting point allows — in particular, I doubt that the absence of a common scale amounts to the absence of a determinate resolution — but I am not certain I can pinpoint every slip, and I also wonder whether monism really delivers the determinate resolutions it advertises. Please work through the chain step by step: identify which transitions a careful pluralist could accept, which transitions fail, and assess whether the colleague's contrast with monism is earned once the failed transitions are removed.
