I keep returning to a parallel I can't decide is deep or merely superficial. In building reliable services, engineers almost never promise that each individual request will meet its deadline; they set a service-level objective, such as 95% of requests finishing within 200 milliseconds, because under variable load and partial failures a per-request guarantee is either impossible or ruinously expensive. Reliability in that setting is a statistical property of a stream of events, and deadlines are divided into hard and soft ones. Now take the ethics of small promises. A promise seems to bind act by act, not on average: if I told a friend I would meet at 3:00 and I arrive at 3:10, it would be odd to respond to their annoyance by pointing out that my on-time rate this month is still above 95%. Yet ordinary practice also treats a single small lateness as minor and easily excused, while treating chronic lateness as a distinct defect—unreliability—that is not simply the sum of those small latenesses. Is the moral case genuinely the same structure as a service-level objective: a norm that governs a pattern over time rather than each individual event, and that comes in degrees and priorities? If the parallel is deep, what should carry over from scheduling to promising—buffering, admission control, the lesson that perfect per-instance guarantees under uncertainty are infeasible or exorbitantly costly? If it is superficial, what is the precise structural difference that blocks the transfer?
