An argument I have encountered, presented in numbered steps:

(1) Assume an Everettian (many-worlds) quantum mechanics in which every measurement outcome with nonzero amplitude is realized in some branch.
(2) A device is constructed such that a spin measurement has two possible outcomes: on one outcome the experimenter dies immediately and permanently; on the other outcome a mechanism clicks and the experimenter survives.
(3) The experimenter commits to a long sequence of n such measurements.
(4) Described externally, after n runs there exists at least one branch in which the experimenter survived every run.
(5) Described from the first person, experience can continue only along branches in which the experimenter survives; in a death branch there is no subsequent experience of having died.
(6) Therefore, conditional on beginning the sequence, the experimenter should have credence 1 in experiencing survival at every run, no matter how small the survival amplitude is.
(7) Hence quantum immortality: in an Everettian world, a conscious being should never expect to observe their own permanent death.

I suspect the move from (5) to (6) is where the argument goes wrong, but I cannot state the precise principle it violates. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, say where ordinary intuitions about probability go wrong. If it is not valid, identify exactly which step fails, name the fallacy with precision, and say what a defensible first-person credence would be before the sequence begins. Finally, compare the argument to the claim that a lottery ticket holder should be certain of winning because some ticket will win: if the cases are relevantly different, explain the difference; if they are analogous in the way that matters, say what follows.
