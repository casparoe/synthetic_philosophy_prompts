At a typography conference after-party, I overheard two acquaintances, a type designer named Elena and a linguist named Ravi, argue for half an hour about whether a single mark in a festival wordmark is really the letter "a." The mark in question is the second letter of the word "FABLE," set for a literary festival. It is drawn as a closed ring with a separate vertical stroke placed just to its right, close but not touching. In the full word, nearly everyone reads "fable" without hesitation. Displayed alone as a decorative emblem, however, the mark is often read as an "o" followed by an "l," or as a purely abstract device, rather than as an "a."

I did not take a side, but I could not settle their argument either. I want a careful adjudication of the exchange below.

Elena said the mark is an "a" for the following reason. A letter is a functional unit of a writing system, not a fixed shape. What makes every "a" an "a" is that a community of readers, in normal contexts of use, reads it as "a." Here readers do exactly that: the wordmark says "FABLE." It is therefore an "a," much as the single-story "ɑ" is an "a" even though it differs from the double-story "a." The isolated reading, she argued, is a laboratory artifact; letters rarely occur isolated in real use, so failure outside context tells us nothing categorial.

Ravi replied that this confuses recognition with identity. In the word, top-down knowledge of the intended word is doing the classificatory work; the glyph does not itself carry the cues for "a." A real allograph of "a," he said, must pull some of its own weight by standing in contrastive relations to rival letters, such as "o." A ring is the canonical shape of "o," and a detached stem is a spare part. The glyph is therefore an "a"-slot filler, not an "a." Its isolated failure shows that it borrows its identity from the surrounding spelling rather than earning it.

Elena answered that shape-based criteria would fragment the letter into as many categories as there are fonts. The double-story "a," the single-story "ɑ," and countless stylized forms share no single topology, yet all are "a." What unifies them is not shape but functional slot: they contrast with other letters and combine into the same written words. So the festival glyph occupies the same slot in normal reading, and that is all membership requires.

Ravi granted that the category is functional but denied that function floats free of shape. The writing system, he said, is maintained by shared production and perception constraints that keep letters discriminable without lexically reconstructing every word. If any ring-plus-spare-part can count as "a" whenever context permits, writing drifts toward whole-word recognition and stops being alphabetic in the relevant sense. He then sharpened the point: a bare vertical stroke is often used for lowercase "l," uppercase "I," and digit "1" in many fonts, but we do not say the stroke is all three at once; we say it is ambiguous and context selects a reading. Likewise, the ring with a detached stem is not an allograph of "a": it is an equivocal shape, selected as "a" in the word but not properly in the contrastive range of "a."

Elena replied that this concedes her point. If the same shape class can be "l," "I," or "1" depending on context, then context is not a contaminant of letter identity; it is part of how letter identity works. A community that reads the festival mark as "a" in its normal contexts expands the contrastive range of "a," exactly as has happened with earlier convention shifts. The isolated failure merely shows the new allograph is context-dependent, not non-existent.

Ravi said the bare-stroke case cuts the other way. It shows that a shape can be shared by several categories while remaining ambiguous, not that each context creates a new allograph. To be an allograph of "a," a shape must fall within the standing range of forms that the system can assign to "a" on their own, however broad that range is. The festival mark fails that test; it falls in the range of "o." He concluded that the real disagreement is whether a form must carry a minimal contrastive load by itself to count as an "a," and that Elena's argument has not shown why it may borrow the whole load from the word.

Elena's final move was that "its own weight" is a metaphor smuggling in essentialism. A letterform, she said, is what it does in a practice; there is no "by itself" that is not already a history of conventions. If the community accepts the mark as "a" in the relevant contexts, it simply is an "a," and the only question is an empirical one about whether readers do accept it.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: for each move, say who is right about what, what should be conceded, and how the dispute should be narrowed or split into separate questions. Then give an overall verdict on whether the festival mark is an "a," and on which of the two disputants is more right overall. Finally, say how confident you are in your adjudication, and identify the specific point you are least sure about.
