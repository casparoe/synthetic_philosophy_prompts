Professionally, I have learned to distrust my own hunger for shapely narratives — an odd confession for a novelist, but true: the stories I write are made shapely by me, after the fact, and I have watched myself perform the same retrospective shaping when I read history. My current novel forced me into the question of why some nations are rich and others poor. I encountered the institutional explanation (secure property rights, credible commitments, constraints on rulers), the geographic explanation (soils, disease burden, coasts and navigable rivers), and the cultural explanation (trust, thrift, attitudes toward work). Each is a beautiful story, and each flatters the reader by making the outcome look inevitable. Each also works very hard to explain away its exceptions.

From this experience I built a rule. I will not assign serious credence to any explanation of comparative national wealth unless it specifies, in advance, what the world would have looked like had the explanation been false, and unless that specification could in principle be checked against cases the author did not select. Since nearly every historical explanation fails this test — the institutional account cannot rule out that wealth simply buys good institutions; the geographic account cannot say why similarly endowed places diverge; the cultural account cannot show that prosperity does not create the measured values — my rule leaves me agnostic among all three. I have come to regard that agnosticism as the intellectually honest position. But I also worry that I have built a standard no historical claim could pass and then called the standard “hygiene” so as to avoid taking any position at all.

Is this rule defensible as a policy? Under what conditions does it protect me from hindsight-shaped stories, and under what conditions does it collapse into blanket skepticism or a bias toward the null? Could it be restated to keep the protection without forcing agnosticism — for instance, by treating competing historical explanations as more or less plausible rather than demanding outright falsification? I would like the verdict to be self-critical: please say explicitly which argument or evidence would change your mind about the rule, and where your reasoning about it is most likely to fail.
