I've been trying to sort out what I actually believe about how much an author's intention determines the meaning of a literary work — not the tidy position I'd defend in a seminar, but what my gut says across particular disputes. Here are the verdicts I keep landing on. A novelist, at publication, tells interviewers that the death of the dog in the last chapter is a figure for the death of his mother; the chapter can be read that way, or as nothing more than dog-pathos, and I think the mother-death meaning is the meaning of the chapter. The same man's private diary from the period of writing says the same thing, and the printed chapter can plausibly carry it, but he never said it public and no reviewer at the time picked it up; I find myself not wanting to say the chapter just means that. Decades later he announces in a new preface that the dog was only ever about grief and never about the mother; I don't think this changes what the book meant. In another case, an author insists his novel is an allegory for the decline of rural Ireland, but every scene that would have carried that reading was cut before publication and no competent reader I know can get it out of the printed text; I say the finished novel simply doesn't mean that, whatever he intended. An anonymous sixteenth-century poem, with no authorial documents at all, doesn't bother me: its meaning is fixed by the best reading a competent reader of its genre and period would give, no facts about an author needed. The closest I can get to a principle is: a work means what its author intended to communicate, but only where the work itself, read against the conventions the author was using, can bear that meaning. As stated, though, that principle looks like it would make the diary case come out the other way, and I can't tell whether that's a bug in the principle or an error in my gut. What principle, or small set of candidates, best captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up, and why should that one go rather than a nearby one? And what does the resulting view predict for the case I keep going back and forth on: a novelist who encodes a cryptographic message in the chapter headings, with the decryption key left in his will, so that a reader who decodes it gets a second meaning — is that second meaning part of what the novel means? Please keep clearly separated what is settled in the philosophy-of-interpretation literature and what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
