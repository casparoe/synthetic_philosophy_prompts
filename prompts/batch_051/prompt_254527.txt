I spent my working life as an evolutionary biologist, and I have only recently begun to read philosophy of biology in a systematic way, so I am more interested in working through a case carefully than in a survey of positions.

Consider the Swampman thought experiment: a creature is assembled by chance, molecule-for-molecule identical to an ordinary human adult but with no evolutionary or developmental history. The case is often presented as separating two accounts of biological function. On an etiological account, a trait's function is fixed by the history of selection that explains why the trait is present. On a causal-role account, a trait's function is simply its contribution to some capacity of the containing system, whatever its history. The standard case is supposed to divide them: the creature's heart pumps blood, so the causal-role account ascribes to it the function of pumping blood, whereas the etiological account, at least on a token-level reading, says it has no function at all.

Now vary the case in two ways, and run each account through both variations carefully.

First variation. The swamp assembles not an adult but a single cell that is molecule-for-molecule identical to a fertilized human zygote. The cell is implanted, carried to term, and raised in an ordinary family, and the resulting person develops normally and is healthy by every measure. Does this person's heart have the function of pumping blood? Does the etiological account give the same answer here as in the standard adult case? Does the causal-role account? In particular, does it matter for the etiological account that the heart was produced by an ordinary developmental process rather than assembled directly? If the answer depends on whether functions attach to trait types or to token traits, trace through both readings and say where they part company.

Second variation. Return to the adult case, but suppose the swamp event is not a one-off. Such assembly events have occurred repeatedly over a long enough period that a population of swamp-assembled organisms exists, its members reproduce with one another, heritable variation arises, and natural selection operates on that population for many generations. Take two individuals: a founder, assembled by chance before the population took shape and now long dead, and one of that founder's great-grandchildren, born in the ordinary way into the evolving swamp lineage. On the etiological account, does the great-grandchild's heart have the function of pumping blood? Does the founder's heart? If the answers differ, identify exactly what confers function on the one and withholds it from the other. Does the founder's heart acquire its function retrospectively once the lineage has undergone selection, or is a trait's function fixed once and for all by the history available at the time the token exists? Does the causal-role account register any of these distinctions, and if it does not, is that a strength or a weakness of the account?

Finally, say where the two accounts still diverge after these variations, and whether either variation collapses the original dispute or merely relocates it.
