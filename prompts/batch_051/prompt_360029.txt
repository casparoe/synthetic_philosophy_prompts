I recently heard two people I know—call them Nadia and Peter—in an argument I wasn't part of. They agree on the facts of an upcoming election for a single powerful executive office. Three candidates are running. X and Y are the only ones with a realistic chance of winning; Z, whose platform matches Nadia's convictions almost exactly, is polling around two percent. Both accept that X would govern substantially less badly than Y on the whole: Nadia regards one plank of X's platform as gravely wrong—a sweeping surveillance measure—but she thinks Y would be worse on nearly every other dimension and would actively harm people she cares about. Nadia said she planned to vote for Z, and Peter said that was a disguised vote for Y. Their exchange went roughly like this:

1. Nadia: "A vote isn't just a push on a lever. It's the one official act in which I get to record what I stand for. If I vote for X, I'm ratifying the surveillance measure with one hand while condemning it with the other. I won't make myself complicit in something I believe is wrong. I'd rather cast a vote that actually expresses my convictions, even if Z can't win."

2. Peter: "You're treating a vote like a diary entry. But a vote is a share in a collective decision procedure, and we have a civic duty to use that procedure for what it is: a way of choosing between the candidates who can actually govern, not a way of signaling private virtue. Since you agree Y is worse than X, a vote for Z—compared with a vote for X—just leaves the worse candidate slightly more likely to win. You don't have to like X to be obligated to lower the chance of the outcome you yourself think is worse."

3. Nadia: "My single vote won't decide the election. So your causal test proves too much: if the probability of my vote changing the outcome is effectively the same either way, then my vote for X is just as causally negligible as my vote for Z. Once you admit that, the only non-negligible difference left is the expressive one—what the act says about me—and I'd rather it say what I actually believe."

4. Peter: "That's the lottery error in reverse. A one-in-a-million chance of preventing a large harm is still a large expected harm, if the harm is large enough. A small probability isn't a zero probability, and it doesn't make expected-value reasoning inapplicable. You're also comparing the wrong alternatives: X and Y are the only ones who will actually hold the office. The choice between them is a choice between two real futures. Z isn't one of them."

5. Nadia: "Then apply expected value consistently. Small parties can have real long-run effects by crossing thresholds—public funding, debate access, forcing the major parties to compete for their voters. A vote for Z is a tiny probability of a large shift in the political agenda. If we're allowed to count tiny probabilities of large effects, my vote for Z has expected value too, and it doesn't require me to attach my name and energy to the surveillance measure."

6. Peter: "You're asking to keep clean hands while a worse person takes power. You agree that Y is worse than X. If Y wins and harms the people you say you care about, your refusal to support X is part of the causal story. The lesser evil is still evil, but less evil is still less—and you're answerable for the margin you could have closed and didn't."

7. Nadia: "That has no floor. If every election I'm told that the lesser evil is less evil, I get moved one notch at a time toward candidates I would have found unthinkable ten years ago. At some point 'lesser evil' stops being a reason and becomes a ratchet. My integrity has to be worth something in present terms, or it will never be worth anything when it matters."

Adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say who is right about what, where the move overreaches or doesn't follow, and which specific claims survive. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing—a distinction or argument that would reframe the dispute. I'm not asking for a verdict on who wins overall; I want to know which particular claims are sound once the other side's reply is taken seriously.
