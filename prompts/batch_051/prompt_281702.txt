You're a philosopher who works on the moral psychology of the emotions and on distributive justice. Work through this carefully, and start by brainstorming a bit before you commit to a diagnosis — I'm not asking you to just declare who's right.

Here's a debate that has always struck me as weirdly stuck, both in academic philosophy and in the op-ed pages: is envying the rich a moral failing or a justified response to injustice? One side says envy is a vice and that's the end of it. It's pain at someone else's advantage, and the impulse behind it is to bring the other person down rather than to make the distribution fair. They'll usually add that this is basically Rawls's view too: envy proper isn't a moral feeling, it tends to make everyone worse off, and a well-ordered society should discourage it, while resentment at actually being wronged is a different thing entirely. The other side says what gets called "envy" is usually just people noticing that an inequality is unjust — that it came from rigged rules or inheritance or plain exploitation — and that this is really resentment, not envy, and a signal rather than a sin.

The wrinkle is that Rawls also had a category that cuts across this, as I read him: even envy proper can be excusable when the gap is large enough, the worse-off are trapped in it, and you can't reasonably expect them not to feel it. In a just society the gap would be justified, so envy wouldn't be excusable; in an unjust one, a bit of vice is also a symptom. The two sides never seem to lock onto the same phenomenon: one points at people who'd rather burn the whole thing down than see anyone else do better, the other points at people who just want fair pay and a stable life, and both call there target "envy."

My hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it's actually several different disagreements stacked on top of one another under a single word — one about what envy is, one about what enviers actually want, and one about whether the background inequalities are just — but I want you to test that rather than take it on faith. Diagnose the sturcture of this dispute. Is it verbal, factual, a disagreement about the justice of the background, or a clash of bare starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what specifically would count as progress — a distinction, an empirical result, a reframing — or should we expect the impasse to persist?
