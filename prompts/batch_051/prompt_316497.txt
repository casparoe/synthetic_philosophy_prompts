Here's an institutional setup that's always struck me as philosophically strange. Turkey is in a customs union with the European Union even though its not an EU member state. Under that arrangement, Turkey has to apply the EU's common external tariff to goods entering from outside the union, and it has to fall in line with trade deals the EU negotiates with other countries, but it gets no vote in setting those tariffs or negotiating those deals. Meanwhile an actual member state like Ireland has also handed its independent tariff-setting to Brussels, yet everyone still calls Ireland a sovereign state and nobody thinks Turkey has become part of the EU's political institutions.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory of sovereignty and membership that this practice presupposes. Is sovereignty the kind of thing that can be sliced into discrete chunks --- tariff-setting here, migration rules there, courts somewhere else --- and shared between seperate bodies without creating a contradiction? Or is that just a legal fiction, and the real question is who has the final say when the chips are down? Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined (which rival theories could all license it), and tell me what would have to change if the EU and Turkey had to operate with the traditional Westphalian picture, where sovereignty can't be divided and a state is either in or out.

Don't bother with citations. Write your answer in American English.
