I have no academic background, but after reading a few popular discussions of Gödel's incompleteness theorems I have spent a long time thinking on my own about the way the same results get used to support opposite conclusions. As I understand the core mathematics, the first theorem says that any consistent, recursively axiomatized formal system that can express a modest amount of arithmetic contains a sentence that the system can neither prove nor refute, and that is true when read as a statement about the natural numbers. The second theorem says that no such system can prove its own consistency, if it is consistent. What interests me is not the theorems themselves but the arguments built on top of them, which seem to run forever without moving.

One family of arguments treats the theorems as showing real limits of computation, prediction, and rule-governed reasoning in general. People argue, for instance, that no computer program could ever match human mathematical reasoning, because a human can see that the so-called undecidable sentence is true while any machine is supposedly trapped inside its own formal system; or that physics can never have a final theory of everything, because any theory rich enough to cover arithmetic will contain truths it cannot prove; or, more broadly, that any system of explicit rules must leave some truths outside itself, so human reasoning is in principle more than the operation of rules. I will call these the expansionist readings.

The other side always replies that this is overreading. The theorems concern a specific kind of object: a recursively axiomatizable formal system strong enough for arithmetic. A human mind, a physical theory, or a legal system is not obviously such an object. Words like complete, true, provable, and mechanism are being pulled from their technical senses into loose everyday senses. A sentence that one fixed system cannot prove may be provable in a slightly stronger or different system, so the result does not say that some particular truth is beyond all possible proof by any mind or any machine. And the inability of a system to prove its own consistency is not a special defect of machines; it is a general feature shared by all systems of the relevant kind, so it cannot mark the boundary between human thought and computation.

I have watched versions of this exchange play out many times in comment sections, popular books, and online essays, and the two sides never seem to move. Each side ends by saying the other simply does not understand the theorem, and then both walk away with the positions they started from. My working theory is that the disagreement is not really about a mathematical result at all, but about what a mathematical result may legitimately show about things outside mathematics, together with a set of unnoticed shifts in the meanings of words like machine, rule, truth, and complete. But I do not trust my own diagnosis, and I would like a professional one rather than another restatement of the theorems.
