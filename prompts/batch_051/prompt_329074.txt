I retired from geochemistry a while back and have been working through moral philosophy on my own, and I keep circling around the lying-versus-misleading debate. Consider a controlled pair: S wants H to believe a false proposition P; in one version S asserts not-P, which she believes is false, and in the other she asserts a true sentence Q (which she believes is true) that she has chosen because H will infer P from it; the same false belief is formed and the same consequences follow. Hold detection, punishment, and deniability aside. My instinct is that the first is morally worse — I'd put it at maybe 0.7 — but a lawyer friend says that once evidance and deniability are stripped out, there's no real moral difference left. What is your credence that the lying version is worse than the misleading version in itself? Decompose the number for me: how much rides on whether moral wrongness tracks what is said versus what is communicated, on whether the hearer is partly responsible for drawing the inference, on whether the residual difference is just social and legal privilege for the literal truth, and anything else you'd include; give rough probabilities, show how they combine, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what would move it. Please answer in American English.
