I teach an undergraduate introduction to political philosophy, and we're about to do Locke on property. The students get the labour-mixing story easily — you put your work into something unowned and it becomes yours — but the Lockean proviso always lands on them as an asterisk, and the spoilage condition even less than that. Two weeks later they talk as if Locke's whole theory amounts to: find something nobody owns, work on it, and it's yours, end of story. I want to run a session where they actually feel why labour, the proviso, and the spoilage limit pull against each other, and in particular see how the proviso could mean merely not leaving anybody obviously worse off or the much stricter demand to leave as much and as good still available for others. The class is fourteen people, we have eighty minutes, it's a mix of first- and second-years, and I don't want to lecture first.

Can you design a runnable game or structured simulation for this — real rules, real but trivial stakes, the kind where the idea shows up in what happens to them before it gets named? Ideally the setup tempts them into acting like Locke's ideal appropriator, and then the rules quietly make scarcity bite: someone ends up with nothing, or with a claim everyone else thinks is unfair, or holding more than they can possibly use while others go without. I want them to notice only at the end that they were re-running the proviso, and to be able to argue both sides of it with some heat by the time we debrief. Stakes should be small — candy, tokens, points, sections of a map — not money and definitely not real property.

For your answer, structure it in this order: (1) Materials and setup — what to bring and roughly how long prep takes, about a paragraph; (2) The activity phase by phase — the actual rules and rough minute-by-minute timing; (3) The debrief — the sequence of questions, in order, that map specific moments in the game onto labour, the proviso, and spoilage seperately, about two paragraphs; (4) What will probably go wrong when I run it and how to handle it, around a paragraph; (5) What the game can't capture about Locke's actual argument that I'll have to supply myself, one honest paragraph. And no reading list or citations; I just want something I can run next week.
