I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether history is more like a science or a story. I keep tripping over the same standoff and want your help untangling it. In the preface to his first book, from 1824, Ranke says the aim of history is just to show the past as it actually was, not to judge the past or hand the present a moral lesson. Then in E.H. Carr's What Is History?, Carr argues that it's a fallacy to think there's a hard core of historical facts existing independently of the historian's interpretation of them. And Hayden White goes further in Metahistory, arguing that historians inevitably shape their material into one of a small set of inherited literary plot forms, and that nothing in the evidence forces a choice between, say, a tragedy and a romance. What I want to know is whether these positions are really in contradiction, or whether Ranke's slogan and the two skeptics are shooting at different targets. Where exactly do their positions part ways, and what would a principled reconcilliation look like, as opposed to a fudge in which everyone comes out sounding right? Give me a verdict I can defend in print: which apparent disagreements are real, which reconciliations are honest, and which are special pleading. And if you can, say whether a working historian doing careful archival or quantative work today can still honestly describe what they're doing as Ranke-ish without taking on board the worst of what White says. Please write the answer in American English.
