At the small AI research organization where I work, we have begun using structured debates between two language-model agents to stress-test technical proposals before acting on them. The setup mirrors the version I have seen discussed for years in LessWrong and Alignment Forum circles: one agent defends a proposal, another attacks it, a human judge can interrupt to request that specific claims be expanded, and both agents receive the same token budget. The practice has caught a number of obvious flaws, but a recent episode has left me uncertain how much to trust it.

The proposal under scrutiny was a change to how we filter and weight the data for one of our fine-tuning runs. The critic produced a long, precise objection: the weighting heuristic, it argued, would silently upweight the very failure modes we were trying to suppress, biasing the loss in exactly the wrong direction. It read as the kind of subtle bug that design review exists to catch, so I spent most of a week verifying it. The central step turned out to be invalid. The argument treated reweighting a per-sample gradient as equivalent to changing how often those samples appear in the data distribution, which does not hold when the weights depend on the model's current predictions. What unsettles me is not that the critic was wrong, but that neither the defending agent nor I caught the move during the debate; the defender conceded a minor subpoint and never addressed the load-bearing step. We were left with a confident, technical-sounding argument that later fell apart under inspection.

I am not sure what lesson to draw. It could be that our protocol was simply underengineered: a judge with more time to drill into individual claims, or a defender prompted to isolate the single most load-bearing premise, might have exposed the problem. But the appeal of debate in the first place is that judging an argument is supposed to be easier than doing the underlying technical work, and here falsifying the argument cost far more than producing it. I have seen general worries discussed that a capable adversarial system could produce arguments that are wrong in ways a judge cannot catch, but I had not expected to meet a miniature version in a low-stakes internal tool. I would like a considered opinion: is this a correctable flaw in how we ran the debate, or does it reveal a structural limit on how far debate-style oversight can be trusted for decisions of this kind? Give me both the conceptual considerations and concrete changes I should make.
