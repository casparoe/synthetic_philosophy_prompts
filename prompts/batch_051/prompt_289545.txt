I was at a reading group on the history of moral philosophy last week, and two members, Rhea and Cole, got into a long argument about Bentham's application of the principle of utility to sexual morality. They accepted the same starting point: Bentham held that private, consensual sexual acts, including some widely condemned in his own day, ought not to be criminalized because they are pleasures that harm no one; the only "offence" is the distress felt by third parties who disapprove, and Bentham was dismissive of that kind of distress as mere antipathy, a manufactured or interested aversion rather than a real cost the law should protect.

Rhea said the position was internally unstable. A consistent utilitarian has to take every pain as it comes: if a majority is genuinely anguished by what two adults do in private, that anguish is as real as the pleasure it resents, and Bentham must either admit that enough popular revulsion could justify suppression or import a filter saying some pains don't count because they are badly grounded. But a hedonic calculus has no test for whether a pain is well-founded; a pain caused by a false belief is still a pain. So his liberal conclusion, she argued, rests on a non-utilitarian principle he never states. Cole replied that counting the pain of a person upset by someone else's harmless pleasure is not an innocent extension of the calculus, because it creates a standing incentive to cultivate one's own vexation. The legislator, he said, has to ask which general rule tends to maximize happiness over the long run; a rule that lets intense popular disgust override consensual private liberty manufactures more such disgust, so refusing to count that species of pain is a debunking of a pseudo-cost, not the importation of a foreign principle. Rhea came back that calling it a pseudo-cost already assumes the liberal standard: from inside the ledger, antipathy is a cost like any other, and its being caused by prejudice is a point about its origins, not its reality. Bentham counted pleasures and pains caused by artificial opinion all the time; if the majority's revulsion is set aside because it is revulsion at something harmless, then "harmless" is doing the work before the calculation, which is exactly the circularity. Cole conceded the point about the ledger but denied the circularity. The work, he said, is done by the legislator's question, not by a prior moral standard: a rule that lets any sufficiently intense public repugnance override private consensual conduct has worse tendencies than a rule that treats that repugnance as giving law no reason, even when, taken case by case, suppressing a particular act would relieve more immediate pain than it caused. That is a thoroughly utilitarian reason, even if it is not act-utilitarian.

The group turned to me, since I had been taking notes, and asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please act as a professional philosopher working on the structure of utilitarian theories and give a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as actually made. Go through each round and say who is right about which specific claim, whether Rhea's circularity charge can be answered on strictly Benthamite or utilitarian grounds, and which of the two is more right overall. It is fine to distinguish act-, rule-, and indirect utilitarianism, but make clear which architecture is doing what work. Think carefully about what each side must commit to before you give the verdict, and say explicitly what, if anything, both of them are missing.
