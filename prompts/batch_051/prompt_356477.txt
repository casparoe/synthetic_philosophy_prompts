I have been turning over the following argument this term, and I cannot decide whether it is a devastating objection to impartial morality or a subtle piece of sophistry. Each step seems forced on me, yet the terminus is one I refuse to inhabit. I would like a precise diagnosis, not a survey of the literature.

(1) Suffering is intrinsically bad wherever it occurs, and the fact that it is felt by a nonhuman animal rather than a human being does not make it weigh less morally.

(2) For the overwhelming majority of wild animals — the very numerous ones, such as insects, small rodents, amphibians, and the larvae and fry of broadcast-spawning fish — existence is on balance bad. Such a life is typically brief, shaped by hunger, injury, disease, and constant predation risk, and is usually ended by a painful death. Most individuals die before maturity; even the survivors experience levels of stress and deprivation that we would consider unacceptable in any animal under human care.

(3) Destroying or converting a habitat — draining a wetland, clearing a woodland, paving a meadow — prevents the animals that would otherwise have come into existence there from existing at all. The suffering those lives would have contained is not displaced onto other animals; it is simply prevented.

(4) If a life would have been on balance bad, then preventing that life from coming into existence improves the world in the same way that preventing a bad event improves the world: it leaves the outcome containing less suffering than it otherwise would have.

(5) Therefore, other things being equal, habitat destruction reduces the total amount of suffering in the world.

(6) Therefore, other things being equal, habitat destruction is not merely permissible but morally good, and where it is cheap relative to the suffering it prevents it may even be morally required.

(7) But that conclusion is unacceptable. It would make clearing the Amazon morally admirable, and would turn the conservation of forests, wetlands, and meadows into the deliberate preservation of an immense source of harm. Any view with that consequence seems, by humane standards, a reductio of itself.

(8) Hence at least one of (1)–(4), or the inference from them to (5) and (6), must be false.

Where, exactly, does the argument go wrong? If you had to reject one step, which would it be, and what does rejecting it cost? I am most reluctant to give up (1), since it looks like the core of impartial morality; but I want to know what each available exit forces me to surrender elsewhere — about obligations to animals, about procreation, about the value of existence itself — before I choose one.
