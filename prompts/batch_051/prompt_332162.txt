You are a microeconomic theorist who works on revealed preference, behavioral welfare economics, and mechanism design. Think carefully before committing to a verdict: first brainstorm the actors and their objectives, then work through the incentives, and only then identify the most likely failure modes.

A consumer-welfare regulator is designing a decision-support system for households choosing among subscription contracts (insurance plans, energy tariffs, mobile phone plans). The regulator's stated goal is to maximize each household's welfare as the household itself would define it: to help people get what they would choose if fully informed and free of momentary distortion, not what a paternalist thinks is good for them.

Here is the proposed mechanism.

Each year the regulator collects a household's complete choice history in the relevant market: every plan selected, every switch, every default accepted without action, and every active choice made on comparison screens, along with the menu of alternatives the regulator believes the household faced at each point. From this history the regulator infers a utility function using the Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP). If the history satisfies GARP, the regulator constructs a utility function that rationalizes the choices, scores every available contract against it, and auto-enrolls the household in the highest-scoring contract. If the history violates GARP beyond a fixed critical level, as measured by Afriat's efficiency index (the largest fraction of observed expenditure at which the choices could still be seen as utility-maximizing; 1 means consistent with GARP, and lower values mean more severe violations), the household is classified as noisy. Noisy households receive no personalized scoring and are assigned a population-average default contract instead.

Once per year a household may file a sworn self-statement of preferences, for example a complete ranking of every available contract. The self-statement replaces the choice-inferred utility function for scoring purposes, but only if the household's choice history is not classified as noisy. The regulator's rationale is this: a household whose choices are consistent has shown that stated preferences and choices can cohere, so its self-statement may reveal welfare-relevant information that limited menus failed to elicit; a household whose choices are systematically inconsistent, by contrast, cannot be expected to have a stable welfare criterion to report, so treating its self-statement as authoritative would simply swap an unreliable behavioral signal for an unreliable verbal one.

Walk through how this system would actually behave. What does it incentivize for sophisticated and for unsophisticated households? How will firms design menus, defaults, and comparison screens in response? What happens to households whose preferences are context-dependent, constructed in the act of choosing, or changing over time? Where is the system gamed first, and which failure modes are fixable without abandoning the entire scheme?

Throughout, separate what is technically settled—for instance, what GARP and the Afriat efficiency index can and cannot guarantee about the rationalizability of choice data—from what is genuinely contested in behavioral welfare economics, such as whether GARP violations constitute welfare-relevant mistakes, whether self-reports should have standing as evidence of welfare, and whether a utility function reconstructed from choices deserves authority over the chooser. Do not present a contested view as the consensus among experts.
