For the past several months I have been trying to work out what I actually think a disease or disorder is, because the question has stopped being abstract for me. My nine-year-old son was assessed twice this year for possible ADHD. One clinician described him as having a classic neurodevelopmental disorder; the second said the real difficulty is a mismatch between his temperament and the school's expectations, and suggested that the word "disorder" is a value judgment wearing a medical coat. Meanwhile my father has been treated for hypertension for years: he has never felt ill, and no one has ever paused over the word "disease." I want to understand where I stand, and I want the other parents in a discussion group I help run to be able to do the same.

Please write a decision tree — or, if you judge that a short sorting quiz with a scoring key would sort people more reliably, a quiz with that key — that takes a thoughtful non-specialist from their answers to seven or eight concrete questions to one of six to eight named positions on the question: What makes a condition a disease or disorder?

The instrument is for a small group of educated parents, several of whom have children carrying psychiatric or neurodevelopmental diagnoses; none of them can be assumed to know the philosophical labels. Every branching question must be answerable as a clear yes/no, or on a simple short scale, without any prior knowledge of the literature. The questions themselves should not mention any philosopher, theory, or "-ism"; only the leaves should carry names.

The named positions that must appear as leaves are: strict naturalism (the biostatistical view); the harmful-dysfunction hybrid; normativism; social constructionism or the labeling view; medical pragmatism (a condition is a disorder, roughly, if it is a legitimate target of medical treatment); and eliminativism (the word "disease" has no single workable analysis and should give way to more specific descriptions). If the logical space you construct forces a further, distinct position that nobody usually names, include it and mark it as unnamed or give it a plain-language label.

The diagnostic content must be built around concrete cases. Please use all of the following as decision points or choice scenarios rather than as illustrations tacked on at the end:
- a nine-year-old who cannot sit still or finish written work and is unhappy in school, whose two clinicians disagree in the way I described;
- a middle-aged adult with elevated blood pressure and no symptoms, who takes a daily pill solely to lower long-term risk;
- a person two months into intense grief after the death of a spouse, not sleeping, losing weight, and unable to return to work;
- a physically short but otherwise healthy child whose doctor offers growth-hormone treatment to increase eventual adult height;
- an adult with chronic pain for which no tissue damage or pathology can be found; and
- the historical fact that a widely recognized psychiatric manual once classified homosexuality as a disorder and later removed the classification, though the underlying facts about people did not change.

I care about the architecture more than the prose. Please build the tree from the logical space, not from the labels: first work out the underlying yes/no distinctions (for example, whether there exists an objective fact of biological malfunction apart from human values; whether harm to the person is required; whether statistical typicality is doing the work; whether social arrangements or the availability of treatment play a constituting role; whether we need a single definition at all), then determine which combinations of answers are coherently available, and only then locate the named positions on those combinations. For any combination of answers that no known position occupies, add a one-sentence diagnosis of why: because the combination is internally inconsistent, because it would be deeply unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored.

For each leaf, give the position's name, the pattern of answers leading to it, and one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment — the case among the six above, or another case, that the view classifies in a way its own proponents would find uncomfortable.

Finally, flag candidly any pair of positions that cannot be separated by the questions you used, and state the single additional question that would separate them if you had one more to ask. If you write a quiz, include an explicit scoring key mapping every answer pattern to a leaf, plus a rule for ambiguous results.
