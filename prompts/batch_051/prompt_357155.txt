I am a novelist, and I have been having a quiet, polite disagreement with my fourteen-year-old daughter's geometry teacher, and I would like a careful view before I say anything further to either of them. My daughter can reproduce a proof of the theorem that the angle at the centre of a circle is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc. She can write it out in the correct order, and when challenged she can justify each line as she goes: this step follows by a named theorem, that one by the equality of angles in the same segment, and so on. But she cannot say why the proof is arranged in that way — why the auxiliary diameter is drawn at the outset, why the argument then splits into cases, what would go wrong if one of the assumptions were altered. She calls what she is doing learning the moves.

Her teacher maintains that this just is understanding: to understand a proof is to be able to produce it correctly and to give a valid reason for each step. The global why I keep pressing for, the teacher says, is either a request for more steps of the same kind or a literary habit that has no authority over mathematics.

As a novelist, I know the difference between following every sentence of a scene and understanding what the scene is doing, and I suspect the distinction is not merely literary. But I do not want to impose a writer's criterion on mathematics, and I do not want to tell my daughter that she understands something she does not understand, or that she fails to understand something she in fact does. So this is not a request for study techniques. My question is: is the teacher right about what it is to understand a proof? If not, what exactly is missing, and what would my daughter need to be able to do, or to see, before we should say she understands the proof rather than merely performs it? And how should a parent without mathematical training talk to her truthfully about that difference without depreciating the competence she plainly has?

Please separate what is settled in the philosophy and teaching of mathematics from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
