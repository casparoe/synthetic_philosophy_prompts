In the near future, suppose a well-replicated series of psychophysics experiments establishes the following. Subjects are exposed to a stimulus that varies continuously and gradually — for definiteness, a probe warming at 0.01 °C per second — and are asked at every moment to report, as sincerely and accurately as they can, whether they are in pain. For a large class of ordinary subjects, the transition from not being in pain to being in pain occurs at a single, perfectly sharp threshold T*, and their reports track the condition without observed error: whenever a subject is in pain, she reports that she is in pain, and the experimental setup is such that her report amounts to knowledge; whenever she is not in pain, she reports that she is not, and this too amounts to knowledge. There is no margin-for-error region, no band of unreliable or wavering reports on either side of T*. The pattern survives distraction, mild fatigue, and modest mood changes, so it is not an artifact of a single highly attentive session. In short, one non-trivial mental condition, being in pain, turns out to be luminous in the strongest sense: the subject is in the condition if and only if she is in a position to know that she is in it, with a sharp boundary in the underlying physical stimulus.

Assume this result is accepted by the relevant scientific community. Give your reasoned prediction of how it would alter the dialectic around the KK principle (the thesis that if a subject knows that p, then she is in a position to know that she knows that p) and around the anti-luminosity argument (which purports to show that no non-trivial mental condition can be luminous, because a sharp cutoff in a gradual transition would require knowledge differences between adjacent cases too similar to support them). Make your chain of reasoning explicit. Address at least the following, and indicate for each link whether you regard it as robust or speculative and what further finding would overturn it: which premise or step of the anti-luminosity argument would have to give way for such a result to be possible; whether showing that one condition is luminous would vindicate the KK principle or merely leave it standing, and why; and what kind of cognitive architecture could implement a perfectly sharp, reliably self-known boundary between the absence and presence of a phenomenal condition.
