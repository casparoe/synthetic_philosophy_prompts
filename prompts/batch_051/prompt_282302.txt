I keep getting stuck on a very specific question about whether I can have probabilities over my own choices. The theory side is basically the whole Bayesian package: degrees of belief over an algebra of propositions, coherence, expected utility, the lot. "I will send this email" is a proposition like any other, and carving it out of the algebra feels like exactly the kind of ad hoc exception that stops the formalism from being a general account of reasoning. In fact it's worse than a hole: the things I use credences and utilities to make are my choices, so I'd be refusing to place credences on the very things the theory is supposed to govern.

But the intuition on the other side is equally firm. When I'm actually deliberating—say deciding at 2am whether to send the email or close the laptop—assigning a probability to sending it feels like it either just is the decision or else turns me into a spectator of myself. If I settle on 80 percent, I've basically decided, and if I say the 80 percent is compatible with still choosing not to send, then I'm forecasting my own behavior rather than choosing it. Deliberation seems to need the question to be practically open, and a probability over the answer seems to close it. One asymmetry keeps tripping me up too: I can comfortably assign a probability to a past choice I don't remember, and I can assign one to someone else's future choices, but the closer the choice gets to mine, now, under deliberation, the less coherent the assignment feels. Which side is tracking something real?

I don't want a verdict that just declares a winner. I want the strongest version of each side and then a negotiated settlement. What would it take for the intuition to be the part I give up—is it a confusion between predicting and deciding, an introspection illusion, a scope problem—and what would the debunking actually show? What would it take for the Bayesian picture to be the part I modify—can I restrict credences to non-agential propositions without breaking the coherence account, or does that just amount to abandoning Bayesianism exactly where it was supposed to do work? Or is there a reading where both survive, something like credences over my acts being conditional on future evidence or on a policy I haven't chosen yet, so deliberation is the process of choosing the condition while the probability remains well defined? I want the costs of each move, and which one you'd actually make, rather than a two-sentence ruling.
