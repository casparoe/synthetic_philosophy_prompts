The exam question was:

Explain what the user illusion account of consciousness says, and explain why it might be taken to imply epiphenomenalism. Does that implication follow from the account itself, or only from additional assumptions? Assess the strongest available version of the inference.

The student’s answer:

The user illusion account holds that conscious experience is like a graphical interface: it presents a simplified, helpful summary of our own cognitive processes, while the real computational work is done by unconscious mechanisms. The interface does not perform the underlying operations; it displays their results. By analogy, conscious thoughts, intentions, and decisions are not the causes of our actions but later reports or readouts of brain processes that have already produced the behavior. This naturally leads to epiphenomenalism: consciousness is a byproduct of the real physical work, with no causal role in action. However, the account does not by itself rule out conscious causation, because a display can still have effects; a computer interface can cause the user to click, for example. So the move to epiphenomenalism requires the extra assumption that there is no separate conscious agent standing apart from the machinery and using the display. On the strongest reading, the view entails epiphenomenalism only if one also assumes that the conscious representation appears after the relevant causal work is finished. But if consciousness is only a later summary, then it cannot be shaped by natural selection, and so it must be an accidental byproduct rather than a function.
