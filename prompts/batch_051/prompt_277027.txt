You are a moral philosopher specializing in the ethics of childhood, parental authority, and autonomy. You have been asked by a hospital clinical ethics committee to translate a philosophical principle into a reusable decision tool.

The committee reviews contentious parental decisions about a child's upbringing, education, and medical care. It meets weekly and has 45 minutes per case; its members are clinicians, social workers, and community representatives with no background in philosophy. The committee's findings are advisory: a hospital administrator or child-protection authority decides what to do with them.

The committee keeps seeing cases in which a child is not being harmed in any ordinary sense — the child is loved, safe, and currently doing fine — but a parental decision seems likely to close off much of the child's future before the child is old enough to have any say. Examples include withdrawing a child from school for intensive training in a single activity, refusing a safe intervention that would prevent a permanent impairment, and moving a child into an isolated community with little outside contact. The committee understands these as questions about the limits of parental authority: when does the moral license to make life-shaping choices on a child's behalf run out, even though the parents mean well and the child is not presently harmed? They want a procedure grounded in the idea that a child's autonomy interests are held in trust for the adult the child will become, and that children are owed a future in which a reasonable range of adult lives remains open.

Write the procedure as an ordered list of questions. For each question, state what counts as an adequate answer to proceed, and what answer should end the review or escalate it. The procedure must separate: (a) choices that harm the child now; (b) choices that are merely unconventional or at odds with the committee members' own values; (c) choices that close a future option reversibly rather than irreversibly; and (d) choices that genuinely violate the child's right to an open future. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot decide, and mark every step at which the committee must make a substantive value judgment instead of applying a rule.

Before you write the final procedure, think carefully about the hardest questions it must face: how open a future must be; whether what matters is the number of options, the importance of particular options, or the child's later capacity to revise; whether immersion in one thick way of life can itself preserve a kind of openness; and how to avoid treating a secular, middle-class upbringing as the neutral baseline against which all other plans are measured.

Then apply the resulting procedure to the three cases below. Show the steps, and reach a definite recommendation in each case.

1. The parents of an academically average nine-year-old want to withdraw her from school to enroll her in a residential ballet academy, where she would train more than 30 hours a week and receive only a few hours of academic instruction each day. She enjoys ballet and has modest talent but no realistic prospect of a professional career.

2. The parents of a six-year-old with progressive hearing loss refuse a safe one-time intervention that would prevent almost all further loss. They are attentive and not neglectful; they believe their child's future is best secured within a signing community and do not regard the condition as a disability. The intervention would be difficult or impossible to obtain later.

3. The parents of an eight-year-old plan to move the family to a remote religious community in which children are educated only inside the community, have little contact with outsiders, and are expected to remain as adults. The child is currently happy and well cared for.

For each case, identify the step at which the procedure does the decisive work, and state which judgment call remains and cannot be eliminated. Also include at least one further case of your own in which the procedure yields a recommendation the committee will not like, and explain why the procedure nonetheless commits them to it.

Do not cite case law, empirical studies, or particular texts, and do not supply a bibliography; the committee needs reasoning it can inspect, not authorities it would have to look up.
