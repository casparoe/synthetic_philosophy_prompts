I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and lately I can't get away from Feyerabend's "anything goes." It keeps showing up in two very different places. One is the op-ed contrarian version: someone cites it to argue that science has no special method, so "trust the science" is just an authority move dressed up as objectivity. The other is working scientists grumbling that "anything goes" is basically an accurate description of how peer review and grant panels actually work — method sections are rationalzations written after the fact, and the real rules are aesthetic, social, political. I'm fairly sure the same two words can't be doing the same work in both places, and I'd like to get clear on what they could be claiming. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses hiding in "anything goes" as a menu? For each, say whether it's a platitude, something substantive and defensable, or just false. Then tell me which reading each of my two crowds is leaning on, and whether that reading would actually support the use they're putting it to. I'm less interested in what Feyerabend "really meant" than in which readings survive scrutiny — if the slogan is mostly bluffing in one of those uses, I want to hear that.
