My team builds a customer-support assistant on top of a large language model, and one of our senior engineers settles every argument about the system's psychology with an analogy. He insists that attributing beliefs, goals, and intentions to the model is just the intentional stance from philosophy of mind — the same stance you take toward a chess computer when you say it "wants" to control the center or is "trying" to defend its king. You don't need the chess computer to have any real mental states to make those statements useful: they compress an enormous space of possible moves into a compact, predictive description. So, he argues, when we say our assistant "believes the user is a doctor" or "is trying to keep the conversation short," we are doing exactly the same thing. His stronger conclusion is that the question "does the model really believe that?" is misplaced, and we should not let debates about internal states slow down our debugging or design work.

I'm not convinced the two cases are alike enough for that conclusion to go through. The chess engine has a small state space, an explicit evaluation function, and a fixed search procedure, and we know exactly which aspects of its representation are available to which computations. Our model is a transformer trained to predict next tokens, with no store of propositions we can point to; yet it tracks facts about the conversation, revises what it says when new evidence arrives, and produces chain-of-thought explanations that behave as if they refer to the same underlying state from step to step. Separately, when we probe its activations, we find internal structure that corresponds to features of the input and conversation — features we would otherwise describe as its beliefs about the user.

I would like a point-by-point audit of how far the chess-engine analogy carries. Lay out what maps onto what in the two cases (a table of correspondences is fine), pinpoint where the mapping breaks down, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the senior engineer's no-commitment conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Then say whether his conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table, and, if the chess computer is the wrong comparison, propose a better analogy and say what it captures that the chess computer misses.
