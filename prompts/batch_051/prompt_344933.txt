I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about whose law should govern the internet, and I noticed something in my own reactions I can't make consistent. If an American shop sells to a customer in the EU, I don't blink at the idea that EU consumer protection rules apply: you reached into their market, you play by their rules. But if an American blogger writes something that's legal in the US, and it gets read in a country where that kind of statement is a crime, I find it genuinely outrageous that that country's courts would claim jurisdiction over the post. My friend kept saying it's the same principle either way: the sale happens where the customer is, the speech happens where it's read, and if you don't want to be governed there, don't publish there. I wanted a principled difference, something about speech being performed by the speaker or readers choosing to look, but by the end of the argument I wasn't sure my "differences" weren't just me liking free speech more than free trade. Is this asymmetry defensible, and what's the strongest principle that makes the two cases come apart? And be honest: does that principle survive the obvious countercases, or is it just a way of smuggling in a conclusion about who should win?
