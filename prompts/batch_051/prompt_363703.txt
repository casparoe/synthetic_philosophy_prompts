I'm a journalist who covers science, law, and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on how legal decision-makers treat naked statistical evidence: probability that points toward a person or company without any individualized evidence tying them to the particular event in question. Over several interviews, a legal theorist who studies this topic has given me her verdicts on a series of hypothetical cases. I want to anticipate how she will rule on a new one, and I'd like your help extracting the principle that best explains her record.

Her earlier verdicts:

1. Blue Bus. A pedestrian is hit by a bus at night. It is stipulated that 80 percent of the buses on that route are operated by the Blue Bus Company and the rest by a single other operator, and that the pedestrian has no further evidence about which bus struck her. She says there should be no liability for Blue Bus.

2. Gatecrasher. A venue sells exactly 1,000 tickets for an event. The records establish that 499 spectators paid and 501 entered without paying, but they do not establish who belongs to which group. A randomly selected spectator is sued for gatecrashing. She says liability cannot be imposed.

3. Prison Yard. One hundred inmates are present in a yard when a guard is assaulted. The evidence establishes that 99 of them participated in the assault and one refused, but there is no way to identify the one who refused. The authorities propose to discipline a randomly selected inmate. She rejects this.

4. Blue Bus with an Eyewitness. The facts are the same as in case 1, except that an eyewitness also testifies that the bus was blue. The witness is generally credible but not infallible. She says there should be liability.

The new case I plan to put to her is this. A factory takes delivery of 1,000,000 identical, unmarked valves from two suppliers. Complete shipping and manufacturing records establish that 999,999 of the valves came from Supplier A and exactly 1 came from Supplier B, but no record or marking can connect any particular valve to its supplier. A worker is injured when one of the valves fails, and every other element of the claim is stipulated. The probability that Supplier A supplied the failed valve is 99.9999 percent. Would she impose liability on Supplier A, if she is being consistent with her earlier verdicts?

I am less interested in the bare answer than in the analysis. Please do the following: identify the rule or rules that would explain all four of her earlier verdicts; state the rival rules that fit those verdicts just as well; say what each candidate rule predicts in the valve case; give your best prediction about her ruling, with reasons for preferring that reading of her; and describe one further hypothetical that would cleanly separate whichever rules the valve case itself cannot.

Please write your reply in American English.
