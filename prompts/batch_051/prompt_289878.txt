Two members of my neighborhood quiz group got into a long argument last weekend, and they asked me to referee it. I have been turning it over since and still do not trust my own verdict, so I want an outside one.

They were entering a charity guess-the-jar contest at a street fair. The organizers gave everyone the same printed information: the dimensions of the jar, the volume of a single peanut, the weight of one peanut, and the weight of the empty jar. Both Celia and Marcus are plainly good at this kind of estimation, and over the years they have won about the same number of such contests. They worked independently from that same information and produced very different figures: Celia estimated 1,250 peanuts, and Marcus 1,900. When they compared notes, the following argument unfolded.

(1) Celia said she immediately lowered her confidence in her own number. Since they started from the same information and are about equally good at the task, Marcus's different number is evidence that one of them has gone wrong somewhere, and she has no independent reason to think it was he rather than she; the sensible place to land is therefore somewhere between the two figures. Marcus said he would not budge. His number is the output of his calculation, and his calculation is his only access to how many peanuts are in that jar. Her surprise at his number, he said, is information about her estimate, not information about the peanuts.

(2) Celia answered that this is a double standard. From his chair, their disagreement tells him something about her; from her chair, it tells her something about him. If everyone reasoned that way, disagreement between equals would never move anyone, even though each would have said beforehand that the other was about as reliable as themselves. Marcus answered that he is not asking her to move, either. The rule is symmetrical, he said: each person should trust their own work, because each person is the only one who can examine their own arithmetic from the inside.

(3) Celia said that merely relocates the problem. Of course his arithmetic looks right to him and hers looks right to her; that is exactly what should be alarming once the two visible results diverge. The inner sense of having worked through the steps, she said, is not a second, independent piece of evidence; it is the same reasoning being endorsed again by the person who made it. So "I checked my own work" does not give him an epistemic standing that she lacks, and invoking it is special pleading. Marcus replied that from the outside the two of them are symmetric, but from the inside each of them has live access to only one chain of reasoning, and no one abandons a calculation they cannot fault simply because a hidden calculation elsewhere came out differently.

(4) Finally Marcus pressed what he took to be the decisive point. They now disagree about the peanuts, but also about what the right response to that disagreement is. If Celia's rule is general — whenever you discover that an equal disagrees with you, lower your confidence — then she should apply it to this very disagreement and become less sure that he ought to move. And if she will not lower her confidence here, then she is being about the higher-level disagreement exactly what she accuses him of being about the peanuts. Celia admitted the force of this, but said that "less sure" does not mean "no view": she could drop from quite confident to only moderately confident that he should move and still honestly hold, and state, that he should.

I do have reactions to each of these moves, but I do not want to slant the case in advance. Please go through the four exchanges and adjudicate them one at a time: say who is more right in each exchange, how much of the other's point survives, and why. Then give an overall verdict: which of the two is more in the right on the whole? And finally, say what neither of them has properly considered that a more careful treatment of disagreement between equals would bring in. I would rather you commit to definite judgments than survey the options and leave me to choose.
