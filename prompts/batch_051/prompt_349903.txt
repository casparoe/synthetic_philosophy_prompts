I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about blackmail, going by my gut on cases. There's the familiar puzzle where two things that are each fine on their own—threatening to reveal something true, and asking someone for money—turn into something clearly wrong when you bundle them together, but I can't find the line that says when the bundle is wrong. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases; I want you to find the principle behind them, if there is one.

1. I find out my neighbor is having an affair. I tell him pay me five thousand dollars or I'll tell his wife. Wrong, obviously; textbook blackmail.

2. Same neighbor, same affair, but he owes me five thousand dollars from a loan he's been dodging for two years. I tell him pay what you owe me or I'll tell your wife. This one feels fine to me, or at least way less bad than case 1.

3. A restaurant gave me salmonella. I tell the owner: refund the meal and cover my doctor's copay, or I'll post an honest review about the food poisoning. Fine; that's just standing up for myself.

4. Same restaurant, same review, but I say: give me free meals for a year or I'll post it. Wrong; that's extortion.

5. I work in accounting and discover my boss is hiding income from the tax office. I tell him: promote me or I report you. Wrong, even though the reporting itself would be a good thing to do.

6. A legislative aide discovers the politician he works for took bribes, and tells him: resign or I take the evidence to the press. Fine, probably even admirable.

7. A friend catches a mutual friend having an affair and says: pay me the twenty bucks you owe me from lunch last month or I'll tell your partner. Wrong, but I cant for the life of me say how it's different from case 2 except the amount.

8. A journalist learns a senator had an affair with an intern and tells him: if you don't give me an exclusive on-the-record interview about it, I'll run the story. I keep flipping on this one—sometimes it just seems like normal tough journalism, other times it feels like blackmail with an interview as the price.

My best attempt at a principle is something like: it's fine to use a threat as long as what you're demanding is something you're independently entitled to, and wrong when you use the threat to extract something you have no right to. That handles 1, 3, 4, and 5 pretty cleanly, but it makes 6 come out wrong, which I don't buy, it makes 8 wrong by default, which I'm not sure about, and it still won't explain why 7 feels wrong but 2 doesn't. So fix it, or scrap it and give me a better one.

Commit to a single principle and defend it. Don't give me a menu of candidate principles and leave me to choose. Tell me exactly where your principle draws the line on some new cases—say, threatening to post a truthful bad review unless the owner fixes a health code violation, or threatening to report tax fraud unless you get paid a "consulting fee"—and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and why I should drop those rather than tie the principle in knots to save them.
