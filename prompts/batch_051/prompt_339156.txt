I am drafting the introductory chapter of a monograph on integrity and impartiality, and I have reached a point where I need an outside audit of the position I have been defending rather than a restatement of it. I teach Williams's two best-known cases every year, and after a seminar discussion last week I realized I had never checked my settled views against one another. I currently hold the following five commitments. (1) In the Jim case, in which Jim is a visitor in a South American village and the officer in command tells him that if he shoots one of twenty captives the other nineteen will be released, whereas if he refuses all twenty will be shot, Jim is not merely permitted to shoot but is required to do so; a refusal that knowingly leaves nineteen additional deaths on the table is wrong, and keeping his hands clean is not an adequate reason. (2) Nevertheless, I accept what I take to be the substance of Williams's one-thought-too-many criticism: if the only justification available to an agent who is about to act is the thought that what he is about to do maximizes expected good, he stands in an ethically defective relation to his own deed, and this defect remains even when the deed is the right one. (3) In the George case, in which an unemployed chemist is offered a post in a chemical- and biological-weapons laboratory while knowing that if he refuses a more zealous chemist will take the post and pursue the research with greater energy, I hold that George ought to refuse even if accepting and working slowly would have better consequences, because taking the post would enlist his agency in a project that is incompatible with the commitments that constitute who he is. (4) I also hold that the deontic status of an action is invariant across agents: if two agents face the same options with the same consequences, the act that is right for one is right for the other, and no appeal to which of them happens to be which can make a difference. (5) Finally, I hold that a right act may leave a moral remainder—an agent can be required to do something that leaves him with reason to regret what he has done—yet the presence of such regret is never evidence that the act was wrong.

I would like you to tell me whether this package is jointly tenable. Be specific: identify which pairs of the five commitments are in genuine tension; which pairs only appear to conflict and would dissolve once the relevant distinction is drawn; and, if I must make a revision, which single revision is least costly and what it would cost. For any genuine tensions, rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally serious. Begin your answer with its bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, without preamble and without restating the question.
