I've been asked to lead a single seventy-five-minute session for a mixed reading group of philosophy and cognitive science graduate students on Dennett's quining qualia argument — the claim that qualia are an incoherent posit rather than a datum any theory of mind must accommodate. I plan to build the session around the Chase and Sanborn case, which I'll put to the students roughly like this: two longtime professional coffee tasters both report that the coffee they taste all day no longer tastes as good as it once did, although neither can detect any change in the coffee itself. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and it is his liking that has faded; the taste itself is unchanged. Sanborn says the reverse: his liking and standards are exactly what they were, but the way the coffee tastes to him has changed. Dennett's pressure point is that there appears to be no fact — not merely no practical test — that settles whether Chase's self-description or Sanborn's is the correct account of "the taste itself," once that supposed item is distinguished from standards, likings, and dispositions to judge. I don't need the argument explained to me; I need a plan for teaching it.

Please give me the session plan. In particular: (1) the order in which the ideas must be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit — above all, what about the concept of qualia has to be pinned down before the Chase and Sanborn case can carry weight, and which tempting starting points would quietly undermine the sequence; (2) why this case, rather than the inverted spectrum or Mary's room, should be the carrying example, and what the example must have and must leave out to do that work; (3) the misconceptions the group will predictably bring or form — I can already hear someone saying Dennett is denying that experience exists, or that this reduces to a bare other-minds skepticism, or that Chase and Sanborn are just unreliable introspectors — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, and how; (4) a final question I can put to the group that would tell me whether they have grasped the argument's target rather than merely learned to repeat its moves; and (5) what I should explicitly mark as out of scope for a single session and how I should mark it as set aside rather than silently avoiding it.
