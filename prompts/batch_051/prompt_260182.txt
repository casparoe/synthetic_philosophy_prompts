I'm a novelist — mostly literary fiction, so I already live inside questions of motivation, intention, and what a story "wanted" to do — and I've got no real philosophy background. Lately I've been trying to get a handle on a view I've seen called free-floating rationales: the claim that something can be explained by a reason that no one ever actually had or represented. The example that sticks with me is a bird's wing. The wing is shaped the way it is because of what it does for the bird, and that "because" is supposed to do real explanatory work even though natural selection never intended anything and no one ever thought the rationale through. The defenders insist these mindless rationales aren't just loose talk.

Please translate that position into the vocabularly I actually think in as a novelist: character motivation and backstory, authorial intent versus the narrator or implied author, organic plotting versus mechanical plotting, foreshadowing, dramatic irony, the feeling that a story demands a certain ending, discovery writing where you only figure out in revision what the book was doing all along. Work through them one by one and restate the free-floating rationale claim as precisly as you can in those terms. Then give me the residue report — what translates cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart at all in a novelist's framework. Finish by telling me whether the residue is a limitation of my vocabulary or a sign the philosophers' claim is blurrier than it sounds.
