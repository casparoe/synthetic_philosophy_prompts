I've been caught recently by a question about information that began with a small, concrete decision of my own: whether to take a genetic test for a condition that has no available treatment or prevention. My instinct is to refuse, but that refusal bothers me because it seems to conflict with the Bayesian result usually called Good's theorem. As I understand it, the theorem says that a costless signal can never lower an expected-utility maximizer's expected utility in advance, because after seeing the signal she can always ignore it and choose the act she would have chosen anyway. Free information should therefore always be at least weakly worth having, yet I keep producing verdicts that appear to contradict this.

Here are the verdicts I want the principle behind, if there is one. I would pay a modest amount to avoid being told the twist in a film I am about to see. I open important letters — exam results, a biopsy report — immediately, even when the outcome is already fixed and nothing I do can change it, because the suspense is worse than the news itself. I think an employer ought to tell a worker about a layoff coming in six months even where the decision is irreversible, and I regard this as more than politeness. If someone offered me one free glimpse of a poker card I could not otherwise see, I would always accept, even if I suspected the glimpse would only confirm I was beaten. If I were somehow certain the information would be accurate, I would refuse to be told the date of my own death. And when a project I backed failed without warning, I wanted to know months later exactly which person or decision had gone wrong, even though the project was beyond repair, because it would tell me whom to trust the next time. Is there one account of when information is worth having that captures all six, or do some of these verdicts have to go?

My own floating hypothesis is that Good's theorem is really only about information whose value passes entirely through the choices it lets me make, while the spoiler and the death date are cases where information carries direct hedonic cost or benefit on top of any choice I might make in light of it, and the layoff case is something else again — perhaps a moral matter rather than a question of expected utility. But I do not yet trust that the distinction is crisp enough to do the work. Please test the hypothesis against the cases, state whatever repaired principle you arrive at precisely, and say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise. Finally, apply it to the case I keep stalling on: a long, detailed referee report has arrived for a paper I have already withdrawn and set aside, and I have since moved to a different project — I want to know whether reading it is more like the poker card, the spoiler, or the death date.
