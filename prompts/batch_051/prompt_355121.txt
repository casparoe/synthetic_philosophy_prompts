I'm reading a book on the philosophy of pscyhiatry, and it gives this summary of Ian Hacking's 'looping effect':

"Psychiatric categories are different from the kinds you get in physics because the things being classified are people, and people respond to finding out how they've been classified. They read about their diagnosis, adjust how they act and how they describe themselves to fit it, and sometimes push back against it too. All that loops back into the category: clinicians revise the criteria, people adjust again, and the whole thing keeps moving. Hacking calls this the 'looping effect,' and he separates our 'interactive kinds' from the 'indifferent kinds' of the natural sciences. His deeper point, though, is that these interactive kinds aren't real kinds. They're made up by classification committees and then taken up by the people being classified, and most of the conditions they name wouldn't exist as stable, nameable illnesses if we just stopped using the labels. So the loop is basically a self-fulfilling prophecy: once a label is in circulation, people conform to it, and the category looks more and more real and validated, even though it's artificial all the way down."

Is this a fair summary? I keep feeling like the gloss is too strong, especially the self-fulfilling prophecy line, but I'm not confident enough to correct it. Walk through the summary sentence by sentence--what's right, what's a normal simplification, and what would make someone who actually works on Hacking wince--and say whether the book's conclusion, that the categories are "artificial all the way down," follows from the parts that are true.
