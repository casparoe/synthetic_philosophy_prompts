I am a software engineer on a team that builds a transaction-review model for a bank. The model issues a decision—approve, flag, or block—for each transaction and writes a rationale, but it cannot execute any decision itself: the pipeline requires a human officer to make the final call, and the model's role is explicitly advisory. In practice, officers accept the model's decision roughly 97% of the time and often pass through its rationale almost verbatim. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been asked to contribute to the responsible-AI section of the model's audit documentation. A colleague insists that the system is not a moral agent and cannot be responsible for its judgments because, in his words, it cannot act on them—it can only recommend, and a human does the acting. That claim seemed plausible, so I tried to make it precise, and the precise version now alarms me:

(1) An entity can bear moral responsibility for a judgment only if it is able to act on that judgment.
(2) Our system cannot act on its judgments; its outputs are advisory, and a human always performs or withholds the action.
(3) Therefore, our system is not a moral agent and cannot be morally responsible for the judgments it renders.
(4) By parity, consider a fully paralyzed person with unimpaired cognition who cannot move or speak and communicates only by spelling messages with an eye tracker. For many of her judgments she is likewise unable to act: she can indicate a decision, but a caregiver must execute it or decline.
(5) It follows from (1) that she is not a moral agent with respect to those judgments and cannot be morally responsible for them.
(6) Yet this seems plainly wrong. If she spells out an instruction directing a caregiver to make a fraudulent transfer from her account, we hold her responsible; we do not treat her as a mere advisory system.

Does the argument fail, and if so at which step? I would most like to keep (6) and the factual description of our pipeline in (2), and I am not attached to my colleague's conclusion, but I cannot identify which premise or inference to reject. Whatever I reject changes how I describe the system and its outputs in the audit, so I need a diagnosis precise enough to tell me which step gives way and what that rejection costs.
