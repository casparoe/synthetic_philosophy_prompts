I am a novelist, and the book I am currently researching centers on a global content-moderation team inside a large social media company. While interviewing people who do this work, I keep tripping over the same puzzle, and it has begun to shape the novel itself. A single post—an accusation against a public figure, a piece of satire, a disputed historical claim—may be lawful in the country where its author writes, unlawful in the country where the person criticized lives, and wholly unproblematic in the country where the platform keeps its servers. Courts in each of those countries nonetheless assert authority over that same content: one orders the platform to remove the post worldwide, another orders it restored, and a third bars the platform from removing it at all. Platforms respond with a patchwork of geoblocking, country-specific deletions, and the rule of applying the strictest law to the whole globe. The familiar way of framing this is: whose law governs the internet? But that framing assumes the real question is jurisdictional, and I am no longer sure it is.

What I want to know is what this practice—the extraterritorial application of speech law, the expectation that a platform must answer to every country in which its content can be read—presupposes about what speech itself is. Does it assume that an online post occurs in every place it is downloaded, so that one utterance is not one act with one location but many acts distributed across readers? Does it assume that the harm of speech is located in the audience while the right to speak is located in the speaker, and that these two things can be cleanly pulled apart? What theory of sovereignty does it presuppose—merely that a state may govern speech that has effects inside its territory, or something stronger, such as a right of cultural or political self-determination over everything readable by its citizens? Are the presuppositions even consistent with one another, and which of them are strictly required by the practice rather than merely compatible with it?

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with a background in philosophy of law, political philosophy, and the philosophy of language and action. Think this through carefully: begin by brainstorming the candidate commitments the practice might carry, then reconstruct the implicit theory it actually presupposes, separating what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flagging where the practice is philosophically underdetermined such that several rival theories would license it. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by its main rival. Please write the answer in American English.
