Analyze the reception of the Appel–Haken computer proof of the four-colour theorem through two rival accounts of what a proof is, and then say where each account breaks down.

The first account—call it the surveyability account—says that a proof is an argument a competent mathematician can in principle inspect step by step and thereby see why the theorem is true; an unsurveyable computation may confer high confidence, but it is more like an experiment than a proof. The second—call it the formal-derivation account—says that what certifies a proof is the existence of a finite formal object whose every step is mechanically checkable; human grasp of the whole is a contingent psychological limitation, not part of what proof is.

The facts of the episode, for reference: in 1976 Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved the four-colour theorem by reducing it to a large finite set of reducible configurations and using a computer to check them, producing a proof no single mathematician could follow by hand. Much of the mathematical community resisted the proof on the ground that it could not be surveyed, even though the disagreement was not about whether the theorem was true. Decades later the theorem received a fully machine-checked formal proof in the proof assistant Coq, and a similar trajectory occurred with Thomas Hales's computer-assisted proof of the Kepler conjecture, whose formal verification was completed later.

Your task is to run the analysis in both directions. First, use each account to make precise predictions about the episode: where should resistance have been strongest, what should have settled it, and what residual dissatisfaction should have remained? Then let the episode test the accounts: which features of the actual controversy does each account explain, which does it misdescribe, and where is each silent? In particular, address these questions. If the dissatisfaction was really about lost understanding rather than about probability of error, is that evidence for the surveyability account over the formal one? If a machine-checked formalization was widely treated as strengthening or completing the original proof rather than as a different theorem, does that support the formal-derivation account? And is it possible the two accounts are not rivals but partial descriptions of different stages of one practice—one governing how proofs are communicated and the other governing what finally certifies them? End by saying what the episode shows each view must concede, if anything.
