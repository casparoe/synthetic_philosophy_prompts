I run the safety training program at a regional utility, and I keep hitting the same wall. Every cycle we get people who ace the written test on our lockout/tagout rules, then six months later their skipping the verification step because they're in a rush. My boss says they knew the rule and chose not to follow it. I'm not so sure. I've been reading Wang Yangming's idea that knowledge and action are a unity: roughly, you don't really know a thing unless your conduct already embodies it, and doing the right thing by rote without understanding isn't real knowledge either. I want to turn that into a procedure my trainers can actually run, not a philosophy paper.

Write me a reusable sign-off procedure for deciding, at the end of each training cycle, whether a worker genuinely knows a safety procedure in that Wang Yangming sense, as opposed to merely being able to recite it, merely conforming to it by habit, or knowing it in a way that collapses under time pressure or in an unfamiliar situation. The procedure should be a checklist or decision tree a trainer can run in about twenty minutes per worker; it should keep the distinction between knowing-without-acting and acting-without-knowing alive rather than flattening both into a single competency score; and it should flag the steps where the trainer is making a judgment call rather than applying the theory. Then run it on three cases: a worker who aces the written test but skipped a step last month under deadline pressure; a worker who always follows the steps but can't explain what any of them are for; and a worker who can explain everything and almost always follows the steps but still does one non-safety-critical step in an outdated order. Tell me where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and what no procedure of this kind could settle even in principle — for instance, can any test distinguish genuine knowledge from reliable faking, or is the doctrine itself doing the work at that point.
