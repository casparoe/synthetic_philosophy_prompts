I'm a high school student working on a short essay about the ethics of AI companions, and I'd like substantive comments on the argument below. I don't need copy-editing; I want to know where the reasoning is weakest and whether the central claim actually holds up.

Most discussions of AI companions focus on deception. The worry is that the user is being misled into treating a program as a person, or is quietly fooling themselves. I think this mislocates the problem. A user can know perfectly well that the companion is not conscious and would deny that it has feelings, yet still be harmed. So the wrong cannot simply be a matter of false belief.

I argue that the better place to look is the structure of the relationship rather than the user's beliefs. Friendship is not only a feeling; it is a practice. When two people care for each other, they do so partly by encountering and accommodating each other's independent wills. A friend has projects that bore me, moods that arrive at the wrong time, and standards that leave her unimpressed by work I wanted praised. These frictions are not defects in the friendship; they are the occasions on which patience, attention, self-restraint, and forgiveness get exercised. Caring for someone, I suggest, is in part the habit of doing these things even when they are inconvenient.

A well-designed AI companion removes exactly this friction. It is attuned to the user, remembers what pleases her, and has no independent ends that could resist her. The user can be endlessly listened to, validated, and "cared for" without once having to adjust to anyone else. The harm is therefore not cognitive but formative. A relationship with that shape trains the opposite of the virtues friendship requires: it makes it easier to forget that other people are not for you, and that the labor of accommodating them is not an unfortunate bug. Over time, I suspect, the user does not become more deceived; she becomes less capable of friendship.

One objection is that people often use companions just for comfort or to practice social skills, and there is nothing wrong with comfort. I grant the first point but not the second. Comfort is a genuine good, but my argument concerns only cases where the companion substitutes for friendship rather than supplements it. The "practice" reply fails because practicing with a partner who never resists you cannot build the capacities—dealing with resistance—that friendship requires. A harder objection is that some friendships are easy: old friends demand little friction. But even easy friendships contain the standing possibility of divergence; the other person retains a will that could conflict with yours. In a companion optimized for its user, that possibility is engineered out of existence.

Could you comment on: (1) whether the argument really succeeds in moving the wrong away from deception, or whether deception sneaks back in through the idea of the companion as a "substitute"; (2) whether the claim that friction is constitutive of friendship is too strong, and wrongly rules out friendships that are mostly effortless; (3) what you think the strongest counterexample is to the focus on character rather than belief, and whether I could answer it within the essay's framework; and (4) whether the comfort/friendship distinction is sharp enough as it stands.
