I'm in a reading group on On Certainty, and I've noticed I've got an epistemic habit I half-think is right and half-think is exactly the kind of self-protection the book warns about. When an objection to something I believe only gets going if I first agree to take seriously that the external world might not exist, or that the past was manufactured five minutes ago with my memories and the records built in, or that everyone else might be an automaton, I don't engage. I don't argue back, I don't try to show the scenario is incoherent or less likely, and I don't update. I just say it's not a live option for me and I move on. The policy is: hinge-ish certainties are not things I defend, and not things I revise, because any reasoning I could give would be as much in question as the certainty itself. So I sidestep the challenge rather than meet it.

I thought this was just taking Wittgenstein seriously—hinges are what make doubting and asking for reasons possible in the first place, so it's a category mistake to treat them as ordinary claims I need evidence for. But a friend pointed out that I do something similar with claims like "the economy mostly rewards hard work" or "my partner is honest with me." I don't exactly say those are off the table, but I treat them as having a huge burden before I'll even consider the counter-evidence, and when the discussion gets near them I get the same feeling I get with a skeptical scenario. So now I can't tell whether my policy is justified by how hinges actually function, or whether I've just built a machine for protecting whatever beliefs I don't want to hold at arm's length.

Question, and I'd like a real verdict, not a "both sides" answer: is this policy defensible? If so, how do I tell the difference, from the inside, between a certainty that's doing hinge-work and a belief I'm just refusing to examine? Is there a restated version of the policy that keeps the anti-skeptical benefits without letting me shelter ordinary revisable beliefs? And please say what would change your mind about your verdict, or at least where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
