On Russell's theory of descriptions:

(1) "The present king of France is bald" is false, because France has no king; the analysis ∃x(Kx ∧ ∀y(Ky → y=x) ∧ Bx), with K for "is presently king of France" and B for "is bald," has no witness x.
(2) "The present king of France is not bald" is also false, for exactly the same reason: there is no unique present king of France to be non-bald.
(3) But "The present king of France is not bald" just is the negation of "The present king of France is bald" — the same sentence with "not" inserted in the obvious place.
(4) A proposition and its negation cannot both be false.
(5) So at least one of (1) and (2) must be wrong, and since both are just Russell's own verdicts, Russell's theory must be assigning the wrong truth value to one of those sentences.

I half-suspect the trouble is somewhere near (3), but I can't make that precise. Is the argument valid? If not, say which step fails. If (3) is the problem, give the two Russellian readings of "The present king of France is not bald" and say which one is the genuine negation of (1) and which one is what (2) needs. If instead the argument is valid, say which premise a Russellian should reject and what it costs.
