I serve on my lakeside community association's Rules and Conduct Panel, the body that reviews citations issued by the board. I am not a lawyer; I joined as a homeowner because I wanted to help keep the neighborhood fair, and until last month I assumed our job was to apply the lake rules exactly as written. A case before us now has unsettled that assumption, and on my own I have been reading about what is called the absurdity doctrine—the idea that a court may sometimes decline to follow a text's literal wording when doing so would produce a result so absurd, or so plainly at odds with the text's evident purpose, that the authors cannot reasonably be thought to have meant it. I don't know how far that idea reaches, especially for a private association's panel rather than a court.

The rule at issue provides that no person may set foot in the lake between sunset and sunrise, and that any violation carries a fine of at least one hundred dollars. At around 9:40 p.m. two weeks ago, a resident waded into the lake to pull out a nine-year-old child who had fallen from a dock and was struggling in the dark about thirty feet from shore. The child survived; the resident was in the water for about five minutes. The board, noting that the text allows no exceptions, has issued the standard citation and says the panel must uphold it. The board's worry is that any mercy here will be turned into an excuse for reckless night swimming later. Several neighbors argue, in response, that no one who wrote a safety rule about the lake could have meant it to punish the very act that saved a child's life, and that applying the rule here makes it absurd.

Please draft the short written opinion I can circulate before our Thursday evening meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action—whether to void the citation, uphold it, or uphold it with stated conditions or mitigation—and should include any follow-up steps and time frame you think we should adopt. The reasoning should be in terms a lay panel can follow. It should also take seriously the strongest argument on the losing side rather than dismissing it, and it should address whether a private association's rules panel is in a position to set aside a text's plain wording in the way I have read that courts sometimes do.
