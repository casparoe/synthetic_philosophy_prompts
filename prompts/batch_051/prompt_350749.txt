Someone in my reading group made an argument from the start of Plutarch's Life of Pericles that I can't shake. Plutarch says (Perrin translation, ch. 1): "Such objects are to be found in virtuous deeds; these implant in those who search them out a great and zealous eagerness which leads to imitation." Then, right after, he adds that when we admire artifacts—perfumes, dyes, a lovely performance—we often delight in the work without any impulse to become like the maker.

Her argument goes:

(1) On Plutarch's picture, the point of reading a life is that it displays virtuous deeds and thereby gives you an impulse to imitate them.
(2) From the page alone you can't tell whether the deeds Plutarch displays actually happened as told, or were cleaned up to display the virtue more clearly.
(3) What you can't tell from the page alone can't make a difference to the life's effect on you.
(4) So whether the deeds in a life are true makes no difference to its moral effect.
(5) So if moral effect is what lives are for, there's no reason to prefer the accurate account over the hagiographic one—often we should prefer the hagiography.
(6) That can't be right. If the subject's virtue is invented, we aren't learning from another person's life at all; we're just reading a story with a good example in it.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) more than I'd like to keep (3), but I want to know what I'd be paying.
