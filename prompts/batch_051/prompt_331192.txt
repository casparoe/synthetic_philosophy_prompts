I have been trying to pin down my beliefs about when one person may be made to suffer for the benefit of others, and I have a tentative principle that I keep failing to reconcile with my own verdicts. The principle is that such an act is permissible exactly when the well-being gained by the beneficiaries outweighs the harm inflicted, where harms and benefits are measured on a single commensurable scale. Test it against the following cases.

A researcher gives one volunteer a brief but maximally intense electric shock so that five other volunteers will not receive that same shock. I think this is permissible.

The same researcher gives one volunteer that same shock, not to prevent harm, but so that a very large number of other people each receive a few seconds of a mild pleasant taste. I think this is impermissible, and my verdict does not change as the number of beneficiaries grows, even into the millions.

The researcher gives one volunteer a mildly bitter taste for a few seconds so that one thousand other people each get an afternoon of moderate contentment. I think this is permissible.

The researcher gives one volunteer a few seconds of bitter taste so that fifty thousand other people each get a single pleasant taste. I think this is permissible, though with less confidence, and I cannot explain why my confidence drops here but collapses entirely in the shock case.

The researcher gives one volunteer an hour of excruciating agony so that ten million other people each avoid a mild one-hour headache. Here I am torn: aggregation tells me this is clearly permissible, but something in me refuses to accept it, and I do not know which judgment to revise.

Is there a principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that best captures the first four verdicts and speaks to the fifth? Please state the candidates precisely, apply them to further cases of your choosing, especially cases involving intermediate pains and pleasures, and say plainly which of my verdicts, if any, a coherent principle forces me to abandon.
