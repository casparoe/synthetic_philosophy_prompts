I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been chewing on an asymmetry in how even careful people talk about square roots. A geometer told me after an interview that he doesn't actually think the positive root of 9 is the real answer; he just has to speak as if it is, so the square root comes out as a single number rather than a pair. That stuck with me. We teach √9 = 3 and treat −3 as the almost-answer, the one that has to hide behind a ± sign. Positivity seems to pick out one root so naturally that nobody stops to justify the choice. But the moment the roots go complex, the preference disappears: the two roots of −1, wich we name i and −i, are treated as perfect mirrors. Swap the labels and no algebraic fact changes; every true statement about one is true of the other, and there is no arithmetic test that tells you which one really deserves the plain name i.

So here's my question. Is the real-root case actually different in a way that justifies the asymmetry, or did we just confuse the reals having an order with there being a fact about which root is priviledged? I'd like the strongest case you can make for treating the positive root as canonical—something deeper than the fact that choosing it is what makes the square root a function—and then an honest verdict on whether that case survives when you look at an equation like x² = 2, where the two roots sit symmetrically on either side of zero and the only thing picking one out is that it happens to be greater than the other.
