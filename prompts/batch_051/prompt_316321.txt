I've been trying to finally make my reflective equilibrium less of a vibe, and I realized my policy about when to trust a clean principle over a specific intuition is apparently inconsistent. I wrote down my actual gut calls on cases where a tidy general principle and a concrete case-intuition point opposite ways.

In physics, I accept theories that make novel, risky predictions that come out right, even when they overturn ordinary intuition about time itself—clocks ticking at different rates depending on motion. The theory wins, not the intuition. But in metaphysics, if a theory says tables and chairs don't really exist and there are only microscopic particles arranged table-wise, I refuse to accept it no matter how much simpler it is. The intuition wins. I also think "better than" has to be transitive: if I'm led down a chain of small pairwise tradeoffs that each feel fine but end at something I find clearly not better than the start, I drop one of the small-pair intuitions rather than giving up transitivity. The principle wins. On knowledge, the three-part "justified true belief" story is about as simple as an analysis gets, but the luck-style cases where justification and truth merely coincide are enough to make me abandon it. The case wins. From set theory, there is a standard, very useful axiom about infinite sets that entails you can cut a ball into finitely many pieces and reassemble them into two balls; my spatial intuition says that's absurd, but I accept the axiom anyway. The principle wins. In ethics, I reject act-utilitarianism because of the hospital case where you can carve up one healthy visitor to save five dying patients. The case wins. Yet in public health I happily go along with aggregative cost-effectiveness reasoning that says fund the cheap intervention that saves thousands of anonymous kids over the dramatic rescue of one named kid, despite the named kid pulling at me much harder. The principle wins.

The pairs that bother me most are the physics-vs-tables one and the public-health-vs-transplant one: in some cases I let a clean principle or an aggregation steamroll a vivid intuition, and in others I let a vivid intuition steamroll a clean principle, and I can't state the diffrence. Is there one principle about when case-intuitions should outrank theoretical virtues like simplicity, generality, and transitivity that captures most or all of these calls? Please state it precisely, say what it predicts about a few conflicts I haven't listed—maybe the Newcomb case where a reliable predictor has already filled the boxes, and the intuition that my dog is concious versus a tidy theory that says only language-users have experiences—and tell me which of my listed calls it forces me to drop. My best current guess is something about only trusting intuitions formed in environments with reliable feedback, but that doesn't obviously separate tables from time dilation, so I'm not optimistic it survives.
