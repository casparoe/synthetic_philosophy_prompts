I'm running a legal philosophy seminar on naked statistical evidence and I want to set a problem that goes past the standard treatments. Take the classic blue bus case, not the gatecrasher variant: a pedestrian is injured by a bus, she can only say it was blue, the defendant company runs 80% of the blue buses on that route, and on the balance of probabilities that seems to make the case, though courts are notoriously reluctant to accept the bare statistic alone. Now change one thing: the case is brought as a private prosecution for dangerous driving, so the standard is beyond reasonable doubt rather than the civil standard. The only extra evidence is a CCTV still showing a blue bus of the defendant's model at the scene around the relevant time, with the number plate unreadable. Walk me through how the main readings of the original puzzle—the 'no bare statistics' view, the orthodox Bayesian view, and the reference-class worry—handle the criminal standard. I especially want to know whether the CCTV still counts as individualised evidence or is just another statistical fact, whether the line for beyond reasonable doubt is a principled one or merely a higher tolerance for bare statistics, and at what market share, if any, each view would say the criminal standard is met.
