I am attracted to an impartialist picture of moral thought: the right action is whatever an agent-neutral standard recommends, whether that standard is maximizing the good or being justifiable to others on terms nobody could reasonably reject. What draws me is the architecture. Every situation becomes a decision problem; partiality toward one's children, friends, or spouse must be licensed by the standard rather than presupposed; and any action can in principle be reconstructed and defended from a standpoint outside the agent. But I have a recurring intuition that refuses to sit with this. Imagine a man whose wife and a stranger are both in danger of drowning and who can save only one. It seems to me not merely permissible but obviously right that he saves his wife. And yet I also think that if he pauses — not in panic, not to work out the logistics, but to consult the impartial standard and confirm that saving her is justified before he lets himself act — something has gone badly wrong with him, and the wrongness is not just that he wasted time. It is a defect of love or character, the intrusion of a thought that should not be there. This is the nerve of the one thought too many objection. The impartialist has a standard reply: the theory tells him to save his wife, and the extra thought it attributes to him — that doing so is justified — is true, so how can a true thought be a defect? But I cannot shake the sense that this move answers a different objection than the one I am making, and that the external, justificatory stance is itself the problem.

I am not asking which side wins. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would have to be true for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a version on which both survive without the agreement being merely verbal. Give me the strongest debunking of the intuition: for example, is it conflating the criterion of rightness with a decision procedure, so that the theory needs only to say what makes an action right and never asks the husband to perform the calculation? Or is the intuition really tracking an objection to alienation, demanding that an adequate moral theory build in a first-personal, non-instrumental place for personal attachments even at the level of the criterion itself? And if they are compatible, be precise about what the compatibility view must surrender: where does the impartial standard fall silent, and why does its silence at that point not amount to smuggling the intuition back in under another name?
