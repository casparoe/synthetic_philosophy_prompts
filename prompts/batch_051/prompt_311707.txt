I retired from a research career in the natural sciences a few years ago and have lately been reading philosophy of science and astrobiology, and I keep returning to how stuck the Great Filter debate seems. One side maintains that the improbable transition is behind us—the origin of life, the rise of complex multicellular organisms, or the evolution of tool-using intelligence—so that simple life is probably common but technological life extraordinarily rare. The other side maintains that the improbable transition is ahead of us, on the grounds that the same capacities that make a civilization detectable also enable it to destroy itself. Both camps treat the same null observation, the absence of technosignatures, as evidence for their view. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is less about any particular transition probability than about what a single datum—our own existence, with no second civilization to compare—can license: whether our existence is something the filter must explain or a selection effect that makes the filter's location unreadable from the inside. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse rather than a verdict for either side. Is it an ordinary empirical dispute awaiting a decisive observation, a dispute about statistical methodology or observer-selection reasoning, or a dispute about how priors may be assigned over events never observed? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress, or is progress not to be expected and why?
