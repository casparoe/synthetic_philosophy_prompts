My philosophy teacher has a reputation for being almost mechanically consistent about honesty, and I have kept a list of her verdicts on deception cases.

- Bluffing in poker: she says it is completely fine, just part of the game.
- A used-car salesman who tells a customer that the car has never failed an inspection, when that is true only because the dealership fixes every fault right before each inspection: she says it is exactly as wrong as plainly lying about the car's condition.
- Researchers who report only the time window in which a drug looked effective and hide the windows in which it was no better than a placebo: she says they are no less guilty of fabricating data than if they had invented results outright.
- A public official who answers a direct question about a bribe with a technically true statement designed to make voters believe nothing happened: she says it is lying in every way that counts.

New case: a classmate is selling his gaming laptop to a friend of ours. She asks him directly whether it has any problems. He says, truthfully, that it has handled everything he has used it for without trouble, but he does not mention that it crashes during long gaming sessions, and he knows she is buying it mostly for long gaming sessions.

I can see more than one principle that would make all four of her past verdicts consistent, and I suspect the principles do not agree about this case. I do not want a survey of possibilities, though. Give me your committed prediction: based on her record, would she say the laptop case is as wrong as lying, less wrong, or not wrong? Which of her earlier rulings is the closest analogy, and does it actually decide the matter? State the principle you would be attributing to her under each plausible answer, defend the one you think is right, and describe one concrete further case that would cleanly separate the competing principles.

Structure the answer in these titled sections, in this order, and keep to the lengths:

- The predicted verdict (3–4 sentences): what she would say about the laptop case, stated as a definite claim.
- The principles that fit her record (two or three short paragraphs, 3–5 sentences each): name each principle, show how it handles the four past cases, and say what it predicts for the laptop case.
- Why this is the best reading of her (one paragraph, 5–7 sentences): the strongest reason for preferring your predicted principle and the main cost you are accepting.
- The separating case (3–5 sentences): a concrete scenario that would force the competing principles apart, and how she would rule under each.
