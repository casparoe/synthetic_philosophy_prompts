I've been kicking around a view about death and I want someone to tell me if it's actually adding anything. Epicurus's argument (roughly: when death is present you arent, and when you are present death isn't, so it can't be bad for you) seems to me to depend entierly on the assumption that a harm has to be located at a moment inside a life. My move: there's another kind of harm, which I've been calling 'horizon harm,' that isn't located anywhere in time. A life has a horizon—the set of futures still open from where you are—and death is bad not because you miss any particular future experience but because it slams that horizon shut. Imagine a novel with its last three chapters torn out: the book is worse, but no page that remains is worse. I think being dead works like that. This also seems to fix the old symmetry problem (why we don't grieve the eternity before we were born): before we existed there was no horizon to close, whereas a life in progress has one. I think this kills the Epicurean argument outright, not just answers it. But I keep worrying I've just reinvented the deprivation account with fancier words, and my view seems to say a life can be made worse without anyone ever feeling it. Is that a bug or a feature? What is this closest to, does the horizon do any work beyond deprivation, and what's the worst implication of the view I'm probably not seeing?
