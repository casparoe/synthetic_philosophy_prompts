The exam question was as follows:

In the Groundwork, Kant contrasts the prudent shopkeeper, who gives correct change because honesty is good for business, with someone who performs the same outward action from duty. Explain the distinction between acting in accordance with duty and acting from duty, and then critically assess the claim that an action has moral worth only if it is done from duty. In your assessment, consider the case of an agent who does the right thing for reasons that intuitively make the action right, but who does not act from duty.

Here is the student's answer:

Kant's shopkeeper gives correct change to an inexperienced customer even though he could get away with shortchanging him; he does this not because it is right but because cheating would damage his reputation and hurt his business. Kant says this shopkeeper acts merely in accordance with duty: his action happens to match what duty requires, but the determining ground is self-interest. Acting from duty, by contrast, means doing the action because it is required by the moral law, so the shopkeeper's action has legality but no moral worth.

For Kant, an action has moral worth only when it is done from duty, and any admixture of inclination contaminates that worth. That is why the sympathetic person who helps others from natural compassion can never have moral worth: if you take pleasure in helping, the action is tainted, and only someone who helps reluctantly, from duty alone, receives full credit. The worth of the action comes from its maxim rather than from its effects, so the same outward action can be worthy when one person performs it and worthless when another does.

The claim that moral worth requires acting from duty faces counterexamples. Consider the agent who pulls a drowning child from a river out of immediate concern for the child's life, without stopping to consider duty at all. This agent acts for the reasons that make the action right, but not from duty, and intuitively the rescue has moral worth. The case of Huck Finn is even stronger: he helps Jim escape slavery out of friendship, while believing he is doing something wrong by not turning Jim in. Huck shows that an agent can have moral worth even when he acts against his own moral beliefs. What these cases show is that acting from duty is not necessary for moral worth. The real criterion is having a motive that reliably produces right action, and any such motive confers moral worth no less than duty would.
