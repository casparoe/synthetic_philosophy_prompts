Okay so in my law class we've been doing statutory interpretation and the absurdity doctrine (I think it's basically the golden rule over here) is bugging me. I put together an argument that seems to show the whole doctrine is question-begging, and I can't find the mistake, but the conclusion feels way too strong. Here it is:

(1) Courts are supposed to apply what a statute plainly says.
(2) The absurdity doctrine lets a court set the plain meaning aside when following it would make an absurd result, like when a drafting slip turns "30 days" into "30 years".
(3) To know a result is absurd and not just surprising or bad policy, the court has to know the legislature could not have meant what the text seems to say.
(4) But the text is basically the only evidence we have of what the legislature meant, because a legislature as a body doesn't have its own mental state and the debates are messy, patchy and contradictory.
(5) So the court is setting aside the only evidence of legislative intent in the name of following legislative intent.
(6) That's question-begging, which means the doctrine either patches the text with something outside it (i.e. the judge's own preferences) or only gets used when the text is already clear enough not to need patching.
(7) So we should drop the absurdity doctrine and follow the text no matter how silly the result.

Something around (3) or (5) feels off but I cant pin it down. I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than end up at (7), honestly. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Also could you answer in British English please.
