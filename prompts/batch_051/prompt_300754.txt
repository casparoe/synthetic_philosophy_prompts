I've been chewing on an asymmetry in how we talk about scientific revolutions, and I can't figure out whether it's defensible. When we look back, we call the person who abandoned the old paradigm and turned out right a "visionary." We call the person who did the same kind of thing—broke with consensus, pushed a wierd new framework, flouted the field's usual standards—and turned out wrong a "crank." But from the inside, at the time, those two looked nearly identical. Same confidence, same evidential gaps, same willingness to tear up commitments everyone else shared. The only real difference is how it came out.

So: is that asymmetry in our judgments justified, or is it just hindsight wearing an epistemology costume? If the successful revolutionary was rational before the revolution succeeded, then the unsuccessful one was rational too, and "crank" would be a verdict on luck rather than method. But if the unsuccessful one wasn't rational, then neither was the successful one at the time—which is basically the worry that science can't be rational during a revolution. I'm aware this is Kuhn-Popper territory, but I don't want a book report on them. Maybe there's a third option: some difference between the two that was there at the time and only got recognized later—like the successful framework explained the anomalies instead of just redescribing them. But then I'd want to know whether that difference can be said without quietly using the outcome as evidence.

I'd like you to commit to an answer: should we keep the visionary/crank asymmetry, reverse it, or drop it? And tell me how confident you are in that verdict and where you're least sure. If you think the asymmetry dissolves once the cases are described carefully, or that I've set up a false choice, say that plainly instead of going along. Use Popper or Kuhn only insofar as they help you answer, not as names to drop.
