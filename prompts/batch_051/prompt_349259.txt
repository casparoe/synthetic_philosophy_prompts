Ok so I'm trying to finally write up my view on the whole separate-the-art-from-the-artist debate, mostly because I keep having the same argument with my roommate (a film person) and I realize I can't state all my commitments at once without tripping over my self. I'm not a profesional philosopher, so some of these are probably stated sloppily, but here is the package I find myself wanting to defend:

(a) The aesthetic value of an artwork—what makes it good as art—sits in the work itself: its form, its content, how it's built, what it makes vivid or perceptible. How it was produced and what the artist did in their private life is a different kind of question, so learning that an artist was a monster shouldn't force me to revise my judgment of the work.
(b) I still think it can be right to refuse to buy, stream, assign, exhibit, or platform the work of someone awful, and maybe even to pressure others not to. But I see that as being about what we do with the art, not about how good the art is.
(c) At the same time, I don't want to be a full autonomist, because I think some works are morally defective from the inside—a novel that takes up a smugly cruel perspective, a propaganda film that lies about its subject—and that defect can be an aesthetic defect too, since it amounts to a failure of attention or imaginative honesty, not just a separate moral blemish bolted onto an otherwise good work.
(d) I also think you sometimes can't even see what a work is doing without knowing what the artist was trying to do and what the intended audience would have picked up, so biographical and historical context is sometimes required for proper appreciation.
(e) And I want a kind of evenhandedness: I shouldn't give a work bonus points because the artist was a saint, and I shouldn't subtract points because the artist was a monster. For evaluation, only what is in the work matters.

This feels like it all hangs together, but my roommate insists it doesn't, and I suspect I'm failng to see where the load-bearing cracks are. Could you audit the set rather than lecture me on the topic? Specifically: which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only look like they're in tension and dissolve once you draw a distinction, and what would be the least costly revision? Please rank the real tensions by severity instead of saying everything conflicts with everything.
