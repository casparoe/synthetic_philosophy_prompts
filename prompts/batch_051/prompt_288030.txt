A friend and I spent last evening going over whether anyone ever gets non-circular reasons to trust their senses, and he produced the following chain of reasoning. Neither of us could find the exit, though I am fairly sure we were tramping around inside what the literature calls the bootstrapping problem and the easy-knowledge problem. I do not want a survey of moves; I want you to commit to the step you reject and defend that choice. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, without preamble or restating the question.

(1) I know an ordinary fact on the basis of color perception, such as that the wall before me is red.

(2) I also know something apparently weaker that does not itself presuppose that my color vision is accurate: that the wall looks red to me. Even a hallucinating subject can know how things look.

(3) From the fact that the wall looks red to me and the fact that the wall is red, I can deduce — and thereby come to know — that on this occasion the look matched the color; this was not a case in which my color perception got things wrong.

(4) Repeating the procedure across many occasions would give me a long list of cases in which the look matched the color: an empirical track record compiled with no instruments and no second observers.

(5) From such a track record I could infer by ordinary induction that my color perception is reliable.

(6) The procedure is entirely general. A palm reader can know that she had a strong impression that a client would soon suffer a loss, know by later observation that the client did suffer the loss, and conclude that the impression matched the world on that occasion. After enough such cases she could infer by the same move that palmistry is reliable. An astrologer could do likewise for the casting of charts.

(7) But we are not willing to accept that palmistry or astrology can be certified as reliable by this kind of private ledger-keeping. If anything deserves the name vicious bootstrapping, this does.

(8) Therefore either the move in (5) is illegitimate even when it begins from ordinary perceptual knowledge — in which case I seem to lose any empirical route to trusting my senses — or there is an asymmetry between perception and palmistry that blocks the generalization in (6), and it is not obvious what it is. Stopping at (8) leaves us with either skepticism about the senses or special pleading.

Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (7), and I cannot see anything wrong with the deduction in (3); what I cannot do is state a principled rule that disallows the bootstrapping in (6) without also disallowing the induction in (5). So do not tell me the options — tell me which one is right and why.
