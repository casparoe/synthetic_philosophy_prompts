Below is a draft essay on Woodward's interventionist account of causation. I would like your comments in four parts, as specified after the essay.

The defining clauses of an intervention deploy exactly the directed causal notions—cause, route through, influence—that the analysis is meant to illuminate. Woodward's response is that the circularity is not vicious, because the project is not reductive: causation, intervention, invariance, and modularity are characterized together, and the package earns its keep by making sense of controlled experiments and of the invariant generalizations they support.

This essay grants that the circularity is not a defect in a reductive definition, because no reductive definition is being offered. But it argues that the circularity is nonetheless damaging at one specific point: the point where the account is supposed to deliver causal direction. To determine whether X causes Y, an inquirer must determine whether some candidate process I is an intervention on X with respect to Y. That requires knowing that I does not influence Y along a route that bypasses X. Yet knowing which routes bypass X and which run through X is close to knowing whether X causes Y. A finite inquirer without prior directed causal knowledge therefore cannot use the interventionist criterion to obtain such knowledge; the criterion presupposes the very information about direction it is meant to make intelligible. Invariance under interventions cannot repair this, because invariance is assessed relative to interventions on a designated variable: one must already know that the manipulation is an intervention on X rather than on Y before asking whether the X–Y relation is invariant under it.

A natural reply is that Woodward never intended a discovery procedure. His account is a conceptual or semantic one, not an epistemology of causal learning. That is fair, but it relocates rather than removes the worry. The worry was always partly epistemic: interventionism seemed to illuminate causation only by appealing to manipulations that are themselves causal, and thus seemed to leave unilluminated how we ever identify manipulations as interventions. In practice, I suspect, we identify them by leaning on lower-level mechanistic and spatiotemporal knowledge—for example, knowledge about momentum transfer, contact, and levers. But if the direction that interventionism needs is fixed by a prior mechanistic conception of production, then interventionism is not a free-standing account of causation. It is, for the feature that matters most, parasitic on the very family of ideas it was meant to discipline.

Please comment on this draft as follows.

1. Exegetical accuracy. Point out any place where the essay misstates Woodward's intervention conditions or his reply to the circularity worry. If the essay attributes to Woodward anything stronger than warranted, say what the weaker, more accurate statement would be.

2. The central argument. Assess the claim that the criterion presupposes causal direction. Could a Woodwardian break into the circle via time order, the asymmetry of certain invariances, or features of experimental design? If so, identify the best such entry point and test it against the essay's argument.

3. Strongest counterreply. Write the strongest paragraph a defender of interventionism could offer in response to the essay, then say whether it defeats the essay's conclusion or merely limits its scope.

4. Revision notes. Choose the weakest paragraph or premise, explain why it is weakest, and propose a concrete revision that would make the essay harder to answer.
