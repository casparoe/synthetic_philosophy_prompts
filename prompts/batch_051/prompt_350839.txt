Two friends of mine recently argued about whether voting in a large election is rational. Dev said it plainly isn't: in an electorate big enough to matter, the probability that any single ballot breaks a tie is so small that the expected value of voting — the chance of being decisive times the difference the outcome makes, even generously multiplied across everyone affected — is worth less than the time and effort of getting to the polls. Mara replied that this calculation counts only the causal channel through which a vote changes the outcome. If you adopt an evidential approach to decision-making, on which an act's value includes what choosing it is evidence about, then your decision to vote is evidence that a large number of like-minded voters, who share your preferences and respond to the same information, are deciding to vote as well. The expected value of that correlated bloc can easily exceed the cost, so voting can be rational even when you have effectively no chance of casting the decisive ballot. Dev called that double-counting: other voters' decisions are not caused by your choice, and to count their expected turnout as part of the value of your act is to treat something your act merely indicates as if it were something your act produces. Mara stood her ground. The same evidential structure, she said, is accepted in ordinary cases: choosing to skip today's workout is evidence that you are the kind of person who will skip many future workouts, and that gives you a reason to train today even though today's session does not cause the later ones. The evidential defense of voting is therefore a consistent application of an established decision theory rather than an ad hoc fix, and in a close election the correlation between your own decision and like-minded others may be substantial enough to do genuine work.
