I'm putting together the final for my upper-level early modern rationalism seminar, and I'm a bit sick of essays that just rehearse the Voltaire caricature of Leibniz on optimism. I'd like an exam question that forces students to actually reconstruct what Leibniz is doing in the Monadology around sections 53–55, where he gives that compressed story about an infinity of possible worlds in God's understanding, each with a claim to existence in proportion to its perfection, and God actualizing the one with the greatest perfection. The question should make them separate the metaphysical claim from the moral theodicy claim: is the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds just a consequence of the principle of sufficient reason plus divine choice, or does it secretly rely on an independent premise about God's goodness? I also want the question to push them to say which premise is weakest, and whether someone could accept the PSR and accept that God exists while still denying that this world is the best possible one.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. In the marking scheme, also name the specific mistake students most often make, the kind that should cost marks. And I'd like the marking scheme to include a note on what would change your mind about the reconstruction you give in the model answer — which argument or consideration would lead you to say the argument is doing something different than you thought, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I don't want a wishy-washy "there are many readings" note; I want a specific claim about the weakest link.
