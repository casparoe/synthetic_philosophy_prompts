I've been rereading Le Ton beau de Marot—Hofstadter's book about translating a tiny, tightly rhymed Renaissance poem—and it's made me realize I have no crisp idea of what makes a translation faithful. The book keeps treating the preservation of the poem's formal constraints (the meter, the rhyme scheme, even the pattern of feminine rhymes) as part of fidelity, not as an optional flourish. But I don't know how to turn that into a definition that doesn't just reduce to 'preserve the form.'

The hard cases I keep testing intuitions on are these: (a) a prose paraphrase of a poem that gets the images and ideas right but gives up rhythm, rhyme, and lineation; (b) a translation of a sestina that keeps the six recurring end-words and their rotation pattern even though it has to pad lines and fudge local details to make that work; (c) a comedy translation where every pun is replaced by a different pun that lands at the same moment, so the jokes work but aren't literal; (d) a homophonic translation that keeps roughly the sounds of the original but not the sense; (e) a translation of a lipogrammatic novel (a novel written without the letter e) that drops the lipogram entirely and just renders it naturally and accurately sentence-by-sentence; (f) a free-verse adaptation that captures the author's voice and mood but preserves neither the form nor the close imagery.

I'm inclined to say that (b) and (c) are faithful, that (a) and (f) are translations but not faithful translations of the poem, that (d) isn't a translation at all, and I keep flip-flopping on (e). Give me a working definition of translation-fidelity that gets my confident verdicts right, run it through all six cases, and tell me where it suprises you or forces you to revise the definition.
