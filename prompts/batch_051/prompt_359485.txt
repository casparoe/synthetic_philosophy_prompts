Effective altruism in its contemporary form—cause-neutral, quantitative, and skeptical of any deep moral divide between a neighbor and a stranger ten thousand miles away—is sometimes discussed as the natural modern heir to Mozi's impartial care (jian'ai), with the Confucian defense of graded love cast as its ancient foil. But the two may not line up that cleanly. Imagine Mozi could examine the now-familiar maxim that one ought to give where each additional dollar does the most expected good, even when that means passing over visible, emotionally proximate suffering at home in favor of less visible but cheaper suffering abroad. On a careful reconstruction of his actual commitments, would he have recognized this as jian'ai finally put into practice, or would he have judged it a distortion of impartial care—perhaps even a covert form of the very partiality it prides itself on avoiding? Answer as a reasoned estimate, not as a documented claim.
