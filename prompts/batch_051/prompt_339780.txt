I have been reading some popular philosophy of science, and a friend who studied the area summarized the problem of verisimilitude for me as follows:

"Popper wanted to explain how science can make progress even though, on his view, no scientific theory is ever confirmed or known to be true. He proposed that false theories can still be ranked by their verisimilitude, or closeness to the truth: one theory is more verisimilar than another when it implies more true consequences and fewer false ones. This was meant to let us say that Einstein's physics is a better approximation to reality than Newton's, even though both are strictly false. But critics showed in the 1970s that the definition collapses. Any false theory implies infinitely many true consequences and infinitely many false ones, so once you compare theories by their complete consequence sets, every false theory turns out to be equally close to the truth. The concept of verisimilitude was therefore shown to be incoherent, and most philosophers have since abandoned the idea that science gets closer to the truth."

I suspect this summary is not quite fair, but I do not know enough to say where the distortion enters or how much it matters. Could you audit it for me? In particular: which parts would a careful defender of the verisimilitude program accept as accurate? Which parts are subtly misleading, and why? What would someone who still works on truthlikeness say in response to the 1970s result? And does the final conclusion — that the concept is incoherent and that philosophers have abandoned the idea of science approaching the truth — survive once the misleading parts are corrected?
