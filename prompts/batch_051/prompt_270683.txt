I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and while reporting a piece on the recent wave of gratitude research and its popular offshoots, I've found myself relying on a distinction that I'd like checked before I put it in front of readers.

The distinction is between what I've been calling addressed gratitude and unaddressed gratitude. Addressed gratitude is the familiar interpersonal case: someone deliberately incurs some cost or effort to benefit me, and I owe that person acknowledgment, recognition, and some fitting response. Unaddressed gratitude is what people are being trained into when they keep a gratitude journal and list the fact that they were born in a stable country, that an illness was caught early by chance, that strangers a century ago built institutions they now inherit. There is no benefactor on the other end of that gratitude.

I want to claim these are not the same attitude with different targets, or the same attitude with a missing target. They answer to different norms. Addressed gratitude binds me to a particular agent: it matters that I express it to the right person, that I don't treat the benefit as luck, that I accept the standing debt rather than merely enjoying the good. Unaddressed gratitude binds me to no one in particular. What it regulates is my orientation to fortune itself: a refusal to treat good luck as a baseline or as something owed, and an attention to how much of my position is unearned.

If that is right, then a lot of the philosophical discussion that treats interpersonal gratitude as the central case is importing norms into contexts where they do not apply, and the popular gratitude-practice literature is doing something stranger: it cultivates unaddressed gratitude while letting people borrow the felt moral structure of addressed gratitude, as though someone were on the other end after all. And I think this confusion has political uses. Being told to "be grateful you have a job" or "be grateful for the healthcare you've got" works by borrowing the docility of addressed gratitude — you owe someone, so stay put — while keeping the benefactor unnamed, so no one can ask what is owed and by whom.

But I can't tell whether I've carved nature at the joints or just relabeled "appreciation" and added a moralizing gloss. Is unaddressed gratitude a real kind of gratitude, or is it appreciation plus a projected or fictional addressee, in which case gratitude-journal gratitude is either derivative metaphor or outright confusion? Is there already a name for this distinction in the philosophical or psychological literature? And is the claim that the two kinds carry different norms actually defensible, or would a single account — roughly, gratitude is benefit-recognition plus whatever stance is due to the source — handle both cases without splitting the phenomenon? Be candid about where this breaks; I can accept the political point being wrong, but I would rather not build a feature around a category error.
