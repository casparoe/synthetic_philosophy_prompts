I'm a retired ecologist—spent most of my career running common-garden experiments on montane plants—and I've only recently started reading philosophy in any serious way. In chapter 1 of Mill's *The Subjection of Women* I hit this passage, which stopped me cold:

“But it would be a mistake to suppose that the difficulty of the case must lie in the insufficiency or obscurity of the grounds of reason on which my conviction rests. The difficulty is that which exists in all cases in which there is a mass of feeling to be contended against. So long as an opinion is strongly rooted in the feelings, it gains rather than loses in stability by having a preponderating weight of argument against it.”

What struck me is how much this sounds like an inferential problem I know from my own work. If you observe a plant only in its native field, you cannot tell whether its height is due to its genotype or to the altitude it grew up at. You need a common garden: grow highland and lowland seeds side by side under one treatment. Mill seems to be doing something similar with the appeal to women's "nature." The existing social arrangements are the treatment, the observed differences in conduct or character are the phenotype, and you can't infer the underlying "natural tendencies" from a single treatment that is pervasive, historical, and protected by feeling.

But I also see a disanalogy. In ecology, the treatment can be changed, replicated, and randomized. For Mill, the treatment—the legal subordination of one sex—is nearly universal and deeply entrenched, and, as the passage says, it is defended by feelings that actually harden when the arguments against them get better. That part has no clean ecological analogue, unless maybe you compare it to a researcher who cannot imagine an environment she hasn't already observed.

So here's what I want: lay out how far the common-garden analogy actually maps onto Mill's argument against inferring women's nature from their condition under subordination. Where does the mapping break? Does the genotype–environment distinction quietly do work that Mill's own notions of "nature" and "custom" cannot bear? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does Mill's underlying conclusion—that no one is in a position to know the natural tendencies of the sexes while the present system is all we have observed—still go through, especially if you update the empirical background with modern behavioral genetics and cross-cultural data? I'm less interested in whether Mill's Victorian facts were right than in whether the argumentative strategy itself is any good once you treat it as a problem of experimental inference.
