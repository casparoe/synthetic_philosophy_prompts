Consider two cases. A student of modest aptitude spends the term working methodically and barely earns a B, while a gifted classmate attends erratically and earns an A without visible strain. Many competent evaluators find the first performance more admirable than the second, and they are prepared to say that the effort itself adds something to the value of what was achieved. Yet the same people often reverse the judgment elsewhere. A diagnostician who reaches the correct diagnosis only after a week of anguished missteps is not a better diagnostician than one who sees it in a minute; a firm that produces the same good with twice the labor is not praised for its struggle but told to become efficient. In these contexts, extra effort is treated as waste or defect rather than as a contribution.

The asymmetry is therefore this: in some cases effort is taken to confer value on an outcome or on the person who produced it, while in otherwise similar cases the same kind of effort is taken to diminish the performance. The question is whether anything justifies this asymmetry, or whether ordinary evaluative practice is simply inconsistent.

Your task is to explain and assess the asymmetry. Test at least the following candidate justifications: that in the flattering cases we are evaluating the person rather than the outcome, while in the unflattering cases we are evaluating the outcome rather than the person; that effort contributes to value only where the difficulty or process is partly constitutive of the achievement, as in climbing, racing, or mastering a craft, rather than where the end is specified independently of how it is reached; and that we never value effort for its own sake but only as evidence of something else, such as commitment, care, or strength of will. Stress-test whichever account you defend against the hard cases: effort devoted to a wicked end; the person who struggles ceaselessly and never succeeds; the skilled agent who deliberately chooses a harder path with no better result; and domains in which effortless speed is itself the mark of mastery. Say, at the end, whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.

Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: identify the argument, consideration, or case that, if it withstood examination, would most likely lead you to a different verdict, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
