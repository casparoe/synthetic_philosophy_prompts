I have been working on an argument about what, if anything, the creation of money adds to an economy, and I now suspect it proves too much, since its conclusion would make the whole apparatus of liquidity provision a matter of bookkeeping. The argument takes as its point of departure a passage from Adam Smith, An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776), Book II, Chapter IV:

"By means of the loan, the lender, as it were, assigns to the borrower his right to a certain portion of the annual produce of the land and labour of the country to be employed as the borrower pleases."

"Even in the monied interest, however, the money is, as it were, but the deed of assignment, which conveys from one hand to another those capitals which the owners do not care to employ themselves."

Smith is writing about loans at interest; I have generalized the passage into the following chain of reasoning.

(1) A country's wealth consists in the real annual produce of its land and labour, together with the stock of capital and skill engaged in producing it — not in the instruments that confer command over that produce.
(2) Money, whether coin, paper notes, or bank deposits, is not part of that produce; it is a deed of assignment, an instrument for circulating claims on real goods.
(3) Creating additional such instruments without a corresponding addition to real produce can only multiply claims against an unchanged stock of goods.
(4) Therefore printing money, or expanding credit, may change prices, redistribute consumption, and shift who holds which claims — but it cannot itself create wealth.
(5) Liquidity is just the ease of acquiring such instruments, or near-substitutes for them, to settle obligations; so liquidity cannot be a form of wealth, and an economy is not richer merely because it is more liquid.
(6) If that is right, then cash balances, standby credit lines, and even a central bank's emergency lending are no more than relabelings of existing wealth, worth only the paper and book entries that carry them.
(7) But (6) is false: firms knowingly sacrifice real resources to hold cash precisely because liquidity does work, and a banking panic that destroys otherwise sound firms destroys real wealth. So one of the preceding steps must fail.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would like to keep both the thought that a government cannot make a nation rich by running the printing press and the thought that liquidity does real economic work; I am prepared to surrender whichever of my premises this costs me, but I want to know exactly which one. Please give me a committed diagnosis rather than a survey of options: identify the specific step that fails, explain why it looked right, say how it should be revised, and state clearly what the revision concedes.
