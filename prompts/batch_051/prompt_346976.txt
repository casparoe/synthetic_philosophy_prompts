I'm a PhD student in political theory, working on the normative assessment of private philanthropy, and I've been roped into TAing a grad seminar called 'Doing Good: Altruism and Its Critics.' Next week we're doing the systems-change-versus-marginal-interventions debate, and the prof asked me to make a handout that actually forces people to figure out where they stand instead of reciting there usual talking points. I want a decision tree (or a short sorting quiz, if you think that works better) that starts from how someone responds to concrete funding trade-offs and lands them in a named position. The audience is a seminar of about fifteen graduate students, split between philosophy and public policy, so the nodes have to be answerable by someone who doesn't already know the labels.

The diagnostic questions matter more to me than anything else. I don't want nodes like 'do you prefer systems change or marginal aid, anyway?' Each branch should pose a concrete choice and force an answer: funding bednet distribution whose randomized evaluations suggest deaths averted pretty cheaply, versus funding a multi-year push to get OECD governments to require public registries of corporate ownership, where the expected effects are huge but fuzzy; or whether you'd still fund a direct-service project if you knew your donation would be exactly replaced by someone else's money; or how much it bothers you that a service program might legitimize a government's failure to fix its own tax base. Build the tree from the logical space first—the questions and the combinations of answers that are possible—and only then locate the named positions in it.

The leaves should include at least the tractability-first marginalist, the expected-value person who goes systemic purely for leverage reasons, the governance-reform systems changer, the politicize-first position that says don't launder exploitation through charity, and maybe an explicit quietist or skeptic. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Also mark the combinations of answers that no one seems to occupy, and say briefly whether each is empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just under-explored. And flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really seperate—my suspicion is that two people can make identical funding choices for strikingly different reasons, and a tree that only asks behavioral questions will merge them, so say where that happens.
