I am trying to work out the principle behind the line I actually draw between what morality requires of a person and what is morally good but optional, and I would like you to help me by doing reflective equilibrium on my verdicts. Below are my gut judgments on a set of cases, then a tentative principle I have been testing, then some further cases where I have not settled my view. Please take the verdicts as data: propose the best principle you can, test it against the cases, repair or replace my tentative principle if needed, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts that principle cannot accommodate and why I should overturn them.

**Cases and verdicts**

1. **Shallow pond.** A stranger's child is drowning in a shallow pond you happen to be walking past. Saving the child would ruin an expensive suit and make you late for work, but it involves no real risk. I think you are required to save the child. This is not above and beyond; it is simply what one must do.

2. **Life ring.** A strong swimmer is eating lunch on a dock when a stranger begins drowning fifty meters out. A life ring is at his feet and throwing it costs him nothing beyond interrupting his lunch. I think he is required to throw it. Failing to throw it is wrong, not merely ungenerous.

3. **Rough water.** The same swimmer sees a stranger drowning two hundred meters out in rough water, with no ring and no boat. Attempting the rescue means swimming out through heavy surf and taking a real risk of serious injury or death. I think going is heroic and optional: it would be excellent to go, and I would not blame him for refusing. I want to say he is not required to go.

4. **Grenade.** A soldier can smother a live grenade with his body; he will certainly die, and five others will probably survive as a result. I do not think he is required to do this, even though it is the act with the best consequences available to him.

5. **Forgiveness as forbearance.** You have a legally valid, well-grounded claim against a small family-owned firm that wronged you badly several years ago. The owners have since acted honestly and are now struggling; enforcing the claim would very likely ruin them. I think enforcing the claim is morally permitted, and forgiving the debt is morally better, but forgiving is not required. It is a paradigm case of meritorious forbearance, distinct in kind from rescuing someone cheaply.

6. **Giving: floor and ceiling.** A comfortably-off person, I believe, is required to give some meaningful portion of her income to effective charities that relieve severe poverty, and giving nothing is wrong. But I do not think she is required to give away half her income; doing that is extraordinary, praiseworthy, and optional. The required amount sits somewhere well below the saintly amount.

7. **Parent and child.** A parent spends a large sum — say, $80,000 — on continued treatment for her child's rare disease, when the same sum given to a charity would save several strangers' lives. I do not think the parent's spending is optional in the way a stranger's donation would be; it is part of what a parent owes her child. If a stranger spent that amount on the same child out of pure compassion, I would call it a striking act beyond duty; for the parent, it feels required.

8. **Two ponds.** While walking, you come across two drowning children in two separate shallow ponds. Saving each child is equally safe and easy, and each would ruin a separate suit. I think it would be indefensible to save one and walk past the second; if the first rescue is required, the second is required too. The fact that you have already done one good deed does not turn the next rescue into an optional extra.

**My tentative principle**

The principle I have been testing is roughly this: an act is required when the good at stake is serious and the cost to the agent falls below a significant threshold — a cost involving no serious risk, no major sacrifice of the agent's central goals and commitments; once the cost crosses that threshold, the act becomes optional, and if it is optional, good, and especially costly, it is supererogatory. This gets cases 1, 2, and 8 right through the low-cost side, and cases 3, 4, and 5 right through the high-cost side; it also explains the floor and ceiling in case 6. But it badly fits case 7, where the parent incurs a high cost and yet the act seems required rather than optional. And I have a further, deeper worry: the principle says an act can be morally better than every permitted alternative and still not be required. I need a story about why the best act is not automatically the obligatory act; otherwise my verdicts look inconsistent. Tell me whether that story exists, and what it is.

**Further cases to predict**

A. **Non-swimmer.** A passerby cannot swim and sees a child drowning in a pond. Wading in would almost certainly drown the passerby and probably not save the child; the only realistically useful act is to run for help. What does your principle say: is running for help required, is wading in supererogatory, and should we say anything at all about whether wading in is good?

B. **Kidney to a stranger.** A healthy adult learns she is a tissue match for a stranger who will otherwise die of kidney failure. Donation carries a small but real risk of serious complications and several weeks of recovery, and it saves one life at that cost. My tentative principle seems to push toward saying donation is required below some threshold, but my gut keeps it optional or at best borderline. Given everything else I have said, which verdict should I accept, and does the principle need repair in order to deliver it?

C. **Parent in fast water.** The parent from case 7 is the only available rescuer when her child is caught in a fast river. The rescue carries substantial but not near-certain risk, far more than wading into a shallow pond. Is she required to go? If your answer differs from what you would say about a stranger attempting the same rescue, explain exactly how special obligations function in the principle: do they change the threshold, add a different kind of reason, or alter what counts as a cost to the agent?

Please state your final principle precisely, with enough structure that I can apply it to new cases, and indicate for each clause which of my cases or further cases motivates it. Then say frankly which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should revise that verdict or reject the principle instead.
