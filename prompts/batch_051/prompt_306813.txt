I spent a long weekend playing a game called Nomic with a few people from my department. The basic idea is that the rules of the game include rules about how to change the rules: winning is partly about getting the group to adopt amendments that favour you, any player can propose a rule-change, and it gets voted on under whatever procedure the current rules specify. I noticed something odd. Even when the written rules were silent or ambiguous, players would justify their moves by appealing to a sort of implicit understand of what the game is: an amendment is 'too self-serving', that's 'not the kind of change this rule is for', that reading would 'make the game unplayable'. My friend, who reads a lot of legal philosophy, says this is an instance of what he calls the rule of recognition: in any functioning legal system, the people running it share an unwritten practice for identifying what counts as a valid rule, even when the explicit text runs out.

I'd like to know whether that claim is testable rather than just a nice metaphor. Suppose we ran a bunch of Nomic games and recorded the proposals, votes, arguements, rule-changes, and how disputes got resolved. What would count as evidence that players are actually being guided by a shared, unwritten rule of recognition, as opposed to merely using the written rules plus their own preferences and social pressure? Design the strongest test you can, say what result would support the claim and what result would undercut it, and then be explicit about what a positive result would and would not show about real legal systems. Where does the analogy between a toy game and an actual state legal system break down even if the test works?
