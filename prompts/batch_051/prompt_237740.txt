Ok, an argument about moral uncertainty has been bugging me, and I think it proves too much but I cant find the step to reject.

(1) Suppose you're 60% sure act-utilitarianism (do whatever produces the most overall good) is the right moral theory and 40% sure a strict promise-keeping deontology (keep promises no matter the consequences) is right, and they disagree about whether to break a promise that would do a lot of good overall. If you take that uncertainty seriously, you shouldn't just act as if the 60% theory is certain. The natural rule is to maximize expected choiceworthiness (call it MEC): for each option, take how much moral value each theory assigns to it, weight those values by your credence in the theory, sum them, and pick the option with the highest total.

(2) But MEC is itself one proposal about how to act under moral uncertainty. A rival proposal—"my favorite theory" (MFT)—says: figure out which moral theory you think is most probably right and just do what it says. And you can't be certain MEC is the right proposal. Say you're 70% in MEC and 30% in MFT as accounts of how to handle moral uncertainty.

(3) By MEC's own logic, you should now weight MEC and MFT at this second level. But to do that you need to know how choiceworthy it is to follow MEC versus how choiceworthy it is to follow MFT. And the choiceworthiness of following a rule seems to just be the choiceworthiness of the act the rule tells you to do—which depends on which first-order theory you use to score the act. Which first-order theory to use is exactly what the second-level dispute was supposed to settle, so the calculation turns in a circle.

(4) You could try to fix that by ascending to a third level—some rule about how to weigh second-level rules—but the same circularity shows up there, and then again at every level above it.

(5) So MEC either never returns a verdict, or it has to be assumed as the fixed background rule at some arbitrary level.

(6) Therefore MEC—the one view that seemed to take your uncertainty seriously across the board—has to exempt itself from being weighted. That's a pretty wild result; it makes the whole project of acting on weighted moral uncertainty look self-undermining. But step (1) still looks to me like the only honest move. If I have to give something up, I'd rather give up (5) than (1), but I want to know what that costs.
