Consider two colleagues. One sees a peer receive an award and feels a tight, uncomfortable satisfaction in the thought that the peer might not really deserve it; the thought, however briefly, is that it ought to have gone to him, and that there would be something gratifying in the peer's losing it. The other discovers that a promotion was decided through a rigged process that favored someone less qualified, and feels a steady, indignant anger that the decision should be overturned. Most of us are prepared to call the first attitude envy and to treat it as something to be concealed—a vice, or at any rate a moral blemish, the sort of feeling we disavow even when we have it. Most of us are equally prepared to call the second attitude resentment, or righteous indignation, and to treat it as legitimate—even as a sign of self-respect and a proper sensitivity to injustice. Yet the two attitudes share a good deal. Both are painful, comparative responses to a perceived inequality in the distribution of some good. Both can be more or less accurate about the underlying facts. Both can motivate action aimed at changing the distribution. And in practice the line between them is often drawn suspiciously: the person who protests an unfair procedure is often said to be only envious, while an envious person who happens to be right that the procedure was unfair is suddenly credited with a sense of justice.

What I want is not a stipulative definition but a justification. Is there a principle that vindicates the ordinary asymmetry—one that explains why resentment is a response we are entitled to feel and express while envy is a response we are expected to suppress—without simply building the distinction into the definitions? The most promising candidates seem to be these: envy characteristically wishes the other to lose the good, while resentment characteristically wishes the wrong to be repaired; envy is directed at another's possession of a good, while resentment is directed at a wrong done through the way the good was distributed; resentment, unlike envy, presupposes a normative claim to the good in question. Each of these distinctions, however, has hard cases. Resentment can be spiteful and leveling-down, envy can be emulative rather than malicious, and envy can track a genuine injustice—it can be the felt symptom that one has been denied something to which one actually had a claim. Rawls gestures at this last possibility with the category of excusable envy, the envy of those whose self-respect is injured by inequalities so large that the resentment-like response begins to look like a reasonable reaction to an unjust arrangement.

I would like the strongest possible defense of the ordinary asymmetry, tested against those hard cases, and then an honest verdict: does the asymmetry survive entire, survive only in a narrowed form, or should it be revised—perhaps by treating the two as points on a continuum, or by replacing the question of which emotion is felt with a question about which claims are justified? And if the asymmetry cannot be fully justified, I want to know what we should say instead when someone dismisses a complaint as mere envy, or redeems an envious grudge by discovering that the process really was rigged.
