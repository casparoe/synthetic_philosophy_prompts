i keep coming back to a strongly directional, almost providential picture of history. not a divine plan necessarily, more like the view that history is the slow painful realization of freedom, with destructive periods functioning as the means by which later forms of life become possible. the theory has real pull for me because it gives history a criterion of progress, it explains why studying the past isnt just antiquarianism, and it lets me distinguish real advances from noise without pretending every change is an improvement. but i also have a very firm intuition that some of the past is simply wasted. i dont mean wasted as in later generations failed to appreciate it, i mean gone without doing any work at all: a village destroyed in a war that decided nothing, a language that dies and leaves no trace in what comes after, people who suffer and then are never remembered by anyone. the theory seems to force me to say these things were nonetheless taken up into the overall direction, or at least were the price of it, and i find that unacceptable, it feels like pretending a murder was an investment. i also worry that my sense that the theory is plausible is an artifact of the archive: the winners who write the record forward are exactly the ones the process favored, so of course the past looks like it was heading somewhere, the people it ruined dont get to file a dissenting opinon. what i want is a negotiated verdict, not a side. steelman both positions: the strongest directional view that could absorb or deflect these cases, and the strongest case that my intuition of wasted past should be debunked, for instance as scale insensitivity or as confusing an aggregate claim about history with a claim about each event. then say which should yield, or whether there is a stable reading on which they are compatible, and spell out what would justify each move. write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, like an email.
