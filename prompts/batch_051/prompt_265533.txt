I've been trying to understand why some true, useful ideas never become common knowledge even when everyone who already holds them would benefit if they spread, and I've come to think the standard talk of antimemes — ideas that resist being remembered or passed on — gets the mechanism backwards. On my view, most antimemes don't resist anything; they simply lack a hook. A hook is a role the idea gives the person who repeats it: something to warn about, correct, accuse, brag about, or recruit with. An idea spreads when retelling it is a social move, and when it supplies no move it stays put no matter how true or useful it is. Example: "the office water filter was last changed on Tuesday" matters to everyone in the office, but there is no social move available in saying it, so nobody passes it along. Add one element — "the office manager lied about changing the filter" — and the same practical content suddenly circulates, because retelling it is now an accusation. I've been calling these hookless antimemes, and I suspect the real predictor of whether a truth becomes common knowledge is not how catchy it is but whether it gives someone something to do in conversation. Is this a genuine distinction, or have I just rediscovered something in memetics or the epidemiology of representations? And where does it fail — what sorts of antimemes are not usefully explained as missing hooks?
