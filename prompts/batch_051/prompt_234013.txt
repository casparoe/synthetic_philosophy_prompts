I've been going down a free-will rabbit hole lately (blame the usual rationalist forum flame wars) and decided to actually read an introductory textbook instead of just trusting blog summaries. The intro-to-free-will chapter glosses Frankfurt cases like this:

"Frankfurt cases are thought experiments designed to show that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. In the standard case, a neuroscientist called Black has secretly wired Jones's brain so that, if Jones is about to decide not to do what Black wants, Black will press a button and force him to do it anyway. Jones decides on his own to do the thing, so Black never presses the button. Since Jones would have acted the same way no matter what, he could not have done otherwise; yet we still hold him responsible because the act came from his own decision. Frankfurt took this to refute the Principle of Alternative Possibilities, which says that moral responsibility requires free will, and to show that determinism is no threat to responsibility."

Something about this reads as off to me, but I cant quite put my finger on it, and the textbook is by someone with actual credentials. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, and what is outright wrong or at least heavily contested by people who work on Frankfurt cases. In particular, does the jump from "Jones could not have done otherwise" to "determinism is no threat to responsibility" actually go through, or is the textbook smuggling in a conclusion?
