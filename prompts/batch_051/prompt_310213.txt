A friend and I have been working through Kant's test where you ask whether you could want everyone to act on the policy behind what you're doing. Neither of us is a philosophy person; we've just been taking turns throwing cases at her and I've kept a list of her calls. So far: a small lie to drag a friend to their own surprise party is fine, because she could genuinely want a world where people tell that kind of harmless, for-their-own-good lie. A false promise to repay borrowed money is wrong, because if everyone borrowed while lying about paying it back, promising itself would come apart. Shoplifting a small item from a big chain store is wrong on the same grounds. But then she says a person who never develops a talent they actually have, because lounging around feels nicer, is also wrong, even though she admits that world is easy to picture; her problem is that you can't really want it, since you'd be willing toward a world where the abilities your life runs on have been let go. And she's totally fine with telling a crying kid their drawing is good when it isn't, on the same harmless-for-their-sake logic as the party case.

The new case I'm trying to predict: a woman's terminally ill father asks her, near the end, whether she's been praying for him. She doesn't believe, but she says she has, because she's sure the truth would just scare him and do no good. Her private policy, the way she'd phrase it, is to tell a dying person whatever lets them face death peacefully when they ask something like that. I genuinely can't tell if my friend files this under harmless-for-their-sake lie (party, kid's drawing) or something closer to the false promise, since the person is being kept from the truth about something that matters to them. What's your best prediction of her call, what rule is she actually running—is it Kant's split between can't-even-conceive-it and can-conceive-but-can't-will-it, or is it really a rule about what makes a lie okay—and what further case would most cleanly seperate the rules that fit her record? At the very end, put the prediction on its own line, like \answer{okay} or \answer{wrong} or \answer{undetermined}, followed by a short label for the rule.
