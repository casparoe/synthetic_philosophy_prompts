Ok so here's one I've been going back and forth on. People usually list two seperate-sounding objections to stratospheric aerosol injection. One is the moral-hazard worry: if we think we can cool the planet on the cheap, emissions cuts will stall. The other is termination shock: if the injection program ever stops abruptly—war, funding collapse, political breakdown, whatever—the greenhouse warming we'd been masking shows up almost all at once. I want you to compare these two, not just define them. Specifically: are they genuinely independent objections, or is termination shock better read as the moral-hazard problem with a longer fuse and a nastier bill—the same "we've let ourselves stay hooked on a risky status quo" worry, just matured? People sometimes suggest you could defuse termination shock with a binding commitment device, like a treaty plus an escrow fund that makes unilateral shutoff very costly over decades. Does that move also blunt the moral-hazard objection, or does it leave it fully intact? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro and no wrap-up. Make one of the five the self-critical point: tell me what would change your mind—which empirical finding or argument would flip your verdict, or where you're least sure your reasoning holds.
