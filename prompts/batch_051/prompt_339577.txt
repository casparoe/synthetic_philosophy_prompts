Consider a constitutional interregnum: a written constitution is still nominally in force, but an electoral crisis has left no lawful path in the text for appointing or removing a government — both leading candidates claim to have won, the electoral authority cannot produce a certified result, and the constitution contains no applicable succession or tie-break rule. Rank the following ways of managing the interregnum from most to least defensible, where "defensible" means best reconciling legality with the continued need to govern. Give one sentence for each item explaining its rank.

(a) The outgoing government stays on as a caretaker under a norm of necessity, limited to routine administration until a lawful path is restored.
(b) The constitutional court assumes temporary rule-making authority, arguing that its power to interpret the law carries an implied residual power to fill gaps and supervise a return to constitutional order.
(c) The legislature, or whichever part of it can convene, elects a provisional executive, on the theory that representative bodies retain residual legitimacy where the text is silent.
(d) The permanent state institutions — the military or the senior civil service — step in to preserve order until they judge the crisis resolved, invoking an unwritten duty to protect the state.
(e) A constituent assembly is convened from below, treating the interregnum as a rupture in which the people resume their original power to reconstitute the order.

Then say in one sentence which item changes rank most if the overriding aim is long-term constitutional stability rather than short-term legality, and why.
