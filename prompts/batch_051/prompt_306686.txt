For some years I have been defending a distinction I call proximate versus formative control, and I want an honest assessment of it. An episode is under my proximate control when, at the moment it occurs, whether I assent to an impression or withhold assent is up to me — whether I let an insult settle into injury, whether I go on feeding a flash of resentment. A disposition is under my formative control when the episodes I choose now feed back into the character that will make future episodes easy or automatic: by repeatedly withholding assent from anger I make angry impressions, if not rarer, at least less immediately convincing, even though I could not have abolished them at the start. The familiar dichotomy of control is binary and synchronic — what I judge, pursue, and avoid is up to me, while my body, property, reputation, and office are not. My claim is that there are really two overlapping domains, the synchronically up to me and the diachronically up to me, and that Stoic self-cultivation does its real work in the overlap: a long series of proximate choices, each of which I could have made otherwise only in a thin sense, gives me genuine influence over a character I never chose all at once. This, I think, answers the familiar objection that even judgment is not up to me because it arises from temperament and training — such judgments are not all proximately up to me, but many are formatively so. I know the modern trichotomy of control (no control, partial control, full control) fills some of this space, and I do not think my view is just that view renamed. Give me the three strongest objections, say whether any is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and tell me whether the distinction earns its keep, already exists under another name, or should be reabsorbed into the familiar dichotomy. Finally, tell me where your assessment is least secure and which consideration or counterexample would most likely change your verdict. Please answer in American English.
