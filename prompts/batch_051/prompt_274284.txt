ok so for a philosophy-of-tech thing at school I'm writing about abandoned software (like orphan works but for programs), and I kept coming back to this one little utility from around 2003 that people still use. Its readme contains what is basically a license:

'You may copy and distribute this program without charge, provided all files are kept together and unaltered. You may not modify, reverse engineer, or create derivative works from this program without the express written permission of the original author.'

The author died in 2016, and as far as anyone can tell there's no company, no estate contact, no way to ask. The program doesn't run on current systems, so someone in a forum made a patch, and the moderators keep taking it down on the grounds that the readme forbids it. Here's what I want to untangle: how should that clause apply to a case the author definately never had in mind? The literal words say no patching. But the point of the clause looks like it was to keep control with the author — and there's no author left to control anything or be harmed. What would the author probably have said if he'd been asked 'after you die, can people patch this so it keeps working'? And should that counterfactual decide it, or should we go by what a reasonable person downloading it in 2003 would have understood? I want the different answers those approaches give and an argument for which one should win. Also, is the author-being-dead case actually different from a case where the 'author' is a company that got dissolved, or where the person is alive but just ignores emails? Or do all of those collapse into 'the permission-giver is unreachable'?
