In a seminar on the French Revolution, our instructor kept returning to the old line that "the revolution devours its children" without ever unpacking it. When a student pressed her to say what she meant, she read us this passage from Robespierre's speech to the Convention of February 1794, usually translated as "On the Principles of Political Morality":

"Every precaution must early be used to place the interests of freedom in the hands of truth, which is eternal, rather than in those of men who change; so that if the government forgets the interests of the people or falls into the hands of men corrupted, according to the natural course of things, the light of acknowledged principles should unmask their treasons, and that every new faction may read its death in the very thought of a crime."

Her gloss was that the passage shows the devouring was not principally a matter of personal ambition, fear, or paranoia: the revolutionary government was openly building a politics in which any new faction would be marked for destruction in advance. I have since tried and failed to pin down what the slogan itself actually claims. It could be the biographical observation that revolutions kill their own makers (Danton, Desmoulins, the Girondins, eventually Robespierre himself); or the structural claim that revolutionary governments contain a built-in tendency to radicalize until earlier revolutionaries are reclassified as traitors; or something stronger, almost teleological — that every revolution is fated to turn on itself. Each reading matters differently for the standing debate over how to interpret the Revolution, where the main options are class-struggle, ideological, and contingency accounts.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses "the revolution devours its children" could be expressing, from the trivial or merely biographical to the substantive and law-like. For each reading, state whether it is plausible, defensible, or false, and say how it would fit — or fail to fit — a class-struggle, an ideological, or a contingency interpretation of 1793–94. Then say which reading or readings the Robespierre passage can actually support and which it cannot.
