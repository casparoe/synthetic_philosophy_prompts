Inverse reinforcement learning treats an agent's observed behavior as evidence about an underlying reward function, which researchers routinely gloss as the agent's preferences. I want a working definition of 'preference' that makes that gloss defensible — one that does not simply equate preference with choice, since several of the cases below are meant to break that equation. The cases: a rat pressing a lever that delivers food pellets; a thermostat that turns on the heat when the room cools below a set point; a commuter who drives the highway most days but takes surface streets when the highway is congested; a chess engine that sacrifices its queen to force mate in three; a smoker who says she wants to quit but still buys a pack most evenings; an employee who stays at the default contribution rate in a retirement plan even though raising it would take only minutes; and a dieter who orders salad when eating with colleagues and cake when eating alone. I am fairly confident the rat counts as having preferences and the thermostat does not, and I do not trust my own judgment on the rest. Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, say where it yields the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. End by telling me which of your final classifications is least secure.
