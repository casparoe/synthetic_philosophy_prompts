Ask almost anyone whether a nurse who rolls her eyes at a patient's questions has done something wrong, and the answer is immediate: yes, she owed that patient respect. Ask whether a nurse who is perfectly polite but entirely without recognition — who never once treats a patient as a particular person, never remembers a name, and shows no interest in whether anyone recovers — has failed in something she owed them, and people hesitate. Few will call it a wrong; they will call her cold, or wonder why she chose nursing. The difference is not in the action but in the attitude the behavior expresses, or fails to express. I find the same asymmetry all over ordinary life. We treat a colleague who speaks to us condescendingly as having wronged us, but a colleague who never greets us, never registers our contributions, and treats us as a role rather than a person is called distant or reserved — not someone who failed us.

So the asymmetry is this: the absence of demeaning attitudes is treated as something we owe one another, while the presence of acknowledging or even positive attitudes is treated as optional. The strongest justification I can formulate is that we owe others only non-degradation, not positive regard, because only the former can be demanded from everyone without also demanding love or admiration. But that is not obviously right: being treated as nobody, sustained over years and in public, can wound as surely as an insult. Please construct the strongest justification available for this asymmetry, stress-test it against a case in which the withheld recognition is persistent, public, and costly to the person ignored, and then say whether we should keep the asymmetry, abandon it, or refine it.
