I keep seeing the cross-country happiness rankings whipsawed between three positions — they measure happiness and the top country really is happiest; they measure some artefact of how different populations use 0-to-10 scales; or they measure a real thing but a government that chases them ruins exactly the thing. Before I read anymore takes, I want the specification: what would a measure of national (or at least cross-cultural) well-being actually have to get right, and adequate for what? Be explicit about the purpose, because I suspect the requirements change if it's a tool for migrants choosing where to live, for a government setting policy, or for researchers studying the structure of well-being. Give me the requirements list — the cases or data patterns it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the psychology or survey-methodology results it can't contradict — with the case behind each one. For each requirement, mark it non-negotiable (a measure that misses this stops being a measure of well-being) or a camp preference (a measure could flatly reject this and still count as some kind of well-being measure). I want at minimum covered: can it collapse felt affect into whole-life evaluation; can it ignore the distribution inside a country and just report a mean; does it have to be robust against cultural differences in scale use, and robust against a government optimizing the published index once it becomes a policy target; and how should a measure handle adaptation (people reporting themselves fine in conditions outsiders call miserable) — correct for it, report it, or stay silent? Then flag any pairs of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied by a single national number, and tell me which single requirement is most damaging for the rankings as currently run, and which one is most damaging for the 'countries should try to top them' idea. Ignore the scorecard.
