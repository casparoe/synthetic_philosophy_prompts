You are an expert in constructive mathematics and the philosophy of intuitionistic logic. I am developing a teaching exercise for a small seminar on the difference between classical and constructive existence claims, and I want a dialogue that does not rely on tired examples like the law of excluded middle applied to an unsolved conjecture. Write a dialogue between two equally competent mathematicians: Ana, a classical analyst who regards nonconstructive existence proofs as perfectly good mathematics, and Ben, a constructivist in the Bishop tradition who holds that a proof that an object exists must either exhibit it or give a method for finding it. Let their disagreement crystallize around one specific theorem: every real number is rational or irrational. At the start, state the theorem precisely. Then have Ana give a classical proof and Ben probe exactly where the proof stops being constructive. The characters should respond to each other’s actual arguments, and neither should be a strawman; each should make at least one significant concession by the end. The climax should be an explicit identification of the single logical rule—not a disagreement about the meaning of the word exists—that the entire disagreement turns on. Begin by briefly sketching the two positions and the theorem in your own words, then write the dialogue itself.
