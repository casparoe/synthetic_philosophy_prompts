I'm applying for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in the history of political thought and need a research proposal of about three pages. The topic is Adam Smith's critique of empire and colonies, and I want you to write the proposal. My half-formed idea is this. The existing readings tend to split Smith's objections into two independent registers. In The Wealth of Nations he criticizes the colonial monopoly as an economic distortion: it misdirects capital, raises prices for consumers, and diverts resources toward a narrow merchant interest while spreading the costs across the public. Elsewhere he attacks the imperial project in moral and political terms: it is the pursuit of empire for the sake of creating customers, carried on by a government captured by commercial interests; it corrupts metropolitan politics, imposes arbitrary rule abroad, and is valued for the grandeur of the system rather than for the welfare of the people caught up in it. Commentators usually emphasize one register and treat the other as secondary, or present them as parallel but separate complaints. I suspect they are not separate. Both trace back, I think, to Smith's account in The Theory of Moral Sentiments of the seductive appeal of systematic order: the tendency to admire a well-arranged plan and to prefer its imagined beauty over the actual happiness and self-direction of the people within it. On this reading, empire is that tendency operating at the largest scale, a magnificent commercial machine whose visible orderliness conceals the waste, suffering, and distorted incentives it produces, and whose very systematic character is what makes it attractive to statesmen and merchants. If this is right, Smith's economic and political criticisms of empire are one unified argument, and the project should reconstruct it and ask what that does to the standard pictures of Smith as a critic of mercantilism, as a proto-anti-imperialist, or as a defender of commercial society.

Write the proposal so that it does the following. State the central question precisely enough that a reader can tell what would count as answering it. Explain why the existing economic and political readings, taken separately, leave a genuine gap—without citing secondary literature; I will add the citations myself. Lay out the method: the specific claims in The Wealth of Nations and The Theory of Moral Sentiments that would have to line up for the unifying reading to succeed, the patterns of argument that would count as evidence against it, and the order in which the work would proceed over the year. Say why this approach is preferable to the obvious alternatives, such as treating the economic critique as self-contained or reading Smith simply as an anti-imperialist. Specify what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing on its own terms. Finally, keep the scope realistic for a one-year fellowship and say explicitly what would be postponed for later work.
