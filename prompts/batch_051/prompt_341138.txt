I've been developing a view I call sedimented striving, and I'd like an honest assessment of it before I build anything further on it. The usual accounts of effort treat it either as a cost we pay for an outcome or as a signal of how much we care. I think there is a third role. When someone repeatedly chooses to do something difficult—practicing an instrument, maintaining a discipline, staying in a demanding relationship—the effort gradually deposits in them a disposition to go on treating that activity as worthwhile, independent of whether their performance improves, whether they succeed, or whether anyone observes the effort. On my view, effort is not just downstream of value; it is partly what makes an end one's own, because the resistance one pushes against is the medium in which a value gets sedimented into the agent. That would make effort intrinsically valuable in a narrow but real sense: not because struggle is noble, and not because overcoming resistance feels good, but because struggle is a mechanism by which values are built into a person. Is this a genuine third category, or does it collapse into something already available—the value of exercising agency, the value of commitment, effort justification, self-constitution? And where does it break? In particular, what distinguishes sedimented striving from stubborn persistence in a worthless project, and does the filter that draws that distinction end up doing all the normative work?
