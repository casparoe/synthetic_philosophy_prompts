You are a social epistemologist with expertise on belief formation in online communities. On the standard picture, an epistemic bubble is a structural gap in exposure: relevant evidence and voices are simply missing, and the bubble would pop if the missing information were supplied. An echo chamber is stronger: outside voices are not merely absent but actively discredited, so that exposure to them would fail because the hearer has already been trained to distrust the source before hearing the content.

Suppose we operationalize echo chamber with the following five criteria. A group is an echo chamber to the extent that all five are satisfied.

(1) Exposure gap. Members systematically fail to encounter a significant range of relevant outside evidence, testimony, or interpretation.
(2) Cultivated distrust. Members are inducted into discounting outside voices in advance, through a standing credibility discount that is not readily revised when those voices produce new evidence.
(3) Conformity pressure. Internal discussion rewards agreement with the community's dominant claims and penalizes, filters, or drowns out dissent.
(4) Credibility management. Some identifiable mechanism — a leader, a moderation policy, a platform algorithm, or an informal status hierarchy — distributes credibility to insiders and withholds it from outsiders.
(5) Epistemic stakes. The claims at issue are matters on which there is genuine external evidence and expertise, not merely questions of taste, identity, or private affiliation.

Before applying the criteria, think briefly about which of the five you expect to do the most diagnostic work in the case below and which are likely to conflict with one another.

Now consider the case. A large online community forms among people with a chronic illness that mainstream medicine spent decades dismissing as psychosomatic; many members joined only after years of being misdiagnosed or disbelieved. The community maintains a collaboratively compiled document of treatments and clinicians, and moderators remove posts that press the view that the illness is purely psychological, on the grounds that the view is false and harmful. Platform recommendation algorithms steer members toward the community's favored sources and away from mainstream medical guidance. Experienced members tell newcomers that established medical institutions cannot be trusted on this topic because of their track record; over time this has hardened into a standing discount that members are expected to apply to new mainstream studies as well, including studies that would correct some of the community's factual errors. Some specific claims circulating in the community are almost certainly false, though the community's core claims have recently been partly vindicated by new research. The community has no formal hierarchy: members who dissent are challenged in discussion and often downvoted, but are only rarely expelled.

Does this community qualify as an echo chamber under the five criteria? Proceed criterion by criterion. For each criterion, say whether the case satisfies it, violates it, or genuinely underdetermines the answer, and give the strongest consideration on each side. Then assess whether the case puts pressure on the definition itself: in particular, whether criterion (2) needs to be supplemented by something like an evidence-responsiveness or reasonableness condition, so that justified protective filtering is not automatically classified together with manufactured distrust. End by giving the single strongest reason to classify this community as an echo chamber, the single strongest reason to withhold the label, and a final overall verdict, however qualified.
