Quick background: I chair the authorisation comittee of a regional chemicals regulator — we assess new active substances for pesticides and biocides, and we also do re-authorisations of existing ones. Our enabling legislation tells us to apply the precautionary principle, but the guidance is basically one sentence about serious or irreversible threats and the fact that gaps in scientific certainty aren't by themselves a reason to do nothing. In practise my colleagues use the principle as a de facto ban the minute any study flags a possible effect. That's turned into a standoff: the manufacturers say we've imposed a hidden moratorium, and half the panel thinks the principle is toothless because we just keep demanding near-certainty anyway. We make about forty of these decisions a year, and each one is reviewable in administrative court, so the written reasons have to track the evidence we actually had. The panel is a mix — one staff toxicologist, a hydrologist, an agronomist, and two lay appointees — and we have about half a day per file and no budget for commissioning new research.

Here's what I'd like: a decision procedure my team could actually run on a file — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it stops being a scientific judgement and becomes a value judgement that has to go to elected officials. Please don't just hand me the seminar version where precaution means better safe than sorry. I suspect the real content is in the difference between the strong version (roughly, don't act unless shown safe) and the weak version (roughly, uncertainty isn't a reason for paralysis, but measures have to be proportionate and revisable), and I suspect a usable procedure will land nearer the weak one — but if so, show me the steps, don't just assert it.

Then run the procedure on three cases, including one where it gives an answer my unit won't like. Maybe these: (a) a new herbicide with a clean standard battery except one equivocal genotoxicity result the toxicologist thinks is spurious; it would cut tillage and soil erosion a lot. (b) a plan to recharge a drinking-water aquifer with advanced-treated wastewater, where the trace-contaminant data is just missing and the alternative is trucking water during droughts. (c) an existing fungicide up for re-authorisation where the only unresolved issue is a modelling study suggesting a theoretical groundwater exceedance. And say plainly what the procedure cannot do — where precaution runs out and some other norm has to take over.

Oh, and please write the answer in American English.
