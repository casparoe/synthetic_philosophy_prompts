I'm a retired physicist who's spent the last few years poking at the philosophy of mathematics, so feel free to talk to me like a bright amateur, not a grad student. There's a counterfactual I keep coming back to while reading about what counts as a legitimate mathematical object, and I want you to reason it through with me.

The standard story as I understand it: Greek geometry treated compass-and-straightedge construction as the marker of a well-posed problem. Curves generated by a combination of motions — the quadratrix, the Archimedean spiral, the conchoid, later the cycloid — sat on the "mechanical" side of the line and were treated as second-class. Descartes then redrew the boundary in La Géométrie (1637): the curves respectable enough for geometry are the "geometric" ones, which line up with curves given by polynomial equations in x and y, while the spiral and the quadratrix stayed "mechanical" and got excluded, because not all their points can be constructed in the right way.

Here's the counterfactual. Suppose that in the 1630s Descartes had instead made his kinematical criterion permissive: any curve traced by a point under a finite sequence of continuous motions — straightedge, sliding, rotating, rolling, ratios of motions included — counts as geometric. So the quadratrix, Archimedean spiral, and cycloid are fully legitimate from the start, and polynomial expressibility never becomes the boundary of acceptable curves.

Give me your best argued prediction of how the next roughly 150 years of mathematics, up to around 1780, would likely go differently. I want the chain of reasoning, not a story. In particular: which later developments — the consolidation of calculus, rectification and quadrature, the study of transcendental functions, the slow move toward the modern idea of a function, the eventual algebraic/transcendental splitt — get accelerated, delayed, or reordered; whether the algebraic/transcendental boundary gets drawn anyway because algebraic tools are just too useful, even if legitimizing curves no longer requires it; and what has to work differently — methods, standardization, teaching, which problems count as solved — for the change to actually stick rather than being quietly absorbed back into a polynomial-equation worldview.

Also distinguish in your prediction between what's robust and what's a guess, and say what would have to be true for your main prediction to be wrong. And since I'm partly using this to calibrate my own hunches: tell me how confident you are in the overall picture, and single out the point you're least sure of.
