I am a philosophy professor preparing a short paper on the metaphysics of the Incarnation, and I would appreciate comments on the draft below before I revise it. The paper does not survey the literature; it tries out one way of making the two-natures doctrine intelligible and tests whether it collapses.

The doctrine I am working with is this. One and the same person is both fully divine and fully human, and the two natures are united in that person in such a way that they are neither mixed into a third kind of thing, nor separated into two subjects, nor converted into one another. The standing difficulty is to say what a nature is such that two complete natures can belong to one person without violating any of these constraints.

My proposal begins with a distinction between two things that are usually run together under the word "nature." On the one hand, a nature may be a kind-essence: the answer to the question what a thing is. On the other, a nature may be a power-profile: a structured package of active and passive powers — capacities to think, will, perceive, grow, suffer, and act — each organized around its own characteristic principle of operation. In ordinary creatures the two coincide: belonging to the human kind and possessing the human power-profile go together. The Incarnation, I propose, is the one case in which they come apart. The one person supplies the single subject of identity, consciousness, and agency, while the divine and human natures enter the picture as two complete power-profiles that this one subject has and exercises. The natures are not two owners, then, but two sources of powers; the person is the single owner.

I think this handles the traditional constraints in a straightforward way. The natures remain unconfused because the two profiles are distinguishable and neither is reduced to the other. They remain unseparated because both belong to the same subject rather than to distinct subjects. And the divine nature remains unchanged because it was always the profile of this person, whereas the human profile is assumed, not produced by altering the divine one.

The difficulty arises with the human rational powers. A power-profile is not a mere toolkit. Intellect and will are powers of a subject: they operate from a point of view, and their acts are ascribed to the one who thinks and chooses. If the human nature is complete, it seems to include a human rational soul with a human intellect and a human will. But then we appear to have two minds and two wills, and therefore two points of view, in the one person — which threatens to introduce a second subject by the back door. If, on the other hand, I insist that the one person is the sole subject of all operations and that the human nature contributes only powers, I seem to have denied the completeness of the human nature, since a human will that is not the will of a human subject looks like a human will only in an attenuated sense. The proposal thus faces a dilemma: either the unity of the person is preserved at the price of an incomplete human nature, or the completeness of the human nature is preserved at the price of a second subject.

My preferred repair is to distinguish ownership from standpoint. A power may belong to one and the same person even if, in exercising it, that person thinks and chooses from a standpoint — a finite, embodied, historical point of view in the case of the human nature, and an unlimited one in the case of the divine nature. What makes the two natures one person, on this view, is that it is numerically the same subject who occupies either standpoint; the standpoints qualify how the subject thinks and wills but do not themselves do the thinking and willing. The obvious worry is that a standpoint is exactly the sort of thing that seems subject-like, and that I have simply stationed two subjects inside one person and renamed them.

I would be grateful for comments on the following three points. First, does the distinction between kind-essence and power-profile do any real work, or does it merely move the problem from the natures to the powers? Second, can the ownership-standpoint repair be made precise enough to block the charge that a standpoint is a subject in disguise, and if so, at what cost? Third, is the dilemma I pose as serious as I make it out to be, or can the doctrine reasonably countenance two minds and two wills so long as they belong to a single subject — and, if so, what notion of "belonging" is being used? Please respond in American English.
