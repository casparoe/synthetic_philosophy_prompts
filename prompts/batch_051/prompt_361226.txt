A friend of mine was telling me about an argument that broke out in his cognitive science study group over a letter-string analogy, and I still can't figure out who was right about what. The problem on the worksheet was: if "abc" changes to "abd", what should "xyz" change to? One guy said "xya," because the rule is "replace the last letter with its successor," and after z the alphabet wraps around. The other said that wrap-around is a rule the example never licenses; the only change you actually saw was "the final letter c got turned into a d," so by analogy you should turn the final z into a d and get "xyd," no cyclicity required. The first guy shot back that "turn the last letter into d" isn't a rule, it's just copying the answer token—on that logic "pqx" would change to "pqd" and "zzz" to "zzd," which ignores that d is the successor of c and that the whole point of the analogy is to map the role, not the letter. The second then said maybe, but a single example doesn't determine whether the relevant role is "letter-after" or "the specific letter d," and if you really insist on the successor role then "xyz" should just have no answer rather than silently adding an alphabet that loops.

They went back and forth for ages and never settled it, and now I'm not even sure the puzzle has a single well-defined answer, which is it's own issue. Could you go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate? For each of the four moves, say whether it's clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right (and why), then say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about how conceptual slippage actually works in these letter-string problems.
