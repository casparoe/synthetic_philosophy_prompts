I am a retired chemist and now serve on the council of a learned society. Our code of conduct states: 'No member shall, in the course of the Society's activities, make any statement that demeans any person or group on the ground of race.' At our recent annual meeting a member, when asked about urban policy, said that a particular district had become 'perceptibly less orderly' and added, 'no one who values the character of the area could be surprised.' In ordinary English those words are not themselves racial, but among a portion of his audience those final words operate as what some philosophers call a dog whistle—a deniable phrase that carries a derogatory coded meaning for those attuned to it—and several members who recognised the convention said they felt demeaned. The member insists he intended only a neutral remark about public order; the district in question is demographically mixed. The clause was drafted in the 1970s with open racial slurs and explicit claims of racial superiority in mind, and says nothing about coded or deniable statements. I have been asked to advise the council whether the rule covers his remark. Set out what a reading of the words, the purpose of the clause, a reasonable member's understanding, and the drafters' likely answer if asked would each require, and then say which approach should be decisive here and why. In particular, should intent, effect on those who can decode the statement, or the understanding available to an ordinary listener carry the weight?
