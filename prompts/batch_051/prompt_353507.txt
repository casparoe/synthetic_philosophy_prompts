I'm a legal philosophy PhD student, and here's an asymmetry I can't stop thinking about. Two people commit the same fraud and cause the same harm. One is caught after a year and goes to prison. The other doesn't affirmatively cover anything up—he just gets lucky, nobody looks—and the fraud surfaces twenty years later. By then the evidence is actually stronger than in the first case, say a signed confession, but the charge is barred by the statute. We treat that second outcome not as a regretable practical compromise but as a legitimate defense, as if time itself had changed the moral situation. Yet the wrong, the harm, and his culpability haven't changed. The only difference is that he got away with it for longer, which makes the rule look like a reward for evasion. What principle, if anything, justifies letting the passage of time—rather than the fading of evidence or the reform of the offender—extinguish punishability? Give me the strongest version of that principle and tell me whether it survives this hard case, where the only thing that's actually decayed is the public's memory.
