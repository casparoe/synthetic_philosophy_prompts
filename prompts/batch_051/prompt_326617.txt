Vary Taurek's drug case as follows. In the original, you have a life-saving drug. One person, A, needs the entire dose to survive; five people, B1 through B5, each need exactly one-fifth of the dose to survive. All six will die for certain without their share. Taurek holds that you need not save the five merely because they are more numerous: you may give the whole dose to A, or toss a fair coin, because each individual's loss is equal and losses may not be summed across persons.

Now introduce one new parameter. The drug is certain to cure A. But for each Bi, a one-fifth dose works only with probability p, independently across the five, where p is known and strictly between 0 and 1. If the dose fails for Bi, Bi dies exactly as if untreated. A, untreated, dies with certainty.

Work through the case for the following three positions, stating for each what it requires or permits as a function of p and locating any threshold at which its recommendation changes:

1. A maximizing consequentialist who counts expected lives saved.
2. Equal-claims anti-aggregationism: a development of Taurek that permits only pairwise comparison of claims, refuses to aggregate across persons, and treats every endangered life as an equal claim regardless of how probable the harm is, so that p does not weaken any Bi's claim.
3. Risk-weighted anti-aggregationism: pairwise comparison without aggregation, but each person's claim strength is the individual survival gain the scarce resource would confer on them, so A's gain is 1 while each Bi's gain is p.

Then answer two further questions. Do the two anti-aggregationist readings of Taurek come apart in this variant, and which is more faithful to the argument of the original case? Is the risk-weighted reading's verdict discontinuous at p = 1, and if so, should we count that discontinuity as a defect or as an acceptable consequence of taking individual claims seriously?

End your response with an \answer{...} line whose contents are, first, the critical value of p as an exact fraction and, second, a one- or two-word label for the main difficulty the probabilistic variant exposes for anti-aggregationism, separated by a comma.
