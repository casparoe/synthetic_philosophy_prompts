I am a journalist who covers ideas at the intersection of politics and international law, and I am working on a piece about a deceptively simple question: does a state need other states to acknowledge it in order to be a state? In my reading I keep coming back to two rival camps. The declaratory camp treats recognition as evidentiary: if a polity satisfies the standard criteria of statehood — a permanent population, a defined territory, an effective government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states — then it is a state, and recognition merely certifies the fact. The constitutive camp treats recognition as partly creative: statehood is a status within a society of states, constituted by rights, duties, and standing that cannot be had unilaterally, so acknowledgment by others is built into what it means to be a state. Both positions claim to make the best sense of the same basic criteria, and both have awkward cases to explain, which is the tension my article wants to dramatize rather than resolve.

To help me map the argument before I interview sources, I would like you to draft a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors, one defending the declaratory view and one defending the constitutive view. Please have them meet somewhere concrete — for example, over coffee after a joint workshop — and give the exchange real responsiveness: each should engage the other's strongest point rather than retreating to a prepared statement, and neither should be a strawman. I would like the discussion to move past the familiar restatement of the positions and consider, among other things, whether recognition is better understood as a one-off act, a standing policy, or an ongoing practice of interaction; whether a functioning polity that no one recognizes and a recognized state with no functioning government are counterexamples of different kinds; and whether each side can concede that the other captures something essential without collapsing into the other's view. Around 1,200 to 1,800 words, in American English, please.
