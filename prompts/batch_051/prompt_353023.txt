Ok here's something I've been trying to get into shape for an essay, and I'd like comments on the core argument rather than prose polish. The essay is about coherent extrapolated volition (CEV), the idea that an AI making big choices for us should aim at what we'd want if we knew the relevant facts, thought things through more carefully, and had grown into the people we'd want to be. My essay's claim is that CEV is path-dependent, and that the usual "do all the idealizations at once" gloss hides the problem.

Let me lay out the argument as notes.

Think of CEV as applying two operators to a person's current state. The epistemic operator E replaces false or missing beliefs with true ones. The character operator C replaces your current desires with the desires you'd have if you'd already become the sort of person you want to be. The standard story, as I read it, treats these as if they commute—as if it doesn't matter which you apply first. I think it does matter, so there isnt a single determinate output.

Imagine someone whose desire to have kids is mostly riding on the false empirical belief that parenthood reliably makes people happier. Apply E first: she learns the actual evidence, the desire deflates, and then C runs on a state where that desire is gone. The output is plausibly childfree-ish. Now apply C first: she becomes the more deliberate, family-oriented person she already wants to be; that person values parenting for non-empirical reasons—a certain relationship, a practice of care—so when E runs afterward, the parenting value survives. Same starting person, same ideals, different result, purely from order.

A defender might say I'm strawmanning, because CEV is really the unique joint fixed point where you apply E and C together until neither changes the state. But I want to push on that. If E and C don't commute, the joint limit isn't guaranteed to be unique just by calling it "joint"; you still need a scheduling rule, and any scheduling rule looks like a substantive moral choice sneaking in. If there are multiple fixed points, or different basins of attraction, CEV doesn't pick one target.

So the comments I want: is the path-dependence worry actually doing work, or have I just re-described the familiar point that idealized preferences are underdetermined? Is the joint-fixed-point reply enough to save CEV, and if not, what would a defender need to add? Which step in the kid example is weakest? Be as nitpicky as you like. Also feel free to tell me if the essay idea is interesting or just obvious to anyone who knows the CEV debates.
