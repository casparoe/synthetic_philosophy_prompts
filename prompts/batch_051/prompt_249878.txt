I've been playing this online game for a couple of years now, and I recently got into a argument on the forums about a contradiction in how the company treats virtual stuff. The game has player housing and a player-to-player marketplace, and people routinely buy and sell virtual houses, furniture, and rare items for hundreds of pounds. The company's terms of service, though, are absolutly clear that players don't own anything: all virtual items are just data with no real-world value, and exist only as a licence to use the game. Yet when someone broke into my friend's account, transferred her rare virtual house to his own account, and then sold it on, the company didn't just ban the thief. They actually helped her report it to the police as theft, and the support team's messages kept talking about the house as her property that had been taken from her. But another friend of mine lost his entire clan hall when a player used an exploit to demolish it in-game, and the company shrugged and said that was just in-game destruction: no real loss, nothing to restore. So the same object gets treated as property when it's stolen, as worthless data when it suits their terms of service, and as fictional scenery when it's destroyed.

I can see that each of these stances is probably self-interested, but I wonder whether there's a coherent underlying view of what virtual objects are, or of what makes a virtual harm real. What theory of virtual objects and virtual harms does the company's practice presuppose, if any? Is there a way to hold all three positions at once without contradiction, and if so, what would that theory say about when a virtual loss is a real loss? And if they were instead genuinely committed to virtual items being completely unreal, what would their policies have to change? Please answer in British English.
