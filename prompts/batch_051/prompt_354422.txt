I teach a 90-minute upper-level ethics seminar of fourteen undergraduates. Design a runnable in-class activity on the question John Taurek made vivid in his rescue cases: whether the fact that an action would save a larger number of people gives, all by itself, a stronger reason to do it. The session should not begin with any lecture on the question, and the terms Taurek, aggregation, and numbers count should not appear until the debrief. The goal is for participants to discover, through their own choices, both the near-automatic sense that five lives outweigh one and the difficulty of moving from each of the five matters as much as the one to the five together matter more than the one. They should also feel the discomfort of the positions that refuse to let numbers count, such as having to treat saving one and saving five as something to settle by a lottery.

Build it as a structured rescue-allocation exercise played over several rounds, with real but modest stakes: no real money, but decisions that carry small course-relevant costs or benefits, such as points toward a minor privilege or priority in choosing presentation dates, so that self-interest is lightly engaged but nothing serious hangs on the outcome. Across rounds, vary the formal setup deliberately. Some rounds should be all-or-nothing: the whole resource can save one person, or the same whole resource can save five. Some rounds should involve the participants holding role cards as the people to be saved, so that over the course of the exercise everyone knows what it is to be the one and also what it is to be one among the five. Vary whether the larger group is described as a bare collection of anonymous strangers or as a pre-existing unit such as a family or a surgical team, and whether the lone person is identified or anonymous. Include at least one round that forces pairwise justification: each side is represented by a single advocate who must address the other side's claimant one-to-one, so that raw headcount is not available as the argument. Include at least one round in which the committee must choose among saving the five outright, saving the one outright, and assigning the decision to a lottery, after which everyone works out what chance each affected person had ex ante.

The idea should show up in what happens, not in what the facilitator says afterward. The debrief should map specific moments of play onto the underlying structure: pairwise comparison versus summing across persons, treating a group as a single claimant versus as a set of individual claimants, and ex ante equal chances versus ex post larger totals. It should also make explicit what each way of responding costs: letting the numbers count requires accepting a kind of aggregation that the pairwise rounds make strange; refusing to let them count leads toward lotteries or toward treating a family and a collection of strangers as interchangeable. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each failure mode, and be honest about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce, such as the actual stake of death and the separateness of persons as felt from the inside, and where the facilitator will simply have to say something in the debrief.
