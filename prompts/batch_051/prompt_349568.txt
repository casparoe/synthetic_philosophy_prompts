The classic "labels don't matter" example is the two square roots of -1: over R the equation x²+1=0 has roots i and -i, and complex conjugation swaps them while fixing R and preserving + and ×, so there's no internal fact about which one is really i. The usual moral is that only the unordered pair — or the whole structure — is well-defined.

But what happens if we vary the polynomial to x³−2 over Q? The splitting field has three roots: r, ζr, and ζ²r, where r=∛2 and ζ=e^{2πi/3}. Under the usual embedding into C, one of them (r) is real and the other two are a conjugate pair. So it looks like the symmetry is only partial: one root is canonical, two stay swappable. Yet over Q the Galois group of x³−2 is S3, and S3 permutes all three roots transitively; the field-theoretic structure by itself doesn't pick out any of the three. The 'real' one is only real because you quietly chose the standard embedding into C or helped yourself to the ordering on the reals. Adding to the weirdness, the full splitting field has no ordering at all (it contains ζ), even though each root-generated subfield is an isomorphic copy of Q(∛2), and Q(∛2) has a unique ordering — so the splitting field contains three copies of that nicely ordered field, and nothing inside the splitting field says which copy is 'the' real one.

So how does the standard i/-i analysis change in this mixed case? Does the unique ordering on Q(∛2) give you a canonical choice of root, or does it just move the arbitrariness up a level, to the choice of which Galois-conjugate copy of Q(∛2) to treat as real? Would a deflationist who says the question 'which root is the real one?' is a pseudo-question, and a constructivist who says stipulating a label is all well-definedness requires, end up disagreeing here, or do both views survive if they draw the right line? And what is the right line between an expression like the sum of all three roots (well-defined no matter how you label them) and an expression like 'the real root' (apparently embedding-relative)?
