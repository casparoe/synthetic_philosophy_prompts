I am trying to reverse-engineer a friend's private rule for decisions made under moral uncertainty. She holds roughly 70 percent confidence in a welfare-based view, on which an act is right just when it has the best expected consequences, and roughly 30 percent confidence in a constraint-based view, on which certain acts—lying, breaching confidentiality, and intentionally killing an innocent person as a means—are prohibited no matter what the consequences. She says she lets both views bear on every decision, but she has never stated the rule by which she combines them. Her record so far, in three cases where the views clearly diverged, is as follows.

Case A. She could tell a minor lie to spare a colleague's feelings, at no other cost, or tell the truth. The welfare view favored the lie; the constraint view forbade lying but treated it as a small matter. She lied.

Case B. She found a colleague's misdirected email and could read it to make an imminent hiring decision mildly fairer, or close it. The welfare view favored reading it; the constraint view forbade breaching confidentiality. She did not read it.

Case C. She could push one bystander off a footbridge to stop a runaway trolley that would otherwise kill five strangers, or do nothing. The welfare view favored pushing; the constraint view forbade intentionally killing an innocent person as a means. She did not push.

The new case is like Case B in kind: she can read a misdirected email, but this time doing so would let her avert a decision that would severely and permanently damage an innocent person's career and livelihood. The welfare view strongly favors reading; the constraint view forbids the breach exactly as before.

Before I ask her, I want a prediction and the reasoning behind it. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion:
1. the principle her first three decisions best support, stated as precisely as the evidence allows;
2. a second principle that fits the same three decisions equally well;
3. what each principle predicts about the new case;
4. your best guess about what she will actually decide, and why that guess is better supported;
5. a crisply specified further case that would most cleanly determine which principle she is actually using.
