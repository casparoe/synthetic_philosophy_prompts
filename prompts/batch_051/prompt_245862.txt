Two people in my department spent an evening arguing about Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and have now asked me to referee; I could not settle it, so I want a point-by-point adjudication of what was actually said, not a general verdict on the book.

The dispute began when Sam said the book's central analogy — between Gödel's incompleteness theorems and the possibility of self-aware minds — is, at bottom, a pun elevated into an argument. Gödel's self-reference, Sam said, is a formal device: Gödel numbering lets a formula refer to another formula indirectly, through a code, and nothing in that mechanical trick implies anything about the kind of self-awareness that matters. Priya replied that this misses exactly what makes the construction remarkable: a system defined purely by syntactic rules, with no built-in notion of aboutness, can nonetheless produce a sentence that is about itself. That, she said, is the only workable model we have for how an I could emerge from a substrate in which no I is given in advance. Sam came back that many systems can represent facts about themselves without being conscious — a program can print its own source code — so a Gödel sentence is self-description, not selfhood. Priya answered that there is a difference between self-copying and self-transcendence: the point of Gödel's construction is not merely that the system represents itself, but that it can express, within its own language, a sentence about itself that its own rules cannot settle, so that the system in effect sees its own boundary from the inside. Sam then said that even granting that structural point, Priya still has no bridge principle from has an undecidable self-referential sentence to has a point of view; the analogy imports the notion of a self rather than extracting it. Priya replied that bridge principles are the wrong standard: an analogy of this sort can be explanatory by transferring a structure — here, a structure of self-implication and limitation — and the burden is on Sam to say why that transferred structure is misleading, not merely why it is not a deductive argument.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each move, rate it from clearly right to clearly mistaken and briefly justify the rating. Then say which disputant is more right overall and — this is where I most need help — identify what both of them are missing about the relation between formal self-reference and self-awareness.
