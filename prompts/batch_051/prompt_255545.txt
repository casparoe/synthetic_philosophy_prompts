I have recently been made the third reader on a grant proposal from a colleague in another department, and I have to decide whether the intellectual framing is sound enough to justify funding. The proposal concerns algorithmic limitations in automated theorem proving, and its background section contains the following paragraph, which the colleague has summarized in their own words. I suspect the paragraph is largely right but too quick, and I need to know where precisely the problems are, because I will have to justify my written verdict.

The paragraph runs approximately as follows:

The Church-Turing thesis holds that every effectively computable function on the natural numbers is computable by a Turing machine, and conversely. Since the notion of an effective procedure is informal, the thesis cannot be proved as a mathematical theorem. Alan Turing effectively conceded this in his 1936 paper On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem, where he observed that any argument showing his formal notion captures everything we mean by computable must ultimately rest on intuition, and is for that reason mathematically unpersuasive. The thesis is therefore not a mathematical assertion at all but an empirical claim about the limits of human cognitive capacities, and it could in principle be refuted by producing a procedure that everyone agrees is effective but that no Turing machine can execute. Since Church's lambda calculus is another model of computation, the choice between Turing machines and lambda calculus is merely a matter of convenience. Finally, the thesis implies that no machine can decide the halting problem.

I would be grateful for a sentence-by-sentence audit. Which parts would a computability theorist accept, which would they wince at, and which are simply wrong? In particular, does Turing's remark in that paper actually support the conclusion that the thesis is an empirical claim about human cognition, and what role does that remark play in his overall argument? Does anything in the paragraph commit us to a particular view about whether the Church-Turing thesis applies to physical machines, or is that left open?
