Over the past year I have reoriented my career around strong longtermism, and I still find its core argument hard to resist: people who will exist centuries from now count no less than I do, their numbers could be astronomical, and rejecting arbitrary time-preference seems to force me to judge actions by their expected long-run consequences. Yet an intuition keeps colliding with that theory. If I can fund one child's surgery, or donate the same sum to a project that claims only to nudge some unspecified process centuries downstream, with no one able to assign a defensible probability to the claim, the theory says the second is the serious option; I cannot accept that verdict, and it does not feel like mere scope insensitivity or ordinary risk aversion. A foreseeable harm to an identifiable person seems to make a different kind of claim on me than an unbounded, diffuse wager on the deep future — not merely weaker in degree but different in kind. I cannot tell whether this intuition is tracking a genuine epistemic limit, what some call cluelessness, our inability to bound the effects of our actions over centuries, or whether it is near-term bias wearing theoretical clothes. Give me a negotiated verdict rather than a side. What would justify debunking and dropping the intuition, and what would justify amending or abandoning strong longtermism? Is a stable intermediate position available — treat present harm as a side-constraint while choosing among the remaining options by far-future expected value, or count far-future considerations only when the intervention is also robustly good now — and does any such compromise survive scrutiny, or is it just a refusal to choose between the theory and the intuition?
