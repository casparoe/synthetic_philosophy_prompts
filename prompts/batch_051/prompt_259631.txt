Consider the well-known post-war German case that H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller both discuss: in 1944 a woman denounced her husband to the Nazi authorities for remarks he had made about Hitler; he was sentenced to death but the sentence was not carried out, and he was sent to the Eastern front. After the war the woman was prosecuted for causing his unlawful deprivation of liberty. The court convicted her, holding that the Nazi statute under which the husband had been punished was contrary to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings, and that it therefore had never been valid law. I want this episode analyzed as a test case for the Hart–Fuller debate, and I want the analysis to pivot on a specific passage in Hart’s 1958 article Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals, where he discusses this case. In that passage Hart argues that the court could have reached the same result without denying the statute legal validity: it could have said that the statute was valid law but too iniquitous to be applied or obeyed, and that such a treatment would have kept the question of what the law is separate from the question of what it ought to be. He treats the court’s appeal to conscience and justice as an instance of the very confusion positivism helps avoid.

With that passage as the pivot, I want a careful case analysis that runs in both directions. What does Hart’s positivism say the court actually faced and what should it have said? What does Fuller’s reply in Positivism and Fidelity to Law say the case shows, particularly his claim that the Nazi legal system failed the internal morality of law and that this failure bears on whether it counted as law at all? Where is Hart’s reading of the episode strongest, and where does it strain? And what does the episode ultimately do to the separation thesis—does it confirm it, expose an anomaly, or show that the thesis was built for calmer cases and never anticipated a problem like this? Keep the mapping disciplined: say who or what is being treated as a legal system here, what the court’s options and justifications were, and where each theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
