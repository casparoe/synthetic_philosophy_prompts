Exam question:

In Chapter 9 of Aristotle's On Interpretation (De Interpretatione), Aristotle considers the statement that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. (a) Set out the reasoning that leads from the principle of bivalence, understood as the principle that every statement is either true or false, to the conclusion that every future event happens of necessity. (b) Explain how the view that future contingent statements are neither true nor false is supposed to block that reasoning. (c) Identify one serious difficulty for this view, and briefly assess whether it can be met.

Student answer:

Aristotle's sea battle argument is aimed at the fatalist idea that the future is already fixed. The reasoning runs as follows. Take the statement that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. Because bivalence says every statement is true or false, this statement must already be true or already false. If it is true, then it has been true all along that the battle would take place, and the battle cannot fail to take place; if it is false, then it has been false all along, and the battle cannot take place. Either way, the outcome is settled before it happens. Since that would make deliberation pointless, Aristotle rejects the law of excluded middle for future contingents. Future contingent statements are neither true nor false until their subject matter is settled; they lack a truth-value. This restores our freedom, because statements about the future no longer fix the future in advance. The main cost is that the view treats the future differently from the past and the present, whose statements do have determinate truth-values. But this asymmetry is harmless: once a future event occurs, the corresponding statement becomes true or false, so no statement is permanently without a truth-value.
