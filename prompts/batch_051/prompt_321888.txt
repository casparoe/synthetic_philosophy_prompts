ok so i've been collecting my own reactions to a bunch of moore-type sentences and i want to know if there's a single principle behind them, or close to one. here's where my gut lands:

1. 'it's raining, but i don't believe it is' — awful to say, even though it's not a contradiction and could easily be true about me.
2. 'it's raining, but sarah doesn't believe it is' — totally fine.
3. 'it rained yesterday, but i didn't believe it would' — fine, just hindsight.
4. 'it rained yesterday, but i don't believe it did' — as bad as 1, so it's not that the pastness of the content does the work.
5. 'it will rain tomorrow, but i don't believe it will' — also as bad as 1.
6. 'it's raining, but i don't know it is' — maybe even worse than 1.
7. 'it's raining, but i'm not sure it is' — only mildly off; i could imagine saying it on decent evidence that falls short of certainty.
8. 'it's raining, but i believe it isn't' — clearly worse than 1, like i'm admitting i'm saying the opposite of what i think.

my half-formed idea is that in the bad cases i'm asserting p while simultaneously announcing i'm not in the state that asserting p is supposed to show — but i can't say whether that state is belief, knowledge, or something weaker, and i can't see what rule makes 3 fine and 4 bad without it just being a rule about the tense of 'believe.'

what's the best principle that gets all or most of these, what does it predict about 'it's raining, but i have no evidence that it is' and 'it's raining, but i won't believe it tomorrow,' and which of my verdicts does it force me to overturn?
