I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I'm teaching a 90-minute undergrad seminar on social choice next week — 14 students, mostly third-years, who have seen majority rule and maybe Borda but have never met Arrow. I don't want to lecture the theorem at them. I want an activity where they try to design a fair group-decision procedure themselves and run face-first into the impossibility, before I ever say the word Arrow.

My rough idea: split the group into a rotating "constitution committee" and a bunch of voters. The decision is where the class retreat should be — mountains, beach, or city — so people can have basically any ranking and nobody has to be an expert. The committee has to write a voting rule that turns any list of individual rankings into a group ranking (or at least a winner). They only hear the desiderata in plain language: the rule has to work for any possible set of rankings; if everyone prefers X to Y the group should too; the group's ranking of X vs Y should only depend on how people rank X against Y, not on how they feel about some third option; and no one person should always get their way. Then we run profiles — index cards with rankings — and the voters watch there own rule tie itself in knots: cycles, deadlocks, someone accidentally becoming a dictator, Borda and runoff each breaking in its own way. Tokens are the payoff: each student gets tokens, and the committee's rule determines which retreat actually get booked, so people care but I'm not paying them.

What I need from you is the actual thing: rules, materials, the exact timeline for 90 minutes, the profile sets to use (and in what order), and a debrief that maps the moments back onto the four conditions. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time. I can already see two problems. One, the committee will try to dodge cycles with a supermajority rule or a status-quo default, and I need a way to handle that that doesn't feel like the game was rigged. Two, and this is the bigger one for me: I don't know how to make independence of irrelevant alternatives feel like a fairness condition instead of a technicality. Students usually think, why shouldn't the presence of a third option matter? I want the game mechanics to punish violations of IIA in a way they feel, not just me telling them afterwards. And be honest about which part of Arrow can't be staged: my suspicion is that the activity can show that all the obvious rules fail but can't show that every possible rule fails, so the proof-level generality has to come from me at the end. Say if that's wrong.
