Last night a friend and I argued for an hour about a rescue case, and I came away no longer sure that the pieces of my Scanlonian view fit together. I am not sure where the fault lies. Before I present the view in a seminar, I want a consistency audit of the following commitments:

(a) An act is wrong just in case no set of principles that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement would permit it.

(b) The individualist restriction: the lesser complaints of several people may not be added together to outweigh the stronger complaint of a single person.

(c) If I can save either one stranger's life or two strangers' lives, and all else is equal, I must save the two; flipping a coin would be wrong.

(d) Consider the case my friend pressed: you can rescue one person, or instead rescue a second person whose rescue would, as a side effect, allow an enormous number of fans to watch a match. You must rescue the person whose life is at stake; the fans' enjoyments cannot be summed to outweigh one life.

(e) The principle I want behind both (c) and (d): numbers count only among complaints that, taken one at a time, are of roughly comparable seriousness to the complaint they compete with; numbers do not count when each individual complaint is drastically weaker than its rival.

Which of these are genuinely in tension? Which merely look that way? Rank them by severity.

  - "commitments": the letter labels involved,
