Suppose we adopt these working criteria for an archive: (1) the items are records that have been selected for keeping under some policy, not merely accumulated; (2) they are arranged or indexed so that particular items can be found later; (3) some person or body with authority over the records decides what enters, what is destroyed, and who may consult them; and (4) they are kept with the aim of outlasting their original use, so that they can serve as evidence about the past rather than only as tools for the present. Now take a large content-recommendation platform. It logs every interaction—views, pauses, skips, searches, reports—and keeps the logs for years, indexed by user, item, and timestamp; a data-governance team sets retention rules, deletes some categories, and can retrieve individual sessions on request. The platform periodically retrains a predictive model on these logs. Most interactions survive only as changes in the model's weights, which are not searchable as records, though they demonstrably encode aggregate patterns of past behavior. Nothing was selected for preservation as such; the logs are kept because they make prediction more accurate and help resolve disputes, and the model is kept because it produces engagement. Yet what any user is offered today is a function of what earlier users were recorded doing, and a future investigator will struggle to say where the boundary is between the record, the system built from the record, and the way the system now shapes what gets recorded. Is this system an archive, or does calling it one stretch the concept past its point? Work through each criterion and say what your verdict depends on. Where the case fails a criterion, say whether the failure is in the case or in the criterion. Then identify the silences of this source: who and what never gets recorded in it, and whether, if it is not an archive, those silences are any less powerful for that.
