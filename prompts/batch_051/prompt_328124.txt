At my church's Tuesday-night Bible study we got stuck on the passage where Paul tells the women at Corinth to cover their heads when they pray or prophesy, and one of the men basically shut the whole thing down with "that was just cultural." Five minutes later he was treating a different Pauline instruction about how the congregation should run as plainly binding today, no "cultural" caveat in sight. I'm not trying to argue one side; I honestly can't tell what work that little phrase is doing as a claim. I want you to lay out the distinct precise theses someone might be asserting when they say "that was cultural" about a biblical command — e.g., that the command was originally addressed to a specific audience, that its underlying rationale doesn't apply now, that following it today would actually be wrong, that it's a culturally-situated expression of a still-binding principle, or something else. For each candidate reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say which readings would actually license dropping the head-covering instruction while keeping the other Pauline commands; I suspect some readings do too much or too little, but I can't make that precise myself.

The thing I keep circling back too: people reach for the same phrase with the food laws in Leviticus but get awfully quiet about the New Testament's instructions to slaves, which look about as woven into the culture of the time as anything in the head-covering passage. Does the reading that handles head coverings force you to handle the slavery passages the same way? If not, what principle lets you stop, and is that principle one someone could defend without already having the conclusion they want? Please don't just give me your favorite interpretation — I want the menu laid out so I can take it back to the group. Answer as a professor who does both philosophy of language and biblical hermeneutics, and maybe start by brainstorming candidate readings before you assess them. Also, wherever the field is split, I'd like you to say so plainly: mark which claims are settled and which are genuinely contested among scholars, and don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
