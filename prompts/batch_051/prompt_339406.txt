Longtime reader of LessWrong and adjacent rationalist forums here, so I know the standard vocabulary; the trouble is I may be misapplying it. I have a self-imposed decision rule for high-stakes personal choices made under what I take to be ignorance rather than risk: for each option, I write down the worst realistic outcome and choose the option whose worst case is least bad, refusing to count best-case or typical-case arguments unless genuine probabilities can be defended for them. Most recently this led me to decline a startup offer and remain in a stable job, solely because the startup's worst case was worse, even though its best case was much better. I adopted the rule after noticing that I systematically overestimate the probability of outcomes I find attractive, and I treat my own best-case reasoning as contaminated. I defend the rule by appeal to maximin and to Rawls's argument that parties behind a veil of ignorance would choose principles to protect the worst off. A friend who has read Harsanyi objects that Rawls intended maximin as a rule for choosing principles of justice, not as a rule for ordinary life, and that behind the veil rational parties would maximize expected utility, not protect against the worst case; he also says that if probabilities are genuinely unknown, minimax regret is a better criterion than maximin. My reply is that when probabilities are genuinely unavailable, expected utility is not even well defined. How can I tell whether I am invoking maximin soundly or merely rationalizing pessimism in decision-theoretic costume? Under what conditions should someone with my known over-optimism use maximin, minimax regret, or expected value in personal choices, and is refusing to weigh unquantified best cases defensible or just risk-aversion dressed up as a principled rule?
