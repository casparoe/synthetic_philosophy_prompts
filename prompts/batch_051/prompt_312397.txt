I spent four decades as an analytical chemist and only seriously started reading philosophy after I retired, so forgive me if I get the jargon wrong. Lately I've been chewing on what adult children owe their parents, and I've come to think the word "owe" is doing alot of unexamined work. I read somewhere — Jane English, I think, maybe you can tell me if I'm garbling her — that a grown child shouldn't be viewed as owing parents the way a debtor owes a creditor, because parental sacrifices aren't a loan that has to be repaid. Her idea, as I understood it, is that what grown children owe their parents looks much more like what friends owe each other: it arises out of the ongoing relationship itself, not out of a ledger of past services rendered.

So here is what I'd like. Give me a working definition of "filial obligation" — that is, a definition of what it means for an adult child to owe a parent something — that gets the following cases right. I've collected these from people I know:

1. A neighbor whose parents paid her entire way through college and graduate school, after which she moved abroad and now calls home twice a year. Her parents have asked her to help cover unexpected medical bills. Does she owe them that?
2. A man whose father beat him regularly as a child. The father is now old, alone, and asking to move in with him. Does he owe his father anything?
3. My own parents were competent but not warm — they fed, clothed, and housed me and not much more. Now they're retired and keep hinting that I should pay for a major home renovation. I honestly can't tell whether I owe this.
4. A colleague has a parent in early dementia who needs someone to take over her finances and visit every week. The two have always been civil but never close, and my colleague has young kids who need a lot of her right now.
5. A friend was raised almost entirely by an aunt who was not her legal guardian, while her biological mother was absent and contributed nothing. The aunt now needs care, and the biological mother has reappeared and also needs care. Does she owe either of them, and is the answer different for the two?
6. A father gave up a promising career to raise his three kids. He now expects one of them to turn down a job on the other side of the country so she can live near him and provide support as he ages.

For what it's worth, the first case feels like a clear yes to me and the second feels like a clear no, and the rest Im genuinely lost on. Run your definition through all six cases, tell me where it gives a surprising answer or no answer at all, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. And I'd like to know whether the friendship model I described actually survives contact with these cases, or whether some of them show it's too thin.
