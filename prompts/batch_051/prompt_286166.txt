I recently started working in a benefits office, and I have been trying to make sense of how conditionality actually operates. I have coined a distinction and want it stress-tested. "Substantive deservingness" would be a judgment about whether a claimant is genuinely trying, in need, or making a real effort. "Procedural deservingness" is different: it treats a claimant as deserving when they keep up with administrative demands—attending appointments, submitting job-search logs, replying to letters—regardless of what those behaviors actually reveal. A claimant who attends every appointment while doing no serious job search is treated as compliant, while a claimant with obvious need who misses one appointment is sanctioned. My working theory is that most welfare conditionality has drifted into procedural deservingness, and that its real function is less to separate the deserving from the undeserving than to ration by ordeal: aid remains available, but it must be bought with time, compliance, and exposure, so that only the sufficiently desperate persist. I want a candid assessment of whether the distinction holds or collapses once you ask how any substantive judgment could be administered without becoming procedural in this way, whether the "ordeal" claim overstates things or mistakes a clumsy attempt at substantive judgment for something more calculating, and whether either idea is already familiar under a different name. Whatever verdict you reach, please also say what argument or consideration would change your mind about it, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
