I moved back to my hometown last year after it lost another chunk of its population, and I've been sitting through city council meetings where they treat every new resident as a win. Around the same time my sister had her fourth kid, and my brother—the argumentative one—insisted these are the same attitude: when a life worth living gets added and no one else is made worse off, things are not worse. I told him that was fine for a family but it can't hold forever, so he wrote out the following argument and told me to find the flaw. I cant find it.

(1) If a family gains a child whose life is worth living, and no one else in the family is made worse off by the child's existence, then the family is no worse off than before. That just seems to be what we mean when we call a birth good news.
(2) Nothing about (1) depends on anything special about families. The feature doing the work is: an extra person with a life worth living, with no one else made worse off. So for any population—a city, a country, the whole species—adding such a person leaves things no worse.
(3) If a population contains one group living extremely well and another group living barely above neutral, then a population in which the same total amount of well-being is divided equally among the same number of people is no worse, and plausibly better. At least the disparity is gone, and no total well-being is lost.
(4) "No worse than" chains across steps: if each move in a series is no worse than the previous one, the endpoint is no worse than the starting point.

Now he runs the machine:

(5) Start with a million people, all with excellent lives. Add a billion people whose lives are just barely worth living, and suppose no one else is affected. By (2), that combined population is no worse than the original. By (3), a population with all 1,001,000,000 people sharing the same total well-being equally is no worse than that. In that new population, everyone's life is only a hair above "barely worth living," because the original excellent total got spread over a billion people.
(6) Repeat the loop. From any population, add a much larger group with lives just barely worth living (by (2), no worse), then redistribute the enlarged total equally (by (3), no worse). Each round pushes everyone's level closer to "barely worth living" while the population grows.
(7) So by (4), after enough rounds you reach an unimaginably large population in which every single person has a life only just worth living, and this population is no worse than the original million excellent lives. And you can run the same loop from any starting size, so the logic applies species-wide.

But that's repugnant. It says my council's boosterism and my sister's new baby are the thin end of the wedge toward a world of trillions of lives barely worth living being no worse than a small world of wonderful lives. That can't be right—or if it is, "worth living" is doing much less than I thought.

I suspect he's reinvented the Repugnant Conclusion but dressed it up in ordinary family-and-civic judgments, which is why it gets under my skin. I'd most like to keep (1) and (4); I could imagine rejecting (3), since "same total, more equal" has always sounded too neat to me, but then I'm not sure which step actually breaks first or how much of our ordinary pro-birth and pro-growth attitudes survive the repair.

Please think through this carefully. You're a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics. First brainstorm at least three candidate places where the argument could fail; then say what rejecting each candidate would cost; then tell me which step I should reject, or whether the conclusion is less absurd than it seems. Write the final answer in American English.
