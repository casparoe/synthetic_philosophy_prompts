I've been going back and forth with a friend who took one epistemology class and now thinks he can settle anything with a slogan. Every time I press him on why past uniformities give us any reason to expect future ones — why all the green emeralds we've seen make it rational to expect the next one will be green, or why the sun having risen every day so far should matter for tomorrow — he just says "the future will resemble the past" and looks at me like the conversations over. But I've been reading about Goodman's grue, and it seems like "resembles" is doing an awful lot of work there: if you define grue as "observed before some future time t and green, or not observed before t and blue," then every emerald we've seen so far is equally grue and equally green, so the past "resembles" both futures. I want to hand him a breakdown rather than just say "that's vague." What are the distinct precise claims that sentence could be expressing, from near-tautological readings ("in some respect or other, the future will be like the past") to strong uniformity-of-nature readings? For each reading: is it trivially true, substantive but defensible, or just false? And which reading, if any, would actually license expecting the next emerald to be green rather than grue — and is that the reading he's probably reaching for when he says it?
