Okay, a math department I know is floating a new exam format for proof-based courses and I want to know how it would actually play out before I vote on it.

The basic rule: every solution has to be written with a justification in the margin for each non-obvious line, like "by the theorem that a continuous function on a compact interval attains its maximum" or "definition of limit, applied with epsilon = delta/3." If a line has no justification, or the justification doesn't actually license the step, the line gets zero points even if the computation is right. You can only use justifications from a fixed list of permitted moves handed out at the start of the course — maybe 30 definitions, theorems, and lemmas. Anything not on the list has to be proved on the spot as a labelled lemma before you use it. The final grade for a problem is the smaller of your computation score and your justification score, so a fully correct answer with three unjustified steps scores like three wrong answers. Exams are open-book and open-notes, and the problems are deliberately designed to be close to homework but wrong in exactly one assumption, so the homework's reasoning doesn't transfer.

What does this actually incentivize? Does it reward understanding, or just a new kind of procedural fluency where students memorize which permitted move goes with which surface feature? How do students game it — canned lemmas proved up front and then applied to everything, justifications vague enough that a tired grader accepts them, citing a theorem that sounds relevant but doesn't actually apply? Where does it fail for genuinely strong students and for weak ones? And can you patch the obvious failure modes without drifting back into ordinary timed problem-solving?
