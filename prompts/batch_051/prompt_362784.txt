Philosophical treatments of the ethics of influence often draw a distinction along roughly the following lines. Legitimate persuasion engages the recipient's rational capacities: it offers arguments or evidence that the recipient could in principle assess and accept or reject on the merits. Manipulation, by contrast, bypasses or subverts those capacities; it secures compliance or belief through non-rational channels such as flattery, fear, habit, or cognitive bias, typically without the recipient's awareness that this is happening. The distinction therefore depends, among other things, on a contrast in mechanism (reason-respecting versus reason-bypassing) and to some extent on the influencer's aim.

Consider now an engagement-optimising system — for example, a ranking algorithm for a feed, an interface engineered around variable rewards, or a conversational model trained to prolong interaction — that is continuously adjusted on the basis of behavioural data. Such a system need not settle on a single strategy of arguing or bypassing. It may present reason-shaped content when that maximises engagement and switch to emotionally charged or bias-exploiting content when that works better, even alternating repeatedly for a single user. The system's designers may intend only the aggregate metric, not the particular mode of influence applied at any moment.

Does the standard manipulation/persuasion distinction survive this development? Lay out the premise or premises on which the distinction depends, explain precisely how the engagement-optimising case bears on them, and then assess whether the distinction can be repaired or relocated — for instance, in the designers' intentions, in the asymmetry of information between the influencing system and the influenced user, in the user's capacity to understand how their attention is being shaped, or elsewhere. Where such a repair fails, indicate whether the distinction collapses or becomes one of degree rather than kind.
