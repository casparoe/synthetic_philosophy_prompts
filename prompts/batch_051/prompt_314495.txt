I teach a small philosophy of value seminar, and I've ended up defending—maybe more firmly then I expected—the claim that all-things-considered betterness is transitive. The undergrads keep bringing spectrum arguments: line up a long chain of outcomes, each one only a tiny bit worse than the one before, so you're inclined to say no individual step makes things worse, yet the first outcome is obviously better than the last (mild discomfort at one end, a year of agony at the other). The thought is supposed to be that "not worse than" fails to be transitive in such cases, and then maybe "better than" does too, or that our whole notion of betterness comes apart. I already have the standard replies down—the sorites/vagueness move, denying the pairwise judgments, saying the comparison class shifts along the chain—so I don't need those again.

What I want is a map of my actual exposure. What would have to be true, or what kind of case or formal result would have to show up, for someone genuinely committed to transitivity to change their mind? I'm asking which parts of the view are load-bearing: the distinction between "better than" and "at least as good as," the differences between betterness of outcomes, options, and lives, the behavior of betterness when you aggregate across people. Are there results in adjacent areas—intransitive cycles in pairwise choice, majority rule, incomparability or vagueness in value—that should force the issue rather than just nag at it? Tell me which objections I should concede count against the view even while I keep it, which ones would be fatal if they succeeded, and which ones I can dismiss as the same spectrum argument in fancier dress. And be straight with me: is my position so hedged at this point that nothing could touch it, and should that itself worry me?
