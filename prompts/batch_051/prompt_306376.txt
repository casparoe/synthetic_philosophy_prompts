Act as a philosopher of technology familiar with debates about artifact politics and technological determinism. I am an undergraduate in a course on technology, politics, and society, and I have developed a rule for deciding how seriously to take claims that a technical system has its own politics. My rule is this: I accept that politics is built into an artifact only if the political effect would survive substantial changes in the surrounding social arrangements—for example, if the same bridge, software system, or factory layout still excludes, ranks, or coercively shapes people under different property rules, workplace cultures, or legal regimes. If the political result depends mostly on how the system is used, on the institutions around it, or on the incentives of its owners, I classify it as a context-dependent consequence rather than as politics built into the artifact. I also require an explanation to point to a specific feature of the artifact doing the political work, not just to the system's general existence. Is this rule rationally defensible as a way of distinguishing genuine artifact politics from overbroad technological determinism? Where might it produce false negatives—for example, technologies whose politics work by inviting or coordinating a particular social order rather than forcing one—and can the rule be restated to keep its advantages without those costs? Think carefully and consider some edge cases before assessing the overall policy.
