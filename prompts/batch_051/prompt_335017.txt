I am enrolled in a political philosophy seminar on Mill and nineteenth-century liberalism. Next week I present a paper defending a particular reading of the third chapter of On Liberty. My thesis is that when Mill calls for "experiments in living," his core argument is not that individual self-expression has intrinsic value, nor that nonconformity is good for its own sake, but that a society needs a varied stock of tried ways of life if it is to distinguish beneficial modes of life from harmful ones, and that this requires protecting at least the early, low-cost stages of departure from custom. I take the epigraph Mill places at the head of the book, quoting Wilhelm von Humboldt's The Sphere and Duties of Government, as the key to this reading:

"The grand, leading principle, towards which every argument unfolded in these pages directly converges, is the absolute and essential importance of human development in its richest diversity.—Wilhelm von Humboldt: Sphere and Duties of Government."

On my reading, the phrase "development in its richest diversity" is a requirement on a society's collective deliberative resources rather than a celebration of individual uniqueness for its own sake, and I want to insist that Mill's "experiments" metaphor is not rhetorical decoration but the engine of the argument.

The audience is my seminar group: the instructor is a political philosopher who works on autonomy and paternalism and is sympathetic to Mill but wary of romanticism about the self; two classmates are graduate students in moral psychology who know the conformity literature and will hear "experiments" as an empirical claim about how people actually update their way of life; one is a communitarian who thinks Mill starts from a false anthropology of self-standing individuals; and one is a conservative-leaning student who worries that experiments in living are really just experiments in eroding useful traditions. I know they will push back, but I have not done a structured Q&A prep. Please give me a prep sheet for the discussion: the objections I should expect, in roughly the order they will come, phrased as that specific audience would phrase them, each with what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is softest; include the objection I am hoping nobody asks and say why it is dangerous. Also tell me whether the Humboldt passage, as I am using it, actually bears the weight I place on it, or whether it tells more strongly against my reading than I think.
