You're a careful historian of philosophy, especially good on Hobbes's actual mathematical practice and not just his political reputation. Please think through this really carefully, starting by making the analogy precise before you evaluate it.

I'm reading a survey of early modern philosophy, and the author keeps leaning on an analogy they attribute to Hobbes. They say Hobbes thought geometry and civil philosophy are deeply analogous because in both we construct the object ourselves: in geometry we draw the lines and figures, and in politics we make the commonwealth through our own covenants. So the same kind of certainty you get in geometry should be available for political conclusions, as long as you start from the right definitions. The author presents this as a profound insight, not just a rhetorical flourish—it's supposed to explain why Hobbes thought he could turn political philosophy into a genuine science.

I'm not sure the analogy can carry that much. Please treat this as an analogy audit. What maps onto what? In Hobbes's picture, who or what plays the role of the geometer when we switch to the commonwealth? Where exactly does the construction happen in each case? Then identify the disanalogies that matter: for example, in geometry the maker seems to have full control over the constructed object, but in political life we don't make ourselves from scratch and we're also parts of the thing being made. Also, does the analogy quietly assume that whenever you make something, you have complete knowledge of it? Is that assumption defensible, even for Hobbes? Finally, tell me whether Hobbes could still get his conclusion—that political philosophy is demonstrable in a geometric way—once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the real argumentative work all along. Maybe also say if there's a better framing of what Hobbes should have said instead.
