Consider a jurisdiction whose legal arrangements include the following, of a kind recognizable in several Confucian-influenced East Asian legal systems. An indigent elderly person may sue their adult children for a monthly maintenance payment; a tribunal first attempts mediation, and if mediation fails it can issue a support order against any child with sufficient means. The same legal system treats an assault upon a parent as a more serious offense than the same assault upon a stranger, and it recognizes no comparable duty of a well-off citizen to support an unrelated indigent elderly person. At the same time, the system declares all citizens equal before the law.

What theory of care, or of the structure of moral obligation, does this combination of rules presuppose? Two classical positions in Chinese philosophy bear directly on the question. Confucian graded love holds that care properly begins in the family and thins as relationships become more distant, so that duties to parents genuinely outweigh duties to strangers; the graded character is not a defect to be overcome but the root from which wider benevolence is extended. Mozi's impartial care, by contrast, holds that partiality toward one's own family, clan, or state is the root of social harms, and that one should regard other people's families and persons as one's own; on his view this does not abandon one's parents but is what ensures they will be cared for, because others will care for your parents as you care for theirs.

Does the legal arrangement strictly presuppose something like Confucian graded love, or is that merely one theory that fits it while even Mozi's impartial care could license the same practice? Distinguish what the rules presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then spell out what a thoroughgoing policy built on Mozi's impartial care would plausibly look like, and which specific features of the present legal arrangement would have to change under it.
