I'm not a philosopher, but I've been stuck on something for a while and it keeps me up at night. Take the thought that the kettle is on the stove. There are three pieces: the idea of the kettle, the idea of being on, and the idea of the stove. But the thought itself is one thing — it can be true or false — and a pile of three pieces isn't one thing, it's just a pile. So something has to bind the pieces together. Only, the moment I picture the binding as a fourth piece, I need a fifth piece to bind the binding to the first three, and then a sixth, and it never stops. A friend who took some philosophy classes says this is a known dead end and that people have a standard way out, but he wouldn't tell me wether he actually buys the way out, which made me suspicious.

So here's what I want. Build me a thought experiment to my specs, set in an ordinary place (a kitchen, a ticket counter, a bus stop), one paragraph, no fancy terminology. It should show the pieces by themselves, then something being added as the binder, then the exact step where that binder starts needing its own binder. It should also be set up so the usual escape — saying the binder isn't the same kind of thing as the pieces and dosen't need binding — ends up looking like it just renames the unity it was supposed to explain. After the scenario, do the checklist: what the original pieces are, what the first binder is, where the staircase kicks in, why the special non-piece binder move doesn't stop the staircase but moves the problem, and the one feature of the case doing the real work. Then, honestly, tell me how someone who thinks the pieces already fit together, no binder needed, would attack the case — and whether the whole thing only works because it assumes from the start that a thought is made of pieces. I need to know if I've been chasing a ghost.
