I'm writing a position paper for my school's constitutional law seminar on whether a constitution should make some provisions difficult to amend, and I've ended up with a set of commitments that I want checked before I commit them to paper. The background is that I was initially drawn to the idea that a constitution is a kind of Ulysses contract: the citizenry binds itself in advance against its own predictable episodes of fear, partisanship, and short-term thinking, much as an individual might rationally bind her own future self. That idea seemed to license quite strong entrenchment, but I also want to remain a democrat, and I don't think those two desires should be incompatible.

Here is the position I have arrived at, stated as six commitments:

1. Ulysses contracts are rational for individuals, at least when a person reasonably anticipates that her own future judgment will be distorted by temptation, fear, or exhaustion, and she is better placed before the situation to decide what should be done.
2. A constitution is best understood, in part, as a people's collective Ulysses contract: it precommits future majorities against foreseeable failures of judgment.
3. For a constitution to perform that function, some provisions, such as entrenched basic liberties and the amendment procedure itself, must be genuinely difficult to change, even when a current majority wants to change them.
4. Democratic legitimacy nonetheless requires that any constitutional provision be revisable by the citizenry through some duly specified procedure; otherwise the constitution is not self-government but a dead hand.
5. Judicial review is legitimate exactly insofar as courts enforce these precommitments against ordinary legislation, and illegitimate when courts impose their own views about policy or rights.
6. The fact that later generations never consented to these arrangements is not, by itself, a decisive objection: we are all born into countless unchosen but beneficial arrangements, and we accept them partly because they can be altered over time.

I don't see, at least not clearly, which of these commit me to something I should reject, but I suspect the set is not entirely clean. I would like an audit rather than a lecture on the topic. Please identify which commitments are actually in tension, which only look as though they are, and rank the tensions by severity. For each real tension, tell me what would have to give, and what the least costly revision would cost.
