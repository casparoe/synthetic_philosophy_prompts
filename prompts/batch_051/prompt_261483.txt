I've been trying to get clear on whether causation is really in the world or whether Russell was onto something when he said it's a leftover from a bygone age that only survives because it seems harmless. I built the following argument and I can't tell weather it goes through, or where exactly it breaks if it doesn't. Here it is:

(1) The fundamental laws of physics are time-reversal symmetric: if a process is physically allowed, the reverse process is physically allowed too. (2) Causation is one-way: causes bring about effects, and the cause comes before the effect. (3) So if causation were a fundamental feature of the world, its one-way direction would have to be built into fundamental physics. (4) But time-reversal symmetric laws can't, on their own, give you a one-way direction at the fundamental level. (5) Therefore causation isn't fundamental. It's something we read into the world, or at best a large-scale phenomenon that shows up because the universe began in a special low-entropy condition.

Is this valid? If not, name the step that fails and the reading of it. I half-suspect step (5) is sneaking in a false either-or, but I can't get the objection in focus.
