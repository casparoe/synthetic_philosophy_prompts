You are a philosopher of cognitive science who is technically fluent in how large language models are trained and evaluated. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming at least three candidate experimental designs before settling on the strongest one or small battery. Be precise enough that the study could in principle be run, and be explicit about what each possible result would and would not establish.

A colleague of mine claims that today's language models are not merely interpolating within their training distribution. Occasionally, she says, a model makes a genuine intuitive leap: faced with a problem whose most obvious solution path stalls, the model re-describes the problem in a vocabulary that was not present in its training examples, and the re-description makes the answer nearly immediate. She calls this novelty beyond the training distribution, and she insists it is different in kind from emitting a sentence that simply does not occur verbatim in the corpus. Her examples are things like a model replacing an apparently quantitative puzzle with a symmetry argument, or abandoning a failed sequential procedure for a recursive one without being prompted to do either.

I suspect her terms are doing more work than her evidence supports, but I also think some weaker version of her claim is almost certainly true, and I do not want to settle the question by definitional fiat. I want to know what would actually count as evidence.

Your assignment is this. Restate her claim in its most testable form. Then design the best concrete test, or small battery of tests, of whether a model ever does something that deserves to be called an intuitive leap beyond its training distribution rather than fluent recombination of patterns already present in the data. For every outcome you specify, say what it would and would not show. It will help to separate three possible readings of her phrase: novelty of the output string, novelty of the solution route, and novelty of the problem frame. Say which of these a well-designed test can settle, and which it cannot. Finally, identify the residue that no experiment could settle even in principle, and briefly say whether that residue matters.
