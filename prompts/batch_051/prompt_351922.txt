I am an undergraduate serving as a student member of my university's Academic Integrity Committee, and I am taking a course this term on the philosophy of law in which we have been examining limitation periods and the question whether the passage of time itself, once the evidence is secure, weakens the moral case for a sanction. A case before the committee presses directly on that material, and I have been asked to help draft the decision memo.

The facts are these. Seven years ago, a student submitted a senior honours thesis that passed and contributed to their graduating with distinction. The university has recently digitised old theses, and a comparison has uncovered what appears to be extensive plagiarism: roughly two-fifths of the thesis is copied almost verbatim from three published books and one earlier thesis, without quotation or adequate attribution. The evidence is documentary and, under the code's definition of plagiarism, conclusive; there is no real dispute about what happened. At the time, a finding of this kind would have meant failure of the thesis and, in all likelihood, expulsion without the degree.

The former student is now seven years into a career in a field whose entry and ongoing licensing depend on holding the degree in question. There are no other academic-integrity or disciplinary matters on file. The university's academic integrity code contains no time limit: proceedings may be opened whenever a matter comes to light.

The committee must decide whether to open proceedings now, which could lead to revocation of the degree, or to decline to do so. I am less interested in the final yes or no than in the structure of the reasoning. Draft the decision memo, with a clear recommendation — proceed, decline, or some third course, such as a recorded finding that stops short of revocation — and address in particular:

- Which of the standard rationales for time limits actually apply when the evidence is as good as it will ever get: the decay of evidence and memory, the thought that the person before the committee now is not, in the relevant sense, the agent of the wrong, the value of finality and settled expectations, and the institution's own diligence in pursuing matters promptly.
- Whether revoking a degree obtained by fraud is better understood as punishment for a past act or as withdrawing an ongoing benefit that the person still holds, and why that classification should affect whether the delay counts against acting.
- The strongest consideration on the losing side, treated honestly rather than as a straw man.

The memo should be one the committee could actually adopt: an actionable bottom line, reasons a dissenting member would have to take seriously, and a reasoning chain that makes clear which steps are robust and which depend on contestable judgement.
