I have been thinking through, and have provisionally accepted, a version of the quantum immortality argument, and I want a rigorous accounting of what would count against it rather than another rehearsal of the usual objections. As I understand the position: if quantum mechanics is unitary and no collapse occurs, then for any process that might kill me there is a branch of the wavefunction in which a physically continuous, conscious successor of me survives, while the branches in which I die contain no subject who observes anything. From the first-person perspective, then, I should not expect to experience death from any event with a nonzero-amplitude survival outcome, and in a repeated quantum suicide setup the subject should expect to observe survival trial after trial no matter how small the Born-rule weight of the surviving branch becomes.

What I want is a map of the position's exposure. What would have to be true for it to be false, and which of those possibilities are live rather than merely logically possible? Identify the load-bearing premises: candidates include that some no-collapse interpretation is correct; that branch weights do not govern what I should expect from the inside in the way the Born rule governs what an outside observer should predict; that the branching observers are all me rather than mere copies or distinct persons; that my persistence requires only a functioning brain and not some further continuity that branching destroys; and that I am entitled to reason as though I will be whichever subject of experience survives. For each, say how the world would have to differ for it to fail and what would remain of the position if it did.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, where a fatal defeater is one the position's defenders would themselves have to accept as fatal once established, not one its critics already believe. Say which alleged objections they can dismiss as not actually bearing on the argument. Then give an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind, since it is a claim about what a subject should expect from the inside rather than an ordinary empirical prediction. The defeater I currently find most serious is the branch-weight problem; assess it candidly, but do not let my sense of it dictate your ranking.
