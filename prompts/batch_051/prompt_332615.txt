Next month I'm giving a seminar paper at an interdisciplinary AI lab whose attendees include mechanistic interpretability researchers, applied LLM engineers, and two philosophers of cognitive science, one of whom works on explanation. My thesis is that the chain-of-thought faithfulness debate has been run on a false dichotomy: traces are sorted into either faithful reports of internal reasoning or post hoc confabulation, when in fact a trace can be causally useful for auditing while misreporting the model's actual computational route, and can accurately report the route while being useless to an auditor. My argument runs roughly as follows. First, the standard intervention test—delete or negate a stated intermediate step and check whether the final answer changes—establishes only what I call interventionist faithfulness, and this is compatible with the model's having reached the answer by a different process that happens to be coupled to the verbalized steps under that intervention. Second, the same answer is often stable across mutually inconsistent traces produced under prompt rephrasings, which favors the hypothesis that traces function to make the answer legible rather than to make an antecedently real process visible. Third, for debugging and audit purposes we frequently need only a manipulation handle that is causally tethered to the output in the right restricted way, not a veridical record of computation; I therefore close by proposing that audit standards track counterfactual coherence rather than self-report accuracy.
