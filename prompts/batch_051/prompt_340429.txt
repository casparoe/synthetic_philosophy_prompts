I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about choosing when you have no preference, and my gut keeps giving different answers. Picking the left can of soup when the two cans are identical: obviously fine. Flipping a coin between two colleges my kid likes exactly equally, and treating the coin as binding: fine, even sensible. But someone who takes three weeks to decide between two job offers they insist are perfectly equal, until one offer lapses: I want to say that's a rational failure, not just bad luck—even though I also think they literally had no reason to pick one over the other. Buying the pricier of two shirts I'm otherwise indifferent between because "price must be tracking something I'm missing": that strikes me as a mistake, not a tie-breaker. And when a friend, genuinely torn between two equally attractive gigs, finally takes one and then starts saying it's the one they always kind of wanted, I don't think they're lying—I think the preference got created by the choice. Is there one principle about choice under indifference, stated precisely, that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about the case that actually set this off: an admissions committee I'm on deadlocked between two equally ranked candidates, where one member wants to flip a coin, another says we must find a further consideration, and a third says the deadlock itself shows our ranking was wrong? And which of my verdicts would the principle force me to overturn?
