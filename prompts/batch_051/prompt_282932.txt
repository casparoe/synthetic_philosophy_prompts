I've been reading around federalism and subsidiarity since I retired, and something puzzles me. Different countries all talk as if there is a principle that decisions should be made at the lowest workable level, but they carve things up in wildly different ways. The EU runs a common agricultural policy and harmonises product standards while leaving school systems and hospitals to the member states. The US mostly runs schools through local districts and leaves criminal law to the states, but Washington sets drug schedules for the whole country and preempts state law whenever it wants, more or less. Germany leaves policing and universities to the Länder but has one criminal code for the whole country. Switzerland gives the cantons and communes a huge amount of latitude, and still has a single code of obligations. I want to know what view of political legitimacy, or of why some decisions belong at one level rather than another, this hodgepodge actually presupposses. Does it presuppose that some subject matters are naturally local, that people nearer a problem know it better, that the deciding body should be the smallest one that includes everyone affected, or is it all just an empirical bet about outcomes? Reconstruct the implicit theory, be clear about what the practice strictly presupposes versus what merely fits, and say where you think the practice is just historical accident that a coherent theory would have to condemn. Then tell me what would concretely have to change in the systems above if one took a single one of those theories seriously — say, always assigning a matter to the smallest jurisdiction whose decision covers everyone affected. Please write in British English.
