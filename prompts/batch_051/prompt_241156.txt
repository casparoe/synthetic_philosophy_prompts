I have been trying to sort out where I stand on the doctrine of hell, and I keep coming back to a distinction I have started calling the Retributive Horizon. The idea is this: for any finite wrong, there is some finite amount of punitive harm that would fully pay the wrong back; beyond that horizon, further punishment no longer continues a proportionate response to the act, but becomes something else — a decision by the punisher to keep the wrongdoer in an open-ended condition of suffering. A single lie, for example, does not become more deserving the longer the punishment runs; past some point we are no longer paying the lie back but using the liar as a device for something other than retribution. On this account, the traditional picture of eternal conscious torment does not so much claim that a finite sin deserves an infinite, never-completed punishment as it quietly replaces retribution with something like sequestration, or the endless containment of a will that will not stop rebelling. I suspect this is why annihilationists and universalists tend to talk past defenders of eternal torment: the first group hears hell as an impossibly long sentence, while the defenders treat it as a state of the person rather than a debt being discharged in time, so the three positions are less rival accounts of one punishment than rival views about what may legitimately continue after the retributive horizon has already been reached.

I would like an honest assessment of whether the Retributive Horizon is a real distinction or just a metaphor doing argumentative work it has not earned. Is there an existing name for this idea, or a standard place where it has already been worked out? What does it get right, and where exactly does it break? In particular, does it beg the question against the traditional line that any sin against an infinitely great being is itself infinite in guilt, so that no finite retributive budget ever applies? Does it assume that punishment serves only one purpose, or that its purpose cannot permissibly shift once retribution is exhausted? And I would want to know what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about whether the distinction is coherent and does any work, and where your own reasoning about it is most likely to fail.
