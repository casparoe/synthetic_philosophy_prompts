I'm TAing an undergrad free speech course and we keep getting stuck on a case that the students (and honestly me too) are treating way too fast. In 1977 the National Socialist Party of America tried to hold a march through Skokie, Illinois, in full Nazi regalia — Skokie at the time had a very large population of Holocaust survivors. The town tried to stop it with an injunction and a few ordinances (a big insurance bond, a ban on military-style uniforms, a ban on material that incites group hatred), the ACLU ended up defending the Nazis' right to march, and the courts said Skokie couldn't ban them; in the end the group never actually marched in Skokie and held a small rally in Chicago instead. The line everyone reaches for is Mill's harm principle: you can only restrict liberty to prevent harm to others, and offense doesn't count, so the survivors just had to absorb the distress. But the more I think about it the less sure I am that this is what Mill's view actually says, or that the case fits the principle as neatly as the slogan suggests.

That's what I want you to work through — not the slogan but the actual mapping. What would the harm principle have told Skokie's officials they could permissibly do, and why? Is the survivors' psychological distress the kind of thing Mill means by harm to others, or is it offense with extra intensity, and does the answer depend on whether the principle is built on a prior account of interests or rights? Does the harm principle even apply cleanly to expression, or does Mill treat speech as a different kind of case — and where does a uniformed march through a neighborhood full of survivors sit on his line between protected opinion and incitement or harmful action? Then flip it around: what does Skokie do to the theory? I suspect it exposes how much work the word harm is secretly doing, and that the principle can't decide the case with out importing a whole theory of what people have a right to be protected from. Is that right, or does the harm principle come out of the episode looking stronger? And one more thing that nags me: does the fact that the march never actually happened in Skokie matter for the Millian analysis, or is that just a historical footnote?
