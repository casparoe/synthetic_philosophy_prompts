I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I keep getting dragged into the same argument with a friend from econ about epistocracy. His move is always the same analogy: you wouldn't let the passengers vote on how to fly the plane, and you wouldn't put major surgery to a show of hands in the waiting room, so why let ordinary voters decide trade policy, monetary policy, environmental regulation, or the rest? And for him it isn't just a rhetorical flourish — he actually wants the conclusion that restricting the franchise (or at least weighting votes by competence) is structurally the same as putting the pilot or surgeon in charge, because in each case you're deferring to whoever is best positioned to know what to do. I'm not sure it's that clean. I think the analogy quietly imports a bunch of features: that everyone agrees on the destination or the goal of the surgery, that the relevant expertise is uncontroversial and easy to certify, that the passengers all want roughly the same outecome so competence never has to be weighed against affected interests, and that the choice is just a matter of picking the right technical means from a pre-specified menu. But I also don't want to just anti-analogy my way out of a genuinely hard problem, since the principle that the people affected should have a say has its own well-known limits when the people affected don't know what they're deciding about.

Can you audit this for me? I want a correspondence table of what maps onto what in the pilot/surgeon picture as he's using it — the passengers, the pilot, the destination, the cockpit, the training, and so on. Then go through where the analogy holds up, where it breaks, and which of the disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he wants versus which are just cosmetic. After that, say whether the case for restricting or weighting the franchise survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work all along. And if there's a better analogy that captures whatever is right in his point without smuggling in the bad parts, I'd like that too.

Please structure it like this, with these section names and rough lengths. "The mapping" — a compact table plus a few sentences. "Where it holds" — one short paragraph. "Where it breaks" — the longest section, two or three paragraphs, flagging which breaks are load-bearing. "What the analogy smuggles in" — one paragraph, sorted by whether it matters for his argument. "Does the conclusion survive?" — lead with your bottom line, then one or two paragraphs. "A better analogy?" — one paragraph, or explain why none helps. Also, epistemically: mark which parts of this are settled among people who work on democratic theory and which are genuinely contested, and don't present the epistocrat or democrat side as the consensus when it isn't.
