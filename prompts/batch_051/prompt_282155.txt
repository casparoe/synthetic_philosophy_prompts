At a legal-theory reading group I attended last week, two regulars—Nadia, a criminal law scholar, and Peter, a jurisprudence scholar—got into a long argument about desuetude: the doctrine that a criminal statute can lose its force through long, open non-enforcement even though the legislature never repealed it. Nadia set things up with a concrete case. A town still has an 1897 ordinance making it a misdemeanor to operate any public amusement on Sunday. The ordinance has not been enforced in sixty years, and a newly appointed prosecutor has just charged a cinema owner under it. Here is the exchange, as close to verbatim as I can recall.

(1) Nadia: “The rule of law is about making state power predictable. A prohibition nobody has enforced in sixty years was not guiding anyone’s conduct—businesses opened, employees took shifts, landlords signed leases, families made plans, all on the assumption that Sunday operation was lawful. Punishing someone under it now is practically indistinguishable from retroactive punishment: the law was dead as a guide to behavior even if it was alive on the statute books.”

(2) Peter: “You are sliding from ‘the law was not guiding anyone’ to ‘the law is not valid,’ and that is the whole issue. The ordinance went through proper enactment and has never been repealed. If courts begin treating unenforced statutes as void, they are exercising a legislative power—deciding which laws are obsolete—in the guise of adjudication. Whatever else the rule of law says, it says the way to remove a duly enacted statute is through the body that enacted it or a body authorized to review it, not through a judge’s verdict that executive neglect has killed it.”

(3) Nadia: “But the rule of law is not exhausted by pedigree. It also requires generality and evenhandedness, and a dormant statute is a standing invitation to arbitrary enforcement. The new prosecutor is not going to cite every business that has violated this ordinance for decades; that campaign would strike everyone as absurd and cruel. So the law will be used selectively—against the cinema, or the next unpopular defendant. Desuetude is not a power grab; it is a defense of law’s impartiality, a way of telling the state it cannot resurrect an abandoned prohibition to single people out.”

(4) Peter: “That proves too much. Selective targeting is a risk under every broad statute now on the books, and the standard answer is a selective-prosecution or equal-protection challenge aimed at the prosecutor’s motive, not a rule that the underlying statute has expired. Worse, your doctrine rewards official lawlessness: the more blatantly an executive refuses to enforce a law, the stronger the claim that the law is dead. And it offends the very predictability you began with—nobody can say in advance how many years of disuse, or how much publicity, or how much reliance is enough to kill a statute.”

(5) Nadia: “Fuzzy boundaries are not fatal; the law is full of standards—reasonable notice, due process, estoppel—that courts apply without pretending they are exact. Desuetude should not be understood as an automatic clock that repeals a statute, but as an estoppel-like limit: where the state has long and openly acquiesced in conduct, and people have ordered their affairs around that acquiescence, the state cannot later treat that very conduct as criminal. The question is whether the reliance was reasonable, not whether some arbitrary number of years has passed.”

I was a bystander to this argument and have no stake in either side, but I have been turning it over since. Please adjudicate it point by point, without announcing a winner too early and without reducing it to “it depends.” For each numbered move, rate it as clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, or clearly right, and justify the rating. Then identify the deeper issue the two of them are circling without quite naming, and close with an overall verdict on which view is more defensible—and under what conditions you would reverse that verdict.
