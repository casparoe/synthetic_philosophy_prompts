I am a software developer, and a personal episode has made me unable to leave a philosophical problem alone. A close friend's psychiatric medication was recently changed, and the alteration in her mood, motivation, and reasoning was so rapid that I began to ask whether her thoughts and choices were still doing any causal work, or whether the neurochemistry was doing all the work while her mental life merely went along for the ride. I mentioned this to a colleague, who replied that the whole worry comes from a misunderstanding. His analogy has stayed with me: the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware. A payroll program causes the payments; a flight-control program causes the manoeuvres; nobody thinks the program is causally impotent merely because every physical event in the machine has a sufficient physical cause in the silicon. The causal exclusion argument — as I understand it, the argument that since every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, a mental cause distinct from its neural realiser would make behaviour systematically overdetermined, and so the mental cause must be excluded — is therefore, he says, a false competition between levels. He concluded that non-reductive physicalism is in the clear and that mental causation needs no rescue.

I have come to distrust the analogy more than I did at first, and I want it audited carefully. Please set out an explicit mapping: what corresponds to the software, the hardware, the source code, the execution trace, the inputs and outputs, the memory, the compiler, and the operating system? Then identify where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion — that mental events are causally efficacious without being reduced to physical events — from the disanalogies that are merely incidental. Which features of the source domain does the analogy quietly import? And in the end, does the colleague's conclusion survive the audit, or does the analogy smuggle in a kind of reduction or functionalism under the appearance of avoiding it? If you think the analogy fails at the crucial point, say whether a better analogy exists that could do the work he wanted it to do.

Please answer in British English.
