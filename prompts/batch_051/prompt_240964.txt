I have been working through the absent-minded driver problem, and I think I have found a distinction that the usual discussion slides over. I want to test whether it is real or whether I have just given a fancy name to something already in the literature.

I call the distinction one between a decision type and its decision tokens. In the standard two-exit version of the problem, a driver plans to exit with probability p whenever he is at an exit; if he exits at the first exit his payoff is 0, if he exits at the second his payoff is 4, and if he continues past both his payoff is 1. The plan is a decision type, because it is not addressed to a particular exit. But when the plan is executed, it produces two decision tokens: one at the first exit and one at the second. My claim is that the action-stage problem becomes confusing because it treats these tokens as exchangeable, as if they were two independent draws from a single distribution. They are not exchangeable. The second token exists only if the first token issues the instruction continue; its probability of being reached depends on the very plan being evaluated.

I have been calling the proposed remedy the Principle of Token-Responsiveness. It says: when an information set contains multiple decision tokens whose probabilities are influenced by the agent’s own strategy, the action-stage calculation should make those probabilities sensitive to the strategy, not treat them as fixed background facts. If the agent at an exit uses the strategy-consistent probability of being at the first token rather than an unqualified 1/2, the action-stage and ex ante calculations fall into line, and the apparent inconsistency in the absent-minded driver case dissolves. More generally, I suspect that any apparent conflict between ex ante and action-stage rationality in games with imperfect recall comes from pretending that token probabilities are exogenous when they are partly controlled by the plan.

I would like an honest assessment of this idea. Is the notion of a decision token doing genuine work, or is it merely redescribing ordinary Bayesian conditioning on one’s own strategy? Has the point already been made under another name? Where does it break—especially in cases in which multiple tokens are not causally nested, such as games with imperfect recall, the Sleeping Beauty problem, or repeated games with a single plan? Please give me the three strongest objections and tell me which, if any, are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. And please keep two things separate throughout: what is settled in this area, and what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic; do not present one side of a live dispute as if it were the consensus. British English, please.
