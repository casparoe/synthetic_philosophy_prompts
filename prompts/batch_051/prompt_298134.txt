I run the conformance program at an independent test laboratory that certifies hardware random number generators for gambling regulators, lottery commissions, and some cryptographic vendors. A vendor submits a device, its design notes, and a finite sample of its output — we require 10^8 bits and can request more. My job is a pass/fail decision plus a certificate saying exactly what has been certified. The stakes are asymmetric: a certified device that later turns out to be deterministic or predictable by an adversary destroys the regulator's confidence in us, while a wrongly rejected device costs a vendor real money and produces appeals. We evaluate about one submission a month, with one or two engineers who have no formal training in algorithmic information theory, over two weeks, and the certificate must be defensible to the regulator's technical committee, which contains statisticians but no philosophers.

The problem is that our current procedure is a battery of statistical tests — frequency, runs, longest-run, approximate entropy, a compression estimate — and we pass a device when its sample passes everything. I now think that answers the wrong question. A finite sequence, taken by itself, could be produced by any number of processes; any particular finite string, even a run of zeros, is exactly as probable under a fair coin as any other particular string of that length; and the decimal expansion of pi passes most standard tests while being generated by a trivial rule. So randomness cannot be a property of the finite string alone — it must be predicated of a process, or relative to a class of tests, or relative to an adversary — and our certificate ought to say which. But I cannot tell a regulator that the question is ill-posed and leave them without a decision.

Write the procedure my lab can actually run, reusable on every submission, not a memo about any one device. Give the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no rather than moving on; which steps involve judgment the theory cannot eliminate, marked as such; and what the final certificate is permitted to assert and not assert. Ground the procedure in what algorithmic randomness and the foundations of probability actually support — in particular, that algorithmic randomness is defined for infinite sequences relative to a class of tests, that any finite string has a short program roughly its own length, and that process randomness and product randomness come apart. Do not assume my engineers know this; translate it into rules and checks they can follow.

Then apply the procedure to three submissions:
(a) an avalanche-diode device whose sample passes every test, but whose design notes show a deterministic whitening stage that falls back to a fixed lookup table under conditions the lab cannot reproduce;
(b) a device that emits the decimal expansion of pi from a secret offset, with no stochastic component at all, whose vendor argues it cannot be statistically distinguished from a true random source;
(c) a coin-flipping machine whose sample contains a run of 128 heads and fails the current battery, but whose mechanism looks genuinely stochastic.
Include at least one case where the procedure forces your lab into a decision you would rather not issue. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot decide — where, if anywhere, the randomness of a process must be taken on trust, or where the decision is one of values rather than measurement.
