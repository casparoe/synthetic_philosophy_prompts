I teach an intro ethics class for non-majors at a community college — about 24 students, one 75-minute session. We’re about to read the chapter of Mill’s On Liberty on individuality, and last week when I brought up the idea that there’s something wrong with how much people just go along with whatever everyone else is doing, the whole discussion fell flat. Students kept saying that if someone wants to follow the crowd, that’s just their choice, so whats the problem. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of Mill’s point before I name it. There’s a passage near the start of that chapter where Mill says, roughly, that the free development of individuality is one of the chief ingredients of human well-being, and that custom is a standing obstacle to it; he also says people should be allowed to make experiments in living, and that a person who lets the world choose his plan of life for him isn’t really developing the capacities that make him human — he’s just imitating. The last bit is the thing I think they’d resist if they heard it stated outright, but I want them to run into it themselves rather than just argue with the text.

So: design a game or structured activity for the full 75 minutes. It can’t just be a discussion prompt or a handout; it should have rules, maybe roles, and a reason for students to act in ways that reveal the tension between doing what they actually want and doing what gets approval from the group. The point they should come away with is that social approval is not the same as genuinely choosing for yourself, and that losing the chance to try out ways of living is a real cost even if they’d end up choosing the conventional thing anyway. Then give me a debrief that maps what happened in the activity onto Mill’s argument, including the part about custom being a standing obstacle. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what part of the idea the activity can’t capture and I’ll have to supply myself.
