I got into a long argument with a friend about gazelle stotting and we realized that when we both said "costly signaling" we meant two different things. I thought the stot is honest because it's physically hard to fake—the jump is a readout of the gazelle's condition, so the cost is sort of beside the point. He thought the wastefulness itself is what makes it credible: the gazelle is flaunting surplus energy. Then someone brought up the peacocks tail, someone else said repeated interaction and receiver punishment can keep even cheap signals honest, and by midnight we had six positions and no idea how to sort them. I'm half-convinced I've been using the term wrong for years. I help teach a small philosophy-of-biology seminar with a mix of undergrads and zoology students, and I want a decision-tree-style quiz—"which account of honest signaling do you actually hold?"—that sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. Start from the commitment that at least some animal signals are honest (they reliably track something about the sender or the world), and branch on questions a student could answer without knowing the positions in advance, like: "Can a weaker individual produce the same signal at the same cost to itself?" or "Would the signal stay reliable if receivers stopped penalizing dishonest senders?" or "Does the receiver benefit from getting the information right?" I'd like seven or eight leaves. The leaves must include at least: (a) strong handicap, where the waste itself is the guarantee; (b) differential- or marginal-cost handicap, where the cost only matters because higher-quality signalers pay it more easily; (c) index/unfakeable signal, where reliability comes from a physical constraint linking signal to trait; (d) conventional signal kept honest by receiver retaliation or repeated interaction; (e) common-interest signal, where honesty holds because sender and receiver both want the information to get through; (f) sensory exploitation; and (g) Fisherian runaway.
