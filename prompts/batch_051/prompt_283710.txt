Imagine a medium-sized common-law country passes, effective next year, a single new rule of evidence: no court, coroner, or insurance tribunal may find that a miracle occurred on the basis of testimony unless the alternative explanation — that the testimony is false — would itself be more miraculous than the event reported. That's the whole change. It's deliberately a version of the maxim Hume defends, but now it's black-letter law. Normal rules about hearsay, experts, and cross-examination still apply; a preacher can testify that a healing happened, but unless the witnesses-being-wrong story is more miraculous than the healing, the tribunal must treat the healing as not established. Private belief is untouched — churches can preach what they like and people can believe them.

Predict, with your reasoning made explicit, how this country looks fifty years on. I want the chain of mechanisms, not a story: what happens first in the lower courts and insurance claims, how orgainzed religions adjust their public claims and legal strategies, what new professions or forms of testimony (if any) grow up around the threshold, and whether the rule ends up doing what its drafters wanted or something else. I don't much care whether you conclude the rule matters or quietly changes nothing; I care about the argument. Tell me which predictions you're most and least confident in, and what would have to be true for the whole trajectory to go differently.

After the prediction, give me a JSON object with these fields:
- "overall_verdict": a one-sentence summary of where the country ends up.
- "first_order_effect": the earliest predictable change, in one or two sentences.
- "most_confident_prediction": the prediction you'd bet on, with a one-sentence reason.
- "least_confident_prediction": a prediction you're including but wouldn't bet on, with a one-sentence reason.
- "key_mechanism": the single mechanism doing most of the explanatory work.
- "load_bearing_assumption": the assumption that, if it failed, would most change your overall prediction.
- "unintended_consequence": the effect the drafters probably didn't see coming.
