I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and while I am not a trained economist, I am fluent enough in law-and-economics to work with transaction costs, the Coase theorem, negative externalities, bilateral monopoly, property rights, nuisance rules, and rent-seeking. For a piece I am drafting on the boundary between pricing an externality and selling forbearance, I have been trying to think through the paradox of blackmail, and I keep feeling that my usual vocabulary should be able to dissolve it—or at least tell me exactly where the dissolution stops. As I understand the puzzle, the law criminalizes a combination of two acts that are individually permissible: threatening to disclose true information one is legally entitled to disclose, and demanding money. If I may simply publish an embarrassing fact about you, and I may simply ask you for money, why may I not offer to refrain from publishing it in exchange for payment? On its face that looks like an ordinary Coasean bargain in which you pay me to internalize a negative externality my speech would otherwise impose on you, and yet the law treats it as extortion.

I would like you to restate the paradox of blackmail, and the main positions one can take about it, as rigorously as you can in that economic vocabulary—treating reputationally damaging information as a potential externality, secrecy as an imperfectly allocated asset, the payment as a bargain that prices my silence, and the blackmailer as someone who may have invested purely in the capacity to threaten. Then give me the residue report I am actually after: which parts of the puzzle translate cleanly into those terms; which parts require distortion, such as treating reputation as a property right the victim already owns; and which parts have no counterpart at all, such as the distinction between a warning and a threat or the intuition that some consensual bargains should nevertheless be forbidden. Finish by telling me where you think the residue points. Is my economic vocabulary too coarse to capture a genuinely normative distinction, or does the exercise expose the philosophical puzzle as resting on distinctions that are fuzzier than they first appear?
