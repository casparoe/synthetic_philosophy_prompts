I'm a novelist — I write literary fiction and teach a craft workshop — and I have been reading around in the philosophy of language with the enthusiasm of an interested outsider: the old distinction between constative and performative utterances, the idea that saying "I promise" is not describing a promise but performing it, and the later collapse of that distinction into a general account of illocutionary force, felicity conditions, uptake, and the like. I understand these terms only roughly. I would like you to restate this material in the working vocabulary I actually use as a novelist: dialogue as action versus dialogue as exposition; beats, tactics, and objectives within a scene; subtext; the narrator's relation to the reader and the implied author; the contract with the reader established in an opening; suspension of disbelief and genre convention; showing versus telling. Be as faithful to the philosophical positions as you can while using mainly those craft terms, and do not smuggle the philosophy's assumptions into the translation under cover of my vocabulary.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the constative/performative picture map cleanly onto craft distinctions, which parts survive only with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart in how working novelists think about dialogue and narration? In particular, I suspect a novelist's instinct is to say that every sentence in a story is already an act — that there is no inertly descriptive language in fiction, because even exposition positions the reader, makes promises, and does work. Does that amount to siding with the later view that all utterances are performances, or does it quietly dissolve something the philosophers were trying to keep apart? And how should I think about felicity conditions and uptake in a world where a character's "I will" in a wedding scene can fail because another character refuses to take it up, while the reader nevertheless registers the sentence as a performed act in the story? Finally, give me the verdict: is the residue a defect of the novelist's vocabulary — something craft talk cannot say and ought to learn to say — or does it show that part of the philosophical picture is less clear than it looks when forced into the categories of someone who has to make sentences do things? I would also like you to flag which single consideration would most likely change that verdict, and to say where in the translation or in the diagnosis of the residue you are least confident.
