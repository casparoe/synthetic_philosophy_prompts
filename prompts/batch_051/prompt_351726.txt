I serve on the scientific advisory panel of our state's fish and wildlife agency, which must recommend whether to list the Clearwater whiptail under the state endangered species act. The act protects any species, subspecies, or distinct population segment of fish or wildlife, and the petition asks us to recognize the Clearwater whiptail as a species. The lineage is all-female and parthenogenetic. It arose when two sexual whiptail species hybridized, and it now reproduces clonally: males are unknown, and daughters are nearly genetically identical to their mothers. The lineage is genetically diagnosable, ecologically distinctive, confined to a small range of gypsum scrub, and declining under off-road vehicle use. But it exchanges no genes with anything — not with the parent species, because it produces no males, and not within itself in any ordinary sense, because reproduction is clonal. The panel is split. One faction holds that a species is fundamentally a reproductive community, and on that view an all-female clone is at best a unisexual biotype of one of the parent species, not a species in its own right; they also warn that listing clonal hybrids will invite petitions for every distinctive hybrid swarm. The other faction holds that a species is a separately evolving lineage with its own trajectory and diagnostic characters, and on that view the Clearwater whiptail plainly qualifies; they add that withholding protection over a theoretical scruple would let a genuinely distinctive lineage go extinct. Some staff observe that the act could protect the lineage as a distinct population segment without settling the species question, while others reply that this is a dodge that would leave the agency's working species concept unstated and invite inconsistent future decisions. Please draft the panel's short decision memo for circulation before our meeting on the 18th. It should recommend one concrete course of action — list as a species, decline to list, or protect under the distinct population segment category — with reasons the agency could defend in the likely administrative appeal. It should engage the losing position seriously rather than caricature it, and close by stating how the agency should handle future petitions involving asexual or hybrid lineages.
