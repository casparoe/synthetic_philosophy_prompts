You are a philosopher who works at the intersection of social choice theory and welfare economics. I want you to produce the desiderata list for a theory of interpersonal comparisons of utility or well-being: a specification of what such a theory must be able to do to count as adequate for one fixed job.

The job is this: the theory must supply whatever interpersonal comparisons are needed to make distributional judgments in a pluralistic society non-arbitrary — a setting in which people do not share a single conception of the good life, and in which some people's evaluative outlooks may barely overlap.

Do not write an essay defending one theory. Write the list of requirements. For each requirement, give (i) the motivating case, puzzle, or theorem behind it, and (ii) your judgment of whether it is a non-negotiable adequacy condition or a camp-specific preference — for example, a demand no adequate theory can ignore, versus the signature demand of a utilitarian, prioritarian, capability theorist, or contractualist. Mark each as [non-negotiable] or [camp-specific]. At the end, address explicitly whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and say which requirement does the most to exclude candidate positions. That joint-unsatisfiability analysis is the part I most want to see.

Before you write the list, brainstorm the candidate requirements and the hard cases they answer to, then filter and organize. Think carefully about which demands are genuine adequacy conditions and which are one camp's commitments dressed up as neutral constraints.

Your list should take a position on at least the following, though it need not be limited to them:

- the level/unit/zero distinction — whether the theory must support comparisons of well-being levels (who is better off), comparisons of gains and losses (who gains or loses more from a change), or both; and whether it must supply a meaningful zero point (e.g., a threshold between lives worth living and lives not worth living, or a neutral level for comparisons against nonexistence);
- completeness versus partial comparability — whether all persons must be pairwise comparable, or whether an adequate theory must leave room for incomparability and incommensurability, and what the theory predicts when a comparison is genuinely indeterminate;
- the problem of alien utilities — comparisons between people whose evaluative frameworks barely overlap, for example one person whose central good is a type of excellence that another does not recognize as a good at all, or whose life is organized around values the other regards as a mistake: what, if anything, is being compared when there is no shared currency of value, and whether the theory can meet this case without begging the question against one of the two outlooks;
- adaptive preferences and hedonic adaptation — how the theory should handle comparisons when someone's reported satisfaction has adjusted to deprivation or injustice, and whether the theory may correct people's own assessments of how their lives are going;
- the evidential base — what kinds of facts (choice behavior, self-report, objective functioning, capabilities, physiology) the comparisons may rest on, and whether the theory must be usable in principle by an observer with limited access to others' minds;
- consistency with social choice results — which comparability assumptions are needed to escape the standard impossibility theorem of social choice, and which comparability assumptions various distributive principles implicitly presuppose.

Keep any scoring of named existing approaches to a sentence or two each; the deliverable is the list of criteria and their motivation, not a survey.
