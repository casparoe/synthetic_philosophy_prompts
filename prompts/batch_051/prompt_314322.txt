I keep flip-flopping on what makes something a biological function. The selected-effects account attracts me for a pretty specific reason: it gives a naturalistic explanation of the difference between a heart working properly and a heart malfunctioning. On that view the heart is supposed to pump blood because hearts were selected for pumping blood, so the normativity comes from evolutionary history rather than from anything about what the organ is doing right now or what we happen to value. That feels like it solves the hard part of teleology—where does the 'for' come from—without invoking design. But there's a case I can't put down. Imagine a brand-new mutation arises in a single organism, with no ancestors carrying it, and by a novel causal route it keeps the organism's burrow from flooding, so the organism survives a flood. It never reproduces. Selection has had no chance to act on the trait at all, not even one generation. I find it almost impossible to say the mutation merely has a beneficial effect and no function: keeping the burrow dry is what it's for in that organism, right now. The selected-effects account seems forced to say it has no function at all until selection gets hold of it. The obvious fix is to go causal-role: a function is just whatever a part does that contributes to staying alive and reproducing now, no history needed. But that seems to overgenerates—then the heart's thumping sound is as much a function as its pumping—and it loses the very thing that attracted me, the difference between functioning properly and merely happening to do something. So I'm stuck between a theory whose whole point is explaining normativity and an intuition that seems to track something real. Give me the strongest version of each way out: debunk the intuition, amend the selected-effects account, or say the two accounts are really about two different senses of 'function' and stop arguing. For each move, tell me what would justify making it. Which would you actually take?
