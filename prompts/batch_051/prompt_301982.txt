I've been trying to reconstruct Keynes's argument that the whole problem of scarcity will be solved and we'll all be able to live the good life on fifteen hours of work a week. This is from the 1930 essay where he makes that prediction—I'm working from memory so I may have the moves wrong. I turned it into steps so you can tell me exactly where, if anywhere, the argument breaks.

(1) People have two kinds of wants. Absolute needs are the ones we have regardless of how other people are doing—enough food, shelter, sleep, basic security. Relative needs are the ones we have only because having the thing puts us ahead of, or level with, other people.

(2) Relative needs may be insatiable, but absolute needs are satiable: for any person there is a level of material provision past which more stuff makes almost no difference to their wellbeing.

(3) Once a person's absolute needs are saturated, the only thing that would keep them working for extra income is relative needs.

(4) Relative needs don't produce lasting satisfaction: you get the bigger house, feel ahead for a while, then the comparison resets and you need another step up.

(5) So, once absolute needs are saturated, a clear-eyed person would prefer more free time to more income, and would put their energy into leisure, friendship, art, and so on instead.

(6) Within a few generations, technological progress can saturated everyone's absolute needs on something like a fifteen-hour work week.

(7) Therefore, within a few generations, most people actually will choose leisure and the rest over extra income, and the love of money will come to seem like a mild disorder.

I suspect the trouble is somewhere around the jump from (5) to (7)—like its sliding from what a clear-eyed person would prefer to what people in general will do—but I want it pinned down. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest fix a defender of Keynes could make? Does the fixed version still get us to the fifteen-hour week? Answer in American English.
