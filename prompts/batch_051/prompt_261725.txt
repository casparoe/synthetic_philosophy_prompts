I am taking a comparative constitutional law course, and the instructor circulated a handout summarizing the American and European approaches to hate speech regulation. I would like an accuracy audit of that summary, because parts of it seem off to me but I am not confident enough to correct it on my own. The relevant passage, as I remember it, runs as follows:

"The United States is nearly alone among liberal democracies in recognizing no hate speech exception to its free speech guarantee. Under the standard derived from Brandenburg v. Ohio, even advocacy of racial or religious hatred is constitutionally protected unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to do so. A regulation aimed at speech because of its message is automatically unconstitutional; the assumed remedy for harmful ideas is rebuttal rather than prosecution. Western Europe starts from the opposite premise: because human dignity and public order are treated as constitutional values of equal rank to free expression, hate speech is not regarded as protected expression at all. German criminal law accordingly punishes incitement to hatred (Volksverhetzung), and most European states prohibit Holocaust denial. When such laws have been challenged, the European Court of Human Rights has held that speech attacking racial, religious, or ethnic groups falls entirely outside the protection of Article 10, and it allows states a wide margin of appreciation to determine what speech offends a protected group. The difference is ultimately philosophical: Americans rank individual expressive liberty above communal interests, whereas Europeans treat the community's interest in dignity and order as overriding the speaker's interest whenever the two conflict."

Two things in particular bother me. First, the claim that a regulation aimed at a message is automatically unconstitutional sounds more like a slogan than a rule, since American law recognizes at least some unprotected categories, such as true threats. Second, I had understood that the European Court of Human Rights often decides hate speech cases through the structured balancing test in Article 10(2), rather than by excluding the speech from protection altogether. But the instructor is a comparative lawyer, and I am not, so I want to know where the handout departs from the actual doctrines.

Could you go through the summary claim by claim and separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation? In particular, I would like to know whether the closing philosophical contrast survives a more careful account, and how a fairer statement of each side would be phrased. I would prefer an audit that tells me which parts I can still rely on, rather than a list of nitpicks.
