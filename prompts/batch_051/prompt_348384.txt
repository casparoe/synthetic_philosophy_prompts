You are to act as a scholar equally at home in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century rationalist metaphysics and in contemporary quantified modal logic, with particular command of David Lewis's counterpart theory and of the transworld identity views associated with Saul Kripke and Alvin Plantinga. I need you to think through a piece of counterfactual intellectual history with genuine argumentative rigor, not merely to reproduce a textbook comparison.

The project is this. I am drafting a paper for a workshop on the early modern prehistory of possible-worlds semantics, and the paper grew out of a disagreement with a colleague who insists that Leibniz is simply a counterpart theorist avant la lettre, while I suspect the truth is more complicated. As background, Lewisians hold that an individual exists in only one possible world, so that modal claims about Alexander the Great are made true by numerically distinct counterparts in other worlds that resemble him in contextually relevant ways. Defenders of transworld identity hold instead that the very same Alexander exists in various worlds with different properties, and then owe an account of how one and the same thing can vary across worlds without violating the necessity of identity. Leibniz could not have encountered these labels, but he faced closely related pressures through his own machinery, above all the complete individual concept, the containment of all true predicates in the concept of a subject, compossibility among the substances constituting a world, and the identity of indiscernibles. His treatment of possible Adams illustrates the ambiguity: at times he writes as though a changed life would belong to a different Adam, which sounds like counterpart theory, while at other times he anchors modal truth in God's survey of fully determinate individual substances, which can sound more like strict identity across alternative divine decrees.

Your task is to reconstruct, as your best-evidenced prediction, how Leibniz would locate his own position if transported into the modern debate and required to speak in its vocabulary. First identify the two or three firmest Leibnizian commitments that should constrain his answer; then argue whether those commitments push him toward counterpart theory, toward transworld identity, or toward rejecting some presupposition the two modern camps share, and say exactly which presupposition that is. Next, state the strongest objection each modern camp would press against the position you have reconstructed for him, and how Leibniz could reply using only resources already present in his system. Take your time and begin by brainstorming the relevant threads of Leibniz's metaphysics alongside the two modern positions, laying out your interpretive options before committing to one. Do not feel obliged to attempt exact textual quotations, and flag any place where Leibniz would have to innovate beyond his own texts rather than merely apply them.
