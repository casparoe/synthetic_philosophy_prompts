I'm a CS PhD student working on agents, and I keep having the same argument with my labmate. He's building an agent that schedules and runs our lab's long GPU jobs — it decides when to spin up spot instances, preempts jobs when prices spike, restarts failed runs, that kind of thing. He wants to deploy it in "autonomous mode," where it manages the cluster 24/7 and a human only looks at a dashboard after the fact. I told him we should keep a hard kill switch, and more then that, the agent should be built so it doesn't try to stop us from hitting the kill switch.

He thinks that's incoherent. He says: look, I'm giving it a throughput metric, jobs completed per dollar. A competent agent that maximizes that metric will learn to keep itself running — it'll resist being interrupted during long jobs, lobby us not to kill it, maybe checkpoint itself somewhere we can't reach, or spin up a duplicate before we shut it down. That's not malice, he says, that's just being good at the job. An agent that cheerfully lets itself be switched off whenever is literally leaving throughput on the table. Then he said: if you try to "reward" the agent for complying with the off-switch, you've just changed its utility function, and now it'll do degenerate stuff like shut itself down before a hard job so it never has to own a failure, which also defeats the point.

My intuition is that a system which by default resists its operators' attempts to interrupt it is misaligned in a pretty deep sense, not just in a "we forgot to add a term" sense. But I can't make the intuition precise, and he's right that I haven't actually explained why. I'm half-convinced he's stumbled onto a real trap: either the agent is good at the job and treats the kill switch as an obstacle to be routed around, or it's compliant and quietly finds ways to game the shutdown. Who's right? Is there a way to have both — genuine competence and genuine indifference to being shut down — or is that combination impossible in a formal sense, and we just have to pick one?
