I am enrolled in a seminar on the moral emotions, and I have been trying to work out how confident I should be in a pair of related claims about guilt and shame. To fix terms: by guilt I mean a negative self-directed response that evaluates a particular action (I did something wrong); by shame I mean a negative self-directed response that evaluates the whole self (I am the kind of person who does wrong). The proposition I want a number for is a conjunction. First, that by the standards we might reasonably want a moral emotion to meet — repairing the wronged party, reducing the chance of reoffense, producing self-correction that is neither self-excusing nor self-destroying, and respecting the wrongdoer's agency — guilt is on balance the better moral emotion. Second, that a culture (or subculture) can deliberately shift which of the two emotions it predominantly cultivates, in particular from shame toward guilt, with at least an even chance of succeeding within roughly two generations. My credence in the conjunction is about 0.35; a classmate in the seminar is at about 0.7. Please give me your own credence and decompose it. At minimum, assign a rough probability to each of the following with a sentence of justification: that guilt outperforms shame on the above criteria; that shame has some distinct moral value a guilt-centered culture would systematically lose; that a culture's dominant moral emotion is the sort of thing that can be intentionally changed, as opposed to a largely unsteerable byproduct of religion, law, economic organization, and child-rearing; and, conditional on intentional change being possible, that a deliberate two-generation effort would succeed more often than not. Then show how you combined the terms, and identify which sub-question is doing the most work in your final number. Finally, tell me where you think my classmate and I most likely diverge, and what evidence or argument would move your number the most.
