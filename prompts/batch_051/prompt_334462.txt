I am compiling a handbook for humanitarian policy staff who are familiar with the basic histories of the Bengal famine, the Irish Great Famine, and the Great Leap Forward, but who have not read the theoretical literature. I want a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts them into a position on the question of whether famines are best understood as natural disasters or political crimes.

Begin from the following anchored source claim, which I will paraphrase from Amartya Sen's Poverty and Famines because I do not want the wording to do the work. In the chapter on the 1943 Bengal famine, Sen argues that the aggregate availability of food per person in 1943 was not remarkably low relative to earlier years, and that the famine should be traced not to an absolute shortage of food but to wartime inflationary pressures and speculative hoarding that destroyed the exchange entitlements of rural wage laborers. Make this source claim one of the branch points in the tree, not a leaf or an assumed conclusion.

The tree should proceed from first questions to named leaves, built from the logical space of answers rather than from a pre-given list of schools. The root question should be whether a respondent locates the initiating cause in biophysical harvest conditions or in rules, markets, and entitlements. Then branch by further distinctions such as: whether an absolute food shortage is necessary for a famine; whether market responses such as hoarding and price movement are caused by the original harvest shock or are independently political; whether vulnerability is a pre-existing social product; and whether the moral conclusion follows from the causal story.

Use these cases as diagnostic probes, phrased as questions a nonspecialist can answer without knowing the labels:
- Whether the amount of rice available per person in Bengal in 1943 was unusually low compared with the preceding years, and whether the famine would have occurred without wartime inflation and hoarding.
- Whether the loss of the Irish potato crop reduced the island's total food-producing capacity enough to explain the mortality, or whether the continued export of grain and the structure of land tenure did the main causal work.
- Whether the Great Leap Forward famine is better described as a weather event or as the product of a procurement and production system that made local shortfalls fatal.
- Whether a person can consistently hold that famine is caused by biophysical scarcity while also accepting that many historical famines occurred without significant aggregate per capita decline.

Leaves should include at least: the classical food availability decline view, Sen-style entitlement failure, a stronger market-failure or hoarding account, a political accountability view, a colonial or institutional account, a Malthusian natural-disaster view, and an agnostic two-level view. You may add leaves if the logical space requires them. For each leaf, give a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Mark any combination of answers that has no named defender and say whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot actually separate with the questions you have written, and say what additional question would separate them.

Aim for seven to nine leaves and eight to ten branch questions. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; the instrument should be self-contained and usable as a handout.
