I'm prepping for a grad seminar on Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" and my friend has an argument he's weirdly proud of that I can't shake. Here's the passage we're both stuck on (from Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism"):

"Statements which are analytic by general philosophical acclaim are not, indeed, far to seek. They fall into two classes. Those of the first class, which may be called logically true, are typified by: (1) No unmarried man is married. But there is also a second class of analytic statements, typified by: (2) No bachelor is married."

My friend says: look, Quine's whole paper is just running this taxonomy into the ground. His argument, as my friend reconstructs it, goes like this.

(1) If (2) is analytic, it's only because you can turn it into the logical truth (1) by swapping "bachelor" for "unmarried man."
(2) So (2) is analytic only if "bachelor" and "unmarried man" are synonymous.
(3) But there is no non-circular test for synonymy: sameness of meaning just restates the problem; interchangeability in all contexts either fails or smuggles in unexplained modal notions; and defining synonymy in terms of analyticity is a circle.
(4) If there's no non-circular test for synonymy, then there's no fact of the matter about whether two expressions are synonymous.
(5) So "no bachelor is married" isn't really analytic, and more generally no statement is true purely in virtue of meaning.
(6) But the same style of reasoning works for any pair of expressions you like. If there's no fact of the matter about synonymy, then there's no fact of the matter about what any word means. And then "Quine's argument is sound" means nothing in particular, and every sentence is as good as every other, which is absurd.
(7) So Quine's argument must go wrong somewhere.

You're a professional philosopher of language who has taught this paper more times than you can count. Before you commit, sketch the two or three most plausible diagnoses, then pick one and tell me wich step I should give up and why it looked fine. I'd rather keep (3) than (5), honestlly, but I want to know what I'm paying for keeping it. Then tell me what would change your mind — what argument or consideration would make you reject a different premise instead, or where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail.
