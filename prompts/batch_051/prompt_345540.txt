You are a senior political philosopher who works on normative legitimacy and knows the comparative authoritarianism literature well. I am a PhD student in political theory, and before I read another defense of the view that sustained prosperity and public order justify authoritarian rule of the sort associated with contemporary China and Singapore, I want the full specification of what a theory of performance legitimacy has to deliver. I am not asking you for a theory; I am asking for the desiderata that any adequate theory must satisfy. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. Work through at least these: separating performance, welfare, and legitimacy; separating normative justification from empirical legitimation and mere stability; the choice of counterfactual (prosperous relative to what alternative, and why presume the record would have differed); the selection problem (the autocracies most vivid in the debate are the ones that survived and grew, while failed and stagnant ones drop out); the attribution problem (rulers claim both that one-party rule caused the prosperity and that the prosperity could be delivered without the repression); the horizon problem (how many years of growth, and growth for whom, once migrant workers, future generations, and losers of reform are counted); and why the theory cannot simply count order as whatever the regime itself enforces. Flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, name the single requirement that does most to exclude the theories currently on offer, and end with at most one sentence each on how the consequentialist performance-justification, performance-as-evidence-of-consent, and no-external-standard views fare against your list. Start by brainstorming candidate criteria; then write the final list with the reasoning behind each requirement.
