I'm a political philosophy PhD student and I keep running into the free-rider problem from a bunch of directions at once—picket-line crossing, tax evasion, people who don't pay union dues but still get the collectively bargained contract, climate burden-sharing, even the person in our department who never contributes to the seminar coffee fund but always takes a cup. Everyone says "free rider" like the wrongness is obvious, but when I push, the accounts pull apart: some frame it as unfairness, some as exploitation, some as cheating on a cooperative scheme, some Kantian universalization, etc. Before I read any more of them I want the full spec: what would a theory of the wrongness of free riding actually have to get right? For concreteness, assume the theory is supposed to do actual normative work—saying when someone is blameworthy and what they ought to do—rather than just formal modeling of public-goods games.

I don't need a literature review. I want the requirements themselves, each with the case or consideration that motivates it and a note on whether it's non-negotiable or just a camp preference. In particular:

- which distinctions it can't collapse: benefiting from someone's action vs. accepting a benefit; free riding vs. exploitation; free riding vs. "social parasitism" (and whether that's even a real category or just a pejorative); weak vs. strong free riding; not contributing vs. contributing less than a fair share.
- which structural features of collective-action problems it has to track: rival vs. non-rival goods, threshold vs. continuous provision, small groups where one shirker is noticed vs. large groups where no single non-contribution changes anything.
- how it handles the "my contribution doesn't matter" problem. If my not paying for public radio won't change whether it exists, and my not showing up to a demonstration won't change the outcome, am I still a free rider and is it still wrong?
- whether it can handle unchosen benefits, like the neighborhood watch I never asked for or the cleaner air I get whether I contribute or not—can I free-ride on a benefit I'd rather not have received?
- whether wrongness tracks ability to contribute: is the single parent who doesn't pay union dues but benefits from the contract a free rider in the same sense as the comfortable freeloader?

Then the two things I most want from you: which single requirement knocks out the most popular views (I have a hunch its the inconsequetial-contribution case, but make your case), and whether any subset of these desiderata can't be jointly satisfied by one theory. A table is fine for the requirements part.
