So there's this passage in the Pensées where Pascal lays out his famous point that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyranny, and therefore we have to make the just strong, or the strong just. I've been trying to work through what that actually rules in and rules out, and I keep getting stuck on a kind of case he couldn't have anticipated.

Take a nonviolent mass movement — say, a Gandhian satyagraha or the civil rights sit-ins. It has no physical force and refuses to use it, but it's clearly trying to make its just demands effective through mass civil disobeidience, boycotts, and so on. On Pascal's terms, is this just "justice without force" and therefore doomed to be powerless? Or does the movement show that force isn't exhausted by physical coercion, in which case Pascal's neat antithesis needs redrawing? And here's the part that really bugs me: such movements sometimes succeed precisely by provoking the state into using violence, turning the state's own force against its legitimacy. Is that a way of making the strong just in reverse — using the stronger party's violence to convict it, rather than to enforce the justice? Or would Pascal call that something else entirely?

I don't want the standard "Pascal was a pessimist about politics" line. I want you to go through the passage closely and say whether it can accommodate this case, or whether the case exposes a genuine ambiguity in what he means by "force" and by "just." If the text can't handle it, say so, and tell me what you'd have to amend to make it work.
