I am a member of a licensing authority in a small jurisdiction that screens prospective parents before children are placed with them. I want to know what our own track record commits us to in a hard case we are about to hear. Our enabling rules tell us to promote the child's interests, including the child's future autonomy, but the practical question is whether we have actually been applying one principle across our prior decisions rather than pattern-matching to vivid features. Here are the decisions, compressed:

1. We licensed a couple who planned a demanding but reversible early musical education: daily practice and competition travel from age five, ordinary schooling retained, no representation contract, and an explicit discontinuation condition if the child lost interest for a sustained period.

2. We refused a couple who planned to place their six-year-old in a residential gymnastics academy for thirty-plus hours per week, with no ordinary schooling and a management contract binding the child until age sixteen. The child is talented and currently enthusiastic.

3. We licensed, with conditions, a couple from a separatist religious community. Their plan ended formal schooling at fourteen in favor of apprenticeship in community trades; we required solid literacy and numeracy instruction, an explanation of how the child could leave the community, and access to an independent advocate on request.

4. We licensed a couple whose plan specified using preimplantation genetic diagnosis to select an embryo that did not carry a variant causing a severe, early-onset, fatal metabolic disorder.

Now we must decide whether to license a Deaf couple whose plan specifies using preimplantation genetic diagnosis to select an embryo carrying a variant that makes profound congenital deafness overwhelmingly likely. They intend to raise the child in a fluent sign-language household and a robust signing community, to ensure written-language competence, and they argue that they are selecting for a culture and an identity, not imposing a harm.

What should we decide if we aim to be consistent with our precedents? Please do all of the following: formulate the candidate principles that fit the four earlier decisions; identify which of the earlier cases is the closest analog to this one and why; state which decision each candidate principle would require in the present case; state what principle we would be committing ourselves to by granting versus denying the license; and name the further hypothetical case that would most cleanly separate the remaining candidate principles. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so rather than pretending they force one outcome.
