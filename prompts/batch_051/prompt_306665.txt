In Section X of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume discusses an Indian prince who refuses to believe the first reports that in cold countries water becomes solid enough to bear a man's weight. Hume observes that the prince's uniform experience had never produced anything resembling frost; the reported facts were not conformable to the course of his experience, so it would have taken very strong testimony to move him. Hume's judgment is that the prince was proceeding as a sound reasoner even though he was mistaken. Now consider a contemporary analyst of political risk. She is told, by a knowledgeable informant, that a long-stable empire will disintegrate within two years. She treats the report against a reference class of empires and long-lived autocracies, finds the base rate for sudden disintegration low, and concludes that the report is probably exaggerated or misleading. Many people would call this the outside view and praise her discipline, even after the empire collapses. The two inferential moves look structurally identical: assign a low prior probability to a surprising event on the basis of past uniform experience or base rates; receive vivid testimonial evidence that the event is occurring; decline to give that testimony enough weight to revise the prior. Yet one case is routinely used to illustrate resisting anecdote, and the other is Hume's illustration of how a rational inquirer can be trapped by a parochial sample. Is this asymmetry in our evaluations justified? Find the strongest principle that would treat Hume's prince and the disciplined forecaster differently, test that principle against nearby cases, and give a verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned. In particular, say whether the apparent difference is just that we know the prince was wrong and the forecaster was wrong, and whether excluding that hindsight collapses the two cases.
