Exam question: "Does clicking 'I agree' to standard online terms count as agreement, or is it just a legal fiction? Explain how courts treat clickwrap versus browsewrap, and say whether unconscionability doctrine fixes whatever gap there is between legal enforceability and actual agreement."

Student answer: "At common law a contract requires a meeting of the minds, but most jurisdictions have shifted to an objective theory of assent: what matters is what a reasonable person would infer from outward conduct, not the parties' private mental states. That is supposed to explain why clickwrap is enforced. If you have to click an 'I agree' button and the terms are on an adjacent link, courts will generally hold you to them, since the click is an unambiguous manifestation of assent even if you never read a word. Browsewrap is different: terms hidden in a link at the bottom of the page usually aren't enforced because there is no affirmative act signaling agreement. So the law's picture is internally consistent—it demands a public signal of assent, not actual understanding. The worry that no one reads boilerplate is handled by unconscionability doctrine. Procedural unconscionability is about whether a term's content is one-sided or harsh, for instance a warranty disclaimer; substantive unconscionability is about the bargaining process, like take-it-or-leave-it pressure or terms being buried. A judge who finds a term harsh can strike it. Since that mechanism removes the terms a user would reject if informed, the terms left in force are ones users would have accepted anyway, so calling the click 'agreement' isn't a mere legal fiction after all."
