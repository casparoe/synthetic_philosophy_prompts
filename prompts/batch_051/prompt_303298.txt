I've been going in circles on a paper about Hofstadter's superrationality, and a friend gave me an argument that seems to shows the whole idea collapses in the Luring Lottery. I can't find the bad step. The setups, as I understand them: in the Platonia dilemma, 20 people, no communication, can each send a telegram; if exactly one person sends one, that person gets a billion dollars, and if zero or more than one send, nobody gets anything. Superrationality tells each person to send with probability 1/20. In the Luring Lottery (which Scientific American actually ran in the 80s), each reader sends a postcard with any positive whole number of entries they want; the prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries, awarded to a randomly chosen entry.

Now the argument:
(1) In symmetric choice problems, a rational player should choose the strategy that would be best for each player on the supposition that all rational players in the same situation choose the same strategy.
(2) That principle selects p=1/20 in Platonia, since any common pure strategy gives everyone zero and p(1-p)^19 is maximized at p=1/20.
(3) So the same principle should select some common strategy in the Luring Lottery, since that game is symmetric too.
(4) But in the Luring Lottery, for any common strategy you propose, a player who holds the other players' strategy fixed does better by submitting a number of entries roughly equal to the expected total submitted by the other players. So whatever common strategy all were going to follow, each player would prefer to unilaterally switch.
(5) And if everyone can see that, then by the very reasoning that makes rational players converge on the same answer, they all switch; but then the expected total from others shifts, so the number each wants to submit shifts again, and this never settles—it just ratchets toward googolplex-sized entries, which is pretty much what happened in real life.
(6) So superrationality either gives no determinate answer in the Luring Lottery or gives the absurd answer that rational players bid the prize down to a vanishing fraction of a cent.
The conclusion is that the principle in (1) can't be generally right, even though it handles Platonia. Where does this first go wrong, if it does? I'd like to keep (1) and (2), but I can't tell whether the real trouble is in (4), (5), or the move from (3) to (4). What should I give up, and what does it cost?
