I have been working through Fearon's rationalist account of war and trying to extend it to AI development races, but the extension seems to lead somewhere absurd, and I suspect there is a mistake I cannot locate. Here is the argument as I currently have it.

(1) A core rationalist finding about war is that war is ex post inefficient: because fighting destroys resources, there is almost always some peaceful settlement that would leave both sides at least as well off as fighting would.
(2) The standing puzzle is why rational states nevertheless fail to reach such a settlement and end up fighting.
(3) The logic of (1) and (2) does not appear to depend on anything specific to interstate war; it seems to apply to any costly conflict, since if a conflict destroys value, there should be some mutually preferable ex ante division of whatever the conflict is about.
(4) An AI development race is a costly conflict: labs spend enormous resources, cut into safety margins, and risk outcomes that no lab wants, when the eventual gains from AI could in principle be shared.
(5) By parity of reasoning, rational AI labs should always be able to negotiate some division of the expected gains that leaves every lab better off than racing, so genuine AI races among rational actors should not occur.
(6) The same reasoning would also rule out price wars, costly litigation, strikes, and arms races, yet many of these occur among sophisticated, resource-rich actors that we would not want to dismiss as simply irrational.
(7) So either all such actors are systematically irrational, or the rationalist framework cannot really explain any costly conflict.

I do not want to accept either horn, and I also do not want to give up the idea that AI races are a real strategic phenomenon with rational participants. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If you have to reject a premise or a step, tell me which one, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs.
