I am not an academic, but I have spent the past several years reading old scientific papers and histories of science in my spare time, and I keep running into the same pattern: the name attached to a discovery, a law, a syndrome, or a mathematical object is very often not the name of the person who first formulated it. Once I started noticing this, I could not stop, and I have moved from treating it as a minor curiosity to holding a fairly strong view. My view is that misattribution is not an occasional accident but the normal way scientific credit works: credit reliably goes not to whoever gets there first, but to whoever makes an idea legible, teachable, and useful to the right community at the right moment, so eponymy is mostly a fossil of persuasion and pedagogy rather than a record of priority. There is even a name for the pattern, Stigler's law of eponymy, but I want to defend the underlying view rather than lean on the label.

Please tell me what would count against this view. I want an honest map of how exposed it actually is: which cases, mechanisms, or arguments should most unsettle me, and which would be merely awkward? For example, I can imagine that fields or historical periods in which priority is carefully tracked, or cases where the "wrong" eponym reflects a real conceptual difference rather than a popularity contest, might cut against me, but I do not know how damaging those would be. I would personally find it most persuasive if someone showed that the pattern is a real regularity across many fields and periods rather than a handful of famous anecdotes. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender of my view would have to concede it or could reasonably dismiss it. Finally, tell me whether my view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — that is, whether it can absorb any counterexample by redefining what counts as "making an idea legible" — and if so, whether that is a serious problem for a view of this kind. Please write your answer in American English.
