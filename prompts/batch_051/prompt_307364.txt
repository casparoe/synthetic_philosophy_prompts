I'm a philosophy professor and I've taught the usual aesthetics survey for years, but this term I'm doing a dedicated seminar on Tolstoy's *What is Art?* and I want to pin down my own reading before I present it. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want to know if the set hangs together.

(1) For Tolstoy, art proper isn't about beauty or imitation; it's about infection: the artist transmits a feeling he has actually lived through to an audience by means of movement, sound, lines, words, etc. (2) Sincerity is the keystone. A work made by someone who doesn't really feel the feeling is counterfeit art no matter how technically polished, while a genuinely felt feeling can carry a formally clumsy work. (3) But good art also has to be accessible to a nondiscriminating audience—a peasant, a worker, a child, not just a trained coterie. If a work requires special interpretative apparatus, it's bad art. (4) The value of a work also depends on the quality of the feeling transmitted: the best feelings are ones that unite people, especially the feeling of universal brotherhood, and feelings that divide, flatter, or merely entertain are worse. (5) At the same time, I'm convinced Tolstoy would reject didactic art. A work made deliberately to teach a moral lesson is bad art on his terms because the feeling is staged rather than sincerely felt. (6) Finally, I think Tolstoy's criticism is meant to be objective, not a matter of taste: once you know whether a work is sincere, infectious, accessible, and what kind of feeling it conveys, there's a fact of the matter about whether it's good.

I can feel some of these pulling against each other, but I don't want to stack the deck by saying which. I'd like you to go through the commitments pairwise and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are. For the real tensions, rank them by severity and say what the cheapest fix would be in each case. Then tell me which single revision would leave the overall interpretation most coherent—and what it would cost.
