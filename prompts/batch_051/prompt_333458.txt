I've been working through the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas, the accepted sources of cognition, and I find myself pulled in two directions. On the standard fourfold scheme, perception, inference, and comparison are joined by verbal testimony, śabda, as a distinct source of knowledge in its own right. The Nyāya claim is not merely that we learn a great deal from other people, which no one disputes; it is that testimony cannot be reduced to inference, or to any of the other pramāṇas. In inference, I know something through a sign only because I have myself grasped the invariable connection between signs of that kind and what they indicate — the smoke and the fire. In testimony, by contrast, I can come to know that the road to the next town is flooded without having established any such connection between the speaker's utterance and the state of the road. I depend on the speaker's own perception, memory, or reasoning, and the Naiyāyikas characteristically add that śabda is the assertion of a trustworthy speaker, an āpta, not just anyone's utterance. I find this genuinely attractive as cognitive cartography: it respects the fact that our epistemic powers are complementary, that a hearer's warrant need not recapitulate the speaker's, and that the division of cognitive labor is a basic feature of human life rather than a convenient shortcut that must be cashed out in individual inference.

But I also have a firm, hard-to-dislodge intuition that this makes testimony too independent. Even when the theory is restricted to statements by an āpta, it seems to me that the justificatory work is being done by my grounds for taking the speaker to be trustworthy — her track record, her incentives, her access to the relevant facts, the general regularity that people usually assert what they take to be true. Those grounds, however implicit and compressed, look like an inference, and if I strip them away, the bare fact that someone said p does not seem to be a reason for p at all, only a cause of my believing it. On this intuition, the Nyāya view mistakes a piece of social technology so smooth that it feels immediate for a distinct epistemic faculty; a hearer who simply defers, with no evidence about the speaker, has been moved rather than justified. The apparent independence of śabda is purchased by smuggling an inference through the front door under the heading of establishing the speaker's trustworthiness.

I am not asking to be told which side wins. Give me the strongest version you can of the Nyāya claim that śabda is an independent pramāṇa, and the strongest version of the reductionist intuition that it is ultimately a disguised inference. Then tell me what it would take for each to give ground. Should the intuition be debunked — perhaps as confusing the psychology of accepting a report with the structure of the epistemic standing it confers — or should the independence claim be amended, restricted, or abandoned? Or is there a reading on which both are right, because the hearer's local evidential situation and the communal division of cognitive labor are being described at different levels? I want to know what would justify each of those moves, not merely which one you prefer.
