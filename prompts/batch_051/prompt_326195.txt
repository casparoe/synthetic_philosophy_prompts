I work as a quality and compliance lead at a midsize medical device company, and one of my recurring jobs is to convene "expert panels" that vote on binary judgment calls: does this failure analysis show the risk is below our threshold, is this supplier's process validated, does this anomaly warrant a field action. We run one of these maybe twice a month, the stakes are high (recalls, patient harm, regulatory trouble), and I usually have about half an hour to decide how to run the decision before the panel even meets. The panelists are smart engineers and managers but not trained in probability or decision theory, and management likes majority votes because they spread the accountability around; they're also the ones who have to sign off on my final decision rule, so whatever I use has to be explainable in a sentence or two. I've read a little about the Condorcet jury theorem — roughly, that if each voter is more likely right than wrong and their mistakes are independent, a majority of a big group is more reliable than any single voter, but if those conditions fail the majority can be worse then a single competent person. I want to turn that idea into a procedure I can actually run, not a lecture.

Act as an expert on formal epistemology and collective decision-making, and think carefully before you answer. Start by brainstorming the distinct conditions under which a majority vote is trustworthy, better than one expert, or worse than one expert; then turn those into a checklist I can run in thirty minutes without doing anything beyond rough estimating. The person using it won't have heard of the theorem, so keep the steps in plain language and say which distinctions matter enough to keep and which can be dropped. For each step give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to pass, where the procedure stops with "do not aggregate, use a designated expert," and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a panel of six engineers, none of whom designs batteries, votes yes on whether a battery's failure probability is under 10^-6 after they all read the same test report, while one in-house battery scientist who isn't on the panel has filed a written dissent; (b) a panel of eleven, none with statistical training, votes on whether a cluster of complaint reports is a real signal or just noise; (c) a panel votes on whether a product's user interface is "safe enough to launch," with several members saying it's fine and others saying it feels rushed. Make sure at least one of these reaches an answer I won't like, and say explicitly why I'll dislike it. End with what the procedure cannot do and where it will silently misfire.
