I am preparing a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on population ethics, and I would like a decision tree that sorts participants into the position on the moral priority of preventing suffering that their case judgements actually track, rather than any label they happen to endorse. The instrument should be a compact tree with roughly eight branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves, aimed at readers who understand basic consequentialism but are not expected to know the taxonomy in advance.

The leaves must include at least: classical hedonistic utilitarianism, prioritarianism, weak negative utilitarianism, strong negative utilitarianism, lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism (on which sufficiently severe suffering cannot be outweighed by any amount of less severe suffering or well-being), antifrustrationism (on which only preference frustration is intrinsically bad and satisfaction has no positive value), and the procreative-asymmetry position (on which the absence of suffering is good in a way that the absence of happiness is not bad). You may add leaves if the logical space demands them.

Build the tree from the questions rather than from the labels. Each branch node should be a concrete question or a forced choice between two short world-descriptions, phrased so that a student can answer without knowing which position it points to. Work through a fixed battery of cases, including at least these three: one world containing no sentient lives at all; one world containing a single life of considerable happiness that also includes one episode of intense, unavoidable pain; and one world containing many lives that have unsatisfied preferences but no felt pleasure or pain. Use, in some order, the distinctions that actually split the positions: whether happiness has positive value in itself or only the reduction of suffering has value; whether sufficiently severe suffering is lexically prior to all lesser suffering and well-being or merely weighted more heavily; whether the locus of value is felt experience or preference satisfaction and frustration; whether the absence of additional lives that would contain some suffering can itself be better than their existence; and whether the view is a claim about which outcomes are better or a claim about what we have decisive practical reason to do.

Each leaf should carry its name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. For every combination of answers to these distinctions that no named position occupies, say briefly whether the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate on the diagnostic cases alone, and describe what additional case or distinction would separate them.
