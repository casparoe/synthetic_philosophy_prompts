Next month I'm giving a twenty-minute lunch talk at our company's machine learning offsite, and I want the Q&A afterward to go well. I work on a product team that builds recommender and content-moderation systems, and for the past year our ML platform group has been pushing an org-wide initiative to define a single safety-and-values score that every model can optimize against. The plan is to fold content quality, user safety, fairness, legal risk, and a handful of other considerations into one weighted objective, so product and leadership can reason about trade-offs on a single dashboard number. I have been asked to present the dissenting view. My thesis is roughly this: human values are not one underlying thing wearing different disguises; they are genuinely plural, and some of them cannot be traded off on a single scale without misrepresenting at least one of them. Building them into one scalar reward function, or a weighted sum with lexicographic constraints patched on top, is not a neutral engineering detail. It is a substantive claim about what people care about, and we should stop treating it as mere implementation convenience. I have no formal background in philosophy. I arrived at this view by debugging an RLHF reward model that was quietly papering over a real conflict between user autonomy and predicted harm, and then by reading around in adjacent material about value pluralism and incommensurability. I can defend the engineering intuition, but I will not be able to cite the academic literature on demand.

Please write me a preparation memo for the question period. The audience is mostly applied ML engineers who think in terms of loss functions, benchmarks, and reward hacking; two product managers who want a metric they can put in a dashboard; a privacy lawyer who tends to ask about documented, defensible criteria; and a VP who keeps saying we should have one number so the company can decide trade-offs consistently. Give me roughly eight to ten objections in the order they are likely to come at me, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Keep the whole memo under 1,400 words.
