I am a novelist drafting the climactic scene of a novel. The scene is a long late-night argument between two men in their late sixties: a retired historian who has spent his career on the American founding, and a moral philosopher who works on responsibility and blame. Their friendship collapsed years ago over Thomas Jefferson, and on this night they are not trying to win so much as to find out how far each can move the other. I want the argument to carry real philosophical weight rather than sounding like a costumed version of a campus debate, so I need a dialectically serious exchange, not a set of talking points.

Write the argument as a dialogue of roughly 1,500 words, mostly direct speech with minimal stage direction. Here are the positions. The historian defends a chastened version of the man-of-his-time defence: not that slaveholding was acceptable then, and not that Jefferson was powerless to know better, but that moral judgement across centuries should be graduated by what an agent's own world made visible and what it made costly to act on, and that Jefferson partly saw and flinched from a truth he was unusually well placed to confront. The philosopher rejects the defence outright: the suffering of the people Jefferson held in bondage did not depend on which standards Jefferson inherited, and a defence that turns an explanation of how a decent man did terrible things into a partial mitigation does moral work it has not earned.

Neither man should be a caricature: the historian is not a cultural relativist who thinks the past gets a pass, and the philosopher is not a smug presentist who treats history as a stack of indictments. Each man must press the other's hardest case rather than his own. The historian should force the philosopher to say what cross-century moral judgement is for, if not for graduated judgement; the philosopher should force the historian to say whether his defence ever genuinely excuses anyone, and why it does not collapse into a blanket excuse for whoever happened to hold power at the time. Have each man make one significant concession that revises an exact claim he entered with, but do not end in a bland both-sides-have-a-point truce: the two positions should end measurably different from where they began. Do not include citations, footnotes, or references to specific scholarly works; I will add period details and attribution myself.
