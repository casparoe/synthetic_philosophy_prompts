Me and some friends have a philosophy of probability reading group, and we've been stuck for six months on a single question: can a finite sequence be random? The math isn't the problem — everyone knows the standard machinery, Kolmogorov complexity, Martin-Löf tests, von Mises collectives, and nobody disputes any of the theorems. The fight is over what we're even asking. One camp says randomness is a property of the sequence itself: 0110101110 is more random than 0000000000 because the first is incompressible up to a small constant and the second has an obvious short description, and that's a fact about the strings, modulo a choice of universal machine. A second camp says that gets it backwards: randomness attaches to the process, not the output. If I type 0110101110 on purpose, it isn't random, even though it's the same string my Geiger counter might have produced — so no finite string is random or nonrandom intrinsically. A third camp says the whole thing is epistemic: calling a string random is always relative to background knowledge, because the same string flips from random-looking to obviously nonrandom once you learn it's the binary expansion of pi. Everyone agrees on the examples and nobody has moved in months. I suspect the world 'random' is doing three jobs at once (metaphysical, semantic, epistemic) and the disagreement is at least partly verbal, but maybe it's something harder: a genuine split about whether probability lives in the world or in our descriptions. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given the diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or whether the original question is just ill-posed.
