I serve on the board of a private research foundation that funds work at the intersection of philosophy, economics, and public policy. We are considering a grant proposal from a group of political philosophers and decision theorists who want to develop what they call a far-future emergency powers framework. Their central claim is that once one takes expected-value reasoning about the long-term future seriously, the expected value of even modest reductions in existential risk dwarfs the value of ordinary near-term political goods, so that familiar constraints such as democratic deliberation, civil liberties, and prohibitions on paternalistic coercion can come to look like local niceties that should give way when they obstruct a far-future imperative. The applicants propose to work out, with analytic precision, the conditions under which governments or international bodies would be justified in overriding those constraints—for example through surveillance, manipulation of public discourse, restrictions on movement or reproduction, or suspension of democratic procedures—when such measures are plausibly necessary to keep humanity on a trajectory that avoids existential catastrophe. The board is divided. Some members consider this a serious, if uncomfortable, extension of longtermist thinking that should be funded precisely because it forces the field to examine its own implications. Others think the project is normatively dangerous even if methodologically competent, and that funding it would lend institutional legitimacy to what is, in effect, a careful philosophical argument for authoritarianism.

I have been asked to draft the board's decision memo. It should give a clear recommendation—fund, reject, or fund with conditions—together with the reasoning, and should be a document the board could actually act on rather than an abstract essay. It should state the strongest version of the proposal's underlying argument, address the question of whether the foundation's act of funding itself carries moral or political significance, and give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. If conditions are recommended, they should be specified concretely and defended against the objection that they would simply kill the project.
