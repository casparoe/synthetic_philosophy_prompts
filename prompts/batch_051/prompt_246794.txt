I sit on my university's animal research ethics committee as the lay member, which means I am the only person in the room who is not a scientist. A proposal has come before us from a behavioural neuroscience lab that works on learning in bumblebees. The protocol involves tethering individual bees and using mild electric shocks as a punisher while they learn an odour-discrimination task; the shocks are described in the application as 'mild', but the bees visibly struggle and try to escape. Our current policy protects vertebrates and cephalopods and simply does not classify insects as animals to which welfare provisions apply. The researchers argue that bees are useful models precisely because their behaviour can be explained without supposing any subjective experience, and that extending vertebrate-style protections to a bee would be a sentimental category mistake. Two other committee members disagree sharply: they point to the bees' sophisticated navigation, social learning, and apparently play-like behaviour, and argue that we should apply a precautionary principle until we know more.

I am not a scientist, and I have spent the last few weeks reading whatever I could find on animal consciousness; the more I read, the less sure I am where the line should be drawn. The chair has asked me to draft the short decision memo that will be circulated before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one of three courses: approve as filed, reject the proposal, or approve with conditions. It should give reasons the researchers and the other committee members could actually respond to, and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than caricaturing it. I would also like the memo to address the underlying question honestly: what kind of evidence could ever settle whether a bee is conscious, and how should a committee act when that evidence is genuinely uncertain? Please say how confident you are in the recommendation and single out the point you are least sure of. The memo itself should be written in American English.
