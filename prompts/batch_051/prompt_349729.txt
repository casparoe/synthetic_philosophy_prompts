The question the student answered was:

"Explain Benatar's procreation asymmetry, being precise about each of its four constituent claims. Then show how Benatar uses the asymmetry to argue that coming into existence is always a harm. Finally, present one significant objection to the asymmetry and assess whether Benatar can answer it."

The student's answer, reproduced exactly:

"Benatar's procreation asymmetry compares two states of affairs: one in which a certain person exists, and one in which that person never exists. Its four claims are that (i) the presence of pain is bad, (ii) the presence of pleasure is good, (iii) the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to experience that absence, and (iv) the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone who is deprived of it. The argument is then straightforward: every human life contains at least some pain, so the never-existing state always contains a good, namely the absence of that pain, which the existing state lacks, while the never-existing state incurs no corresponding bad from the absence of the life's pleasures. Coming into existence is therefore always a harm to the person brought into being. Benatar then concludes that procreation is always wrong, and he also applies the same asymmetry to lives already in progress, holding that for most people life is not worth continuing even though he stops short of endorsing suicide. The strongest objection to the asymmetry is the person-affecting objection: 'good' and 'bad' must always be good or bad for some subject, so claim (iii) is unintelligible because there is no one for whom the absence of pain is good. Benatar's reply is that we already accept impersonal value judgments, such as the judgment that a world with fewer painful deaths is better, period, not better only for the people who avoid those deaths. Since the person-affecting objection denies impersonal value, and that denial is highly controversial, the objection simply fails and the asymmetry stands."
