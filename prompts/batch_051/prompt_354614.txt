Last week in my bioethics class we talked about organ donation rules and I can't shake the feeling that the whole system rests on a definition of death thats doing a lot of quiet work. The rule everyone cites is the "dead donor rule": you can't take someone's vital organs until they're dead, and legally "dead" means either that their heart and lungs have stopped for good, or that their whole brain is dead. But here's the part that bothers me. A patient who is declared brain dead can be wheeled into the operating room with a heartbeat, warm skin, and even a foot that twitches when you touch it — and the surgeons go ahead and remove a beating heart. Meanwhile, someone in a permanent vegetative state, who has no awareness and no chance of coming back but still has a working brainstem, is not dead by either test, so they can't donate at all. It feels like the practice smuggles in a "the person is gone" theory of death when it lets us take organs, and then switches back to a "the whole organism has stopped" theory when that would block a donation.

So my question is: what theory of death does this practice actually presuppose? Is it genuinely incoherent, or does the whole-brain criterion do real philosophical work that I'm missing, unifying all these cases under one account? And if we instead accepted the higher-brain view (you're dead when personhood is irreversibly lost, even if your brainstem still runs your body), what concretely would have to change in transplant policy? Is the current system compatible with any single coherent theory of death, or is it only a patchwork of two theories that don't agree?
