I’m in a decision theory reading group, and a dispute keeps recurring that I’d like you to adjudicate. We’ve been working through the principle of total evidence, and I’m attracted to the standard result usually called Good’s theorem: for an expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization, the value of free information can never be negative. The proof is compelling. Before you see the evidence, your expected utility conditional on receiving it and then choosing some act is at least as high as your expected utility from declining it, because you could always ignore the evidence and do the same thing you would have done without it; if the evidence is irrelevant to the choice, you lose nothing, and if it is relevant, you gain by selecting a better act. And yet I have a firm intuition I cannot shake. Suppose I am offered a free genetic test for a currently untreatable condition, where the result will not affect any medical decision, any financial decision, or any decision about how to allocate my remaining time. If the result is bad, I will simply know that I carry the condition and will likely suffer in a particular way, and I would rather not know. I do not think I am confused or information-averse in some merely irrational sense. I think the knowledge itself changes the value of my situation, and that this gives me a reason to refuse the test.

I want you to approach this as a professional decision theorist and philosopher of rationality. Begin by stating carefully what Good’s theorem does and does not show, and then lay out the strongest case for the theorem’s practical conclusion that I should not decline free information and the strongest case for my intuition that, in the genetic-test case, refusal is rational. A central issue is whether the theorem already contains the obvious reply—that any psychic cost from knowing is just another consequence to be included in my utility function, in which case the information was never truly free, and the theorem is not in trouble—or whether that response stretches expected-utility theory in a way that makes the theorem vacuous for exactly the cases where information seems to matter to us. I do not just want a verdict. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theorem to be restricted to a narrower class of cases than people usually claim, and whether there is a reading on which the theorem and the intuition are compatible. Please think carefully and give me the strongest version of each position before saying which move you would make and why.
