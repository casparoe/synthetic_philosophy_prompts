I'm a philosophy PhD student, and I've been dragooned into setting the take-home final for an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "What Counts as Evidence in Philosophy?" The course is about the uneasy relationship between intuitions about particular cases and general principles, and I want the essay question to hit transitivity head-on, with simplicity dragged in as the apparent tiebreaker.

Here's the case I want the question built around. Take a spectrum of outcomes A0,...,A100. A0 is an ordinary pain-free afternoon; each step increases the pain by an amount too small to reliably discriminate from the one before (a fraction of a percent in intensity, a few seconds of duration, whatever); A100 is hours of severe migraine. For each adjacent pair, the intuition goes: if a subject cannot tell the difference between two outcomes, then neither is all-things-considered better than the other. But everyone agrees A100 is worse than A0. So one of the pieces has to give — either 'not worse than' isn't transitive after all, or 'can't tell the difference' doesn't guarantee 'not worse,' or one of the adjacent-pair intuitions is wrong. I want the students to have to say which, and at what cost, and whether parsimony can legitimately break the tie.

Please write: (1) the essay question itself (roughly 150 words, worth 30% of the final, students have 48 hours), (2) a model answer of around 700 words, and (3) a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer has to make, and each penalty should be tied to an identifiable mistake — e.g. confusing intransitivity with incomparability, or treating case intuitions as either self-certifying or worthless. Don't make the question lead the students to the answer.

Also: in the model answer and the marking scheme, explicitly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this stuff, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. If the right response to the spectrum is genuinely disputed, the key should say so and reward students for noticing the dispute.
