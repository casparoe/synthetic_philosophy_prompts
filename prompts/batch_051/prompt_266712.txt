I work on a forecasting team at a mid-sized insurer, and I've developed a rule that keeps annoying my colleagues. Whenever someone argues that method X is "the best," the evidence they reach for is always a streak of internal bake-offs: X won on churn, then on claims frequency, then on fraud flags. My rule is that those past wins count for nothing unless the person can say why the next problem is drawn from the same class of problems as the bake-offs — not just "it's still insurance data, so probably," but an actual shared structure in how the data is generated. I don't mean they count for a bit: I set them to zero. My reasoning is that the no-free-lunch theorem says that, averaged over all possible problems, no learning algorithm is better than any other, so a method that looks good on ten datasets has only shown it fits the particular distribution those datasets came from; if you can't show the next problem is sampled from that same distribution, the streak is just a local fact about those ten datasets. My colleague Priya says I've taken a theorem about all possible worlds and turned it into an excuse to ignore the one world we actually live in, and that I'm demanding a kind of proof of similarity that induction itself can never provide. She says she's perfectly happy to bet that the next insurance prediction problem is more like past insurance prediction problems than like a random function, and that that's all the assumption she needs. Last week this actually mattered: we had to choose between the model that won the last three bake-offs and a bespoke model a new hire had built specifically for this problem, with no track record at all. I held out for the bespoke one, not because I thought it was better, but because under my rule the incumbent's whole record read as blank. Two people on the team now say I'm not being careful, I'm just being stubborn.

So: is my policy defensible, or am I making the perfect the enemy of a decent working approximation? Under what conditions does a streak of success on past problems genuinely warrant expecting success on the next one, and does the no-free-lunch theorem bite the way I think it does, or is it mostly beside the point once you grant that real problems aren't drawn uniformly from the space of all possible functions? If my actual rule is too strict, what's the version of it I should actually keep — and where should I draw the line between a fair demand for a similarity argument and a refusal to let induction get off the ground?
