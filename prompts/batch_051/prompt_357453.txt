In a discussion about moral theory choice, someone defended a simple, single-principle view against a pluralist theory of prima facie duties by appealing to an analogy: choosing a moral theory is like choosing a scientific theory. In science, we prefer hypotheses with fewer free parameters and fewer ad hoc fixes, and we do not abandon a well-confirmed theory every time an observation fails to fit—we hold the anomaly aside and look for error. So, he argued, we should prefer the moral theory with the fewest basic principles and treat scattered counterexample intuitions as anomalies to be explained away rather than as immediate refutations. I would like you to audit this analogy. Give an explicit mapping of correspondences: what in moral theorizing is supposed to play the role of the data, the theory's parameters, the predictions, the experiment, the auxiliary assumptions, and the anomaly? Then identify where the analogy breaks in ways that matter for the conclusion—for instance, whether moral intuitions stand to moral reality as observations stand to physical reality, whether simplicity plays the same epistemic role in both domains, and whether moral theories make predictions in the relevant sense. Finally, say whether the conclusion about tolerating the intuitive costs of a simple theory survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy for moral-theory choice might be if this one fails.
