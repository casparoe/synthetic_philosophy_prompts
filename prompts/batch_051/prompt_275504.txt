I am a novelist, and I am designing an institution for a novel set in a city-state whose legal order is built on a particular reading of the common prior assumption: two ideal reasoners who begin from the same prior cannot rationally persist in disagreement once their evidence has been pooled and each knows the other's conclusion. The founders concluded that the disagreement that remains in ordinary society must therefore be caused by unequal evidence or unequal priors, and that a well-designed institution could remove both sources. The result is the Register.

Every citizen, upon coming of age, must file one personal prior: a probability distribution over a finite, statutorily fixed list of factual questions that may come to be contested in litigation. A filed prior may be revised at most once per decade, and only at a public hearing at which the citizen must announce any recent change in occupation, property, or domestic position. When two citizens litigate, the tribunal does not weigh the evidence each side presents in the ordinary manner. It compels both parties to deposit all evidence they hold concerning the contested questions, punishes deliberate withholding or fabrication, and then constructs a synthetic common prior by mixing the two litigants' filed priors with the prior of a randomly drawn citizen at weight one-third each. It conditions this synthetic prior on the pooled evidence, treats the resulting posterior as its findings of fact, with any contested proposition taken as established when the posterior assigns it probability greater than one-half, and applies the ordinary law to those findings. It then apportions the cost of the proceeding against the party whose filed prior lies farther from the posterior under the tribunal's published measure of divergence, reasoning that the farther prior contributed more to producing a disagreement that should not have survived the Register.

Analyze this institution as it would actually operate. What would it incentivize citizens to do when they first file a prior, when they petition for revision, when they decide whether to settle, and when they present evidence? How could a skilled litigant or adviser game the filing rules, the decennial hearings, the random-citizen weight, the evidence-pooling rule, or the cost-shifting rule? Where does the founding reading of the common prior assumption fail to do the work expected of it, and where would the mechanism relocate disagreement rather than remove it? What second-order effects would emerge across the city? Finally, which of its failure modes could be repaired within the Register, and which would require abandoning the founders' premise? Does the scheme select for genuine convergence, or for something else?
