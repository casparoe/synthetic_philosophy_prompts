I have come to accept, on reflection, a position about musical emotion, and I hold an intuition that cuts against it, and I cannot decide which of them should give way. The position is that music is not literally sad, because only a creature with a felt life can be in an emotional state, and a pattern of sound has no such life. What we call a sad piece of music is, on this view, a pattern that resembles the outward expression of sadness — slow tempo, soft dynamics, a drooping melodic contour — together with a measure of convention governing particular modes and intervals; many of the features we reach for, such as the minor mode, seem to carry their associations by training rather than by nature. The theory's virtues are real: it keeps the concept of an emotion from collapsing into metaphor, it explains how music moves us through mechanisms of resemblance and association that we already accept, and it does not posit an unanalysed expressive property somehow lodged in the sound. Yet the intuition will not give way. When a passage turns to the minor, or a voice sinks over a descending bass in a requiem, I do not merely judge that the music resembles sadness or brings it to mind; I hear the sadness as a quality the sound itself has, in something like the way I hear a chord as dissonant rather than merely as something that cues a feeling of tension. I want the strongest version of both sides on the table. Then tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of the word 'sad' — or a redescription of the experience — on which both can survive. I am not asking which side wins; I am asking what each concession would cost, and how I would know I was making the right one.
