The standard view is that indexicals—words such as I, now, here, and this—cannot be eliminated without loss: their standing meaning underdetermines what they contribute in a context, and descriptive substitutes such as the speaker of this utterance or the time at which it is produced appear to smuggle an indexical back in. Suppose someone claims something stronger, namely that a fully workable natural language could in principle lack pronouns and indexicals altogether, relying instead on proper names, context-dependent descriptions, and gesture to do the same work. Treat that claim as empirical to the extent that it can be, rather than as a purely verbal dispute. Design the strongest test you can: construct a miniature artificial language with no pronouns and no indexicals, teach it to human subjects, and run them through tasks that seem to demand self-location or temporal localization—warning others of a danger whose location matters only relative to the speaker, coordinating two agents who see different scenes, recounting one's own earlier actions without a first-person form, or tracking an object across a conversation without this, that, or it. Specify concretely what pattern of success or failure would count for each side, and be clear about what a successful outcome would establish. Then separate the questions such a test is apt to run together: could such a language be learned and used fluently; could its speakers pass the tasks without indexical content being smuggled in somewhere in the setup; and would any residual failure be a fact about the language or about the underlying thought? Finally, say what no behavioral test could settle—including whether speakers might be thinking de se (about themselves) or de nunc (about the present moment) thoughts that their language simply declines to express.
