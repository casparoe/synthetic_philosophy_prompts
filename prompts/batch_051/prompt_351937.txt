Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about the backward induction paradox, and I ended the evening genuinely uncertain which side of me was right. I am drawn to the theory: in a finite sequential game with common knowledge of the structure, the payoffs, and the players' rationality, backward induction yields the unique subgame-perfect path. No individual step seems weak. In the centipede game this means the first mover should end the game at the first node, even when the accumulated pot is still tiny, because the same reasoning swallows every later node from the end. I also have a firm intuition that two intelligent, well-motivated people actually playing such a game for meaningful stakes would not stop at the first move, and that a player who passes early is not making an obvious mistake. My friend insists the intuition is simply a refusal to accept the logic: if you grant the premises, and if the game is finite, first-move exit is the only coherent prediction, and any inclination to pass reveals that you are secretly assuming either irrationality or uncertainty about the other player. I cannot find a bad premise, and yet I cannot bring myself to say the passers are being irrational.

To make it concrete, here is the version we argued about. Two players, A and B, alternate over 60 moves; A moves on odd moves, B on even moves. A pot starts at $100 and grows by 20 percent each time a player passes. If a player takes, the game ends, the taker receives 70 percent of the current pot and the other receives 30 percent. If both pass through all 60 moves, they divide the final pot equally. Backward induction is immediate: at move 60, B takes, since 70 percent is better than 50 percent; so at move 59 A takes, since taking now yields 70 percent of the current pot while passing yields 30 percent of a pot grown by only 20 percent; and the same logic runs all the way back to move 1. I understand that derivation completely and cannot locate the error.

What I want is not a verdict that simply picks the theory or the intuition, but an account of what would justify each possible resolution. Specifically: should my intuition be debunked as an artifact of not really crediting common knowledge of rationality and of the jointly known final node, and how would one tell that this is the right diagnosis rather than a convenient dismissal? Should the theory be amended or abandoned, for example by introducing small probabilities of cooperative or irrational types, uncertainty about the finite horizon, signaling or reputation-like considerations, or a distinction between what is rational in a formal model and what is reasonable in an interpersonal encounter, and what would count as evidence that the amendment is principled rather than ad hoc? Or is there a reading on which the two are compatible because backward induction is a claim about equilibrium in a fully specified game while the intuition is a claim about what real agents with rich and mutually observed minds would do when the game is presented to them, and how would I test whether that compatibility is genuine rather than equivocation? If you had to assign blame, how much should be assigned to the intuition, how much to the theory, and how much to the tacit assumption that a single word like rational must mean the same thing in both?
