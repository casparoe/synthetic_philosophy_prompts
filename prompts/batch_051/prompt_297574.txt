I am a novelist, and I am currently writing a protagonist who is a committed utilitarian — not a caricature, but someone who can state and defend her principle under real pressure. While researching her, I have been reading Mill's Utilitarianism against Rawls's A Theory of Justice, and I have run into two claims that look as though they cannot both be accepted at face value.

In Utilitarianism, Mill characterizes the standard of right action as the general happiness rather than the agent's own. The agent is to hold her own happiness and that of everyone else in exactly the same regard, appraising conduct roughly as a disinterested and benevolent spectator would. Later in the book, when he tries to make the principle plausible, he reasons that because each person desires her own happiness, each person's happiness counts as a good, and that the general happiness is what you get by summing these separate goods across persons.

In A Theory of Justice, Rawls argues that classical utilitarianism gets its social principle by taking a pattern of rational choice appropriate to one person over time — who may sensibly accept a present loss for a larger later gain — and applying it to society as a whole. Sacrifices imposed on some then get justified by the larger sum of satisfactions enjoyed by others. For Rawls, this is already the mistake: it reasons as if there were a single composite person whose losses and gains could be traded off against one another, and it thereby fails to take the distinction between separate persons seriously.

Read alongside each other, Mill appears to be describing precisely the impartial summing that Rawls says dissolves distinct persons into one; yet Mill presents that same summing as equal regard for each person, not as the erasure of separateness. I need to know whether these two can be reconciled, and at what cost.
