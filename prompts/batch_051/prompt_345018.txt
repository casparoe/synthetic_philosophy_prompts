I'm putting together a sorting quiz for a discussion group on epistemology, and I'd like the quiz to diagnose which response to the epistemic regress problem a person actually holds, rather than which label they prefer. The regress problem, as I want to frame it, is the Münchhausen trilemma: any reason offered for a belief can face the question why that reason should be trusted, so a chain of justification must either continue without end, loop back on itself, or halt at something asserted without further support. The quiz should be called What You Actually Do at the End of the Line.

Make it eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete case in which someone is pressed for further reasons: a juror asked to justify trusting their memory, a student asked why modus ponens is truth-preserving, a researcher asked to defend induction, a dinner guest asked to prove the table exists, and similar ordinary justificatory pressures. Give each question two or three answer options that capture different intuitive responses to the regress. No option should be a disguised way of saying "I am a foundationalist" or any other named position, and no question should presuppose the labels.

Provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least these leaves: classical foundationalism, fallible or modest foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism, and a skeptical or Pyrrhonian stance. Build the key from the space of possible answer combinations first, and then locate the named positions in that space. Where a combination is unoccupied, say whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or like an underexplored position. In particular, try to operationalize two distinctions: whether the respondent treats a justificatory stopping point as a defect in the belief or as the normal end of a practice of giving reasons, and whether a wide, revisable circle of mutually supporting beliefs can transmit or raise justification rather than merely test it for coherence. I would also like the quiz to address whether a contextualist or pragmatic stopping-place view — roughly, that justification may stop when a shared context supplies a purpose, and that this is no failure — is a genuinely distinct leaf, or collapses into one of the above positions given the cases you write.

The person taking the quiz should not need to know any technical vocabulary, and the scoring rules should be mechanical enough that someone could classify a batch of completed quizzes without guessing. End by saying how confident you are in the overall mapping, and single out the one question, leaf, or scoring rule you are least sure of.
