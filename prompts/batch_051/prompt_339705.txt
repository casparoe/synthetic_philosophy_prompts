Please give a careful conceptual explanation of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and, more specifically, of the role that the "impartial observer" construction plays in turning it into an argument for utilitarianism.

I am not looking for a historical survey or a formal proof review; I want to understand the structure of the argument. My current understanding, which may be wrong, is roughly this: if each individual's preferences satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and if a social preference ordering also satisfies those axioms together with some kind of Pareto condition, then the social utility function can be represented as a weighted sum of individual utilities. Harsanyi then introduces an observer choosing among possible "identity lotteries"—an equal chance of being any person in society—and argues that this observer's expected-utility-maximizing preferences yield an unweighted sum, or average, of individual utilities.

What I find puzzling is the conceptual move from a theorem about the mathematical form of an aggregation rule to a substantive moral conclusion. In particular:

1. In what sense, if any, does the impartial observer construction justify the equal weights, as opposed to merely restating a prior commitment to impartiality in probabilistic language?
2. Where exactly does interpersonal comparability of utility enter or fail to enter? The von Neumann–Morgenstern framework is usually said to involve only intrapersonal comparisons, since utility representations are unique only up to positive affine transformations of a single person's preferences, but the summed social utility function seems to require more than that.
3. Is the theorem best read as showing that utilitarianism follows from rational constraints plus impartiality, or as showing only that if one has already accepted a fairly specific impartial expected-utility representation, one gets a utilitarian-looking formula?

Please explain this in a way that makes the philosophical stakes vivid—why the result has been thought significant, and which premise a non-utilitarian should most plausibly resist.
