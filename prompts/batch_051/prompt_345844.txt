The more I read about data-protection law, the less I get the foundational idea. Pretty much every privacy regime has a carve-out: once a dataset is "anonymized" or "de-identified" — names and direct identifiers stripped — it stops being treated as personal data at all. You can share it, sell it, combine it with other datasets, whatever; no consent, no purpose limits, no access rights. Yet re-identification researchers have shown over and over that this stuff gets linked back to individuals with surprisingly little effort, and the same legal systems are extremely strict about identifiable data being used for even trivial purposes. So I'm trying to pin down what view of privacy this practice actualy presupposes. Does it presuppose that the harm to privacy is exhausted by a link between data and a name, rather than by what can be inferred about a person? Is that theory coherent given re-identification? And what would a privacy regime look like if it instead presupposed that privacy protects inferences about people, or the contexts of information flow, rather than identifiers? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined by it.
