I've been going in circles on whether there can be real reasons without anyone whose reason they are. My gut says different things on different cases, and I can't find the line.

Take the house fire. It was faulty wiring, nobody was negligent, it just happened. I still think the fire ought not to have happened. Not just "it would have been better if it hadn't"—a genuine ought, but there's no one who ought to have done anything differently. I stand by that.

Then the neighbor's cat. My neighbor's in the hospital and his cat hasn't been fed. I think someone ought to feed the cat. But I also think that's not really true until there's a definite person—the cousin, the guy two doors down—whose responsibility it becomes. An unowned "someone ought" feels like it's missing a piece until it lands on somebody.

Then the pre-human climate. I think it's true that before any people existed, there was reason for the climate not to tip into a runaway greenhouse. No one was around to have that reason, and the climate can't have reasons. That doesn't bother me.

Then the drowning kid. If I'm the only person on the beach, the reason to jump in is mine, obviously. But I'm not sure whether it's a free-floating reason that landed on me once I became the only one who could act, or a completely different kind of thing.

Then the brutalized soldier. He's been so ground down that he can't see any reason to disobey an order he still, on some level, knows is monstrous. I want to say the reason not to obey exists, and is his, even though it isn't in his head in any useful sense. But that's weird—a reason with an owner who can't get at it.

And the species. I think the species ought not to go extinct, and I don't think that ought waits for any person or institution to be responsible for it. No hedging on that one.

The pair I can't reconcile: I'll say the fire ought not to have happened with no one to blame, but I won't say "someone ought to apologize" unless there's a particular person who wronged someone. Apology-oughts seem to need an owner; fire-oughts don't. Why?

I had one tentative idea: maybe the split is between oughts about how the world should be (those can float) and oughts about what someone should do (those need an agent). But the drowning kid case is about what someone should do, and the reason still seems to become mine... which sounds right, but i'm honestly not sure the principle survives it.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about cases I haven't listed—like "there ought to be a cure for this disease" when no one alive could find one, or "there's a reason for no one to enter the room" when five specific people are standing right outside? And which of my verdicts do I have to give up?
