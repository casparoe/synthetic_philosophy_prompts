I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I would like to check whether the following reasoning holds up. I have been trying to classify large language model hallucinations: cases in which a model gives a fluent, confident, factual-sounding answer that is false or has no grounding in its training data, such as an invented case citation, a nonexistent API parameter, or a fabricated historical date.

Recently I encountered Harry Frankfurt's distinction between lying and bullshit. As I understand it, a liar deliberately asserts something he believes to be false in order to mislead. A bullshitter is different: he is not necessarily trying to get anyone to believe a specific falsehood. He is indifferent to whether what he says is true; he speaks because the words are useful to him in some other way, such as impressing an audience or getting through an occasion, and he does not take the truth to be the constraint on what he says.

That distinction suggested the following argument to me:

(1) Lying, in the ordinary sense, requires an agent that can intend to deceive: something that believes or at least suspects the claim is false and wants the hearer to accept it.
(2) A large language model has no beliefs, wants, or intentions in the literal sense, so it cannot lie, however false or misleading its output may be.
(3) Bullshit, as Frankfurt describes it, is likewise not a property of the words alone. It is a way a speaker relates to what he is saying: he knowingly sets the question of truth aside and speaks anyway, because his real aim lies elsewhere.
(4) A large language model does not set questions aside, has no real aim of its own, and merely samples from a learned distribution, so it cannot literally adopt the bullshitter's relation to speech.
(5) If a hallucinated assertion is neither a lie nor bullshit, then the remaining option is that it is an error: an output that fails to meet the standard a user reasonably brings to a factual question, namely that the answer be correct.
(6) Therefore, model hallucinations are errors, not lies or bullshit.

The conclusion feels too quick, but I cannot see where the inference breaks. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, which premises carry the weight, and is each of them true? If a step fails, say exactly which step fails and what the strongest corrected version of that step would be.
