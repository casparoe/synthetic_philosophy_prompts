There is a structural resemblance I cannot stop noticing between prototype theory in the psychology of concepts and word embeddings in computational semantics, and I want a rigorous assessment of whether it is deep or superficial.

On the prototype side, a concept is a summary representation: a central tendency over weighted features abstracted from encountered instances, and categorization is a matter of graded similarity to that prototype. On the embedding side, a word is a dense vector in a high-dimensional space induced from co-occurrence statistics; semantic relatedness is measured by geometric nearness such as cosine similarity, centroids over vectors yield category-level representations, and vector arithmetic captures analogy-like regularities. Both are pictures on which meaning is a position in a similarity space learned from distributional experience, and both make typicality fall out of distance from a center.

What I want is a precise verdict on this mapping. Is a dense embedding vector best interpreted as a prototype, and if so, in what sense are its dimensions features? Or is the shared geometry doing different work in the two theories? State the mapping, say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one literature to the other, and identify at least one concrete claim the parallel predicts that turns out false—for example, about how failures of prototype composition such as the pet-fish case, where the most typical pet fish is a poor example of either a pet or a fish, should appear in embedding space, or about whether embedding-based typicality should behave like conceptual typicality. Where do the structures match, and where do they come apart?
