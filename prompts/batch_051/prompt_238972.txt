You are a moral philosopher specializing in exploitation and reproductive ethics. I want a working definition of exploitation precise enough to do real classificatory work in debates about paid surrogacy and egg donation, and precise enough to distinguish exploitation from the distinct charge of commodification. Treat the European-bans-versus-American-market contrast as a rough idealization: many European jurisdictions prohibit commercial surrogacy and payment for egg donation on the ground that paying for eggs or gestation itself is the wrong-making feature, while several US states permit both when the arrangement is voluntary, informed, and compensated. I am not asking for legal detail or citations; I want the moral concept.

Test your definition against the following cases, and say where it surprises you or forces revision:

1. A US college student sells her eggs for $8,000 to pay tuition; she is fully informed about the short-term risks and has alternative ways to borrow, though at high interest.
2. A woman in a lower-income country works as a gestational carrier for foreign intended parents for a fee equal to roughly two years of local wages; the agency's fee is several times larger than hers, and she cannot read the contract's legal language.
3. A woman carries a pregnancy for her sister without payment, under steady family pressure; she agrees but privately resents it.
4. A woman donates eggs six times in two years at $10,000 per cycle after a clinic advertises the process as safe, easy money, while its staff downplay the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome.
5. A financially secure professional woman serves as a compensated surrogate in a US state because she enjoyed her own pregnancies and wants to help; she is paid $45,000 with strong contractual protections.
6. A man is paid $75 for a sperm donation that the sperm bank later sells for several hundred dollars per vial.
7. A woman sells a kidney to a broker for $6,000 because she has no other way to pay her family's medical debt.

I lean toward calling cases 2, 4, and 7 exploitation; cases 1, 5, and 6 not exploitation; and I am genuinely unsure about case 3. Use those leanings as data, but protect the definition rather than my intuitions.

Begin by briefly distinguishing unfair-advantage, harm, and impaired-consent notions of exploitation. Then propose the single best definition you can; run it through all seven cases; and finally say whether the European objection is best understood as an objection to exploitation at all, or as an anti-commodification view, and where your definition draws that boundary.
