For the new season of a legal-philosophy podcast, I am interviewing a legal philosopher in the positivist tradition who has been arguing, in public talks and essays, that the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals were legally illegitimate at their core, however welcome their outcomes. On his view, the charges of crimes against peace and crimes against humanity did not correspond to enforceable criminal law at the time the acts were committed, so the tribunals were applying law retroactively in violation of the principle of legality (nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege); he treats that principle not as a technicality but as the constitutive rule separating courts from political violence. He is remarkably good at deflecting the obvious objections. When his interlocutors point to the moral enormity of the defendants' acts, he answers that this is a settled premise and does no legal work; when they invoke natural law or the moral conscience of humanity, he answers that a court can either apply law that existed when the acts were done or legislate retrospectively under the name of adjudication, and that the second option should be called politics rather than justice. I want the interview to test this position rather than merely elicit it, and to avoid turning into a recital of the crimes.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each main question followed by a bracketed note stating the reply the guest is most likely to give and a follow-up question keyed to that reply, with a brief explanation of why that follow-up is hard for his position. Organize them under these headings, in this order, with approximate lengths: "Framing the view" (~100 words), "The principle of legality" (~350 words), "Victors' justice and selective prosecution" (~250 words), "The costs of the view" (~150 words), "Closing question" (~100 words). The questions should press the points where the position is under most strain: whether customary international law had in fact crystallized the relevant prohibitions by the 1930s; whether the tribunals could and should have confined themselves to ordinary war crimes that already had a treaty basis; whether the principle of legality is a principle of justice or only a domestic rule of restraint that can be set aside in the face of mass atrocity; whether his critique applies differently at Tokyo than at Nuremberg; the victors' justice objection, including the asymmetry that Allied conduct such as the firebombing of Dresden and the atomic bombings was never prosecuted by any comparable tribunal; and the apparent implication that organized mass killing remains lawful until some positive lawmaker says otherwise. End with a closing question about what institution, had it existed in 1945, or what evidence, had it come to light since, would have led him to accept at least one retroactive prosecution or to revise the retroactivity charge.
