I recently married, and my partner and I spent months arguing about surnames — whether one of us should take the other's, what our children's surnames should be, and what any of this actually means. The experience left me with a set of strong but apparently inconsistent intuitions about what given names and family names do, and I would like to know whether a single principle can be found underneath them. Here are the verdicts I find myself holding. (1) When a close friend changed her given name, I regard the old name and the new name as referring to the same person, and yet I do not think the old name is simply another correct label for her; using it feels like more than a factual error, and I want an account of names to explain that. (2) By contrast, Mary Ann Evans and George Eliot strike me as two ways of picking out one and the same person, with no residue: the pen name is just a public alias. (3) If an adult voluntarily changes their surname at marriage, I think the new surname still refers to the same person and, more surprisingly, that it does not really change which family they belong to either; the surname feels like a legally recorded convention rather than a deep fact. (4) But when a child is given one parent's surname and not the other's, I cannot shake the sense that the surname is doing more than recording a convention: it marks a lineage through one parent, which is why it slightly misrepresents a family in which both parents are equally parents. (5) Patronymic surnames, of the sort common in Iceland, strike me as not being family names at all; they are descriptive tags that happen to sit in the surname slot, and they change every generation. (6) Finally, when a naming convention places the family name first, as in Hungarian or Japanese usage, I do not think the order changes what the names refer to or what roles they play; only the surface order differs.

I suspect that (3) and (4) cannot both be kept as they stand, and perhaps (1) and (2) are in the same kind of tension. Is there a coherent account of what given names, family names, and name changes do — what they refer to, what they signify beyond reference, and when a person may, must, or must not use one name rather than another — that captures most of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, show how it handles each case, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. If several principles fit, give me the strongest candidates and explain what further cases would decide between them.
