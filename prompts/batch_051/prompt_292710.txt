When people report an uncanny coincidence to me — an old friend calling the moment I think of them, two strangers at a wedding discovering they were born on the same day in the same hospital — I follow a fixed rule: I acknowledge that the event is unexpected, then I refuse to let it move any belief. My reasoning is that the birthday paradox, together with the way anecdotes get selected, explains such reports completely. Among all the people, days, meetings, songs, and passing thoughts in a life, collisions of low-probability features are common; the one collision that gets reported is exactly the one noticed. I apply this rule even when a coincidence is offered as evidence for something specific: a real connection, a common cause, fate, or at least a reason to investigate further. Is this rule rationally defensible? The birthday result says a particular pair sharing a birthday is unlikely while some pair in a room sharing one is likely. Does that distinction justify ignoring each reported coincidence, or does it only justify some systematic discount? Can the rule be restated to preserve the sceptical insight without treating every coincidence as void, and how would I tell from the inside whether a given coincidence is noise or genuine evidence? I would also like you to say what would change your verdict: which argument, or which feature of a reported coincidence, would do most to make you reject my rule outright rather than merely refine it.
