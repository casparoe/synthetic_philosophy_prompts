I have been thinking about what it is for something to be fragile, and my verdicts on particular cases refuse to fall under one rule. A wine glass sitting on a stone floor is fragile. The same glass packed in shock-absorbing foam, so that dropping the package would not break it, is still fragile. The same glass fitted with a device that hardens its surface the instant it is struck, so that ordinary knocks no longer break it, is still fragile, though I am less confident. A solid steel ball is not fragile, and I still say it is not fragile if a trickster has arranged to turn it into thin glass and then shatter it should anyone strike it; the knock would leave it in pieces, but that does not make the ball fragile. I also say that a steel ball is not fragile when it contains an internal mechanism that shatters the ball whenever it is knocked, even though, again, the knock would destroy it.

Is there a principle about fragility that captures these verdicts, or close to it? I would like the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each part of it, its verdicts on cases I have not considered, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would also like to know what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a drinking glass treated so that it survives table-top drops but shatters under a firm hammer blow. Please answer in British English.
