In a working group on whether genetic data should receive special legal protection, one participant has been arguing from the claim that DNA is a code. His argument runs roughly as follows. The genome is an information-bearing text: the four bases are an alphabet, the genetic code is a cipher mapping nucleotide triplets to amino acids, transcription and translation are reading and execution, replication is a copying process, and a point mutation is a typo. From this he concludes that there is a fact of the matter about what a genome says, that a replication mistake is an error in the text rather than simply a variant, and that the genome can be said to carry misinformation, not merely different information.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy rather than a yes-or-no verdict. Please give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the alphabet, the word, the sentence, the message, the sender, the reader, the typo, and the meaning. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusions being drawn from the disanalogies that are harmless for those conclusions. In particular, address which of the participant's three conclusions — that a genome has a determinate content, that replication mistakes are errors in more than name, and that a genome can carry misinformation — survive once the disanalogies are on the table. Say whether some weaker version of the code claim is well-founded and, if so, what work it can still do.

Please indicate how confident you are in the overall assessment and single out the point you are least sure of. Please write in American English.
