I have been teaching the fragments of Pascal on justice and force, and I find myself caught between a theory I am drawn to and an intuition I cannot easily surrender. The view I take from Pascal is roughly this: no political order can rest on justice alone, because justice without power is inert, while force without some semblance of justice is merely arbitrary. What actually stabilizes a legal order is established custom backed by power. The theoretical virtues of this position are substantial. It explains why legal systems persist without having to establish their justice. It avoids grounding political obligation in contestable claims about what is right by nature. And it gives a clear practical rule: defer to the settled and effective law of one's society rather than to one's private judgment.

But I also have a firm intuition that an effective legal order is not automatically a legitimate one. If a regime reliably enforces its commands, that is a fact about its power and its stability, not about its moral authority. The intuition is clearest in cases where a regime is both highly effective and deeply unjust. I want to say that durability is one thing and entitlement to obedience is another, and that losing the difference would leave us unable to criticize any regime that succeeds at enforcement. The theory seems to collapse the very distinction my judgments of injustice depend on.

I can see three possible ways to resolve the clash. The first is that the intuition should yield: the distinction between effectiveness and legitimacy is a moralistic overlay, and the realistic description of political life is that effective order is the only standard we actually have. The second is that the reading of the view is too strong: the point is not that justice does not exist or does not matter, but that human partiality makes it impossible to identify or agree on justice reliably before effective institutions exist, so custom and force are epistemic fallbacks rather than replacements for justice. The third is that the theory should yield in its strong form: it conflates the question of why a legal order persists with the question of why it obligates, and the intuition exposes that conflation.

Give me the strongest version of each of these three positions, then say which one should yield. I want to know what would justify giving up the intuition, what would justify defending the theory by weakening it, and what would justify rejecting the theory's stronger claim. In particular, distinguish what the theory can concede while retaining its explanatory power from what it cannot concede without ceasing to be the view I was drawn to in the first place.
