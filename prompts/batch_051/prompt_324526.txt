I am a novelist, and the book I am working on now centers on a merchant in an eighteenth-century port city who is scrupulously honest in his commercial dealings and quietly ruthless in his private attachments. At a dinner with two other novelists, the discussion turned to whether such a person is psychologically coherent, and it quickly became a wider argument about whether market society forms character or deforms it. Neither convinced the other, and I have not been able to decide on my own whose position is the stronger. The exchange, as I reconstruct it from my notes:

1. Elena began from the civilizing view. A commercial society runs on dealings between strangers, so it trains people in punctuality, promise-keeping, and civil treatment, because a merchant's reputation is her capital. Over decades those behaviors stop being cold calculations and settle into second nature. The market civilizes not by making anyone virtuous from the inside but by making decency a person's interest first and a person's habit afterward.

2. Marcus answered that the sleight of hand occurs in exactly the move to second nature. A habit installed because it paid under observability is not the virtue of honesty; it is a conditioned response calibrated to the chance of being found out. And the deeper lesson the market teaches is the constant translation of everything — a promise, a friendship, an obligation — into terms of better or worse exchange. That habit of pricing things is less civilization than a corruption of the capacity to value what is not for sale.

3. Elena came back that he was appealing to an unobservable inner state. The only currency of character we actually have is conduct. A merchant whose honesty has survived fifty years of real temptation, including obvious chances to defect, has demonstrated the stability Marcus says he lacks; to convict him on the strength of an imagined world where nobody would find out is to judge character from a world with no witnesses and no evidence. A theory of character that must reach into counterfactual worlds to condemn an actual, spotless record has cut itself off from character as we know it.

4. Marcus replied that the counterfactual is not a fantasy; it is already inside this world. Markets continually manufacture one-off transactions, asymmetries of information, panics, and the chance to defect without consequence, and the historical record is full of impeccably polished market actors defecting in just those gaps. Stability across circumstances is the definition of a trait. So a merchant whose honesty applies only within the fine print of incentives has not acquired honesty; he has acquired a role. And a society that mistakes role-playing for virtue does not civilize people; it trains them to perform.

5. Elena said that roles are the only thing that has ever run a society larger than a village, and the market's version of them is uniquely good at spreading decent dealing to strangers. A disposition stable across every circumstance one can engineer to defeat it describes a saint, not an inhabitant of any known society. If a society of such role-players treats strangers more reliably and with less violence than the alternatives actually on offer, then what Marcus calls role-playing and what he calls character have converged in practice. The remainder is a private ideal, and she saw no reason to treat it as the real thing.

At that point the evening broke up, so I never heard Marcus's rejoinder. Since my own interest is the kind of person it is possible for my character to be, I have been turning the argument over without reaching a verdict. I would like a point-by-point adjudication: for each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, dubious, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
