I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a well-meaning protagonist who repeatedly shields a younger relative from the consequences of his gambling, borrowing, and broken promises. Each rescue is sincerely compassionate, yet each one leaves the young man more passive, more reckless, and more certain that he will be saved again. Writing these chapters has pushed me toward a philosophical disagreement I would like help in thinking through. Schopenhauer took pity — the immediate, non-egoistic participation in another's suffering — to be the one genuine source of moral action. Nietzsche regarded pity with deep suspicion: he thought it was often masked contempt, a way of keeping the pitied person weak and turning his misery into something displayed, and therefore a way of adding to the world's suffering rather than relieving it. As I worked, I noticed that Nietzsche's worry resembles an idea from the economics of insurance: moral hazard, the tendency of a guarantee against loss to alter the protected person's behaviour so that the loss becomes more likely. The analogy would be that pity stands to a person's agency roughly as insurance stands to risk-taking — a benevolent intervention that can elicit the very conduct it is meant to cushion.

I cannot decide whether this parallel is deep or merely verbal. Moral hazard seems to presuppose that the protected agent responds to incentives with enough foresight to change his behaviour, whereas Nietzsche's critique might not require calculation at all: the damage could run through habituation, loss of self-respect, or the subtle contempt carried by the one who pities. Schopenhauer, for his part, could reply that true pity aims at the other's genuine good and therefore would know when help merely deepens helplessness. Is there a structural correspondence between these two cases, and if so, what exactly maps onto what? Where does the analogy break, and what would a novelist have to get right about pity for the parallel to illuminate a character rather than flatten him?
