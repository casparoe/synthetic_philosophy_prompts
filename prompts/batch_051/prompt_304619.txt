I'm a reporter who covers open-source and tech culture, and I've spent a few weeks talking to people about the GPL and what counts as a derivative work. One lawyer on the FSF side kept reaching for the same analogy: a proprietary program that links against a GPL library is like a book that lifts ten pages out of someone else's novel and then declares itself an independant work because the page numbers were changed. The engineer on the other side kept giving me another picture: linking is like two books being sold side-by-side in the same cardboard box, or a drill and the seperate battery it clicks into.

I can tell these analogies are carrying most of the argument, but nobody in my notebook actually stress-tested them. So I'd like an audit of both. What maps onto what—where do source code, object code, a symbol table, a dynamically loaded library, and a running process fit into each picture? Where does each analogy break, and which breaks matter for the derivative-work question rather than just being loose talk? What does each analogy quietly import from printed books or power tools that software doesn't have? And the question underneath: does the conclusion that linking makes a derivative work—so the GPL legitimately reaches across the linker—survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is it resting on an over-literal picture of what a program is? If neither analogy can bear the weight, what would a better one be?
