I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on moral conflict, and our central question this term is whether there can be genuine moral dilemmas — situations in which two moral requirements conflict without one simply overriding the other, so that every available action is wrong in some respect and the agent cannot avoid wronging someone. One move that has occupied us is the idea of a "moral remainder": even when an action is the right thing to do, it can leave behind regret, a felt need to apologize or compensate, and the thought that something is still owed to the losing party. The anti-dilemma side claims such remainders do not show that the chosen action was wrong, since they can be fully explained by the value the unfulfilled requirement was tracking; the pro-dilemma side treats them as evidence that a real requirement was violated.

I would like you to construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The case should present an ordinary, realistic conflict — a workplace, a family, a school, a hospital — in which the agent faces two requirements of the same general kind, grounded in the same underlying consideration (for instance, two equally serious promises to two different people), so that an anti-dilemma theorist cannot dissolve the conflict by ranking the two requirements or treating one as merely provisional from the outset. The scenario itself should be one paragraph. It should be engineered so that, on a first pass, a defender of genuine dilemmas will confidently say that whatever the agent does she wrongs someone, and that this is not merely an apparent conflict waiting to be ranked. At the same time, the case should be one that the anti-dilemma theorist can describe entirely in terms of a remainder attached to a right action: every morally striking feature of the case — the agent's anguish, the intuition that the losing party has a claim against her, the sense that something must be made up to that party — should be explicable without conceding that she did anything wrong. The point of the case is to stress-test whether the moral-remainder strategy can genuinely absorb the intuitions that ordinarily motivate belief in irresolvable dilemmas.
