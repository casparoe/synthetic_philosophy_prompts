I'm giving a talk to my old reading group next month, and I want a question-period prep sheet rather than a restatement of the talk. The position I'm defending is a modest realism about mechanistic interpretability: at least some of the structure current interpretability methods recover — in particular sparse autoencoder features and the circuits assembled from them by activation patching — is really in the trained network, in the same sense in which a good scientific taxonomy is really in the phenomena and not merely imposed by the observer; the recovered features are a better-than-arbitrary coarse-graining of what the network computes. My argument sketch is as follows. First, if the decompositions were merely imposed, their ability to generate novel, robust, out-of-sample causal predictions — patching and steering interventions whose effects persist across contexts and sometimes transfer across related models — would be a standing coincidence, and realism is the natural explanation for that predictive success. Second, different groups, seeds, and methods keep converging on roughly the same features and circuits, which is also surprising unless the structure is there to be found. Third, the usual fallback — that the residual stream has no privileged basis, so every decomposition is as real as any other — conflates a fact about vector spaces with a fact about computation: most alternative bases do not yield a small, predictive, causally coherent grammar, and the ones that do are precisely the ones interpretability finds. I'll close by claiming the instrumentalist now owes an account of why useful fictions generalize and transfer, and I'll say the burden of proof has shifted.

The audience is about a dozen people I know well. Four are interpretability researchers who build sparse autoencoders themselves but keep insisting, in papers and blog comments, that features are just linearly decodable directions and that there is no fact of the matter about which direction is the feature. Three are ML engineers who think interpretability is not science until someone predicts training dynamics or scaling behavior from a circuit-level account. Two are philosophers of science whose default stance is that representation is relative to an interpretive scheme, and who will press on what the word real is doing in my thesis. The rest are graduate students who will mostly watch. The hard questions will come from the first two groups more than from the philosophers.

Write me the prep sheet. Order the objections by a combination of how likely this audience is to raise them and how much damage they do to the modest-realist position. Phrase each objection the way this specific kind of person would actually put it, say how much I should concede and what I should hold, and give each a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, with a note on where that reply is soft. Include the one question I should be hoping nobody asks, and tell me how to answer it if they do. At the end, say how confident you are in your claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
