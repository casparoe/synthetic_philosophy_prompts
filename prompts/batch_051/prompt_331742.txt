Hey, I'm a software engineer and I've been reading some philosophy on the side, mainly philosophy of science. Lately I've been stuck on Karl Popper's argument in The Poverty of Historicism that there can't be laws of history the way there are laws of physics. The version of the argument I keep thinking about goes roughly: to predict the long-run future of society you'd have to predict what we're going to discover between now and then, and you can't predict now what you'll only know later, because if you could predict it you'd already know it — so a predictive science of history is impossible in principle, not just really hard. I'm probably mangling it a bit.

The thing is, my day job is ML, and a lot of what I work with seems to be doing the thing Popper says can't be done. There are now models trained on huge archives of news, economic indicators, social media, and digitized historical records that forecast civil conflict, food shortages, migration spikes, even short-term coup risk, and some of them beat human experts. Nobody wrote down anything like a law of history; the models are just statistical pattern matchers. But the forecasts are still about large-scale historical processes.

So I can't figure out weather Popper's argument still bites, or whether it was always aimed only at grand historicist theories (Marx, Hegel, that sort), so this new stuff just isn't the same target. I can see a few possible responses: (1) the models only make short-term conditional forecasts, so they don't touch his argument at all; (2) they actually undercut the key premise, because you can forecast the social effects of coming discoveries you yourself can't articulate by latching onto leading indicators in the data; (3) his argument was about laws, not forecasts, so comparing the two is a category mistake. But I don't have enough philosophy background to tell which of these is honest and which is just defending Popper because I like the argument.

Could you help me work through which premise of his argument the ML stuff actually bears on? Mainly I want to know whether the argument survives in its original form, needs a repair, or should be treated as dead for practical purposes even if the logic still goes through.
