I'm taking a graduate seminar on nuclear strategy, and one of the other students—someone who works mostly with formal deterrence models—keeps defending an argument that I find hard to answer, even though its conclusion seems impossible to live with. He sets it out as follows. (1) A nuclear threat deters an adversary only if the adversary believes the threatener would actually carry it out when challenged. (2) A threat is credible only if carrying it out would be in the threatener's interest at the moment of the challenge, not merely at the moment the threat was issued. (3) At that later moment—say, after an invasion of an ally or a limited strike on one city—a massive nuclear response would kill millions and invite retaliation against one's own cities, with no corresponding strategic gain. (4) Rational actors do not knowingly choose an action that is worse for them than the available alternative. (5) So a rational adversary should expect no massive retaliation after it crosses the line. (6) So the threat of massive retaliation was never credible in the first place. (7) So nuclear deterrence cannot actually work through threats of retaliation; if deterrence has worked, its real mechanism must lie somewhere else.

I would sooner abandon almost any step than give up the minimal rationality in step (4), and I also believe, on the strength of the Cold War record, that deterrence did alter adversaries' calculations, so I would most like to keep something like the mechanism described in step (1). Tell me where the argument goes wrong, or, if it is sound, which premise I should reject and why. What do the main positions in the deterrence debate—in effect, the different places one can push back—say about each numbered step, and what does each rejection cost? I am less interested in a label for a view than in the diagnosis: why the weak step looked self-evident, what a corrected picture of deterrence looks like, and which of the other premises survive in it. Please write this up as continuous prose only, the way one would in a long letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
