I serve on the standing research-integrity panel of a research university. The panel reviews misconduct findings from departmental inquiries and makes binding recommendations to the provost. I need to circulate a written opinion before Friday's meeting, and I want you to draft it.

The case concerns a fifth-year doctoral student in cell biology accused by a postdoc of fabricating data in a draft manuscript and a conference poster. The departmental inquiry found no direct proof—no witnessed alteration and no document in which the student admits or discusses making up numbers—but the circumstantial case is substantial. The instrument log records fewer experimental runs than the reported sample sizes would require; two images in the poster appear, on quantitative comparison, to duplicate features in a way that would be highly unlikely by chance; and the postdoc testifies that she never observed the cells the student reports measuring in three separate experiments. The student's explanation is plausible in each part but strained in combination: a misremembered schedule, time on an instrument in another lab whose log has gone missing, and a file corrupted during compression that happened to produce the duplicated regions.

If the panel finds fabrication under our usual preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, the recommended sanction is revocation of degree candidacy and a permanent transcript notation that would effectively bar the student from the field and from several professional licenses. The student has a postdoctoral offer that is contingent on clearing this review.

The panel is split three ways, and the memo should settle which approach to take. One faction says we should apply the preponderance standard: the only question is whether the evidence makes fabrication more likely than not, and the costs of the answer belong to the sanction, not to the finding. A second faction says we should require clear and convincing evidence in high-stakes cases like this, because it would be improper for the panel to claim to know, or even to conclude, that fabrication occurred on evidence that leaves the matter genuinely in doubt, given how damaging a false positive would be. A third faction says we should keep the preponderance standard for the factual finding but separately require a higher degree of confidence before imposing the career-ending sanction, so that the panel makes both determinations explicit.

Draft the decision memo I would circulate: a clear recommendation among these options, or a defensible refinement of one of them, with the reasoning for it and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the side you reject. Aim for roughly 900–1,200 words. The memo should be something the provost could act on and the dissenting panelists could respond to. Do not name philosophical positions and do not cite any literature; make the argument in the committee's own terms.
