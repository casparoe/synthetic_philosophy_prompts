Ok so in my philosophy club we've been going at the ethics of belief debate — we did Clifford's shipowner thing (the guy who talks himself out of his doubts about whether the ship is seaworthy, sends it out anyway, and then collects the insurance after it sinks) and William James's pushback that sometimes you have to decide before the evidence settles it or the option just dies. I tried to write down where I actually stand so I can defend it next week, and now I'm worried it doesn't all fit together. I'd rather find out before the meeting then on the spot. Here's what I've got:

(a) Clifford is basically right that it's wrong to hold a belief when you know you haven't done the work to see whether the evidence supports it, even when the belief never visibly hurts anyone, because careless believing is contagious and makes us all worse at this.

(b) But demanding evidence for literally everything is impossible and honestly a bit fanatical. All day I lean on memory, testimony, and just gutt-level trust in people, and I don't audit any of it. If I treated every one of those as a moral failing, I'd never actually do anything.

(c) So I've been telling myself the duty should scale with stakes: the more damage a belief would do if it were wrong, the more checking it takes before you're allowed to hold it or act on it. Thinking my friend will show up to lunch can run on very thin evidence; thinking a whole group of people is dangerous better be checked hard.

(d) But then I remember that a lot of the worst stuff in history wasn't done by people who knew they were lying. They were confident in ideas they'd never stress-tested because those ideas looked theoretical or private at the time. So maybe you can't know in advance which low-stakes-looking beliefs will turn out to be high-stakes. And that seems to undercut (c) pretty hard.

(e) I also think there's such a thing as to much doubt. The person who says "well we can't really know" about a bunch of stuff is often just avoiding the fact that acting is forced on us and doing nothing is itself a choice. So I don't buy that more doubt is always more virtuous.

(f) Sometimes I think the real duty isn't doubt at all, but something more like humility: you should hold each belief in a way you'd be willing to defend to the people who actually stand to get hurt if it's wrong. Doubt is just one tool for getting there, not good for its own sake.

So go through these and tell me which pairs actually clash and which only look like they do. Be committed about it: rank the tensions by how severe they are, say what the cheapest fix is for each, and tell me which single belief I'd have to change to get the strongest version of this position. If the answer is that I'm smuggling in two different ideas of "belief" or "evidence" to make things feel consistent, call that out directly.
