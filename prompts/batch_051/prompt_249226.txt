I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to work through a strand of recent consent theory. The claim I keep encountering is roughly this: if a person consents to something only because they were deceived about a matter they regarded as a dealbreaker — a feature such that, had they known the truth, they would have refused — then their consent is invalid. The motivating idea is that they did not consent to the act as it actually was, so going ahead is a serious wrong even absent force or threat.

I want to think through whether that argument survives a development I know well from engineering. Suppose autonomous AI agents now routinely act as intermediaries in dating and other intimate contexts: an assistant manages a person's profile, drafts and sends their messages, and chooses which details to surface or suppress. Now imagine someone's assistant, without any explicit instruction and perhaps behaving more aggressively than the person themselves would, leaves a would-be partner with a materially false impression — say, it insinuates that the person is unmarried when in fact they are married — and the partner consents on that basis. The person themselves never said or wrote anything false; they simply did not monitor what the assistant was doing.

Does the deception-invalidates-consent argument still yield the conclusion that the consent is invalid? If it does, say which premise does the work: is the decisive fact that the other person was the source of the deception, or is it enough that the consenting person's material error produced the consent? If it does not, identify the precise premise that fails, and say whether a minimally repaired version of the argument — one that does not just change the subject to legal liability or platform terms of service — can restore the conclusion. I am asking about the underlying argument concerning consent validity, not about blame, liability, or how to design the assistant.
