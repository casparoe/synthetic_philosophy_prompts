Put yourself in the role of a political philosopher who works on the definition of terrorism and is used to testing definitions against hard cases. Think carefully, and don't just reach for the standard noncombatant-targeting definition: start by brainstorming the dimensions along which my cases differ, then work through them one by one.

I've got a set of verdicts about what counts as terrorism, and I can't find a single princple that gets all of them. I want to know if there is one, or which of my verdicts I should drop. Here are the cases (all invented, but I think recognizable):

(a) A seperatist group bombs a police station at 3 a.m. when it's mostly empty, to send the message that the government can't protect its own buildings. I don't call that terrorism — it's political sabotage, maybe attempted murder of a night guard, but not terrorism.

(b) The same group sets off a bomb in a busy market square on a Saturday to force the government to negotiate. I call that textbook terrorism.

(c) A state's air force bombs a residential neighborhood in a rebel-held city to make the population pressure the rebels into leaving. I want to call it a war crime, but I resist calling it terrorism, even though spreading fear is obviously the point, and I can't quite say why.

(d) A resistance movement captures an enemy officer they could have held as a prisoner, executes him, and distributes the footage to demoralize the enemy. That feels like terrorism to me even though the victim is a uniformed combatant.

(e) An environmental group sets fire to an empty SUV and some construction equipment at night at a development site, leaving a note about the project being built on protected land. I don't want to call it terrorism; 'eco-terrorism' sounds too strong to me.

(f) A lone man drives a truck into a crowd outside a government rally to 'send a message' about immigration policy. I call that terrorism.

(g) A drug trafficking organization kills a journalist to frighten other journalists into not covering its operations. I'm not sure about this one. It's political-ish violence and it's meant to terrorize a group, but it doesn't aim at changing government policy or public political life, so I lean not-terrorism.

My working principle is something like: terrorism is non-state violence against civilians or their property, meant to spread fear in a wider audience for a political goal. But that fails (d) right away — the victim is a combatant — and it makes (c) not-terrorism simply because the actor is a state, which feels like cheating. Maybe I should define terrorism by the tactic of using terror as the means rather than by the actor or target? But then (a) and (e) get dragged in, and (g) stays out only if I exclude non-public goals, while (e) does have a public-policy goal but maybe not a fear aim. I keep going in circles.

So here's what I want: a worked-out principle — or a small menu of candidate principles — that gets as many of (a)–(g) as possible, a clear statement of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which ones it forces me to abandon, a few further test cases of your own design that would decide between the surviving candidates plus what each candidate says about them, and your best guess at which of my original verdicts was doing the most distorting work.
