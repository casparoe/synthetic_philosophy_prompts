I teach an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of artificial intelligence: sixteen students, one seventy-five-minute session, no programming assumed. Design a classroom activity that makes participants feel the core inverse reinforcement learning problem — that a finite sample of observed behavior underdetermines the reward function or preference one attributes to it, even when the behavior is optimal. The game should run with cards, masking tape, a grid drawn on the floor or whiteboard, and points; nothing should require a computer or real money.

Structure it in three phases. In Phase 1 a volunteer traverses a simple gridworld while satisfying a hidden objective card; everyone else bets points on what the objective is, then the card is revealed and bets are settled. In Phase 2 a second volunteer follows the exact same route on the same grid under a different hidden objective card; after the reveal, the group should reckon with the fact that the same performance was rational under both objectives and that their Phase 1 confidence was unjustified. In Phase 3 the class can spend remaining points on counterfactual queries: alter one square or one rule and ask the agent what they would have done under their hidden objective in the modified grid. The discovery should be that counterfactual choices, not the observed sequence of moves, are what finally separate objectives that the shared path could not.

Write out the grid layout, the materials, at least six objective cards written so that pairs are observationally confusable on the chosen path, the point and betting scheme, a full timeline, and the facilitator script for each phase. Then provide a debrief that maps each phase onto the technical ideas of trajectories, demonstrations, rationalizable reward functions, policies, optimality, and negative or counterfactual examples, and that connects the game to why real systems that infer preferences from clicks or demonstrations face the same ambiguity. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time — for instance a volunteer who cannot manage to act against their own real preferences — and how to recover. End with an honest note on which part of inverse reinforcement learning the activity cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply, especially why ambiguity in a toy grid is not a flaw of the example but a feature of the inference problem.
