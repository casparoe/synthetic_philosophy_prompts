I'm TAing an upper-level intro to phenomenology course and grading a short-answer question on the Husserl unit. The question was: "What does the epoché accomplish, and how does the phenomenological reduction go beyond it? Is Husserl's procedure a form of Cartesian doubt or skepticism?" A student wrote the answer below, and I want a point-by-point assessment before I put a number on it. Grade it out of 20, and separate what the answer gets right from anything that's subtly off, with comments I could actually defend if the student pushes back.

[Student's answer:] "The epoché is Husserl's suspension of the natural attitude, which is our default assumption that the world exists independently of us. In the epoché we don't deny this assumption, we just put it out of action — like a referee calling time out rather than saying the game never happened. This is different from Cartesian doubt because Descartes uses doubt to find an indubitable foundation, whereas Husserl isn't trying to prove the world. Once the epoché is in place, what remains is the pure ego and its lived experiences, and the reduction is the further step where we trace all sense and existence back to the constituting activity of this ego. So for Husserl the world becomes a content or product of consciousness for me, which is why he says the world is only ever a phenomenon and not a thing in itself. That's not skeptical because it doesn't deny the world; it just relocates it."
