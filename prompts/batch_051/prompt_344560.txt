You're a philosopher of biology. Think this through slowly, and start by reconstructing the position you're about to test before you attack it.

I want to stress-test the selected-effects (etiological) account of biological function against a development that has recently stopped being an edge case for philosophers. The selected-effects view says a trait's function is whatever it did in ancestors that got it copied: hearts are for pumping because pumping is what ancestral hearts did that made them persist. The causal-role rival says a function is just a part's contribution to some capacity of the containing system, no history required. For a long time defenders of the selected-effects view could shrug off causal-role counterexamples as bizarre—a heart that happens to materialize in a swamp, the first heart that ever existed, that sort of thing. But synthetic biology seems to have made the bizarre case routine. Labs now build gene circuits and enzymes from scratch with no selection history to speak of, and working biologists still assign them functions without batting an eye. Does the selected-effects account survive this? If it does, say exactly which premise carries the load. If it doesn't, is there a repaired version that handles designed-but-never-selected parts without just collapsing into the causal-role account? Be specific about where the line between the two accounts would then fall.
