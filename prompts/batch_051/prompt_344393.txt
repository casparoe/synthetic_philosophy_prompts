You are a professional political philosopher specializing in the ethics of public office and thoroughly familiar with the problem of dirty hands as debated from Machiavelli and Weber through Walzer and Williams. I am going to describe a counterfactual: a single change in a social-psychological fact, with everything else held fixed unless it is itself a downstream effect of the change. Your task is to give me a reasoned prediction of how politics changes, not a story — a chain of reasoning about mechanisms, incentives, and constraints, with explicit confidence assigned to each link.

Consider first the relevant background. The problem of dirty hands asks whether a political leader may sometimes be required, all things considered, to do what is nevertheless morally forbidden, and whether the fitting response to doing such a thing includes a residual moral remainder: persistent guilt, compunction, or a sense of having wronged identifiable people, even when the action was justified. That state has traditionally been private and easy to counterfeit. The counterfactual removes exactly that privacy.

Starting on 1 January 2030, a new and by 2032 universally verified fact about human psychology establishes itself. Whenever a person holding senior political office — head of government, cabinet minister, or any official with comparable final authority over life, liberty, or war — knowingly and deliberately authorizes, in the belief that the public good requires it, an action that the officeholder themself sincerely regards as a serious violation of a moral constraint toward identifiable victims (for example, ordering detention without charge, attacking a target with reasonably foreseen civilian deaths, or lying to the public about the reasons for a decision), that officeholder undergoes within 48 hours an involuntary moral-remainder reaction. The reaction consists of a distinctive, severe, and publicly visible set of symptoms — sleeplessness, tremor, weeping, and a characteristic hoarseness — whose intensity tracks the seriousness of the violation as the officeholder sees it. The reaction cannot be suppressed, faked, or produced without the underlying act; the absence of the reaction is equally unfakeable. Because the mechanism tracks the agent's own sincere moral beliefs, a leader who genuinely sees no wrongdoing in such an act shows no reaction. Assume that by 2032 everyone — politicians, staff, journalists, voters, rivals, and foreign governments — understands these rules.

Focus on mature competitive democracies over the three decades from 2030 to 2060, though you may note briefly where you expect authoritarian regimes to differ. Predict how the practice of politics changes, and make your reasoning visible. Begin by brainstorming the candidate mechanisms in play — selection effects, accountability incentives, delegation, rationalization, public interpretation — and how they interact. Then organize your prediction around at least the following: (1) selection into office: who stands, who is elected, and how campaigns change; (2) the making of dirty-hands decisions: how leaders and cabinets make, delegate, delay, or avoid decisions that require forbidden means, and whether the dirty work migrates downward to subordinates below the threshold or outward to informal, unaccountable actors; (3) public response: how voters read a leader who displays the reaction, how they read a leader who refuses dirty action at high cost, and how trust, forgiveness, and retrospective judgment change; (4) the normative debate: how the public and philosophers reassess the claim that a guilty politician is morally better than an untroubled one. For each area, distinguish effects that follow robustly from the changed incentives from effects that depend on contestable assumptions about human behavior, and state the assumptions. End by saying which prediction you would endorse with the highest confidence, and what would have to turn out differently for your central prediction to be wrong.
