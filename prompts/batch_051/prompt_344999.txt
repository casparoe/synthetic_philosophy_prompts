I'm a high school student writing a capstone paper for my AP World History class, and I keep coming back to the question whether the past can be told from everywhere at once. My teacher passed me an argument that seems to show a non-Eurocentric world history is impossible, and I cannot accept the conclusion, but I also cannot locate the step where the reasoning goes wrong. I've numbered the argument as I understand it.

(1) Any historical narrative must select: it singles out some events, people, and processes as significant and leaves out indefinitely many others.
(2) Selection requires criteria of significance, and criteria of significance are never neutral. They express a standpoint: the questions, interests, and priorities of some situated observer.
(3) Eurocentrism is the mistake of using a European standpoint while presenting it as the natural, universal one: European periodization, European points on the map and timeline, and European achievements as the implicit standard of development.
(4) Therefore, a truly non-Eurocentric world history would have to be written from no standpoint at all, or equivalently from everywhere at once.
(5) But a history from everywhere at once is impossible. If it gives equal weight to every event, it never becomes a narrative; significance is flattened, and there is no shape, scale, or plot. If it gives some events more weight, then the criteria by which it does so come from somewhere, and we are back at a standpoint.
(6) If the historian instead writes from a different particular standpoint, say one centered on the Indian Ocean trading world, she has not escaped the problem; she has only relocated the center. The history becomes Indian Ocean-centric or Sinocentric rather than Eurocentric.
(7) So every world history must be centered somewhere, and a non-Eurocentric world history is impossible in principle.
(8) But if every history is necessarily centered somewhere, then the charge that a particular history is Eurocentric cannot carry the principled force people usually give it; it collapses into a preference for one center over another.
(9) Yet the charge does seem principled. When a world history treats the Americas before 1492 mainly as a prelude to European arrival, it seems to be making an error about those societies and their own political rhythms, not merely expressing a stylistic preference one could reasonably decline.

So the argument proves too much: it turns what looks like a cognitive failure, a distortion of the past, into a matter of taste. I most want to keep (1) and (9). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If rejecting that step forces a different formulation of what a non-Eurocentric world history asks for, tell me what it is and show how it can still explain the failure described in (9).
