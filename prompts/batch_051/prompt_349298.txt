I spent most of yesterday arguing with a friend of mine who does game theory, and he kept insisting that common knowledge of rationality is enough to pin down exactly what players will actually do in a game. I kept pushing back but could never quite say which step of his argument was too strong, and now I cant tell whether the argument is actually fine and I just dislike how tidy it is.

His argument, as I remember it, went like this:

(1) Start with any finite game where the payoff matrix is common knowledge, and where each player is rational in the ordinary sense: whatever she does, it is a best response to some belief about what the other will do.

(2) Now strengthen that to common knowledge of rationality: each knows each is rational, each knows each knows, and so on all the way up.

(3) This kicks off a deletion process. Any strategy that can only be a best response to an irrational play gets struck; then any strategy that can only be a best response to a strategy that is only a best response to an irrational play gets struck; and the process repeats until nothing more disappears.

(4) What is left has to be a set of self-consistent predictions—each player doing what they do becasue they correctly anticipate what the other is actually doing. But each best responding to what the other actually does is just the definition of a Nash equilibrium.

(5) So common knowledge of rationality forces Nash play. Concretely, in matching pennies—row wants to match, column wants to mismatch, and the only equilibrium is both randomizing fifty-fifty—common knowledge of rationality forces both players to flip fair coins, and anyone who plays a pure side has to be irrational.

I feel like step (4) is doing something sneaky, but I could not formulate precisely what. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what is the correct version of that step?
