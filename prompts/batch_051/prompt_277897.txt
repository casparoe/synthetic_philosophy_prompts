I am putting together the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of science, and I have been given responsibility for the essay question on inference to the best explanation (IBE). The students have already worked through Lipton's distinction between the loveliest and the most likely explanation, van Fraassen's bad lot argument, and the Bayesian reply that explanatory virtues are merely heuristics for setting priors. I want a question that rewards students who can do more than reproduce those labels: it should force them to say whether the fact that we find an explanation intellectually satisfying gives us any reason to think it true, and to take a defensible stand on whether IBE is an independent form of inference, a disguised form of Bayesian updating, or a pragmatic policy with no special claim to truth.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be a rubric with four criteria, each weighted out of ten (forty marks in total): reconstruction of the problem; treatment of the bad lot argument; assessment of the Bayesian reduction; and originality of the student's own position. For each criterion, give descriptors for a weak, a competent, and an excellent answer, and note one characteristic error that markers should watch for. The question and the model answer should not call for citations, bibliographies, or recall of particular texts beyond the named arguments; credit should turn entirely on the quality of reasoning displayed.
