I've come to accept a strong version of the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I want you to tell me what would count against it. My view is not just the weak Duhem–Quine point that auxiliary assumptions can always be adjusted to protect a hypothesis. It is the stronger claim that for any finite body of evidence, there are indefinitely many mutually incompatible theories that accommodate that evidence at least as well as any rival, and that no appeal to the evidence alone can single one out. I also take the view to be epistemically consequential: scientific theory choice is never settled by evidence alone, and what breaks the underdetermination are extra-empirical qualities of theories—simplicity, unification, elegance, explanatory power—that are not themselves forced on us by the data. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for sources; I want a structured map of what would count against the position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including what a committed defender should themselves concede.

Please write the response in the following five sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths.

1. The position sharpened (about 250 words). Restate my view in your own words and distinguish the versions a critic might target: underdetermination of a single hypothesis by data given auxiliaries; underdetermination of whole theories by all possible evidence; transient underdetermination, which might be resolved by further data; and permanent underdetermination, which could not be. Say which version the rest of your response treats as the one I am committed to.

2. Decisive defeaters (about 600 words). What, if it were shown, would seriously damage or refute the strong permanent underdetermination claim? Consider at least: an argument or result showing that evidential support can be characterized so that the data do rule out all but one theory from a suitably constrained set of live alternatives; a demonstration that alleged empirically equivalent rivals are mostly notational variants or reparametrizations of one another rather than genuinely distinct theories; and evidence that the extra-empirical virtues I appeal to are themselves empirically grounded, for instance by a reliable track record of selecting true theories. Rank these from most to least decisive and say exactly what each would establish if successful.

3. Merely awkward defeaters (about 400 words). What would weaken the position without refuting it? Consider historical cases where apparent underdetermination dissolved under better evidence; the fact that many constructed rivals are gerrymandered or built after seeing the data rather than being projectible; and the fact that scientists in practice treat some theories as decisively supported even when an underdetermination argument can be reconstructed around them. For each, say whether it narrows the scope of the view, lowers its stakes, or merely irritates its defenders.

4. What a committed defender should concede (about 300 words). Which of the above points, or which possible findings, should I grant count against my view even if I do not think they are fatal? Be specific about the costs I ought to accept—for example, that permanent underdetermination may be a cheap point about the infinite flexibility of language rather than a substantive empirical finding about science.

5. Verdict: exposure and insulation (about 250 words). Is a view like mine well insulated against refutation because it is a second-order claim about epistemic possibility rather than a first-order empirical claim, and should that worry me? Is that kind of insulation a defect in a philosophical view of this sort, or is it appropriate to what the view claims? End with the single strongest reason to give the position up even if no individual defeater is decisive.
