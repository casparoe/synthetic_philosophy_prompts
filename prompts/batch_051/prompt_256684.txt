I retired from a career in physics a few years back and started sitting in on a philosophy of time reading group to keep the gears turning. One of the regulars laid out the argument below, and although the physics steps are textbook to me, I can't find the bad joint where it stops being physics and starts being metaphysics. I'd like to keep my old commitments, but the conclusion seems like an awful lot to buy with a coordinate transformation.

(1) In special relativity, simultaneity is frame-dependent: for any two spacelike seperated events, there are inertial frames in which they are simultaneous, frames in which one comes first, and frames in which the other comes first. (2) Take an event E—say, a flare erupting on a star 1,000 light-years away—that happens 200 years from now in the frame in which I'm currently at rest. E and my reading this sentence are spacelike seperated, so there is an inertial observer moving at constant velocity for whom E is simultaneous with my reading this sentence. (3) For that observer, E is happening right now. (4) If an event is happening right now for some observer, then that event is real; it isn't merely potential or as-yet-undetermined. (5) So E is already real, even though it lies in my future. (6) And you can run the same move for any event outside the light cone, at which point—he says—the idea of a single objective present marking the edge of reality collapses: the present is just a projection from an arbitrarily chosen frame, so the block universe is true and presentism is false.

I tried the obvious comeback: maybe "simultaneous in some frame" is only a coordinate label and doesn't make anything real, which would make (4) the villain. He answered that the moment I single out one frame's simultaneity as the real one, I've reintroduced absolute simultaneity—a privileged frame with no experimental signature—and if I don't single one out, presentism has to make existence itself observer-relative. I'd rather keep (1)–(3), and honestly I'd give up presentism if that's what the argument costs, but I still can't tell whether the bad step is (4), or (6), or something in the slide from "a moving observer's now" to "real." So: where does this go wrong, if it does? Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is the most expensive one to keep and what a presentist has to accept instead.
