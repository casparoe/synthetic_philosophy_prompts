For a thought experiment in legal philosophy: suppose a medium-sized wealthy democracy, starting tomorrow, abolishes its blackmail statutes but changes nothing else — libel, privacy, extortion-by-violence, and contract law stay exactly as they are. It becomes legal to demand money (or a favor, a job, etc.) from someone in exchange for not revealing true, otherwise-lawful information that would embarrass or damage them; actually revealing the information remains legal, and merely asking for money remains legal; only the combined pay me or I keep quiet, otherwise I tell everyone has been decriminalized. Part of what I want is the old puzzle that two separately legal acts become illegal when bundled, so don't just assume the bundle is obviously harmful. Give me an argued prediction of the next twenty years, with the mechanisms explicit. Does an actual blackmail industry emerge, and if it does, why would victims ever pay rather than call the bluff? What happens to how candidly people talk, to the market value of sensitive information, and to whether people preemptively leak their own secrets? I want you to separate predictions that are robust from ones that hinge on assumptions — about reputational recovery, anonymous disclosure, whether a blackmailer can credibly promise to stay quiet after being paid, whether people can precommit to never paying — and say what would have to be true for the outcome to tip the other way.
