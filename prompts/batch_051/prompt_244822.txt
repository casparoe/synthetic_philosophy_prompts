I have been arguing with a friend about whether the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals were legitimate courts or merely the victorious powers punishing their defeated enemies. His position is that the trials were victors' justice in the strongest sense: the defendants were convicted under categories such as crimes against peace and crimes against humanity that, he claims, did not exist as individual offenses at the time the acts were committed, and the judges were appointed entirely by the winning side. He keeps reaching for one analogy to make the whole case. The trials, he says, were like two teams finishing a match, after which the winning team announced that the losing team's tactics had been fouls all along, rewrote the rulebook to say so, and then sat as the referee for the penalties.

I would like an audit of that analogy rather than a full trial of the trials. First, give the correspondence table: what maps to the game, the players, the rules, the foul, the rewriting of the rules, and the referee. Then lay out the disanalogies. Some I can already see, though I am unsure how much weight they bear. A game is entered voluntarily and its rules are usually agreed by both sides, whereas the defendants had not consented to the legal order now applied to them; yet the interwar period had produced treaties renouncing aggressive war, so the conduct was not exactly uncondemned, even if individual criminal liability was far murkier than the tribunals made it look. The analogy also imports the idea of a single shared rulebook, but the winning side never subjected its own wartime conduct, including the firebombing of cities and the atomic bombs, to the same legal scrutiny, and at Tokyo the emperor was kept out of the dock for political reasons while his subordinates were tried. That looks less like a late rule change than like selective enforcement. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for my friend's conclusion that the tribunals lacked legal legitimacy, and which are distractions?

Finally, set the analogy aside and tell me what remains. If the game analogy is doing more work than it can bear, is there still a serious retroactivity objection to Nuremberg and Tokyo, and what is its strongest statement? Conversely, what is the strongest reply on behalf of the tribunals? Does anything like my friend's conclusion survive, namely that even if the outcomes were morally welcome, the legal form in which they were delivered was defective? If the game analogy fails, I would also like to know whether some other comparison does better, or whether the friend should abandon analogy altogether.
