In my emergency powers seminar we've spent three weeks on the same fight about COVID lockdowns and border restrictions, and I honestly cant figure out why nobody has moved. The two sides look like this. One group says the measures were legitimate only if the law actually authorized them in advance; if a health secretary or a governor imposed a lockdown or closed a border under a vague emergency statute, or stretched an old public health provision no one had interpreted that way, then the act was illegitimate even if it saved lives, because the whole point of the rule of law is that officials stay inside their legal box. The other group says that's insane: legitimacy tracks the public good, so when the choice is between following a rigid rule and preventing real harm, acting for the public good is what makes the measure legitimate, and legality is at most one consideration among others. We read John Locke's Second Treatise of Government, chapter XIV "Of Prerogative," and our professor keeps leaning on this passage, but both sides think it supports them:

"nay, it is fit that the laws themselves should in some cases give way to the executive power, or rather to this fundamental law of nature and government, viz. That as much as may be, all the members of the society are to be preserved: for since many accidents may happen, wherein a strict and rigid observation of the laws may do harm; (as not to pull down an innocent man's house to stop the fire, when the next to it is burning) and a man may come sometimes within the reach of the law, which makes no distinction of persons, by an action that may deserve reward and pardon; 'tis fit the ruler should have a power, in many cases, to mitigate the severity of the law, and pardon some offenders:"

So here's what I want. Why has this argument actually stalled? Is it a hidden empirical question, a verbal dispute about the word "legitimate," two groups answering different questions, a difference in bedrock intuitions about procedure versus outcomes, or something else? I have a working theory that one side is asking whether the act was legally authorized at the time it was done and the other is asking whether the community ought to accept or ratify it after the fact, and that the Locke passage cuts weirdly across that split — but I'm not confident. Diagnose the disagreement using the passage, say what Locke's fire-and-pardon example actually commits him to and whether it really transfers to a border closure or a lockdown, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis.
