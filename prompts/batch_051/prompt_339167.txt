I keep running into a version of the following argument about computational irreducibility, and it pushes somewhere I don't want to go, but I cant find the bad step. It goes:

(1) If a process is computationally irreducible, there's no shortcut to predicting its future states: any computation that determines where the process will be at time t must take about as many steps as the process itself takes to get to time t.
(2) An explanation of a system's behavior by a set of laws is a way of deriving later states from those laws plus initial conditions; deriving is computing, so such an explanation would have to be a computation of those later states.
(3) By (1), any such computation would be at least as long as the process being explained — you'd effectively be re-running the system, not illuminating it.
(4) But something that merely re-runs a process doesn't explain it.
(5) So, for a computationally irreducible process, no law-based explanation of its behavior is possible.
(6) Weather, brains, and markets are computationally irreducible in the relevant sense.
(7) Therefore, a lot of nature is in principle inexplicable — not just hard to predict.

I half-suspect the slide is somewhere around (2) or (3), maybe conflating "the derivation takes as many steps as the process" with "the derivation just is the process." But I might be rationalizing. So: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and on what reading of it? If it is valid, is the conclusion as strong as it sounds? And answer in American English.
