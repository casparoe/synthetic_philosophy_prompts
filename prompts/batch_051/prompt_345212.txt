I have been developing a small theory about how conventions turn into norms, and I want it stress-tested. By "convention" I mean roughly what philosophers mean: an arbitrary solution to a recurring coordination problem, sustained by mutual expectation. I distinguish two stages. A bare convention is a stable arbitrariness: everyone in a group does X, expects everyone else to do X, and would rather do X given that everyone else does; but everyone also acknowledges that some alternative could have served equally well, and no one treats X itself as required. A calcified norm is the same behavioral regularity after it has lost that avowed arbitrariness: people now treat deviations from X as wrong in themselves, feel entitled to disapproval or sanction, and cannot explain why X is the rule except by saying that it has always been the rule.

My central claim is that calcification has a single engine. Because deviations from a bare convention have to be explained or excused, and because the only reason anyone can actually give for following the convention is "this is what people do around here," that explanation gets mistaken for a justification over time. The prevalence of the practice gets heard as a reason for the practice. Each time someone demands a justification and receives prevalence back, a residue of oughtness is deposited; the oughtness ratchets upward and never fully dissipates when the question goes away. I call this the justificatory ratchet.

An example that fits my theory is tipping. Presumably it began as something like a coordination about what to offer, but at some point the fact that everyone tips became the reason one ought to tip, and now stiffing a server is treated as morally wrong. I think the ratchet explains why the wrongness seems located in the act itself rather than in its consequences or in any explicit promise.

What I want checked: (1) Is this a coherent mechanism, or is "prevalence heard as a reason" doing secret work it cannot do? (2) The obvious counterexample is the side of the road we drive on: it has been a stable arbitrariness for a long time, yet nobody in a left-driving country moralizes about people who would prefer to drive on the right. Why did that convention never calcify while tipping did? The best repair I can offer is that calcification only occurs when a deviation can be read as a message from the deviator to the others — as ingratitude or disrespect — whereas driving on the wrong side reads as incompetence or confusion, not as an insult. Does that repair save the theory, or does it reduce it to the banal claim that norms form where violations signal bad motives? (3) Has this already been worked out under some name I should know? I suspect it is a clumsy rediscovery of something in the conventions-and-norms literature, and I would rather hear the nearest existing account than keep reinventing it.

Please reconstruct the theory in its strongest form before criticizing it, name the closest existing ideas if you can do so without guessing, and identify exactly where the ratchet mechanism breaks or stops being distinctive. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states in a single sentence your overall assessment of the theory.
