I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on probability and philosophical method, and we have just spent two weeks on self-locating belief. I want to check a policy I follow when I evaluate probabilistic arguments, because the doomsday argument has forced me to notice it.

The policy is this: I refuse to update on any premise that treats an indexical fact about me — my birth rank, the era I occupy, which observer I am among several — as if it were a random draw from some reference class, unless the reference class can be specified in advance and justified on grounds that do not themselves depend on the conclusion the argument is meant to establish. In the doomsday case, the lecturer asks me to treat my birth rank as a uniform draw from the set of all humans who will ever live; since a random draw is unlikely to land among the earliest few percent of humans, I am supposed to conclude that a very large future population is improbable. I notice that my policy simply cancels the argument: the move from 'I am human number roughly N' to 'there will probably not be vastly more than N humans' depends on a self-sampling assumption, and I refuse to license that assumption unless someone first gives me an independent reason to think the reference class is the right one. I do the same thing with Sleeping Beauty cases and with fine-tuning arguments that require a 'what kind of observer am I?' premise — the moment the reasoning needs such a premise, my credences stop moving.

My friend says this is ad hoc. I happily update on ordinary random sampling when the sampled object is not me, but I impose an extra justificatory burden exactly in the cases where the method would push me toward conclusions I find uncomfortable — such as the claim that humanity is probably near the end of its run. That worries me. I cannot tell from the inside whether I am applying a sound restriction on self-locating evidence or protecting my beliefs from challenge.

Please act as an expert in formal epistemology and Bayesian reasoning. Start by brainstorming, as openly as you can, the strongest case for my policy and the strongest case against it, before you commit to a verdict. Then address the following. Is a blanket refusal to update on indexical evidence defensible, or does it throw away information I should be using? Under what assumptions about reference classes, sampling, and self-locating belief would my policy be reliable, and under what assumptions would it be exactly the kind of bias my friend suspects? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps whatever is good in it while avoiding the costs — for instance, should I be discounting such evidence by some factor rather than ignoring it outright? And how could I, from the inside, distinguish between following the rule because it is sound and following it because it spares me the doomsday conclusion?
