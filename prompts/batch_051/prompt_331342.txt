You are a specialist in contemporary theories of well-being, particularly debates about whether variety, novelty, and adventure are genuine components of a good life or merely instruments for other goods. Think like a philosopher who is used to reverse-engineering someone's implicit axiology from their case judgments; begin by brainstorming candidate principles before you settle on one.

I keep a file of how a senior colleague of mine, a philosopher of well-being, rules on short life-vignettes. She has never stated her official theory; all I have are her verdicts. Here are five recorded judgments:

1. The Shepherd. Fifty years of the same annual rhythms—same pastures, same village, same work—with no desire for anything different, no boredom, and a settled feeling of being at home. She judged this a deeply good life. When a student asked whether more variety would automatically have made it better, she said that would depend on what the variety did for him: novelty as such, she insisted, is not something a life simply accrues.

2. The Sated Beachcomber. A decade of comfortable and perfectly pleasant days—same porch, same walk, same meal. He is not depressed, but he is bored and restless, says he feels hollowed out, and would give anything for a surprise. She judged this a substantially bad life, worse than the Shepherd's despite the comfort; she located the loss in the monotony itself rather than in any acute suffering.

3. The Serial Reinventor. Every few years she sells her belongings, moves to a new city, and learns a new trade. The pattern is stressful and her average felt pleasure is unremarkable, but she wholeheartedly wants this life, reports feeling alive, and says a fixed existence would feel like sleepwalking. She judged it a very good life and treated the reinvention as central to that verdict.

4. The Reluctant Nomad. A lifetime of forced uprooting; she never wanted the moves, found them disorienting, and longs for a permanent home. The perpetual novelty brought her no felt pleasure. She judged the life bad for her, adding that she could find no redeeming plus in the constant change itself.

5. The Constant Connoisseur. His pleasures, unusually, do not habituate: the same breakfast, the same walk, and the same book delight him on the ten-thousandth repetition just as on the first. He wants nothing more and is never bored. She praised this as one of the best lives in the file.

The new case, which she will rule on next week, is this:

Rina is a collector of firsts. She has a pleasant life and is not, and never has been, bored or restless with its sameness. After years of reflection she forms a settled, self-chosen aim that she regards as making a life better: to make her own life dense with firsts—five hundred species of wildflower seen, three hundred debut novels read, forty alphabets learned. She pursues this aim steadily. But the firsts are affectively flat: a first wildflower feels no better to her than a repeated view; the program relieves no restlessness, since there was none; and fulfilling the desire has the cool quality of checking a box she is glad to have checked, not a warm one. Her engagement with the items is thin—a month later she can say little about most of them—and the list plays no larger role in her sense of her own life beyond being kept and counted.

I want your committed prediction. Please do not leave me with a bare menu of possibilities.

Work through it as follows. First, brainstorm the candidate principles that could rationalize the five recorded verdicts, and check each principle against all five cases; discard or flag those that strain. Second, say what each surviving principle predicts about Rina. Third, commit: how will my colleague rule on the question of whether Rina's first-collecting makes Rina's life better for her than it would have been without the project—yes, it adds to her well-being, or no, it adds nothing? Give your reasons for preferring the principle behind that prediction. Fourth, identify the strongest rival principle that fits the recorded cases but would yield the opposite Rina verdict, and specify a single follow-up vignette that would most cleanly separate your preferred principle from that rival. Say what you expect my colleague to say about that follow-up case under each.

I don't need a literature survey or citations; I need a sharp diagnostic reconstruction with a definite verdict.
