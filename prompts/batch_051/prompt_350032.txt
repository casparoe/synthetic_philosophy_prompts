In my course on populism and democratic theory, my instructor repeatedly invokes the will of the people as if the phrase does real argumentative work. Most recently, when I objected that a referendum result is often a narrow majority aggregated from voters who understood the question in very different ways, he replied that the result still expresses the will of the people and that this is why it ought to be respected. I suspect the phrase hides several distinct claims that slide into one another. Could you lay out the different precise propositions the will of the people might be asserting—for example, about aggregating individual preferences, about the existence of a collective agent over and above individuals, about majority outcomes carrying normative force—and give a verdict on each reading? I want to know which readings are trivial, which are substantive but defensible, which are false or indeterminate, and which reading, if any, actually supports treating a referendum result as binding. I have not studied social choice theory, so please spell out the technical content rather than gesture at it. Finally, please separate what is settled in democratic theory from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
