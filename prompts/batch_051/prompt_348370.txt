I teach a course called Interpretation and Argument, and in the unit on rules we practice the four classical approaches — plain meaning, purpose, drafters' intent, and the reasonable reader — on short clauses from private associations rather than only statutes. I am writing a problem around the old Death in Damascus story, and I want the analysis to sit at the corner where legal interpretation meets puzzles about foreknowledge and inevitability. The clause, from the 1889 charter of a fictional fraternal aid society, reads: "The Society shall pay one hundred pounds to the named survivor of any Member who shall die while travelling on the Society's business, save where the Member's death was, at the time the journey was undertaken, already certain to occur in the course of that journey."

The case the Society's claims committee must resolve is this. In 1924 a member received a warning, from a source the Society's own records treat as unusually reliable, that he would be killed in Damascus on 14 October. To avoid the place entirely, he arranged no Damascus business and on 13 October set out for Aleppo on Society business, taking the direct road. En route he learned that a bridge on the direct road had collapsed; the only way to reach Aleppo by the appointed day ran through Damascus, so he took it, and there, on 14 October, he was killed. No one disputes that the warning, whatever its source, was accurate in what it predicted; the dispute is over whether, when he undertook the journey, his death was "already certain to occur in the course of that journey," given that he undertook the journey precisely because the warning made such a Damascus death seem certain.

Please write the analysis the committee should adopt. Work through what each approach yields: the plain words of "already certain"; the purpose of such a saving clause in a mutual-aid society, including what hazard it existed to prevent and whether that hazard is present here; what the 1889 drafters would likely have said if asked about strikingly reliable predictions, deliberate rerouting to avoid a fate, and unlooked-for detours; and what a reasonable contemporary reader would take the clause to mean. Then take up the philosophical difficulty hiding in the word "certain": whether an accurate forecast can make the forecast event inevitable, and whether a reading that turns solely on predictive accuracy rather than avoidability would make the saving clause swallow the general promise of payment. Conclude by saying which approach should govern, and whether the survivor is paid.
