I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I teach undergrads a rough four-part test for when a compelling theory should override a firm intuition: (i) the theory has a track record of surviving risky predictions, not just post-hoc fitting; (ii) the intuition can be debunked by something independently plausible (a framing effect, scope insensitivity, status quo bias) without assuming the theory is false; (iii) the theory gives a principled account of why this kind of intuition misfires, not an ad hoc patch for one counterexample; and (iv) abandoning the intuition doesn't commit you to abandoning a whole cluster of related intuitions you'd want to keep.

Now here's the borderline case I keep chewing on. A person is told up front that the medical outcome for her family member is already settled, and she is offered the chance to pay a small amount to learn whether the family member would have survived under the treatment that wasn't chosen. The information cannot possibly change any decision: there are no future, comparable choices, and nothing about the current state depends on what she learns. Still, she has a powerful, stable intuition that paying is reasonable, and when I run this as a vignette most people agree with her, even after it's pointed out that the information has no instrumental value. There's a standard result that says this intuition has to be wrong—a rational agent should never pay for information that cannot change what she does, because the expected value of information is then zero: every possible message leads to the same act, so she's paying to end up in a branch with the same payoff as doing nothing.

Does my test say the theory should win here, or does the case break the test? Go criterion by criterion and say honestly where each criterion underdetermines the answer. Then tell me whether the actual lesson is that paying for this information can be rational after all (because the theory's model of welfare is too narrow), or that my four criteria are missing a clause—something about whether the information itself is a source of utility—and what the amended test should say.
