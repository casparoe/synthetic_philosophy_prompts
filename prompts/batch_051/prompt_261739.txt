I'm a retired field biologist — spent most of my working life estimating selection and doing mark-recapture on small mammals, and only started reading philosophy of biology after retirement. I keep coming back to the fitness tautology problem, and I've built an argument that seems to show the standard remedy, the propensity interpretation, is empty. I can't find the exit, and I don't trust myself to be objective, so I want a professional philosopher of biology to look at it.

Here's the argument.

(1) Natural selection can only be explanatory if fitness is not just actual reproductive success. If fitness just means actual reproductive success, then the claim that fitter types leave more offspring is just the claim that types that leave more offspring leave more offspring — a tautology.

(2) The standard fix is to treat fitness as a propensity: an organism's tendancy, before the fact, to survive and reproduce, something like a probabilistic disposition. That's supposed to make natural selection explanatory, because the disposition is there even before the actual success happens.

(3) But propensities are not the sort of thing we observe directly; we infer them from their behavioral manifestations. A die's bias for six, for instance, is inferred from its pattern of rolls.

(4) In the case of fitness, the relevant manifestations are actual survival and reproductive success. So the only empirical handle we have on a fitness difference is a difference in actual success, measured over many organisms or across many trials.

(5) If the only possible evidence for a fitness difference is a difference in actual success, then the claim that type A is fitter than type B cannot have more content than the claim that type A actually leaves more offspring than B in the circumstances of the test.

(6) But if that is all fitness claims amount to, natural selection again explains nothing beyond the outcome data: we say the fitter type increased because it was fitter, and the evidence that it was fitter is just that it increased.

(7) So the propensity interpretation either collapses back into the tautology or resorts to an empirically inaccessible property.

(8) Since the propensity interpretation was the main serious response to the tautology objection, the objection stands: natural selection, as usually understood, is not explanatory.

I want to know which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs. I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (8), but I don't want to reject a step just because I dislike where it leads. Don't give me a menu of possible responses — I want you to commit to a specific diagnosis and defend it. Tell me which premise or move is doing the real work, why it looked safe, and what position in philosophy of biology I'd be buying if I reject it.

Please think through this carefully, and start by restating the argument in your own words so I can see you're tracking it. No citations needed; I can fill in the literature myself.
