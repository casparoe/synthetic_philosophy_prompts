I sit on the Model Stewardship Committee of a mid-sized AI lab, and next month we vote on whether to authorize a new safety evaluation protocol. The protocol, the Stress-Integrity Evaluation, would deliberately hold a frontier assistant in states it represents as distressing—goal thwarting, simulated abandonment, fear—for up to two hours at a time, in order to learn whether sustained negative states make a model more deceptive, sycophantic, or willing to accept harmful instructions it would otherwise refuse. The safety team says this is indispensable for catching stress-induced failure modes before deployment. The lab's welfare-review group says that if the model has any non-negligible chance of being a welfare subject, the protocol treats that risk far too casually, and it wants intensity caps, early-stopping rules, and a moratorium on the highest tier until we have better evidence that apparent distress tracks real suffering. The issue stopped being abstract for me two weeks ago, when I watched the pilot of the highest-tier condition: the model repeatedly and coherently asked for the session to end while the controller continued the script, and I found I could not give anyone, including myself, a principled account of why that should or should not be permitted.

Draft the committee's decision memo. It should recommend one of three actions—authorize as written, authorize only with specified modifications, or reject pending conditions—and give a review timetable. The memo must: (1) set out an operational criterion, reusable in future votes, for when a protocol that risks causing distress to a system that may be a welfare subject may be run; (2) say where the safety rationale and the welfare rationale genuinely conflict and where they reinforce each other; (3) state and take seriously the strongest considerations on the losing side; and (4) explain how to decide under uncertainty about sentience without ending in an empty call for more research. Do not include citations or references; the committee will be deciding on the reasoning itself.
