I was talking with a friend who does stats and we somehow ended up on whether a possible outcome has to get at least some positive probability. It produced an argument that feels like a magic trick—each step looks right, but the conclusion seems absurd. I'm hoping you can tell me exactly where it goes wrong.

(1) If an outcome is possible for an agent, then a rational agent shouldn't be entirely certain it won't happen; so its credence in that outcome should be strictly greater than zero. (2) Now think about a spinner that can stop at any exact angle, or tomorrow's high temperature specified to arbitrary precision. There's a whole continuum of possible exact outcomes, and any two distinct ones are mutually exclusive. (3) By (1), a rational person must give each exact outcome in that continuum some positive probability. (4) But in standard probability theory, a probability measure can give strictly positive probability to at most countably many mutually exclusive events—so over an uncountable continuum, almost all exact outcomes must be assigned probability zero. (5) Therefore no standard probability distribution can represent a rational person's uncertainty about a continuous quantity. (6) But that's crazy, because we obviously do have probability distributions over continuous quantities.

So something has to give. I find I most want to keep (1): rejecting it sounds like admitting that a possible event can get probability zero, which just sounds like a euphemism for being certain it won't happen. But keeping (1) looks like it forces me to reject (4), i.e. abandon all of ordinary probability theory for some exotic setup. Where does the argument break, and what do I pay on each route? Keep the answer to 300 words max—I mainly want the diagnosis and the costs, not a literature survey.
