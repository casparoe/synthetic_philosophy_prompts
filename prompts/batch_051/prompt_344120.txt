I am a novelist, not a professional philosopher, and I have begun to suspect that one of my narrative instincts rests on a double standard I cannot defend. In my current book, set in a coastal town during the storm season, a trustworthy character reports two things to a sceptical friend: first, that she saw something unlikely but perfectly natural -- a bird far outside its usual range, in poor light, for a few seconds; second, that she had what she can only describe as an experience of God -- an overwhelming sense of a presence that addressed her, in a moment of calm after a long grief. Writing the scene, I noticed that the first report is treated by the other characters as evidence, however weak, about the bird; the sensible questions concern her eyesight, the light, and whether anyone else was looking. The second report is treated as evidence about her state of mind and her history, but not as evidence about God at all. The asymmetry does not concern how much the report establishes; it concerns what the experience is allowed to count as evidence for in the first place.

My question for you is whether anything justifies that asymmetry, or whether it is simply an inconsistency we live with. Put the strongest form of the question through two matched cases: the same reader-trusted character claims (a) a surprising but natural perceptual experience, and (b) a vivid religious experience. In the first case we treat the experience as a legitimate, if fallible, basis for belief in what was seen, unless defeaters appear; in the second we demand independent corroboration before the experience may count as evidence for God. My question is not whether the religious claim is true, but whether this difference in evidential standing is defensible. If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case where the perceptual report is also unique, fleeting, private, and shaped by expectation and culture; if you attack it, say what follows -- must we then treat every sincere report of a presence as evidence for that presence, or is there a middle ground that does not smuggle the asymmetry back in? In particular, consider whether the asymmetry might be kept at the level of how much confidence an experience warrants but abandoned at the level of whether it warrants anything at all, or the reverse.

Please organise your reply into five titled sections, in this order. Under 'The asymmetry stated', restate precisely what differs in the evidential treatment of the two reports. Under 'Candidate justifications', give the two or three strongest reasons one could offer for treating religious experience as an inferior class of evidence, stated as charitably as possible. Under 'Stress tests', test each candidate against the modified cases I mentioned and any further variants you think are sharper; say which candidates survive. Under 'Verdict', state whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and specify at which level -- whether a report counts as evidence at all, or how much confidence it should produce -- your verdict applies. Under 'What I might be missing', note honestly what would change your verdict. Aim for a paragraph or two per section, and do not supply a bibliography or citations; I want the reasoning itself.
