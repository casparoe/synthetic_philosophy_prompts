I have settled into a policy, and I want it interrogated rather than reassured. It grows out of reading Szasz's argument that what gets called mental illness is not a disease in the way tuberculosis is a disease but rather a metaphor for patterns of problems in living. My everyday version of the view is this: I refuse to let psychiatric labels do explanatory work. If a friend says our mutual acquaintance did something cruel because of his depression, I retranslate the claim into something about what he has been feeling, what situations he was trying to escape, and what he was pursuing, and I treat the label itself as adding no explanation. I do the same to myself: I will not say my anxiety made me cancel the talk; I will say I was frightened of being judged and chose to avoid it. In short, I treat a diagnostic term as a compressed description of a pattern, not as an agent inside the person that causes the pattern.

Recently the policy has been put under pressure. A close friend was diagnosed with bipolar disorder, and although I find the term useful as a way of pointing to a pattern in her moods and decisions, I distrust every sentence in which the diagnosis is offered as the explanation of her behavior — including sentences she herself offers. The people around me say I am denying something real about her. Am I? Under what conditions would my retranslation policy lead me systematically astray, for instance by concealing cases in which behavior really is being driven by a disease process, or by making me uncharitable to people for whom a diagnosis is a useful and liberating self-description? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps the skepticism about medicalized moral explanations without treating all diagnostic talk as empty? I want the assessment to take seriously that I am not a clinician and mostly meet diagnostic explanations in ordinary conversation rather than in medical settings. Please say how confident you are in the assessment, and identify the particular point on which you are least sure.
