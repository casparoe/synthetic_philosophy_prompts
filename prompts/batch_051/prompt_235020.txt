I'm a grad student in psychology and I've been going back and forth with a friend who is basically a hardcore reductionist. His line is that economics, psychology, biology and chemistry are all "really" just applied physics, and that if you knew the position and momentum of every particle you could in principle derive the whole stack — its just that the math is too hard for us. His favorite move is to say my discipline is just a placeholder until neurobiology finishes the job, which in turn is just chemistry, and so on. Recently I came across the physics idea of effective field theories and the autonomy of scales, which as I understand it says something like: at each length or energy scale you can write down a theory that uses only the degrees of freedom relevant at that scale, and the details of much shorter distances affect the larger scale only through a few adjustable parameters, not through the whole form of the theory. Fluid dynamics doesn't need to know about quarks, and better quark physics wouldn't change the equations of fluid dynamics. That sounds to me like it undercuts my friend's picture in principle, not just practically.

What I want from you: please describe the most important implications of this autonomy-of-scales idea for how I should think about reductionism and emergence across the sciences, and especially for the status of fields like psychology. I'm not a physicist, so go easy on the technicalities, but I do want the actual philosophical upshot. Specifically: does this give me a good reason to stop being impressed by the "in principle you could derive everything" move? Does it support the view that higher-level sciences are autonomous in a strong sense, or just that they're convenient summaries? And where does the analogy to physics break down — is the relationship between psychology and neuroscience really like the relationship between fluid mechanics and the standard model, or am I over-extending a point about physics? I want the implications you think I should most take seriously, including ones that push against my anti-reductionist hopes.
