I am a senior product designer at a consumer technology company, and I have just been assigned to lead a working group on what we internally call “happiness-preserving defaults”: features that quietly restrict, reorder, or ration a user’s choices because we believe the choices they would otherwise make are bad for them. The same week I was given this assignment, I read Book V, Chapter 5 of *The Brothers Karamazov*, “The Grand Inquisitor,” and the chapter has unsettled me in a way I cannot simply file away. I am thinking especially of the part of the Inquisitor’s speech where he explains that the error in Christ’s teaching was to suppose that ordinary human beings can bear freedom, and that what the mass of people actually want is miracle, mystery, and authority; that they are happier when their choices are made for them, and that freedom only torments them. I do not want to quote from memory, so I will say it this way: in that passage, the Inquisitor does not merely predict that people will fail at freedom; he claims that freedom itself is a burden from which most people would, if they understood their own good, gladly be relieved.

I need a decision procedure my team can actually use, grounded in what that passage and the surrounding chapter imply about the relation between freedom and happiness. We decide on defaults, feature changes, and product surfaces that affect several million people, and we must sign off on a proposal in a one-hour review meeting. No one on the team has a philosophy background. I want a reusable procedure, not an essay, and it must be usable in the meeting without special preparation.

Please write me that procedure. It should take the Inquisitor’s argument seriously enough to extract from it the conditions under which a decision to restrict or override a user’s choice in the name of their happiness is justified, and turn those conditions into an ordered set of questions a product team can ask. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, what kind of evidence would be relevant, and where the procedure should stop with a no. It should also mark the points where the procedure cannot settle the matter and the decision must be handed to a person because it is a question of values rather than prediction. I want it honest about where the procedure is doing moral work disguised as product judgment.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals the working group will likely consider:

1. Automatically limiting a user’s daily feed to a set number of minutes by default, on the grounds that users later report being happier when they spend less time in the app.
2. Adding a mandatory delay before a user can disable a privacy-protective filter, on the grounds that most users would be happier with the filter on and only disable it impulsively.
3. Reordering what a user sees so that the most engaging material appears only after they have seen material the team regards as more substantively valuable, on the grounds that users are bad judges of what will actually satisfy them.

Make sure at least one of these applications ends with the procedure telling us we should not do something the company would prefer to do, and say plainly where that happens and why. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which features of the Inquisitor’s argument it deliberately refuses to operationalize, and which assumptions about other people’s happiness it simply has to take on faith.
