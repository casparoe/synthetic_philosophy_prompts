Robin Hanson, in “The Great Filter – Are We Almost Past It?” (1998), writes:

> “There thus exists a great filter between death and expanding lasting life, and humanity faces the ominous question: how far along this filter are we?”

Hanson’s argument runs from the apparent deadness of the space around us to the existence of at least one highly improbable step between lifeless chemistry and a visible, expanding, lasting civilization. Later in the essay he defends the premise that any successful lineage, even a machine one, would remain under competitive pressure to spread, so that we would eventually see it. Suppose we now treat artificial minds as the likely outcome of advanced life, but take seriously the idea that designed minds may not be shaped by natural selection in the way that premise requires: they may not reproduce, may not be outcompeted by more expansionist rivals, and may be satisfied by compact local computation. Does Hanson’s argument still go through, does it need repair, or does the Great Silence stop being evidence for a filter? Identify which premise the new assumption bears on, and what the least costly repair is. I’m asking about the logic of the argument, not for an empirical survey of astrobiology or SETI.
