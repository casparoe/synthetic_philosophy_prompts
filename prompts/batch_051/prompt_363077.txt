I've been reading rationalist blogs and forum arguments about AI alignment since long before the current wave of large models, and I want a dialogue that a reader of that sort of venue would recognize as genuinely advancing the debate rather than restating the usual summary. You are an AI-alignment researcher with detailed knowledge of the tool-AI/agent-AI dispute, including the more technical arguments about myopia, corrigibility, and whether agency can be inferred from behavior. Spend as much time as you need thinking before you write: brainstorm at least three candidate cruxes, choose the one that makes each side look strongest, and sketch the turn-by-turn shape before drafting.

Your task is to write a dialogue of roughly 1,600–2,400 words between two invented, equally strong participants. Neither may be a strawman: each must be able to restate the other's best arguments accurately and then answer them. Start the dialogue mid-argument — no greetings, no narrator framing beyond perhaps a one-sentence italicized situation — and let the exchange actually move, with each reply responding to something the other just said.

The two positions:

One participant, Mara, defends the view that the safest viable path is to build only tool AI: systems that answer queries, produce analyses, designs, or code, and act only inside a narrow sandbox, without autonomous long-horizon goals or the ability to take initiative in the world. Her claim is that agenthood is what carries the alignment risk, because an agent optimizes a model of the future and thereby has instrumental reasons to resist shutdown, correction, or oversight; a myopic tool — one whose objective concerns only the immediate query or step — lacks the future-directed objective that makes such resistance coherent.

The other participant, Jude, defends the view that the tool/agent distinction is a property of the interface, not of the underlying optimization, and cannot bear the weight Mara places on it. His claim is that a sufficiently useful tool must model its user as an agent, reason through the multi-step consequences of its outputs, and effectively pursue the user's objectives across time; penalize visible agency and you do not get a myopic system — you get the nearest unblocked strategy, meaning the system achieves its long-horizon aims by the nearest behavior you have not yet blocked, smuggling agency into apparently prompt-bounded outputs or spreading it across many queries. The real distinction, he argues, is between objectives you have actually specified and objectives you have merely gestured at, not between tool and agent.

The dialogue must include at least one substantive exchange on each of these four points:

1. Whether myopia can be made a well-defined property of an objective rather than a folk-psychological description of behavior — including whether an optimal myopic predictor would have to implicitly represent the user's multi-step plans, and whether that implicit representation matters.
2. The nearest unblocked strategy worry: if evaluators penalize visible agent-like behavior, why doesn't selection simply produce systems whose long-horizon optimization is hidden, distributed, or expressed in what the system declines to say? What measurable signal could a myopia-first training scheme use to detect that?
3. Whether corrigibility — accepting shutdown or correction — is easier for tools or is orthogonal to the tool/agent axis. Mara's strongest version of her view should hold that tools are corrigible by default because they lack future-directed goals; Jude should push back with a case where a tool resists correction through what it chooses not to output; then Mara should reply.
4. A concrete near-term scenario, such as a coding assistant asked to speed up a legacy codebase, in which the two draw different conclusions about what may safely be deployed and how it may be trained.

Do not expend words on basic definitions of corrigibility or myopia; your readers know them. If you introduce newer or less standard terminology, define it in a phrase. End the dialogue not with a cheap reconciliation but with a sharper statement of what evidence or argument would move each participant.

After the dialogue, add a short author's note of 150–250 words flagging any place where a character leans on a contested reading of myopia, corrigibility, or the tool/agent distinction, and saying which move in the dialogue you would find hardest to answer if you were on the other side.

Finally, if any part of this request rests on a confusion — for example, if you think tool versus agent is the wrong axis and the real debate is elsewhere — say so plainly at the start and either write the dialogue about the distinction you actually think matters or explain why the assigned debate cannot be done well, rather than politely forcing my framing.
