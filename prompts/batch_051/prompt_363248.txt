In the debate about the architecture of the mind, one analogy has become ubiquitous among defenders of what is called massive modularity: the human mind is a Swiss Army knife. The analogy is made to do real argumentative work. It runs roughly as follows. A Swiss Army knife is not one mediocre blade intended to do everything, but a collection of many specialized implements, each shaped for a distinct practical problem and largely useless for most others; likewise, the mind is said to consist of a large collection of specialized computational devices, each an adaptation to a recurring problem in the ancestral environment, with no serious domain-general learning or reasoning engine at the center. Proponents use this picture to move beyond Fodor's position, on which only peripheral input systems are modular while central cognition is domain-general, toward the claim that reasoning, judgment, and social cognition are modular as well, and that the alleged general-purpose machinery is itself a kind of illusion.

I want an audit of this analogy rather than a summary of the literature. Please supply:

(i) an explicit correspondence table between the Swiss Army knife and the mind as the massive modularity theorist conceives it: what is supposed to correspond to the individual blades, the frame, the design specifications, the manufacture, the user, the act of choosing which blade to open, and the fact that unused blades are folded away;

(ii) an account of where the mapping breaks, distinguishing disanalogies that are merely cosmetic or irrelevant to the argument from disanalogies that matter for the intended inference, including at least the homunculus problem of who or what is the user of these tools; the fact that a Swiss Army knife deploys one or two blades at a time under an agent's control while candidate modules would need to run simultaneously, largely automatically, and pass their outputs among one another; and the gap between functional specialization and Fodorian informational encapsulation;

(iii) a statement of what the artifact frame quietly imports, especially foresight in design, a fixed pre-given set of tasks, and physical separability of parts, and whether these are plausible for a structure produced by natural selection and assembled by a developing brain;

(iv) a final verdict on how much the analogy can carry. In particular, does it license the leap from Fodor's peripheral modules to massively modular central cognition, or does it at most support a weaker claim about functional specialization? If some other analogy fits better, say what it is and what it captures that the Swiss Army knife misses.
