False theories can plainly differ in how close they come to the truth: the claim that the solar system has nine planets is false, but it is far closer to the truth than the claim that it has nine trillion. Karl Popper wanted to make this intuitive ordering precise. On his original proposal, define a theory's truth content as the set of true claims it entails and its falsity content as the set of false claims it entails; then theory A is at least as close to the truth as theory B just in case B's truth content is included in A's truth content and A's falsity content is included in B's falsity content (with at least one inclusion strict for greater closeness). This proposal ran aground: as Pavel Tichý and David Miller showed, when A and B are both false the two inclusions can be jointly satisfied only in a degenerate way, so Popper's criterion never lets one false theory count as closer to the truth than another.

Responses to this difficulty split into two broad families. Content-based approaches keep the truth-content/falsity-content template but try to weight or filter the consequences that count, on the grounds that every theory entails endlessly many gerrymandered or irrelevant consequences that should not contribute to truthlikeness. Likeness-based approaches instead treat closeness to truth as similarity between possibilities: a theory's truthlikeness is a function of how similar the worlds (or models) in which it is true are to the actual world, usually via a distance metric over worlds. The content family faces the challenge of saying which consequences matter and how much they contribute without presupposing the very notion it is trying to define; the likeness family faces the challenge that the resulting ordering seems to depend on a choice of similarity metric that critics regard as arbitrary.
