In the centipede game, backward induction prescribes that the first mover takes at the first node, but experimental players frequently pass at nodes where the standard theory says they should take. The explanations for this that I have collected are: (i) social preferences — the displayed monetary payoffs are not the players' true utilities, because players also care about fairness or about the co-player's payoff; (ii) incomplete information and reputation — if a player assigns positive probability to the co-player's being a cooperative or non-equilibrium type, then passing can be rational even for a strictly selfish player, both because continuing reveals useful information and because a player may want to build or protect a cooperative reputation; (iii) small stakes and noisy choice — laboratory payoffs are too small for the backward-induction differences to matter, and a little decision error drowns out the predicted advantage of taking. I am fairly sure there is a fourth standard explanation I am leaving out. State what it is, explain what feature of the puzzle it captures that (i)–(iii) miss, and make clear why it is not simply a restatement or special case of (ii).
