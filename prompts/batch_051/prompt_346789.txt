I have been trying to reconstruct a half-remembered argument from an old discussion of fine-tuning, and I want to know whether it goes through. The argument claims that the fine-tuning of the physical constants gives no reason to prefer a multiverse over a single universe with brute constants.

(1) An observation E confirms a hypothesis H over a rival R only if E is more probable under H than under R.
(2) Let H be the multiverse hypothesis: there are many universes whose constants vary, so at least some will be life-permitting. Let R be the single-universe hypothesis: there is one universe, and its constants are drawn once at random from the same underlying range.
(3) On R, the probability that the single universe has life-permitting constants looks very small, since life-permitting combinations are a tiny fraction of the range.
(4) But we could not possibly observe a life-hostile universe: any observer necessarily finds themselves in a life-permitting universe. So the probability that we observe a life-permitting universe is 1 under both H and R.
(5) Therefore the evidence is equally probable under H and R, and it cannot confirm H over R.
(6) So fine-tuning gives no reason to prefer the multiverse over the single-universe rival.

I suspect the trouble is in (4), but I cannot state exactly why, and I have seen sophisticated people defend precisely this conditionalization on our own existence. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the correct way to state the probability in question?
