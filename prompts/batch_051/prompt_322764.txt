Consider the following argument that the Buddhist doctrine of karma, understood as a theory of moral desert, cannot be reconciled with the doctrine of no-self (anatta).

(1) Moral desert is a relation between an action and the very agent who performed it: a being deserves reward or punishment only for what that same being did.
(2) According to the no-self doctrine, there is no single substantial self that persists across rebirth; what runs from one life to the next is only a causal continuum of mental and physical events.
(3) Therefore, the being born in a later life is not numerically identical to the being who performed the earlier action; it is at most a causal successor.
(4) If a later being is not numerically identical to an earlier actor, then the later being did not perform the earlier action.
(5) A being cannot deserve reward or punishment for an action it did not perform.
(6) Therefore, on the no-self doctrine, the later being cannot deserve the pleasant or painful circumstances that karma attributes to an earlier life's actions.
(7) Therefore, karma as moral desert is incompatible with no-self.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and on what reading of the key terms? If it is valid, identify the premise a defender of karmic desert would be best advised to reject, and explain what the rejection commits them to — for example, a distinction between ultimate and conventional identity, or an account of desert that tracks causal-psychological continuity rather than strict numerical identity.
