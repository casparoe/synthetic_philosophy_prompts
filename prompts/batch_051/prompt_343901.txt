I am drafting a two-page proposal for a one-semester research fellowship in political theory, and I want the proposal organized around a question I cannot quite bring into focus. The topic is what I have been calling constitutional gaps: situations in which the standing rules stop, as opposed to ordinary cases in which parties disagree about what the rules mean. An interim government holds office after an irregular transfer of power; a legislature loses quorum during an emergency yet officials still act; an election law specifies day one and day ninety but says nothing about day forty-five. Somebody governs in these intervals, and I want an account of what makes that governing legitimate or illegitimate rather than merely unavoidable. I keep returning to one passage in John Locke, Second Treatise of Government, Chapter XIV ("Of Prerogative"), section 160:

"This power to act according to discretion, for the public good, without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it, is that which is called prerogative: for since in some governments the lawmaking power is not always in being, and is usually too numerous, and so too slow, for the dispatch requisite to execution; and because also it is impossible to foresee, and so by laws to provide for, all accidents and necessities that may concern the public, or to make such laws as will do no harm, if they are executed with an inflexible rigour, on all occasions, and upon all persons that may come in their way; therefore there is a latitude left to the executive power, to do many things of choice which the laws do not prescribe."

Please turn this into the proposal. The question should be stated precisely so that it has a determinate answer: when the rules run out, is the authority of whoever acts in the gap best understood in Lockean terms, as discretion directed at the public good outside or even against the standing law, or is that model the very thing a constitutional order should exclude, so that we need a distinct account of who may act and by what standard? I want the proposal to show that the obvious existing positions leave the question open: rule-centered approaches tend to treat gaps as defects to be cleaned up by retrospective authorization or by catch-all interpreters rather than as situations that call for their own normative theory, and sovereignty-centered emergency accounts tend to treat the gap as the moment where bare decision reappears beneath the rules, which describes the fact of decision without justifying it. The method should be a close reconstruction of Locke's argument in Chapter XIV read against his later treatment of tyranny and the people's judgment that power is being used against the trust placed in it, tested on pressure cases such as caretaker governments, lapsed emergency powers, and ratification after the fact. Say what a useful result would look like — I suspect it would be a small number of criteria for distinguishing legitimate gap-filling authority from usurpation — and say honestly what would make the project too large for one semester, such as sliding into a general theory of legitimacy. Also state what would count as the project's failing. Do not include citations or a bibliography; I will add the scholarly apparatus myself. Please write the answer in American English.
