Next month I am giving a workshop talk on Avicenna's flying-man argument to a philosophy department with a strong cognitive-science orientation. The audience will be mostly philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists who work on the bodily self: body ownership, interoception, agency, and disorders such as depersonalization and out-of-body experience. One member of the audience is a specialist in Avicenna, and I expect her to care most about whether I read the flying-man passage correctly, but my professional risk comes mainly from the empirically minded part of the room.

My thesis is that the flying-man thought experiment still does serious work against strong versions of the embodied-self view. I am not defending the claim that the argument proves an immaterial substance separable from the body. I am defending the narrower claim that the thought experiment isolates a minimal self-awareness whose content is not bodily, and that a bodily-self account must explain this without merely pointing to the brain processes that make such awareness possible. My argument has three moves: (1) the experiment is best read as a subtraction procedure, in which one starts with ordinary self-awareness and removes all body-related contents; (2) what survives is not merely an artifact of the setup but a datum to be explained, namely the pre-reflective for-me-ness of experience that bodily-self theorists themselves often acknowledge; and (3) typical empirical responses confuse the enabling conditions of self-awareness with its content, or else shift to a diachronic or social notion of the self, which is not what the minimal-self debate is about.
