In my ethics seminar we've been stuck for weeks on a version of the moral saints question: is a life of maximal moral goodness actually a desirable life? The pro-saint side says that if moral reasons are the most important reasons there are, then the person who responds to them as completely as possible is living the best human life, and missing out on messing around, writing weird poetry, or having non-moral passions is just the price of having your priorities right. The anti side keeps pushing the line from Susan Wolf's paper: a person whose every deliberation is moral would have to be sort of flat, because being a close friend, being funny, or caring about a personal project all seem to require caring about things for reasons that aren't moral. So the saintly life isn't the best life; it's an admirable but impoverished one.

What frustrates me is that we basicly agree on all the facts. Nobody disputes that such a person would do enormous good, be reliable and kind, and nobody in the room thinks friendship or humor are bad. And yet neither side has moved an inch, and I'm starting to think we aren't actually arguing about one question. My working theory is that the pro side means by 'moral saint' something like "a person who always does what morality requires," while the anti side means "a person whose psychology contains nothing but moral motives," and that a good chunk of the fight is verbal. But I can't tell wether that's right: it might instead be a bedrock disagreement about whether non-moral excellences are part of what makes a life go well, or a hidden empirical claim about whether one person can be maximally moral and still be a fully realized friend, artist, or goofball.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, with reasons, not a survey of the literature. Is it verbal, substantive, a difference about which cases count as data, a clash of starting intuitions neither side can argue for, or a hidden factual question? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an empirical finding, a reformulated question — or should we expect it to stay stalled because the question is ill-posed?
