I'm on the editorial board of a math journal that publishes a lot of combinatorics, and the recurring fight is what to do with proofs like the four-colour theorem or the Kepler conjecture, where the human-readable part is a reduction to a finite case and then a computation runs through more cases than any referee could genuinely check by hand. A colleague who works with proof assistents floated the following scheme, and I want to understand how it would play out before I raise it with the rest of the board.

The scheme: a consortium of journals and one large funder set up a shared registry for computer-assisted proofs. On submission, authors must deposit the code, the input data, and a short lemma saying exactly what the computation is claimed to establish; the journal referees only the human part, namely that the theorem follows from that lemma and that the lemma is the right thing to prove. The computation itself is never referee-checked. Instead, the consortium attaches a bounty to each deposited proof, funded by an author submission fee and topped up by the funder. A replication bounty goes to the first outside team that independently reimplements the computation from the published spec and confirms the lemma. A larger bug bounty goes to whoever shows that the deposited computation fails to establish the lemma. Papers carry a pending mark until either the replication bounty is claimed or five years pass with no bug bounty being paid out.

I'm not asking whether the scheme is good, fair, or principled. I'm asking how it would actually behave. Walk me through what it does to authors—how they write and document their code, which problems they pick, and how they chop proofs into lemmas—and what it does to replication teams, journals, and the funder. Where does the scheme get gamed first? What second-order effects appear? And are the failure modes the kind you can patch, or are they baked into the idea of paying people to replicate computations?
