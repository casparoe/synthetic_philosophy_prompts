I have been reading C. Lloyd Morgan's An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894), and I keep tripping over an apparent inconsistency in what he says about his own canon — the principle that became one of the most cited rules in comparative psychology. The canon is usually boiled down to a ban on interpreting an animal's activity as the outcome of higher psychological processes whenever it can be fairly interpreted as the outcome of processes lower on the scale of psychological evolution; the word 'fairly' seems to carry weight, since the lower explanation has to be an adequate one and not merely a logically possible one. Morgan's signature case in the book is his terrier Tony, who learned to open a garden gate: a casual observer would have described the feat as insight, but Morgan had watched the gradual trial-and-error shaping of the behavior and argued that the clever-looking end product required no intelligent foresight at all. So far the position sounds austere.

But Morgan appears to have intended something much weaker than the slogan suggests, and he said so once he noticed readers turning the canon into a general preference for mindless explanations. In later writings and responses to critics he insisted that the canon was a rule about what counts as adequate evidence in a particular case, not a substantive verdict that animals lack intelligence or consciousness, and he explicitly allowed — indeed warned against ruling out — the higher interpretation when a species had provided independent signs of the relevant capacity. On this reading the canon does two jobs at once: it blocks a specific anecdotal fallacy, and it leaves the higher interpretation available, perhaps even required, once independent evidence exists. Are these two things in tension? Is there a reading on which they are not merely compatible but are the same methodological point stated twice, and is that reading textually honest or special pleading? I would like you to take a definite position: give the interpretation of the canon and of Morgan's clarifications that you think requires the least special pleading, and say exactly what work the word 'fairly' does in it.
