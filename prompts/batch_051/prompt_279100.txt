I am the undergraduate representative on my university's Ecological Intervention Review Panel, and this term I am taking a module on the ethics of biodiversity conservation. The panel has received an application from a conservation genetics group in our School of Biological Sciences for permission to carry out a small field trial of a threshold-dependent gene drive on the invasive black rat (Rattus rattus) population of the university's island field station. I have been asked to draft the short written opinion that will be circulated before Friday's meeting, and I would like you to prepare the document I will circulate.

The island is a small offshore reserve owned by the university. It supports a large breeding colony of shearwaters and storm petrels, but their eggs and chicks are heavily predated by rats introduced from boats roughly a century ago. Ten years of census data show a steep decline in the seabird colony while the rat population remains dense. The applicants propose releasing laboratory-reared male rats carrying an engineered toxin-antidote element tightly linked to a recessive sterility cargo. The element's toxin is only partly compensated in single-copy carriers. Unlike a homing drive, which copies itself into most offspring, this element is threshold-dependent: because single-copy carriers have reduced viability, the element can spread through a population only if it is released above a critical threshold frequency. The applicants' models indicate that a release above that threshold would allow the element to reach near fixation on the island, after which the sterility cargo would crash the rat population within six to eight generations and give the seabird colony a chance to recover. They stress two safety features. First, the architecture is supposed to be self-limiting across space: the occasional rat reaching the mainland would be a rare single-copy carrier far below the threshold and should be purged within a few generations. Second, they argue that the intervention is reversible on the island within a meaningful time frame, because releasing unmodified rats could push the local frequency back below the threshold. On this basis the applicants describe the trial as locally contained rather than genuinely irreversible.

The panel's difficulty is partly empirical and partly philosophical. The threshold frequency rests on laboratory cage experiments with a small number of lineages, and the model depends on uncertain estimates of island-mainland migration, density dependence, and the rate at which the toxin and antidote components separate through recombination. The panel has no field precedent from any wild mammalian gene-drive release, so the predicted threshold is largely model-based. One group on the panel accepts the containment argument in principle but regards the contingency plan for a mainland escape as unrealistic, because it presumes that unmodified rats could be released near scattered docks and farm buildings without landowner consent. A second group argues that delay is not a neutral option: every year of inaction is itself an irreversible ecological loss, likely the extirpation of the seabird colony, and it regards the threshold-dependent design as the most prudent proposal yet put before us. A third group holds that the university should not authorise the first wild release of a synthetic mammalian gene drive while the rate of construct breakdown and the element's behaviour in wild populations are still largely matters of modelling rather than evidence.

Please draft the panel's written opinion, addressed to the university's Research Governance Committee. It should set out the decision clearly; recommend one concrete course of action — approve the trial, approve it with conditions, or reject it; specify any conditions, staged review, and time frame if approval is recommended; and engage in good faith with the strongest consideration on the losing side.
