Exam question: Explain what Wolfram means by computational irreducibility, distinguish it from chaos and from undecidability, and then evaluate how far it constitutes a limit on scientific explanation.

Student answer:

Computational irreducibility is the property of a process whose later states cannot be found by any computation substantially shorter than simulating the process itself. The rules and initial state determine the later states, but there is no shortcut, closed form, or compressed derivation; the most efficient method is simply to run the process step by step.

It is not the same as chaos. Chaos is sensitive dependence on initial conditions: arbitrarily small uncertainties in the starting state grow quickly, so long-term forecasting demands finer and finer precision. That is a point about measurement and error propagation, not about whether any computational shortcut exists. It is also not the same as undecidability. Undecidability means that no algorithm at all can answer the relevant question. In computational irreducibility the algorithm does exist, namely simulation; the point is only that any algorithm that works must do about as much computational work as the process itself.

On explanation, the strongest version of Wolfram's limit is this. If the only way to derive the system's behavior is as long as the behavior itself, then the behavior has no compressed description; in other words, the trajectory is algorithmically incompressible. It follows that computationally irreducible systems are unpredictable in the same sense as chaotic systems: we cannot know the outcome before it happens. Understood this way, irreducibility blocks the classical scientific ambition of anticipating the future from a compact statement of the laws and initial conditions.

The best reply is that explanation is not exhausted by prediction. We can still explain an irreducible system by giving its rules and initial state, and by giving coarse-grained or statistical descriptions of its behavior. What irreducibility excludes is only one specific explanatory good, namely a compressed predictive derivation of individual outcomes. I therefore side with the moderate view.
