In my aesthetics of everyday things class I keep coming back to letters, because they're the cleanest example I know of a thing that stays one thing while its shape wanders all over the place. The a with the little roof in a serif face and the plain circle-with-a-tail a in most handwriting share almost no geometry, but nobody hesitates to say they're both a's.

So here are the criteria I give my students for a mark being the letter a. (1) Shape: it looks like a recognized variant — a closed bowl, and whatever extra stroke it has sits on the right side and doesn't rise much above the bowl. (2) Role: it does the job of a in a writing system, contrasting with e, o, d and the rest, so that its identiy isn't fixed by its look alone. (3) Intention: the person who made the mark was aiming at an a. (4) Uptake: a competent reader reads it as an a.

Now the borderline case. Take a sentence written entirely in mirror script, every glyph flipped left-to-right. In context the flipped a is little trouble: the writer meant a, a reader practiced with mirror script reads it as a, and it contrasts with the other mirrored letters exactly the way a normal a does. But show the same shape alone on a flashcard and people — often the same people — call it a backwards squiggle, not the letter a. Put it into a normal sentence and it jumps out as a stray glyph, not an a.

I can't have it both ways, or maybe I can, but I want to know which. Is the mirrored a an a? If you say no, you're letting shape veto role, intention and uptake, and I'd like to know what gives shape that authority when it plainly can't settle identity across typefaces. If you say yes, you owe me an account of why the isolated shape doesn't count. Then tell me whether the right lesson is that my criteria are wrong, or that a letter is just a messier category than the four-part list lets on.
