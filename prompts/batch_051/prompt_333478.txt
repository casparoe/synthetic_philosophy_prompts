I've been arguing with my roommate about judicial review and I keep hitting a question I don't know how to state cleanly. In the US (and plenty of other places), a legislature gets elected, passes a statute, and then a handful of judges who were never elected—or were confirmed decades ago and can't be removed by any current majority—can strike the statute down because it conflicts with a constitution ratified by people who are all dead. My roommate says this is just obviously anti-democratic: the living majority is governed by dead people and nine lawyers. But the same system also runs regular elections, protects the speech of dissenters, and describes itself as a democracy, and most people seem fine with the arrangement in the abstract even when they hate particular rulings. What makes it stranger is that courts don't do it consistently: a court will strike down a restriction on political speech with very little deference to the legislature, and then turn around and say it must defer heavily to the same legislature on economic regulation, even when individual justices think the law is dumb. So the practice seems to presuppose some picture of democracy or legitimacy that isn't just "whatever the current elected majority votes for"—but I can't tell exactly what it is, and I don't trust the judges' own justifications to reveal the implicit theory.

So: what philosophical committments does judicial review actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory of democracy, legitimacy, the people, or the relation between present and past majorities that the practice only makes sense under. Reconstruct it. Then distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag the places where it's philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would each license the same behavior. Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? Last, tell me what would concretely have to change in the practice—not just in its rhetoric—if you replaced the presupposed view with a straightforward procedural-majoritarian view of legitimacy, where a law's legitimacy comes only from being passed by a current elected majority. Also, I'd like you to say how confident you are about each part of the reconstruction and to single out the point you're least sure of.
