A person who, under her own name, repeats a malicious and false rumor about a colleague is blamed without hesitation, and doing it under a pseudonym does not, in most people's judgment, dissolve the responsibility. But when the same falsehood circulates through thousands of anonymous forwards, each one relaying something already in circulation, ordinary judgment shifts: the harm is now attributed to an impersonal wave — the public, the discourse, the platform — and each participant can sincerely report that he merely passed along what everyone was already saying. The diffused case produces more harm, yet the responsibility attributed to any one participant collapses as the number of participants grows. Kierkegaard argued that this collapse is not a natural consequence of scale but an artifact of the press, which manufactures a public that is an abstraction in which each person can act without ever acting as an individual, so that no one ends up answerable for what everyone did. Is there a defensible principle that justifies treating the diffused case as morally different from the face-to-face case, or is this asymmetry simply an accepted evasion of responsibility? I want the strongest candidate justification, tested against nearby cases — for instance, an organizer who authors the rumor and then deliberately lets the public carry it, or a village of twelve in which every forwarding is noticed — and an explicit verdict on whether we should keep, reverse, or give up the ordinary pattern of judgment.
