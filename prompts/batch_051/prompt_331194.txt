In my free will seminar we've been going back and forth about Frankfurt cases, and I ended up building an argument that now makes me kind of queasy: each step looked fine when I wrote it out, but the conclusion feels way to strong for me to actually accept. I'd like you to tell me exactly where it goes wrong — or, if it isn't flawed, which premise I have to eat and what it costs.

For background: Frankfurt cases are meant to show that the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP) is false — that someone can be morally responsible for what they did even if they couldn't have done otherwise. In the standard setup, Jones decides on his own to do something bad, while Black is standing by to make Jones decide that way if Jones had been about to decide otherwise; Black never has to intervene, and most people still judge Jones responsible. The usual diagnosis is that responsibility depends on the actual sequence: Jones's actual deliberation and decision were his own, so the missing alternatives don't matter.

So here's the argument I built.

(1) If responsibility is grounded in the actual sequence, then once an agent's actual sequence is his own, the absence of alternative possibilities is irrelevant.
(2) If the absence of alternatives is irrelevant, then the way the alternatives were blocked should also be irrelevant — only the actual sequence matters.
(3) So take a case where a neuroscientist directly causes Smith's decision to donate to a charity by stimulating his brain, and also has a backup device: if Smith had been about to decide not to donate, the implant would have made him donate anyway. Smith couldn't have done otherwise.
(4) From (1) and (2), Smith is responsible just in case his actual sequence is his own.
(5) In the direct-implant case, his actual sequence isn't his own — it's the neuroscientist's doing — so Smith isn't responsible. That's fine.
(6) But now soften the manipulation. The neuroscientist doesn't touch Smith's brain directly. Instead, he plants a moving article about famine relief in Smith's news feed, knowing from a well-validated predictive model that Smith will read it, weigh the reasons it gives, and decide to donate. He would have intervened only if Smith had shown signs of not being persuaded. Smith reads the article, deliberates about the reasons, and decides to donate.
(7) In this softer case, Smith's actual sequence — reading, deliberating, deciding on the basis of reasons he takes to be good — looks exactly like Jones's in the Frankfurt case. The only difference is that a manipulator arranged the inputs and stood ready to intervene.
(8) If the actual-sequence view is right, then Smith is responsible in the softer case, because his actual sequence is his own in just the way Jones's is.
(9) But if Smith is responsible in the softer case, it's hard to see why he's not responsible in the direct-implant case too. The line between "arranging the inputs someone deliberates over" and "directly causing their decision" is a difference in how the actual sequence was made available, not a difference in the actual sequence itself. And step (2) says only the actual sequence matters.
(10) So the actual-sequence reading of Frankfurt cases either collapses into saying Smith is responsible in the direct-implant case — which seems crazy — or it has to slip in a requirement about how the alternatives were blocked, which sounds like alternative possibilities sneaking back in.
(11) Therefore, either Frankfurt cases don't give a stable way to reject PAP, or we have to say that a directly manipulated person is responsible in a way most of us would flatly deny.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), because I find the actual-sequence diagnosis of Frankfurt cases really compelling, but I also really don't want to end up at (11). So: where does this go wrong? If it's valid, which premise should I drop, and what does that do to the Frankfurt-case argument against PAP? Please don't just lay out the options and leave me to choose — take a definite position and defend it. And can you lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
