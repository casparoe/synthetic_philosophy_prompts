I keep running into the phrase "responsibility gap" in discussions of autonomous systems, and I have realized that people use it to mean several different things at once. Roughly, the term gets used for situations in which a learning or autonomous system causes a serious harm, but there seems to be no human or institution that can fairly be held morally or legally responsible for it — not because the investigation is incomplete, but because the relevant decisions were distributed across designers, trainers, operators, and the system's own learning process, many of them made before anyone could know how the system would behave. Some authors treat such gaps as real features of increasingly autonomous systems; others say the gaps are an illusion and the cases are just ordinary negligence or distributed responsibility misdescribed. I want a definition that can do the sorting rather than inherit the confusion. I care about both moral and legal responsibility; if your definition can serve only one, say which and why.

Here are the cases I want the definition to get right. (1) Deliberate remote kill: an officer remotely pilots a drone and deliberately fires on a clearly marked ambulance; I am confident this is not a gap. (2) Known product defect: a toaster with a documented wiring defect catches fire and injures its owner, after the manufacturer ignored repeated reports; I am confident this is not a gap either. (3) Learned trading flash crash: a reinforcement-learning trading system whose designers specified only a profit reward function learns a strategy that triggers a flash crash, and no one can reconstruct the learned policy that placed the orders; this feels like a gap. (4) Automated-vehicle edge case: a self-driving car hits a cyclist in a scenario that appeared in neither its training nor its test suite, and the safety driver had roughly 0.8 seconds between the first unusual sensor reading and impact; this also feels like a gap, though less securely. (5) Rubber-stamp review: a hospital system flags claims for denial and a human reviewer must approve each denial, but the volume makes meaningful review impossible, and a clear error harms a patient; I genuinely do not know how to classify this. (6) Triage-score bias: a hospital uses a machine-learning model to allocate ICU beds, the score tracks race through zip-code and cost-history proxies that no one chose in order to discriminate, and following the scores results in a Black patient being denied a bed; I am torn. (7) LLM threat sent verbatim: a person asks a large language model to draft a threatening message to an ex-partner, reads it, and sends it unchanged; I would call this no gap, but colleagues insist there is something gap-like in it, and I want the definition to say something principled either way.

Please propose a working definition, test it against each of the seven cases and state the verdict for each, say where the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend any surprising verdicts. Then flag the single verdict you are least confident in and why. After the prose, give me a JSON object with the following fields: "definition" (the one-sentence working definition), "components" (an array of the separate conditions or clauses that make it up), "verdicts" (an array of objects with "case", "classification" — one of "gap", "no_gap", or "borderline_or_no_answer" — "deciding_clause", and "confidence" — one of "high", "medium", or "low"), "least_secure_verdict" (a one-sentence explanation of which verdict is least secure), and "revision" (one or two sentences on whether and how the definition was revised after testing it on the cases).
