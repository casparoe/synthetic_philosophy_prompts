I run a small modeling group at an aerospace supplier, mostly supply-chain simulations plus some fluid flow. Every time we scope a new project, the team burns a couple weeks trying to find a closed-form shortcut or a compact formula before we let ourselves just simulate the thing; and when a formula does fall out of a simplified version, the engineers overtrust it. I keep coming back to this bit from the section called "Computational Irreducibility" in Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science:

"When viewed in computational terms most of the great historical triumphs of theoretical science turn out to be remarkably similar in their basic character. For at some level almost all of them are based on finding ways to reduce the amount of computational work that has to be done in order to predict how some particular system will behave."

Wolfram's move is that some systems just don't allow that kind of reduction. I want a reusable procedure my team can actually run on a new modeling request: the questions to ask in order, what counts as evidence that we should stop hunting for a shortcut, how to tell the difference between "we havent found it yet" and "there isn't one," and where the decision has to go to a person rather than the checklist. Then run the procedure on two cases: a queuing network our client wants a closed-form for, and turbulent flow past a new wing profile — and make at least one of those come out as an answer we wont like. It has to be runnable in half a day by people who aren't complexity theorists, and defensible to a VP who isn't a modeler. Be honest about where the procedure itself is a judgment call dressed up as a rule, and what it can't decide. Finally, say how confident you are in each main claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
