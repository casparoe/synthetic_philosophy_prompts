Since I began tutoring my nephew in arithmetic, I have been bothered by an asymmetry in my own practice. I correct his answers with complete confidence, as though each new exercise has a single right result that is already fixed by everything we have done before. But when I try to make explicit just what fixes it, I find I have only a finite list of completed examples and my own sense of how to continue in the same way. I can always describe a different way of continuing that agrees with every example in that list and yet parts company with my way on the very next one. In most other areas of life I readily accept that a finite record of what someone has done does not by itself dictate what he will or should do next. In teaching arithmetic, however, I act as though the finite record does exactly that. I want to know whether this asymmetry can be defended. If it cannot, I ought to stop treating my nephew's divergent answers as simply wrong. If it can, I need a better account of what, in the arithmetic case, reaches out and settles the new exercise.
