I keep failing to make precise a distinction that mathematical practice seems to rely on constantly: the difference between a construction being well-defined and its being canonical. I want a working definition of 'canonical' — as distinct from merely well-defined, if you think they are distinct — that gets the following cases right.

1. The identity map V → V on a vector space V.
2. The map V → V** sending a vector v to the functional that evaluates at v.
3. The dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space.
4. Multiplication of cosets in a quotient group G/N when N is normal, defined by [a][b] = [ab].
5. The positive square root √4 = 2, as against the equation x² = 4 having the two solutions ±2.
6. Choosing a basis for the plane, considered as an abstract two-dimensional real vector space with no preferred coordinates.
7. Labeling the two roots of x² + 1 as i and −i, given that complex conjugation swaps them and preserves the algebraic structure completely.
8. The algebraic closure of Q, which is unique only up to non-unique isomorphism.
9. The indefinite integral ∫ x dx = x²/2 + C, treated as specifying a family of functions rather than a single function.

My own leanings: 1–4 are paradigm cases of a construction being canonical or uniquely forced, 6 is a paradigm case of a non-canonical choice, and 5, 7, 8, 9 are the ones I cannot settle. In 5 and 7 I cannot tell whether an order preference is doing real mathematical work or only recording a convention; in 8 the object feels canonical from far away and uncanonical up close; in 9 the constant is arbitrary yet nothing about the object seems lost.

Propose the definition. Then run it through all nine cases, say exactly where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, tell me which of your verdicts is least secure, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, use the definition to answer whether it matters how the two roots of x² + 1 are labeled, or whether 'does it matter' is too coarse a question. If you think the opposition between well-defined and canonical is itself confused, or that 'canonical' will not support one definition that handles these cases without gerrymandering, say that plainly instead of forcing the cases to fit. I would rather have the confusion named than receive a definition that only pretends to work.
