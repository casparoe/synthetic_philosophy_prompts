I write about the behavioral and decision sciences for a general readership, and I am planning a hands-on evening workshop about the St. Petersburg paradox and the wider problem it exposes: expected-value reasoning with long-tailed or unbounded payoffs produces valuations that almost nobody will act on when the gamble is made concrete. The game itself is simple: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if the first heads comes on flip n, the payoff is 2^n units. The expected value is the sum of probability times payoff over every possible stopping point, and that sum does not converge; yet most people will pay only a small amount to enter.

Design a runnable seventy-five-minute activity for twenty-four adults at a science festival, no prior exposure assumed and no real money. Use tokens or points, make the stakes concrete, and pick an incentive-compatible mechanism—a second-price auction, a posted-price market, sequential buying and selling of a bankrolled or truncated version, or something better. The activity should get participants to price one or more versions of the gamble themselves and to discover the gap between their own bids and the prices implied by their own explicit calculations before the facilitator names the paradox. Give me materials, roles or stations, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact payoff schedule for each variant used, and a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the formal structure of the game. The debrief should also separate resolutions that merely cap the expectation from resolutions that change the agent's own valuation, and should connect the exercise to everyday pricing of lottery tickets and tail-risk insurance. Add a short section on what will predictably go wrong the first time—anchoring in early bids, arithmetic stalling the room, token stakes not being taken seriously, a single confident participant setting the market—and how to handle each. End with an honest note on which feature of the original paradox a finite classroom version cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must therefore supply.
