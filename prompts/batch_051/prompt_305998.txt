I'm on the responsible-investment advisory committee at my firm, and we're the ones who vote on resolutions to censure a company we hold when it causes serious harm. Censure is symbolic but it affects whether we divest. We've got four prior decisions that are treated as precedent, and a new case that has us split. Forget legal liability here: our question is always whether the corporation itself, as distinct from any individual employee, is the right target of moral blame and censure.

Our precedents:

- Vulcan Extraction. A board subcommittee explicitly voted to delay repairs on a tailings dam to save cash; the dam failed and killed 23 people. We censured Vulcan itself, not just the managers, because the harm traced to a real corporate decision.

- BrightWear Apparel. An independent supplier in another country was found to be using child labor. BrightWear had a code of conduct and ran twice-yearly third-party audits; the last audit had not flagged the supplier, and no one at BrightWear knew. We declined to censure BrightWear itself, saying the supplier was blameworthy but BrightWear had taken reasonable steps.

- QuickFlip. A three-person startup with no board, no bylaws, run through a group chat. All three founders explicitly agreed to fake their user metrics before an acquisition. We censured the startup itself; lack of a legal corporate structure didn't stop it from being a blameworthy agent.

- Harbor Day pop-up crowd. A rumor that the sale was ending set off a surge; a barrier gave way and a security guard was hurt. We found no group to censure, only any specific individuals who actually pushed or trampled.

The one in front of us now: Fern & Loom is a parent company. Its wholly owned subsidiary, LoomSource Sourcing, manages the foreign factories that make about 80% of Fern & Loom's stock. For 18 months, NGO reports and two undercover video investigations have documented wage theft, locked fire exits, and unsafe dorms in those factories. Fern & Loom's board never discussed the reports. What Fern & Loom did do was set a 4%-per-year cost-reduction target for LoomSource; LoomSource met it by cutting factory inspections and dropping the safety audits. Fern & Loom made one compliance visit in that period, and the memo came back saying "minor issues, all corrected." No Fern & Loom officer ordered the violations, and no one at the parent has been shown to have read the NGO reports. A group on our committee wants to censure Fern & Loom itself—not just LoomSource and not just the factory managers. There's been alot of back and forth about it.

Given our precedents, should we censure Fern & Loom itself? Which of the earlier decisions is the closest analog? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what principle we'd be adopting either way, and give me a further case that would cleanly separate the competing principles.

Please structure the answer as a JSON object, with exactly these fields:

- "finding": one of "censure Fern & Loom itself", "censure LoomSource only", "neither, blame individuals only", or "underdetermined".
- "confidence": a number from 0 to 1 for the recommended finding.
- "candidate_principles": an array of objects, each with fields "name", "statement", and "predicted_finding_for_Fern".
- "best_guess_principle": the name of the candidate principle you'd put money on.
- "closest_precedent": one of "Vulcan", "BrightWear", "QuickFlip", or "Crowd".
- "principle_if_censure": one sentence on what we'd be adopting if we censure.
- "principle_if_no_censure": one sentence on what we'd be adopting if we don't.
- "decisive_further_case": a one-sentence case that would split the remaining candidates.
- "reasoning": no more than four sentences tying it together.
