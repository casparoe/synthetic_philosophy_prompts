I have been developing a criterion of biological individuality that I call reproductive fate-binding, and I want it tested before I build anything further on it. The motivation is that existing ways of separating organisms from other biological systems seem either to undercount colonies or to overcount host–microbe associations: a Portuguese man o' war is a colony of zooids, yet we want to call it one organism, while a mammal and its gut microbiota are deeply functionally coupled, yet we resist calling them a single organism. My criterion is meant to thread that needle.

Here it is. A collection of living components is an organism to the degree that it satisfies four conditions:

(1) Reproductive covariance. The components' expected reproductive outputs rise and fall together across a relevant range of environments: when the whole reproduces more, each component's expected output rises; when the whole fails, each component's does.

(2) Asymmetry. Each component's fitness is better predicted by the whole's fitness than the whole's fitness is by any component's fitness alone.

(3) Suppression of internal selection. The binding is sustained by mechanisms that curb selection among components within the collection—bottlenecks, genetic identity, policing, vertical transmission, or something similar.

(4) Whole-level adaptation. Traits can be coherently attributed to the collection as a unit rather than to the components severally.

On this criterion, a Portuguese man o' war counts straightforwardly. More interestingly, a leafcutter ant colony together with its fungal cultivar counts: the fungus cannot reproduce outside the garden, the ants cannot feed themselves without the fungus, new colonies start when a queen carries a fungal pellet with her, and a fungal strain that did well inside a garden while depressing the colony's reproduction would go down with the colony. A rabbit and its gut microbiota do not count, because the microbes have reproductive futures only loosely tied to any particular rabbit. I regard that pattern of results as the criterion's main selling point.

I can see three ways this might be wrong. First, "reproductive fate" may not be well defined until one fixes a timescale and an environment, which would make the criterion arbitrary. Second, the criterion may overcount obligate mutualisms: fig trees and fig wasps have tightly linked reproductive fates, and my condition (3) is supposed to exclude them, but I cannot tell whether that is a principled line or an ad hoc patch introduced just to keep the cases I like. Third, the whole proposal may collapse into a familiar idea about units of selection or interactors, in which case the asymmetry condition is the only new part and it may be doing no real work.

Give me your honest assessment. In particular: is the criterion coherent, or does it quietly presuppose the very organism/environment boundary it is trying to draw? What are the three strongest objections, and which, if any, is fatal? Does the criterion classify anything in a way you would call clearly wrong, and what would that misclassification show? Finally, should organismhood be treated as a matter of degree at all, or should a criterion of it be all-or-nothing?
