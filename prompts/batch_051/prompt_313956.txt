My friend and I spent a long evening arguing about the digger wasp Sphex and what it shows about free will. Her position was roughly this. The wasp stings a cricket into paralysis, drags it to the threshold of her burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect, comes back out, and drags it in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while she is inside, she repeats the whole sequence—drag to the threshold, inspect, drag in—and will keep doing so indefinitely. The behavior looks intelligent but is not: it is a closed routine that the wasp cannot pause, reassess, or reroute in light of the changed situation. She called this kind of rigidity sphexishness, and her claim was that sphexishness, not determinism, is the proper opposite of free will. On her view, what makes us freer than the wasp is that we can notice when a standing pattern of action is failing, treat that observation as a reason to stop or revise the pattern, and then do so. Determinism is compatible with that capacity; sphexishness is what undermines it.

I am a control systems engineer, not a philosopher, and I think natively in terms of controllers, plants, feedback loops, set points, error signals, disturbances, open-loop versus closed-loop control, adaptive control, gain scheduling, and supervisory loops. I pushed back that an adaptive controller already does something close to what she was praising: it compares predicted and measured behavior, treats a persistent mismatch as an error signal, and retunes its own gains. We would not call an adaptive autopilot free. And if you answer by adding a supervisory layer that adjusts the adaptation law when that layer's own assumptions drift, you have built a tower of controllers with the rigidity merely relocated one level up.

Please restate this debate in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in the control-engineering vocabulary at all, plus a verdict on whether the residue is a defect of my framework or a sign that the philosophical view is adding something real.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the cleanest translation of my friend's position; (2) the cleanest translation of my objection; (3) the part of her position that only translates with distortion; (4) the part that has no counterpart in control-engineering vocabulary at all; (5) your verdict on what the residue means.
