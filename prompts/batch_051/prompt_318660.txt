I help keep the accounts for a small fraternal society, the Linden Grove Mutual Benefit Club, which has built up a rainy-day fund of $100,000 over many decades. A new treasurer has proposed that, at each monthly meeting, we put the entire fund into an investment opportunity that works like a coin flip: if the coin comes up heads, the fund gains 50%; if it comes up tails, the fund loses 40%. He says the average result of one round is a 5% gain (the plain average of +50% and -40%), so over the long run the fund should grow enormously; he keeps pointing out that after ten years a thousand dollars would have become something like $350,000, and he argues that the members are being too nervous because the fund is in it for the long run, not for one coin flip. Another member, who works as an actuary, says this is exactly backwards. He says the 5% figure is an “ensemble average” — an average over all the parallel histories the fund could take — while the number that matters for one actual fund moving through time is a “time average,” and that average is something like minus 5% per month, so the fund will almost surely shrink toward zero. The two of them argue past each other, and I do not trust my own understanding of compounding enough to decide.

I would like you to work through how our proposed rule — every month the entire pot is wagered on this coin flip, with every member’s share always kept equal — would actually play out. Please take the role of someone who works on repeated gambles and the design of investment rules, and think through this carefully, starting with the month-by-month mechanics. Lay out what the fund balance does over time, why the treasurer’s expected-value calculation is true but apparently misleading, and whether the actuary’s “time average” is the right thing to compute. Then move to the institution itself: what members would actually experience if we never changed the rule, who is incentivized to push for it or to game it — for example by timing withdrawals, by borrowing against an expected share, or by keeping the rule once the fund is badly down — and where the scheme would break down in practice. Finally, say whether a modified rule, such as wagering only a fixed fraction of the fund each month, would rescue the underlying idea, and what fraction-of-the-fund rule would be best if we want the typical member to benefit over time.
