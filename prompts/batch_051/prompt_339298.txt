I am a retired research biochemist, and I have come to philosophy late; the habit I cannot shake is that I distrust a distinction unless it can be made to do work sorting cases. The distinction that currently troubles me is the one the doctrine of double effect requires: the difference between a harm one intends and a harm one merely foresees as a side effect.

Please give me your best working definition of "intending a harm" in the double-effect sense — the harm as part of one's plan or a means to one's end, as against a foreseen but unintended accompaniment. I do not want a history of the doctrine; I want the concept built for use, with any auxiliary conditions you judge necessary.

Then run your definition through the seven cases below and say how it classifies each. For each case, either confirm my provisional judgment or argue me out of it.

1. Munitions factory. A flight commander bombs a munitions factory because it supplies the enemy's army. He knows the raid will kill civilian workers in a dormitory block beside the plant, but no part of the raid's purpose depends on their deaths. My provisional judgment: not intended.

2. Terror raid. The same commander bombs a residential district containing no military installation, in order to kill enough civilians to break the enemy's will to fight. My provisional judgment: intended.

3. Obstructed labor. An obstetrician without surgical facilities must deliver a woman in obstructed labor who will otherwise die. The only available procedure is a craniotomy, which kills the child while reducing the head so that delivery can occur. The reduction is what saves the mother, but the physician cannot separate the reduction from the death. My provisional judgment: intended, though this is the case about which I am least comfortable.

4. Terminal sedation. A physician sedates a dying patient into unconsciousness to relieve intractable pain, knowing that the sedation may depress respiration and shorten life. The shortened life contributes nothing to the pain relief. My provisional judgment: not intended.

5. Signal box. A runaway trolley will kill five track workers. A signalman throws a switch that diverts it onto a maintenance spur where one worker is trapped. The switch works the same way whether or not the spur is occupied; the lone worker's death is foreseen but does not contribute to the diversion. My provisional judgment: not intended.

6. Footbridge. A bystander pushes a heavy workman off the footbridge into the trolley's path so that the trolley will strike his body and stop before reaching the five. The impact that stops the trolley is the impact that kills him. My provisional judgment: intended.

7. Mountaineer. Two roped climbers are caught on a collapsing cornice above a crevasse. The lower climber has fallen and cannot be recovered; the upper climber, himself sliding toward the edge, cuts the rope so that the lower climber falls to his death and the upper climber can arrest his own fall. The death is foreseen, but the upper climber is cutting to be rid of the weight, not in order that the man be dead. My provisional judgment: I do not know.

Present your answer in the following sections, in this order:

- Definition. State the working definition and any auxiliary conditions. Roughly 150 words.
- Application. One paragraph per case, roughly 50–80 words each, giving your classification and your reasons.
- Forced revisions. Say where the cases strain the definition and how you would amend it. Roughly 150 words.
- Least secure verdict. Identify the single classification you are least confident in, say whether the difficulty belongs to the definition or to the case, and state what additional fact would settle it. Roughly 100 words.
- Final definition. Give the definition in final, amended form. Roughly 75 words.
