I've come around to the view that futarchy — roughly, citizens decide what outcome metrics count, and prediction markets decide which policy best gets us there — is the best scheme we have for turning factual disagreement into policy. Not because markets are smart, but because they give people with genuine information a way to profit from being right and punish people who talk without putting anything on the line; committees and voters have neither property. So my position is: for any high-stakes policy question where we can define a decent outcome metric, a well-designed prediction market over that metric, conditional on each policy option, will in expectation pick better policies than expert deliberation, voting, or some blend of them. I'm not claiming political feasibility; I'm claiming epistemic superiority.

What I want from you is a serious map of what would have to be true for this to be wrong. Not a lazy list of "markets can be manipulated, people are irrational." I want the defeaters sorted by how much they should actually move me, from fatal to merely awkward. For each one say whether it's a live problem or just logically possible, what the world would have to look like for it to sink the view, and how a futarchy defender would respond. Where do defenders themselves concede ground, not just critics? I'm especially interested in the structural stuff: thin markets for long-horizon questions; the weirdness that for the losing policy the conditional outcome is a counterfactual you never get to observe, so I'm not even sure what settles those contracts; Goodhart-style pressure on whatever metric gets chosen; and the fact that a market can only pick among options someone already put on the ballot, so it can't generate the new policy ideas that deliberation sometimes does. And I want an honest verdict at the end: has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind or just what a mature institutional proposal looks like?

Also, keep two things separate for me. What's actually established about prediction markets — lab results, field results, formal results — versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. Don't present a contested view as the settled consensus just because it's the one I happen to hold.
