I keep encountering a particular style of argument about what special relativity implies for time, and I would like to know whether it has an established name and, more importantly, exactly how strong it is. I understand the starting point well enough: there is no observer-independent relation of simultaneity in Minkowski spacetime. For spacelike-separated events, different inertial frames disagree about their temporal order, and for any such pair there is some frame in which they are simultaneous. The step that puzzles me is the one taken next. The argument asks me to consider an event I would ordinarily call future: something that happens on a planet in Andromeda at a date later, in my current frame, than the event of my asking this question. Because the two events are spacelike separated, there is an inertial frame — roughly, that of someone moving toward Andromeda at modest speed and passing me just now — in which that Andromeda event is simultaneous with my asking the question. The argument then runs as follows. Whatever is simultaneous with a real event is itself real; what is real cannot depend on an arbitrary choice of frame; therefore the Andromeda event is as real as the event occurring here and now. Since the choice of distant event was arbitrary, the same reasoning can be repeated throughout spacetime, and the supposed specialness of the present gives way to a block in which all times are equally real.

I know that eternalism and block universe are names for the conclusion, and I have seen the simultaneity step illustrated by what is called the Andromeda paradox. What I have not found is the name and assessment of the argument itself — the move from the frame-relativity of simultaneity to the equal reality of all times. I am after more than a label. Does the relativity of simultaneity really amount to a relativity of reality, or only to a relativity in how spacetime is sliced into instants? What further premises does the argument require — for instance, that existence is frame-independent, or that being simultaneous with a real event transfers reality — and which of those premises do presentists or critics of the argument normally resist? Finally, how exactly does this argument differ from the weak and widely accepted claim that simultaneity is relative, which by itself does not seem to force eternalism? I would prefer a treatment that states the argument in its strongest form and then marks precisely where it can be refused; if the argument has no settled name, suggest one and define it so that it is cleanly separated from the Andromeda paradox and from generic eternalism.
