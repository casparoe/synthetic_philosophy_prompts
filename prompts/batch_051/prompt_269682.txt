Consider prosaic alignment: the project of making current machine learning systems do what their operators intend, without assuming any new theory of agency. Within that project, a recurring slide runs from a behavioral claim — the model produces helpful, harmless, useful outputs on the distributions where it has been trained and evaluated — to an attribution claim — the model tracks the objective we trained it for, rather than merely reproducing outputs that resemble what human raters approved. The attribution claim is easy to state and hard to pin down, because any finite evaluation can in principle be passed by a sufficiently large set of shallow heuristics. The claim to examine is this: an RLHF-fine-tuned language model that generalizes to novel prompts is, at least in part, tracking the underlying objective across rewordings and edge cases, and not simply executing a mosaic of surface heuristics correlated with the preference data. Design a concrete test, or battery of tests, that could distinguish objective-tracking from heuristic reproduction in a current system. Say exactly what observed outcomes would count as evidence for each side, and then say what a positive result would and would not establish. In particular, identify what part of the phrase "the model tracks the objective" remains untouched by even the best behavioral and mechanistic evidence you can specify, and why that remainder is a conceptual residue rather than a merely unsolved measurement problem.
