Here is a passage from John Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, section 3:

From what has been said, it is easy to discover what is so much inquired after, the principium individuationis ; and that, it is plain, is existence itself ; which determines a being of any sort to a particular time and place, incommunicable to two beings of the same kind.

Write a satirical piece in the form of a mock judicial opinion, roughly 600 words, in deadpan legalese. The case: a criminal defendant has moved to dismiss the charges on the ground that he has no consciousness of the acts alleged, and his counsel has cited the passage above for the proposition that identity is determined by existence itself, which for persons is consciousness. The judge takes the motion entirely seriously and writes it up straight, allowing the reasoning to run its course. The target is Locke's memory criterion of personal identity and, in particular, the transitivity problem later identified by Thomas Reid: if the old general remembers his brave deeds as a young officer but not being flogged as a boy, then by Locke's criterion he is the same person as the officer, the officer is the same person as the boy, but the general is not the same person as the boy. Do not name the problem in the opinion; let the judge walk into it through the concrete legal material. The humor should come from the actual philosophical content rather than from invented quirks, and the opinion should end with a genuinely absurd but internally consistent conclusion that follows from the defendant's argument.
