Take the sorites paradox in its canonical form: since removing one grain never turns a heap into a non-heap, repeatedly removing single grains should never produce a non-heap, yet plainly it does. Now re-run the paradox as a problem about a language, and add an element the heap case does not have.

Suppose a community's everyday spoken language drifts by small, ordinary steps from colloquial Latin in the first century BC to Tuscan in the fourteenth century AD. Stipulate that mutual intelligibility is symmetric, that every generation is fully mutually intelligible with the generation before and after it, and that no one in the chain ever notices from the inside the moment at which the language changes. But mutual intelligibility is not transitive across the whole chain: a first-century colloquial speaker and a fourteenth-century Tuscan speaker cannot hold a conversation. Alongside this continuous spoken chain runs a written chain with different dynamics. In each generation, scribes keep producing texts they call Latin, but they correct themselves against a fixed corpus of older texts rather than against their own speech. At several points the written chain is deliberately reset: in the ninth century a reform pulls written Latin back toward older models after spoken usage has moved on, and in the fourteenth century a literary movement decides that the vernacular deserves the prestige of a serious literary language and begins calling it Italian, treating it henceforth as separate from Latin.

The word Latin therefore stays attached to the written chain for the whole period, while the spoken chain receives the name Italian only after a self-conscious break.

Show how this version forces each standard answer to the sorites to say something new. The tolerance step seems to hold along the spoken chain, so each standard view — the hidden sharp boundary, the tolerant predicate, supervaluationism, contextualism — must locate the transition from Latin to Italian somewhere, or explain why there is no single transition. Does the deliberately reset written chain dissolve the spoken-chain paradox, or does it merely create a second sorites about the relation between the written and spoken chains? Does an act of naming create a boundary in the spoken chain or only in the written one? And does the case show that Latin and Italian sit on two different kinds of series rather than two points on one series, which is why the question of when Latin became Italian refuses a datable answer?
