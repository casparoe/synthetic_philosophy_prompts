I've noticed something in a few philosophy papers and in ordinary arguments, and I'm not sure if its got a name. People sometimes talk about truth as if it's a real property a claim has, like a coin being made of copper. But a lot of actual uses of the word true don't seem to do that at all. If I say the cat is on the mat, and you say that's true, you've basically just agreed that the cat is on the mat; nothing extra seems to have been added. If I say everything in the witness's statement is true, I seem to be using true as a compressed way of asserting a long list: he was at the garage, and he saw the man, and the man was wearing a red coat, and so on. And if I say, if what she said is true, we're in trouble, the word true just points back to whatever she said, the way a pronoun points back to a noun. I'm not sure how to seperate a case where true is genuinely describing a claim from a case where it's just doing this pointing-and-reasserting job, and I wonder whether the whole debate over whether truth is a substantial property might partly be a disagreement about which of these uses is the basic one. Does this family of uses have an established name? I'm less bothered about the label and more about the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its boundaries are.
