After retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been reading philosophy with the enthusiasm of a late convert, and I keep returning to a question I first learned to pose as a question about systems and state spaces: could a finite mind, in principle, exhaust its own experience? Put informationally, suppose the mind has some very large but finite repertoire of distinguishable states and the world presents it with only so many distinctions it can register. Then novelty begins to look like a depletable resource: there would be a point after which nothing genuinely new can occur. Against that stands the thought that experience is not a fixed inventory but something generated — recursively or compositionally — by the mind's own activity, so that the finitude of storage or of input does not impose a ceiling on what a life is like. I would like you to read the 1993 film Groundhog Day through this disagreement.

The film is a controlled thought experiment. Phil Connors, a television weatherman, is confined to repeating the same day, February 2, in Punxsutawney, Pennsylvania, with the same people, the same weather, the same small accidents. For a long stretch he treats the town as a finite inventory to be solved: he learns the day's contingencies, catches the boy falling from the tree before it happens, times the armored car and takes its money, saves the man who chokes in the diner, and works through every available strategy of seduction, escape, and eventually suicide. The film is unflinching, for a comedy, that he reaches the point of having been through what the day offers — that he has exhausted it. Then the film appears to change its mind about what the day is. The same day becomes unexhausted: he studies the piano and ice sculpture, learns French, remembers the names and troubles of a large cast of townspeople, tries and fails to save an old homeless man, and slowly turns from extracting novelty from the town toward being of use to it, above all to his producer Rita. The question I want pressed is whether this second phase refutes the exhaustibility worry, merely postpones it, or changes the subject.

Please give me a careful reading of this turn, taking the philosophical issue seriously in both directions. Does the early phase actually establish that a finite world exhausts a finite mind, or does it show only that he exhausted one instrumental relation to that world? Is the late phase genuinely inexhaustible, or is it newly varied only because his changed conduct changes how others respond to him — so that the world now supplies new inputs rather than his mind supplying new distinctions? Where does the film locate the inexhaustibility of experience, if it does: in the unboundedness of skill, in attention, in moral development, in love? And finish by saying what the film contributes to the philosophical problem that a clean argument about finite state spaces could not, and where its resolution, if it has one, is too cheap or evasive. I want a reading that does not treat the film as a mere illustration, and that is willing to say where the film ducks the problem it seems to raise.
