I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar—about fifteen students, ninety minutes, mix of philosophy majors and CS students who will want to defend the computational view of mind. The topic is the symbol grounding problem: the worry that a system can manipulate symbols perfectly correctly by following rules, while none of the symbols mean anything to the system, because meaning can't come from more ungrounded symbols. I don't just want to tell them the usual story; I want them to be inside the situation. The activity should let them run part of a symbolic system—moving tokens, matching them, transforming them, maybe earning points—so that the room is doing all the observable things a competent system does, and then it dawns on someone that at no point did any of the tokens get tied to anything outside the system. Don't announce the target beforehand, and don't settle for an activity where they just discuss being a Chinese room; it has to happen to them.

Please design it end to end: rules, materials, roles, a 90-minute timeline, a simple payoff or scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the syntax/semantics gap, derived vs intrinsic intentionality, and why adding more symbols or rules can't fix the problem. The hard part is that real students carry grounded meanings around in their heads, so the activity has to make it hard for them to quietly import those meanings—maybe by banning natural language, pointing at objects, and meaningful gestures. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'd guess somone smuggles grounding in through a meaningful glance or by treating one token as the name of another student), how to handle that, and which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce—so I know what I still need to say in the debrief.
