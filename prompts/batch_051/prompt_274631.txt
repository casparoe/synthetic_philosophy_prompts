For my grad seminar on risk and rationality I'm putting together the standard explanations of the Ellsberg paradox — the single-urn version: 30 red balls and 60 black-or-yellow balls in unknown proportion, where (with equal stakes) most people prefer to bet on red over black, but also on black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow, which no single additive probability function can rationalize. So far I have four stories:

(1) Maxmin over a set of priors: the agent treats the unknown composition as a range of possible distributions and maximizes the minimum expected utility — betting as if the worst compatible urn is the real one.

(2) Choquet capacities / non-additive weights: the agent has something like probabilities, but they are capacities and don't need to be additive. The ambiguous events get underweighted relative to the known ones, which yields the two Ellsberg choices.

(3) Smooth second-order aversion: the agent has a probability distribution over possible urn compositions and is averse to the spread among those "probabilities of probabilities" — formally, a concave function wrapped around expected utilities.

(4) Source dependence: people are more willing to bet when they feel informed or competent about the process behind the odds, so ambiguity aversion is less about the probabilities themselves and more about the source the bet is drawn from.

I have the feeling I'm forgetting a well-known fifth explanation, maybe the one that actually captures what Ellsberg himself thought was going on. What is it, and what does it add that (1)–(4) don't? Then tell me honestly whether it's any good — in particular, does it explain both of the standard Ellsberg choices or only one of them? Also, if I've garbled any of the four, or they collapse into each other more than I'm suggesting, say that plainly; I'd rather be corrected then get a fake fifth to round out a list.
