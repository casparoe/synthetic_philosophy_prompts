I'm a novelist, and I keep noticing a parallel I cant decide about. I'm strict about the internal rules of the world I set up — how the magic works, what a character would never do. Now and then I break one of those rules to make the ending land. The shape of my excuse is always the same: (1) the rule existed to serve the story as a whole, (2) at this point following the rule would wreck the story, (3) so the very thing the rule served justifies setting it aside this once, and (4) readers will actually accept that, but only if the book has earned enough trust earlier. I realized this is almost exactly the shape of how states justify emergency powers — lockdowns, closing the border, suspending normal legal rules. The law exists to protect the public, the emergency means the law's own purpose requires breaking it, so the purpose of the law justifies the temporary suspension — and whether people accept it as legitimate seems to depend on something like trust. The obvious difference is that my reader can put the book down and a citizen usually can't. So: is this a real identity of structure — a system breaking its own constitutive rules in service of the point of the system — or am I just pattern-matching? If it is real, does anything transfer from the novelist side? Readers have a sharp sense for when a broken rule is an earned twist and when it's a lazy deus ex machina; is there a matching distinction on the political side between an emergency measure that reads as legitimate and one that reads as a power grab? And where exactly does the mapping fall apart?
