I keep having the same argument with a friend about whether a rich neighborhood is better off in any respect that matters if nobody in it gets any happier or freer from the wealth—like, is there something good about the sheer abundance itself, even setting aside what it buys. The argument got weirdly concrete when my city's planning commission actually floated a scheme that tries to reward abundance not as individual wealth but as public, visible plenty. Here's the mechanism. The city sets up an Abundance Index covering unpriced goods: fruit trees on public land, open hours at community gardens, meals served at free community dinners, little free library book swaps, miles of maintained trails, completed public artworks, that sort of thing. Every quarter, an abundance levy is skimmed off property tax revenue, and each household receives an equal dividend scaled by how much the index went up. Any resident can nominate a new category for the index, and new categories get added by a majority vote at quarterly open meetings as long as at least a couple dozen people show up. I have my own hunches about how this ends, but I want you to treat it as a mechanism rather than as a position on whether prosperity has moral worth. Walk me through what it incentivizes, how it gets gamed, which forms of abundance get counted and which get squeezed out, what happens to people who don't have spare time to produce photogenic abundance, and where the whole thing breaks down first. Can any of those failure modes be fixed without gutting the underlying idea that abundance is worth something over and above what people can pay for?
