For a philosophy of biology seminar, I have been collecting my instructor's rulings on short cases in which she says whether one heritable variant is fitter than another in a specified population and environment. In lecture she introduced two readings of fitness. On the realized reading, fitness is just the number of viable offspring an individual or type actually leaves; on the propensity reading, fitness is an expected reproductive output fixed by the organism's traits and environment before the actual outcome is settled. She noted that the tautology objection—the charge that if fitness is merely realized reproduction, then the principle that selection favors the fitter collapses into an empty truth—applies with full force only to the realized reading. She has not told us which reading she accepts; that is what the semester's cases are supposed to reveal.

Her rulings so far:

1. In a population of seed-eating finches, large-beaked birds survive a drought at higher rates than small-beaked birds, and over fifteen years the large-beak allele rises from 15 percent to 80 percent. Ruling: large-beaked birds are fitter; the claim that selection favored them is empirical.

2. A point mutation in a plant alters no expressed gene. Across many replicate plots, carriers and non-carriers show no difference in survival or seed production, and the mutation's frequency changes only by drift. Ruling: no fitness difference.

3. A strain of E. coli grows 20 percent faster than a competitor on a novel carbon source and, in every replicate of a competition experiment, drives the competitor to effective extinction. Ruling: the fast-growing strain is fitter in that medium.

4. Brightly colored male guppies attract more mates but also suffer more predation than dull males. In a long-term field study, bright males and dull males leave equal average numbers of offspring. Ruling: bright color is not fitter in this population.

5. A drought-resistant crop variety outyields a susceptible variety in dry years but underyields in wet years; over twenty years containing both kinds of season, the two varieties leave equal total seed. Ruling: the resistant variety is fitter in dry years and less fit in wet years, with no context-independent ranking.

The new case is this. A single individual insect in a pest population carries a new, perfectly heritable mutation that lets it detoxify the pesticide applied to its field. Non-carriers are almost always killed by the pesticide before reproducing; carriers, by contrast, are very likely to survive, mate, and reproduce normally after exposure. But before this mutant can mate, a gust of wind blows it into a pond and it drowns.

Predict the instructor's ruling about whether the mutant type is fitter than the susceptible type in that pesticide-treated field, and explain the reasoning behind the prediction. In doing so: identify the interpretations of fitness with which her earlier rulings are jointly consistent; say what each predicts about the new case; give your best prediction and the reasons for favoring it over the alternatives; and, if your prediction places her in the propensity family, specify whether you mean an individual-level or a type-level propensity. Close by proposing one further case, distinct from the new case, whose verdict would most cleanly settle which interpretation of fitness she actually holds.
