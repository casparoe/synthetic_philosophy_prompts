I am a data engineer with no formal training in linguistics. My mental toolkit is built around serialization formats, lossy and lossless codecs, sampling and quantization, schema design, backward compatibility, and the distinction between recording a signal and recording a model of the process that generated it. I would like you to restate a position from the study of writing systems in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can.

The position is roughly this: a phonemic alphabet is not a neutral transcript of speech; it is best understood as a theory of a language's phonological structure. Its graphemes stand in for segments at a particular level of abstraction — phonemes rather than the full acoustic stream of phones — so that the script records which sound differences are contrastive and deliberately discards the differences that are predictable from context. Linguists sharpen the point by contrasting shallow orthographies, which track surface pronunciation closely (Spanish and Finnish are the usual examples), with deep orthographies, which preserve morphological or historical information even where pronunciation has diverged (the English alternation between 'sign' and 'signature' is a standard illustration). The broader claim is that no writing system encodes the full phonetic signal; every script selects a principled subset of it, and the selection embodies an analysis of the language.

Please translate that position, claim by claim, into my vocabulary: encoders and decoders, lossy compression with a codebook, sampling rate and quantization, schemas and selected fields, backward compatibility, and the storage-of-a-signal versus storage-of-a-model distinction. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the position map cleanly onto my concepts? Which parts did you have to distort or supplement to make them fit? Which parts cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I would also like an explicit verdict on the following hypothesis of mine: that the entire position reduces to the claim that an alphabet is a badly designed lossy codec for speech. If your translation shows that something is lost, tell me whether the loss reflects a shortcoming of my framework, a place where the linguists' claim is less precise than it sounds, or both.
