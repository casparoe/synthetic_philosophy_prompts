Everyone in my department keeps giving me the same advise: if you want to work on panpsychism, tackle the combination problem. Fine, but as far as I can tell the combination problem isn't one thing, it's a pile of distinct worries that share a name. The way I carve it up, there's the subject-summing problem, which is about how lots of little subjects are supposed to fuse into one unified subject when subjects don't seem like the kind of thing you can add together. There's the grain problem, which is about the mismatch between the smooth, homogeneous character of much of our experience and the fine-grained microstructure of whatever physical stuff supposedly realizes it. There's the palette problem, which is about how a small stock of basic experiential ingredients could combine to produce the whole range of qualities experience actually has. And there's the boundary problem, which is about which physical assemblies count as having a point of view at all, and where one subject ends and the next begins.

I'm picking a dissertation topic and I can realistically spend three years on only one of these. I want a defended ranking, not a survey and not a reading list. Order them by which is most worth working on first if the goal is to either make panpsychism credible or show it can't be saved. Say what solving each one, or showing it unsolvable, would actually change for the overall view, which ones are upstream of the others, and which one you'd bet a dissertation on. The hard part for me is that the most fundamental and the most tractible seem to point at different sub-problems, so be explicit about how you're weighing those and what would make you reshuffle the ranking.
