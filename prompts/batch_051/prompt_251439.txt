my flatmate has an argument that knowing how is just knowing that, and then that this can't be right, so something in the middle has to go. i've rewritten it as he gave it to me:

(1) if s knows how to φ, then there is some way w such that s knows that w is a way for s to φ.
(2) knowing that w is a way for s to φ is a case of knowing a proposition, i.e. knowing-that.
(3) so knowing how is a case of knowing-that.
(4) knowledge-that is the kind of state that testimony can transmit: if someone tells you a true proposition and you take their word for it, you can come to know it.
(5) so if knowing how is just a case of knowing-that, you could give someone know-how by telling them the right propositions.
(6) but you can't: someone could be told every true proposition about how to swim and still not know how to swim. they'd sink just the same.
(7) therefore (1) is false, and knowing how isn't a kind of knowing-that after all.

he says the argument is valid, so the only question is which of (1), (4), or (6) is the least costly to reject. i'd definately like to keep (6) because it seems obvious, and i'm tempted to reject (1), but i don't know what i'd be paying. which step should i reject, and what does rejecting it cost? please answer in British English.
