Act as a professional philosopher of mathematics. Think carefully before committing yourself: start by brainstorming two or three different ways of interpreting the practice I describe, then tell me which interpretation you find most defensible and why.

I have no academic background in mathematics or philosophy; I've just been reading about infinity on my own — Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's diagonal argument, the result that the even numbers and the natural numbers can be paired off one-to-one while the real numbers cannot. What strikes me is that working mathematicians treat these as genuine truths about size: a collection can be the same size as one of its proper parts, and some infinite collections are larger than others. Yet in ordinary life the whole is always larger than any proper part, and nobody believes a real hotel could have infinitely many rooms or that anyone could finish counting an infinite collection. The same people who accept Hilbert's hotel as correct mathematics usually deny that an actually infinite number of physical objects exists.

What view of mathematical existence, and of what "same size" means, does this standard mathematical practice presuppose? In particular, does accepting Hilbert's hotel and Cantor's results force someone to hold that completed infinities exist as objects, or can the whole practice be read as a formal game that merely talks as if they exist? Which parts of the practice strictly presuppose a metaphysical commitment, and which parts are compatible with several different philosophies of mathematics? Finally, suppose a strict finitist — someone who rejects every actual infinity and admits only the potentially infinite, the always-extendable — were put in charge of rewriting the standard curriculum. Which results and habits of thought would have to be abandoned, and which could be kept by being restated as claims about arbitrary finite extensions?
