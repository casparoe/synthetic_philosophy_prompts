The moderators of a large general-interest discussion forum (on the scale of a few hundred thousand monthly active users) are frustrated that early outrage-bait posts get upvoted before slower, more careful replies arrive, and that old high-karma accounts can vote carelessly because their accumulated score makes them hard to displace. They propose the following change. Whenever a user casts an upvote or a downvote, a small fixed fraction of that user's current karma — say 0.2% — is placed in escrow attached to the post; the vote itself still affects the post's ranking immediately. Fourteen days later, a panel of twenty randomly selected users who did not vote on the post reads the thread and votes, by simple majority, on whether the post was "valuable" or "not valuable." If your original vote matches the panel's verdict, you receive your stake back plus a pro-rata share of the stakes forfeited by users whose votes mismatched. Panelists receive a small flat karma stipend and cannot serve more than once a month. The moderators hope the rule will reward early accurate judgment, punish reflexive voting, and even encourage high-karma users to vote against the crowd when they believe the crowd is wrong, since each individual vote risks only a capped amount.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a policy to approve or reject. Walk through what it incentivizes among posters, ordinary voters, high-karma voters, and panelists. What would the first two weeks of a post's life start to look like, and what kind of content would thrive or die under the rule? Where is the scheme gamed first, and can the obvious patches resist the same pressures? In particular, examine the case of a post that is substantively correct or valuable but broadly disliked by the kind of user likely to serve on a panel: what does the mechanism do with such posts over repeated rounds, and does it remain distinguishable, in practice, from a system that simply pays people to flatter the median panelist?
