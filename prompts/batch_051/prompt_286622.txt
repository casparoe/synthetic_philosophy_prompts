Since retiring, I have been reading around the foundations of social choice, and I keep returning to a small puzzle for which I have no name. Imagine two people who must jointly settle whether a certain book is read by the first person alone, by the second person alone, or by neither. Each is granted final say over whether he or she reads it: if the group is choosing between an outcome in which the first person reads and one in which the first person does not, the first person's preference settles it, and likewise for the second. Suppose further that whenever the two of them unanimously prefer one outcome to another, the group is required to rank that outcome above the other. For a perfectly coherent choice of individual preferences, these two principles between them force the group to rank the three outcomes in a circle, so that each is outranked by another. My training is in the natural sciences, and I am used to treating a paradox as a sign that one of the premises is false; here each premise seems almost too weak to object to, so I suspect I am missing a distinction. Is there an established name for this specific conflict between leaving personal matters to the individual and respecting unanimous preferences, as distinct from the broader impossibility results one hears about when preferences are aggregated? If it is a special case of something more general, how does the narrower phenomenon differ, and where do its boundaries lie: what sort of preferences, and what assignment of personal vetoes, would make the cycle disappear? If there is no settled name, what would you call it, and how would you define its edges?
