"Suppose a three-person hospital review panel must decide whether a drug company is liable for injuries from a drug. Under the rules, liability (R) requires both that the company knew the drug was dangerous (P) and that the drug caused the injury (Q); so R is true if and only if P and Q. The panel members reach these individual judgments:

- Ben: P false, Q true, therefore R false.
- Cal: P true, Q true, therefore R true.

(a) What does majority voting give for P, Q, and R?
(b) Explain why this kind of case is thought to create a problem for treating a group as a rational agent that forms its own judgments.
(c) Briefly describe one procedural response to the problem, and give one disadvantage of that response."

Here's the student's answer, typed up exactly as submitted:

"(a) P gets two votes (Ann, Cal), Q gets two votes (Ben, Cal), and R gets two votes against (Ann, Ben). So the majority position is P, Q, and not-R.

(b) Each panelist has a logically consistent set of judgments, but the majority set is inconsistent: the group accepts P and Q but rejects R, even though R follows from P and Q. This is basically a majority cycle, like Condorcet's paradox, just with propositions instead of candidates. So there is no coherent majority will, which is why the case is a problem for treating the group itself as a rational agent with beliefs. The general phenomenon is called the discursive dilemma.

(c) A natural response is the premise-based procedure: the group votes only on P and Q, and then lets logic determine R. This procedure is clearly the right one because it makes the group track the reasons behind its conclusion. The conclusion-based procedure, by contrast, just takes a vote on R and should be rejected, since it ignores the panelists' reasons and lets the bottom-line verdict float free of the evidence."
