I've been reading around the Fischer controversy for years and I still can't settle where I actually land. So, straight question: what is your credence that the strong version of the Fischer thesis is broadly right — that imperial Germany in 1914 deliberately risked, and to a large extent planned, a European war to break out of encirclement, and therefore bears primary, though not necessarily exclusive, responsibility for the First World War? I don't want 'it depends what you mean by responsibility' as a way out; give me a number.

Then decompose that number into the sub-questions your credence actually rides on. At minimum: (1) how likely is it that German civilian and military leaders had come to see war as acceptable or even welcome by 1913–14, whatever the encirclement picture; (2) how likely is it that the blank cheque to Austria was deliberate enabling of a war rather than a miscalulated show of support; (3) how likely is it that Russian general mobilisation, French assurances to Russia, or British ambiguity made a general war unavoidable once Germany had committed; (4) how likely is it that the post-war war-guilt clause, and then Fischer's archival work, overcorrected in the other direction, so the actual distribution of responsibility is flatter than the strong thesis allows; and (5) how likely is it that 'primary responsibility' in this context can even be pinned on one nation rather than being a category error. Assign a rough probability to each and show how you combine them into the headline number. Then tell me which sub-question your credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence would move it most.
