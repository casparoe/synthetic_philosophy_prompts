I've been rereading Charles Dickens's A Christmas Carol in the middle of a local argument about welfare conditionality — a proposal to tie benefits more tightly to work, with a lot of talk about who is genuinely in need and who is gaming the system — and I've started to suspect the story is less radical than it is usually remembered as being. Scrooge's early position is that the poor should go to the workhouse or prison, and that those who are going to die had better do it and thereby reduce the surplus. The story is standardly read as a full repudiation of that position. But the people the spirits show Scrooge look almost suspiciously deserving: the Cratchits are hardworking, warm, and grateful; the charity collectors ask only for money to buy food and drink for the destitute; the need on display is mostly blameless misfortune. What I can't tell is whether Dickens is dismantling the category of the undeserving poor, or just moving particular people out of it — showing Scrooge that the poor around him were never the idle cheats he took them for.

I'd like a careful reading of A Christmas Carol through the lens of the distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor. Does the story dissolve that distinction, or does it leave the distinction intact while correcting its application? Where, if anywhere, does Dickens allow that someone might be idle, ungrateful, or wasteful and still owed help? And what would the story have to show to actually challenge conditionality — the idea that assistance should be conditional on effort or good conduct — rather than simply make those conditions more generous? I'm writing an essay on how Victorian moral economy still shapes contemporary workfare and benefit-fraud rhetoric, and I want to know whether the book offers a real alternative to conditionality or only a kinder version of it.
