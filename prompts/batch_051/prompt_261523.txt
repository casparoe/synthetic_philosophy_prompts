I'm drafting a short essay for a philosophy-of-AI workshop, and I'd like your comments and suggestions on the argument before I commit it to prose. The audience knows the interpretability literature — features, circuits, sparse autoencoders, linear probes — but is split between realists, who think interpretability recovers structures that are genuinely in the network, and instrumentalists, who think it merely imposes useful descriptions. My working thesis is that this dispute is largely misframed, and that the central concept should be projectability rather than representation.

The thesis in brief: evidence that a network represents a feature is always of the form "interventions along this direction (or ablations of this circuit, or activations of this feature) produce predictable changes over some class of inputs and tasks." I want to argue that such evidence licenses only a graded, range-relative claim — the candidate structure is projectable across a certain span of inputs, tasks, and perturbations — and never a binary claim that the network encodes the feature simpliciter. A decomposition is projectable to the extent that the labels and interventions associated with it remain predictively stable under out-of-distribution shifts, transfer to new tasks, and adversarial perturbations, without needing to be repaired or relabeled after the fact.

The move I'm most interested in is this: in the ideal limit, a perfectly projectable decomposition would make the contrast between "really represents" and "merely useful" do no further work. Realism and instrumentalism therefore converge at that limit and differ only in how they treat partial projectability — the realist treating partial projectability as a defect of our current descriptions, the instrumentalist treating it as the normal condition of any finite description. I also want to argue that the usual skeptical results (probes can extract linearly whatever they are trained to find; features go polysemantic or change their meaning under distribution shift) are better read as evidence of partial projectability than as refutations of representation.

Does this argument work, or does it quietly collapse into instrumentalism with some new words? Is projectability too strong, too weak, or too hard to estimate in practice to carry the weight I want? Is the convergence-at-the-ideal-limit point doing genuine argumentative work, or is it just a restatement of the familiar thought that predictive equivalence undermines realism? And where is the argument most likely to fail: in the assumption that interpretability evidence is intervention-based, or in the shift from accuracy to projectability as the relevant norm?

After your comments, please say what would change your overall verdict about the essay's promise: which argument or consideration would most likely lead you to a different assessment of the project, or where your main criticism is most likely to fail.
