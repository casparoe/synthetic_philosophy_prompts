So I got into one of those endless forum arguments about whether omissions can literally cause outcomes, and I tried to taboo the word 'cause' and see what the underlying heuristic actually is. What fell out is something I've been calling the resistance rule, though the name is probably overloaded. The claim: when we treat an omission as causal, we are not using a bare counterfactual — if the person had acted, the harm wouldn't have happened — because that would make every bystander who could have intervened a cause. Instead, we only count an omission as causal when the person was actively supplying the resistance that was holding the harmful process in check, and the harm is what happens when that resistance is removed. A lifeguard who stops scanning causes the drowning; a stranger on the same beach who never starts scanning does not, even though the stranger also could have saved the child. A company that stops maintaining a dam causes the flood; a company that never built a dam does not. A parent who stops feeding a child causes the starvation; a rich passerby who never sends money does not. I realize 'resistance' is doing a lot of work here and I don't have a clean definition of it yet — attention, maintenance, literal force, maybe some mix — but the examples seem to track something real. I want to defend the stronger version: this isn't a legal or moral convention bolted on top of a purely counterfactual account of causation, it's the actual folk rule for omissions, and 'allow' is the word left over when the resistance condition fails.

Does this actually hold up? The places I can already see it cracking: it might exclude people who are naturally described as causing by omission despite never having lifted a finger, or the idea of 'holding a process in check' might be smuggling in the very expectation/duty it's supposed to explain. If you're going to give a verdict, I'd rather you be candid than diplomatic — where does the resistance rule break, what would it wrongly count as causal or wrongly leave out, and what's the nearest thing someone has already defended under another name? Also tell me what would change your mind about it: which counterexample or consideration would actually make you drop the resistance condition, and where you'd most expect your own reasoning to fail if I pushed.
