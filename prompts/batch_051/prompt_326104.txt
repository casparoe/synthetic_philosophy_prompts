I have followed rationalist-blog discussions of decision theory and acausal trade for years, but I have never been able to state the evidential-cooperation-in-large-worlds position with any confidence. My flatmate, who is more immersed in these debates than I am, offered the following summary:

"In a large world containing many agents who reason about each other's behaviour, the fact that you choose to cooperate is itself evidence about what other agents sufficiently like you will choose. Because of that evidential correlation, you should treat your own decisions as votes for what all similar agents do. So you should cooperate in an anonymous, one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a stranger who has read the same arguments; you should also vote in a general election even though your single vote is not decisive; and you should give to interventions even when your own donation is unlikely to be the marginal one. The view in effect says that all sufficiently similar minds should be treated as one distributed agent, so any objection that rests on the smallness of one agent's causal effect misses the point."

I suspect this is mostly right but subtly overblown, and I do not know enough to say where. Which parts would a careful defender of evidential cooperation in large worlds accept, which parts are misrepresentations, and do the conclusions survive a correction? In particular, I am unsure whether the move from 'there is evidential correlation between similar agents' to 'act as if they are one distributed agent' is licensed, and whether the argument really tells one to ignore small causal effects or merely to weigh them against a comparably small evidential effect.
