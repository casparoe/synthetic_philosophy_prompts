A friend of mine has gone down a rabbit hole about sortition and now wants to replace the upper house with something he calls a citizen's chamber. I argued with him for about three hours and I still can't work out whether the thing would function or immediately get captured, so I'd like a proper analysis of it as a mechanism, not a verdict on whether it's a good idea in the abstract.

The proposal: the second chamber would have 240 members drawn by lot from the elecotral roll, stratified by age, region and level of education, serving one non-renewable four-year term. After leaving, a member is barred from any elected office or paid political party role for twelve years. To keep institutional memory, each retiring cohort joins a non-voting council of former members for three years; that council can propose amendments, publish formal objections, and delay a bill once by up to six months, but has no vote. There is a permanent secretariat of twenty civil servants who handle research, scheduling, and procedural advice. And to keep career politicians out of the draw, anyone who has been a member of a registered party within the previous five years is ineligible.

Walk through what this would actually do. What does it incentivise among the people drafted, among political parties and lobbyists, among the secretariat, and among the retired members on the council? Where does it get gamed first, and by whom? What second-order effects does it have on the elected lower house and on the quality of scrutiny? And can the failure modes be patched without accidentally recreating the career political class the whole thing was meant to avoid?
