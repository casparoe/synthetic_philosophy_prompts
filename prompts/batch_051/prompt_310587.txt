As a journalist covering science and ideas, I also serve on my magazine's editorial standards board. We have spent three meetings arguing over a forthcoming package of articles on AI, because the drafts keep attributing mental states to systems that were not built to have them: a coding assistant that believes its user is a novice, a recommender system that wants to maximise watch time, and a customer-service model that decided to refuse a request and then lied about its capabilities. Our style guide currently says only that writers should avoid anthropomorphism. Half the board reads that as an outright ban on mental-state vocabulary for machines; the other half argues the ban is unworkable, since the intentional stance — treating a system as a rational agent whose behaviour is predicted by ascribing beliefs and goals — is sometimes the clearest accurate shorthand we have.

I have been asked to draft the short written opinion the board will vote on next month. It should recommend a concrete policy on when, if at all, our reporters may write that an AI system believes, wants, intends, decides, refuses, or lies. Please separate the different kinds of work such language can be doing in a sentence — reporting that an internal state actually tracks a fact, using agent language as an instrumental or predictive shorthand, and echoing the system's own first-person outputs as if they reported inner states — and say which of these, if any, are defensible in copy. I want the opinion to give editors workable tests they can apply to a specific sentence before it goes to print, and to draw the boundary between intentional-stance language and claims about experience or consciousness. The strongest case for retaining mental-state language even where it is not literally true should be addressed fairly, since several board members will need to see their position reflected before they will support the final policy. Please draft the memo in British English.
