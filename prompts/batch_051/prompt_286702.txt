I am a PhD student working on the philosophy of decision theory, and I would like help thinking through a pattern in a colleague's choices. A friend of mine, an economics graduate student, has been making decisions in a series of small-stakes, incentive-compatible tasks involving draws of colored balls from urns. The amounts involved are small enough that, on the basis of earlier calibration tasks, we are treating his utility for money as linear over this range, so his choices should mainly reflect his attitudes toward uncertainty rather than the curvature of utility. All draws are made with replacement after remixing, and he receives no feedback about the outcome of a draw before making the next choice.

In the first block there were two urns. Urn A contains 50 red balls and 50 black balls. Urn B contains 100 balls, each either red or black, in unknown proportion. He made both of these choices:

(1) Between a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn A is red, and a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn B is red, he chose the Urn A red bet.

(2) Between a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn A is black, and a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn B is black, he chose the Urn A black bet.

In the second block there was a single urn, Urn C, containing exactly 30 red balls and 70 balls that are either black or yellow, in unknown proportion. He made both of these choices:

(3) Between a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn C is red, and a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn C is black, he chose the Urn C red bet.

(4) Between a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn C is red or yellow, and a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn C is black or yellow, he chose the Urn C black-or-yellow bet.

I recognize this as the textbook Ellsberg pattern, and I know that no single-prior expected-utility model, even with a non-linear utility function, can accommodate all four choices. More than one standard ambiguity model can, however, and that is exactly where I would like your help.

The new choice, again using Urn C, is this:

(5) Between a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from Urn C is yellow, and a bet paying $100 if a ball drawn from a new urn, Urn D, which contains exactly 25 green balls and 75 white balls, is green, which would he choose?

Specifically, I would like you to do four things. First, state the candidate rules or models that are jointly consistent with all of choices (1)–(4). Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about choice (5). Third, offer your best prediction about choice (5) and the reasoning behind it, or explain clearly why the earlier choices leave it underdetermined. Fourth, specify a further choice, with events and payoffs fully spelled out, that would most cleanly discriminate between the main candidate rules you identify.
