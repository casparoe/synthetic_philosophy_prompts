Here is the rule. Whenever an argument asks me to care a lot about what happens in the very far future — say more than a few thousand years out — I quietly multiply its practical weight by something like my credence that we are *not* living in a simulation. And I've also stopped reading things whose force depends on very-long-run effects, because I figure the simulation point is a cheap filter. My reasoning is that if we are in a simulation, then the far future of this world might not be the future that matters: the simulation could be shut down, paused, or simply not run long enough to contain that far future. I got this from Bostrom's simulation argument, at least as I understand it, and it has made me mostly unmoved by longtermist arguments about extinction risk or astronomical waste that my friends keep bringing up. Last month a friend tried to convince me to donate to a project aimed at improving the very long-run trajectory of civilization, and I caught myself saying I only care about the next few centuries because beyond that the probability that any of this is "real in the relevant sense" drops off. But I honestly can't specify the shape of that discount — is it a step, an exponential, a one-time haircut? — and I'm worried I'm just using the simulation idea to rationalize not caring.

Is this a defensible epistemic habit? Can the simulation argument support discounting the normative weight of the far future, or does it only bear on what we should expect the world to be like in the relevant branches? What would a defensible version of my rule look like, and where should the discount bottom out — if it works against caring about year 1,000,000, does it also work against caring about year 2100, and if not, what principle draws the line?
