You are a professional philosopher with a specialty in the philosophy of language and the history of science, and I would like you to think this through carefully rather than answer off the cuff. I teach philosophy at a university, and I have been turning an old philological habit into a question about meaning. When a new concept needs a name, scientists and philosophers very often build the term out of Latin or Greek roots instead of ordinary English — photosynthesis, pandemic, qualia, supervenience. Such a term is meant to be something like a miniature definition: its roots are supposed to make the outlines of the concept visible to anyone with the relevant classical vocabulary. But the practice is neither universal nor internally consistent. Some highly successful coinages are opaque in form — quark, byte — and nobody treats the name itself as part of the concept's content. Conversely, some classical-derived terms drift so far from their etymologies that their roots now mislead rather than instruct.

My question is not about the history but about the implicit philosophy of language. What does this practice presuppose about how a term acquires and retains its meaning? Does it presuppose that meaning is fixed at the moment of coinage by the semantic contribution of the roots, with a dead language serving to stabilize that meaning against drift? Or does it presuppose something considerably weaker — say, that the sciences need a neutral, international, prestige-bearing technical register, with the classical roots performing only a mnemonic or ceremonial role? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, explain how much of the practice would need to change if one instead held a use-based or externalist theory of meaning. Please begin by brainstorming candidate presuppositions before committing to your reconstruction.
