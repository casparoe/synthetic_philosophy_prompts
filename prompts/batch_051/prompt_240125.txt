You are an expert in the philosophy of statistical inference, equally at home in the frequentist and Bayesian traditions, and I would like you to think through the question slowly; begin by brainstorming the possible diagnoses each framework could offer before settling on a mapping, and mark clearly where your mapping is forced by the episode and where it is underdetermined.

I teach a graduate seminar in the epistemology of science, and I have come to suspect that the replication crisis is the best test case we currently have for the dispute between frequentist and Bayesian accounts of evidence, because it turns abstract disagreements about p-values, likelihoods, and priors into a concrete question about how a community of competent investigators collectively produced so many fragile results. Analyze the episode through that lens. Take as the episode the replication crisis as it unfolded from roughly the early 2010s in psychology, biomedicine, and adjacent fields: large multi-lab replication efforts reporting that many published effects were weaker or absent on reanalysis or replication, together with the now-familiar practices of p-hacking, hypothesizing after the results are known, selective reporting, low statistical power, and publication bias. For each framework, I want a disciplined diagnosis: what it identifies as the chief epistemic pathology, which rule of inference or decision it says researchers violated, what it would have predicted about the observed pattern of non-replications, what remedy it prescribes, and where concepts such as long-run error control, the likelihood principle, prior probabilities, and posterior odds genuinely do explanatory work rather than merely supplying vocabulary. Then run the inquiry in the other direction. Which features of the replication crisis does each framework genuinely illuminate, and which does it misdescribe or leave silent? Which aspects of the episode suggest that the real failure lay in incentives, career structures, and publication norms rather than in the theory of inference itself? Finally, be willing to say whether an honest proponent of each framework should regard the replication crisis as confirming their position, or whether the episode ought to make both camps uneasy.
