I run a monthly philosophy reading group for grad students mostly trained in analytic metaphysics. They've done McTaggart, presentism versus eternalism, A-series and B-series, all of it, so they come in loaded. Next month I want to spend one ninety-minute session on Dōgen's idea of uji, usually rendered being-time. As I understand it, the core claim is that time is not a container that things pass through; each thing just is time, in its own dharma-situation, so that firewood is completely firewood and ash is completely ash, and the before and after between them are somehow cut off — yet Dōgen is clearly not denying change, and he is not saying moments are frozen or that all times are simultaneous. My fear is that within ten minutes the group will have slotted it into which ever familiar position they reach for first, and whichever one they pick will be wrong in a way I can't undo at the end. Plan the session's explanatory arc rather than just explaining uji to me: what has to be in place before the claim even makes sense, whether firewood-and-ash is the right carrying example or whether I should pick another one and what it needs to do, the misconceptions they'll form in the order they'll form them — I'm guessing first 'so he's a presentist,' then either 'so time is an illusion' or 'so he's an eternalist' — and where in the sequence each should be headed off. Also tell me what I should openly decline to get into with this audience (I don't want to lose the hour on Dōgen's biography or the sectarian context he was writing in), and give me a closing question that would show me whether they have actually got being-time rather than just learned to nod along.
