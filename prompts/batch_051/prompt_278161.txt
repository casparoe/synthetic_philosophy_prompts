So I've been going back and forth with a friend who works in museum education about the Benin Bronzes and whether European museums should send them back, and she kept accusing me of smuggling in a universal-human-patrimony assumption. That got me thinking about Montaigne, because his essay on the Tupinambá does something more unstable than people remember: half the time he's saying their customs make sense on their own terms and we're the real barbarians, and the other half he's leaning on a fairly strong anti-provincialism — the idea that every culture flatters its own customs and calls the rest savage. But he also has this conservative-skeptic streak in the Essays: once you've admitted certainty is impossible, the sensible move is to follow the laws and customs you already have, since custom is the only thing holding society togther.

Here's the task. Bring Montaigne forward to the present, assume he gets a thorough briefing on the modern repatriation debates — the Benin Bronzes, the Parthenon Marbles, NAGPRA, the arguments over whether an object belongs to a particular people or to everyone, and the newer practices of loans and shared stewardship. Then predict, as specifically as you can, where he'd land on two or three concrete cruxes: for instance, should the British Museum return the bronzes outright, would he read the universal-museum talk as just new clothing for the old provincialism, and would his skepticism push him toward "it hardly matters, follow the current legal regime" or toward "the current legal regime is exactly the custom most in need of exposure"? I don't want the flat answer — he was a relativist, so cultures can't judge each other — or the feel-good answer — he'd obviously side with the plundered. I want you to actually decide which of his commitments wins when they conflict, and to flag where he might surprise someone who has only read the cannibals essay.
