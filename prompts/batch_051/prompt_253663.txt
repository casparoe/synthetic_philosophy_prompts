I'm a PhD student in a research group that builds predictive models of water quality, and I keep getting pulled into the same argument. A senior colleague responds to almost any methodological criticism—about omitted variables, extrapolating beyond the calibration range, or the realism of our structural assumptions—by saying that "truth is what works." Since our models reproduce the observed record and guide real decisions that have not yet gone badly wrong, he says they are true, full stop. The junior members of the lab object that a model can be useful without being true: epicycles "worked" for prediction for a long time, and a rule of thumb can be useful while strictly false. Every time I try to intervene, though, I find I can't say what the slogan is actually claiming.

I know enough about the history of pragmatism to suspect that our senior colleague would not easily fit either Peirce or James, and that the slogan is probably a loose mixture of several distinct theses. Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring to our next lab meeting, the distinct precise claims that "truth is what works" might be expressing? Please order them from the most trivial or deflationary to the most substantive, and for each say (a) whether it is a plausible account of truth, (b) how it relates to the accounts of truth defended by Peirce and by James, and (c) whether it licenses the inference from "our model works well enough for our purposes" to "our model is true." I especially want to know which readings, if any, would make my colleague's inference valid, and which readings would reduce his slogan to a tautology that no one could object to but that also carries no weight in an argument about methodology.
