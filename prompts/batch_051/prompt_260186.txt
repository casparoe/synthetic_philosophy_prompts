I started last month as head of responsible sourcing at a mid-sized sportswear brand — it's a family company, and after my uncle visited one of our supplier factories and came home unable to stop talking about it, he made this my whole job. We review supplier relationships about once a month, but when an audit comes back bad or a credible complaint lands, we have days to decide wheter to keep the order, fix the supplier, or pull out. The team aren't philosophers and they all have sales targets, and I'm the one who signs off.

The problem I keep hitting is that our suppliers in lower-cost countries often pay badly and work people hard, but the jobs are still the best option the workers have — pulling our orders might push people into worse work or none. I've read enough to know there's a live argument, sometimes called mutually beneficial exploitation, that an exchange can be both genuinely beneficial to the worker and still exploitative, and that "their alternative is worse" isn't automatically the end of the story. But I've also watched the opposite failure: people using the word exploitation to block any deal where the money is unequal, which would just make the workers poorer.

Write me a decision procedure my team can actually run on a given supplier when we get bad audit results or a credible complaint. It should be a checklist with the questions in the order they matter, what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure says "no, exit" versus "stay and push for changes," and crucially, which steps are really value judgments my uncle and I have to make rather than the checklist. I want it to keep the real distinction between "this deal is worse than the worker's realistic alternative" and "this deal is better than the alternative but still wrong in some way," because I've watched people collapse those two, and the whole decision turns on it.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a factory that pays above the local going rate but has blocked fire exits and won't fix them unless we pay more per unit; (2) a factory that pays below our own supplier code but the workers told an auditor they'd rather we stay than leave, because the alternatives in the area pay much less; and (3) the one I'm dreading — our best supplier on cost and quality, where the audit found kids working in a subcontractor's part of the floor. I want at least one worked example to come out to an answer I won't like, and say so plainly.

Also tell me what the procedure can't settle — the cases where it will still just be me on a phone call making a call. Keep it short enough to hand to a buyer who won't read more than a page, but don't turn it into slogans, and dont cheat by saying "it depends."
