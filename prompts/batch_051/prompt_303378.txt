A friend and I recently spent an entire evening arguing about the moral position of a single, invented soldier, and by the end neither of us had moved at all. The soldier fills a marginal administrative role in an unjust war of aggression; they were conscripted, have absorbed their own state's propaganda, never target non-combatants, and their only violent act, committed once and in self-defence, is to shoot an opposing soldier when their convoy is ambushed. We agreed on all of those facts, and we also agreed that this act made no measurable difference to the war's outcome. What we could not agree on is whether the act was wrong.

My friend took the orthodox line on the moral equality of combatants, broadly Walzerian. He argued that combatants in uniform are symmetrically situated: once a person has been made an instrument of a state, the moral character of their killing is governed by how they fight, not by why their state fights. Because this soldier obeys the rules governing the conduct of war, their killing is no more wrongful than a just combatant's would be. To treat the soldier as a murderer, my friend said, is to hold them responsible for facts about their state that they cannot reliably know and did not choose.

I took the revisionist line, broadly McMahanian. I argued that a permission to kill cannot come into being merely because a state issues a uniform and an enemy provides the occasion. If the war lacks a just cause, then the enemy the soldier shoots is not liable to be killed, however cleanly the killing complies with the laws of war. The soldier may be excused, as someone is excused for a wrong done under coercion and ignorance; but an excuse is not a justification, and to call the act permissible seems to me to deny that the injustice of the war matters at the point where the war is actually fought.

What I want to understand is not which of us is right. That is, I suspect, a well-worn dispute that has run through just war theory for decades without either side conceding much. What interests me is that our argument reproduced the impasse in miniature, even though both positions are prepared to grant a great deal to the other. Both can agree that many conscripted soldiers are blameless; both can agree that the justice of a war is rarely knowable with confidence from inside it; both can agree that the institution of war requires some separation between the wrong of launching a war and the wrongs committed in fighting it; and both can agree that our soldier's individual act was causally negligible. Once these concessions are made, apparently nothing follows about the central question: whether a combatant on the unjust side may permissibly kill a combatant on the just side.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on it. Is the impasse verbal, turning on different senses of 'permissible' — one side meaning justified, the other meaning excused or blameless? Is it a substantive disagreement about whether the unit of moral evaluation in war is the individual act or the institutional and collective enterprise? Is there a hidden empirical question about what soldiers can reasonably be expected to know, or about how negligence in forming one's beliefs operates under duress? Or is the dispute kept alive by incompatible bedrock intuitions about whether an institutional role can confer moral permissions that the role-bearer's own acts did not generate — intuitions that neither side can argue for without already assuming its conclusion? Given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress, whether that is a distinction the debate has underused, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. I am asking for an account of what kind of disagreement this is, not a bibliography or a survey of who has defended which side.
