I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on parental licensing and the child's right to an open future. A state statute creates a parental licensing board and includes this provision:

"No parental license shall be issued unless the Board finds by clear and convincing evidence that the applicant is likely to provide a child with a rearing environment that preserves the child's future as an open one, meaning that the child will retain both the capacity to form, pursue, and revise a conception of the good and access to a reasonable range of the major opportunities from which such a conception might be formed."

The provision was enacted before preimplantation genetic diagnosis was clinically available. A couple now applies for a license. They are both culturally Deaf and plan to use preimplantation genetic diagnosis to select an embryo carrying a known variant associated with profound bilateral hearing loss. They state in their application that they will raise the child in a signing home and in the Deaf community, that they will not seek cochlear implants, and that they believe the child will have a rich and revisable conception of the good within that community; they also acknowledge the child will never have the option of hearing. The Board is split on whether this plan preserves the child's future as an open one.

Please work through what the text, the purpose of the provision, and what the drafters would have said about this case each imply, and say which approach should control. In particular, explain whether a selection made before the child exists falls within the clause at all, whether the loss of one normal human capacity is enough to violate it, and whether the phrase "access to a reasonable range of the major opportunities" should be read relative to the child's actual community or independent of it.
