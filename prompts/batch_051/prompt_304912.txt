So I cover games and ideas, and the more reviews I read the more the standard playbook looks like it's quietly assuming a whole theory about where the art in a game lives. Here's the practice I mean: at most big outlets and award shows, a game gets whittled into separate buckets — narrative, art direction, score, gameplay — and the first three are treated as pretty much self-contained aesthetic achievements. A game can get called visually stunning and still be written off as dull to play; a different game can be defended as art mostly on the strength of its cutscenes and script even when the player's actual choices barely matter. Seperate from that, theres a line of thinking (some critics call it the aesthetics of agency) that says the properly artistic thing about a game is the shape and feel of deciding and acting inside a possibility space — the agency itself, not the film-like parts wrapped around it. My question is what that everyday reviewing-and-awards practice genuinely presupposes. Does it commit to the view that a game's real aesthetic value is in the bits that could survive as a movie, with agency demoted to a delivery mechanism? Or is it more underdetermined than that, just a pile of habits that several theories could live with? And if you hard-committed to agency as the primary aesthetic object, what would a review rubric or an award show built around that actually score — and what would it have to stop caring about? Don't hand me a menu of readings. Plant a flag: tell me which account of the practice you think is strongest and why, and then say what in the practice would have to change if that account turned out wrong.
