I teach a course on the Presocratics and next week we start Parmenides. I always find the students either think he's saying something trivial or something obviously insane before we slow down, so I'd like to open the class by reading a poem aloud, something that does a bit of the philosophical work in advance. Could you write me a villanelle in the voice of the goddess from the proem, speaking to the young man who has been brought to her house? The form should do real work here, because the villanelle keeps returning to the same two lines the way the argument keeps returning to the same two options. Make one refrain the way of what-is, the claim that what-is is and cannot not be, maybe worded a little differntly each time but always the same in content. Make the other refrain the forbidden way, the attempt to think or say what-is-not, which should collapse or refuse to complete itself every time it comes back.

The middle tercets should walk through the signs of what-is one at a time: ungenerated, imperishable, whole or continuous, immovable, complete or without end. At least one tercet should mock the mortals who wander two-headed and trust their senses to report coming-to-be and passing-away, as if being and not-being could be mixed. The closing quatrain should say what has to be given up, and I want it to be the harder version, not just change but plurality, the supposedly separate things, leaving what-is one and continuous and, if you like, like a well-rounded sphere. Someone who works on Parmenides should recognize the argument of B8 in it rather than just the slogans, and an undergraduate meeting him for the first time should come away with the shape of it. Also, don't let the poem treat nothing as if it named a thing; that's the exact failure the argument is meant to expose. I can't speak for the goddess, but I'd take a few rough edges in the poem over a smooth one that misses the argument.
