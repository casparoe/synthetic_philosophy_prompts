You're an epistemologist who works on forecasting, base rates, and the limits of self-knowledge — and I'd like you to think through this properly, brainstorm a few candidate readings before committing, and not just hand me a "both sides are right" compromise. Tell me what would have to be true for each side to give way.

I've been sold on the outside view for years now, and I still think it has the best track record of any rule I've found for making predictions about my own life. The idea is that when you're estimating how long a project will take or how likely it is to succeed, you start with the base rate for a reference class of similar projects and only adjust if you have strong, independent evidence. The inside view, where you build your estimate out of the particular details of your own case, is exactly what drives the planning fallacy: every novel, startup, renovation, and dissertation feels special from the inside, and that feeling is systematically misleading. The superforecasting litterature and the optimism-bias results all point the same way. The outside view also scratches my epistemic-modesty itch — it's a standing policy against treating yourself as the exception, and it gives a tidy, non-arbitrary procedure where most rival views just give vibes.

But I have an intuition I can't dissolve, and it's getting worse the more I use the outside view in practice. In some cases the inside view is the only thing that can see the signal at all. Concretely: a founder deciding whether to start a company with a cofounder they've worked with for a decade, in a market where they hold proprietary data. The base-rate answer is that roughly 90% of startups fail, so the smart move is no. The founder says the 10% tail contains cases with exactly these features — long cofounder history, real private information. The outside view replies that every founder says that, and that the inside view always finds a story for why this case is special; that's the bias. But notice what the theory has done there: it has ruled in advance that private causal information cannot overturn the base rate, because any attempt to overturn it gets classified as narrative self-deception. That makes the outside view unfalsifiable in individual cases, and it seems to throw away the only information that could distinguish "a generic startup" from "this startup." I don't know how to hold that.

And the reference class problem feels worse than people admit. The outside view says: find a reference class and use its distribution. But which one? "Startups," "startups with repeat founders," "startups in this sector this decade," "startups with this funding structure" — each gives a different base rate, and picking among them is itself a piece of inside-view reasoning about which similarities matter causally. So the view that was supposed to discipline the inside view smuggles it back in at the very first step.

So here's the clash, stated plainly. Should my intuition be debunked — is resisting the base rate just the inside view defending itself, a symptom to be treated rather than listened to? Should the theory be amended, for example by restricting the outside view to aggregate forecasting and admitting that one-off decisions by agents with genuine local information need something more? Or is there a stable compatibility reading — e.g., the outside view sets the prior and the inside view supplies the likelihood — and if so, what is the actual rule for when local information licenses an update? What test distinguishes "genuinely predictive inside information" from "a really good story"? Walk me through what justifies each move and what each move costs, before you settle.
