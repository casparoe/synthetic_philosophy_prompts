I've been defending a view to my students and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The view is that in Bayesian inference the prior mostly washes out: once you condition on enough shared data, two people who started with very different priors end up with posteriors in about the same place, so prior choice is a practical nuisance rather than a deep source of stable disagreement. I lean on the convergence theorems to back this up. Stress-test it for me. What would have to be true for the claim to be false, or true only in a misleading way? I want a map of where it actually risks something: formal conditions where it fails outright (which dogmatic priors matter and why, not just the hand-wavey version), cases where it is technically true but so slow it's worthless, and the assumptions I'm quietly importing about the likelihood, the model class, and the data. Also tell me if I've qualified the slogan into unfalsifiability and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind. I'd personally find it most persuasive if you could point to a concrete setup where two reasonable agents looking at the same data would legitimately never come to agree, or would converge on the wrong answer.
