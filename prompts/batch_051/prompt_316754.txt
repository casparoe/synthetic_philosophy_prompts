I am a planner at a regional transit authority. A few times a year I help set long-lived technical standards: rail platform heights, fare-payment rules, electrification voltages, bus and rail vehicle specifications, and the formats we use to exchange arrival data. These choices are expensive to reverse, and the vendors, consultants, and elected officials around us constantly say that whatever we decide now will lock us in for thirty years. Sometimes they say it to argue that we should delay and keep our options open; sometimes to argue that we should adopt the incumbent standard before the region fragments into incompatible systems; sometimes to argue that the new standard is inevitable and we should get ahead of it.

I need from you a reusable procedure, a checklist or a short ordered set of tests I can run in about a week with the data we can actually obtain, for deciding when a proposed standard poses a genuine path-dependence risk and when lock-in is being used as a bluff or as a fancy name for ordinary switching costs. The procedure should do several things. It should separate mere high switching costs from self-reinforcing processes: network effects, learning by using, installed-base complementarities, and political or contractual entrenchment. It should force us to state the counterfactual explicitly, the alternative path we think we are giving up, and to say what evidence would tell us whether the same outcome would probably have arrived anyway, because the board keeps asking whether we would only be choosing the inevitable winner. And it should identify which hedges are worth buying, such as modular interfaces, dual compatibility, sunset clauses, reversible pilots, and escrow of designs, as opposed to delay dressed up as prudence.

Please give me the procedure itself, written so a non-economist on our board can run it without the theory; a sentence or two per item saying which feature of path dependence it operationalizes, with no literature review and no citations, since I need the working version, not a reading list; and then three worked applications from our world. In one, the procedure should return the answer we do not want to hear: we are already locked in and cannot realistically escape. In the second, it should tell us that what a vendor calls lock-in is not path dependence in any sense that should change our decision. In the third, run it on a case where the decisive counterfactual cannot be estimated, and show what we should tell the board when the honest result is that the procedure cannot settle the question. Finally, mark each step that is a judgment call dressed up as a test, and say where the procedure is likely to misfire.
