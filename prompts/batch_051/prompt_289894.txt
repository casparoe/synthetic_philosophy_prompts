I've been reading about how AI labs decide whether a model is safe enough to release, and the whole thing is kinda torn between two different standards. On one side they run the model through a huge battery of test prompts — will it refuse a recipe for a bomb, will it stay non-toxic when provoked, will it avoid lying when it doesn't know — and then summarize with an average: refuses 97.4% of harmful requests, scores 8.1 on helpfulness, whatever. If the average clears a bar, it ships. On the other side, the same companies keep red-teams whose entire job is to find one single worst case. One jailbreak that gets the model to hand over something genuinely dangerous is treated as a serious safety incident even if every average stays at 99.9%, and sometimes its enough to block or delay release all by itself. Those feel like two different theories of what it means for a model to be safe. It's like declaring a bank vault secure because fewer than 1% of randomly tried keys open it, while also admitting that one known key would let anyone in. I want you to reconstruct what the benchmark-and-average practice presupposes about safety or alignment. Does it treat safety as an average property of outputs, like the failure rate of a toaster? Is that a coherent way to evaluate a system where a single output can be catastrophic, or does a catastrophic-risk technology force a worst-case standard where any known severe failure just means not-safe? Be charitable: distinguish what the average-case approach strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is underdetermined so that several theories would license it. Then tell me what an evaluation and release regime built on the worst-case view would actually have to change — and whether you think the average-case practice is defensible or a category mistake. I'd rather you commit to a verdict than split the difference.
