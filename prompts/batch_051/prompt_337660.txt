You are an expert in constitutional law and the philosophy of criminal law, known for working through hard cases criterion by criterion rather than settling for slogans.

In my government class, we studied the void-for-vagueness doctrine. As it was taught to me, a criminal statute is void for vagueness if it fails either of two requirements: fair notice, meaning a person of ordinary intelligence can tell what conduct is prohibited, and non-arbitrary enforcement, meaning the statute gives police, prosecutors, judges, and juries definite enough standards that it does not invite arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement.

I want to test those two criteria against a hypothetical statute I invented. Suppose my state enacts a misdemeanor that reads:

"A person commits student cyberbullying if the person knowingly sends or posts, by electronic means, material about a specific enrolled student, with intent to humiliate that student, where a reasonable student of the same age as the target would find the material severely humiliating, unless the communication serves a legitimate educational, journalistic, or familial purpose."

Now think about two applications. In application one, a student posts a manipulated photo of a classmate with a cruel nickname, and the classmate is deeply humiliated. In application two, a student posts a long, harsh, but non-threatening critique of another student's published opinions; the target says she is severely humiliated because the post makes her look foolish to her friends, and the writer admits he expected her to feel humiliated, though he says his purpose was to show that her reasoning was bad.

Please start by brainstorming the strongest arguments on each side: what would a prosecutor say about the statute's clarity, and what would a defense attorney say? Then work through the statute criterion by criterion, applying both prongs to the statutory language and to the two applications. Be explicit about the places where the doctrine genuinely underdetermines the result, and do not pretend a close case is easy. Finally, tell me whether this example exposes a weakness in the two criteria I was taught. For instance, is the deepest problem the semantic vagueness of "severely humiliating," or is it that the statute delegates a judgment about community norms to whoever counts as a "reasonable student"? And if the statute is too vague, identify the smallest change that would make it clear enough, then say whether even that fixed version would still leave something unresolved.
