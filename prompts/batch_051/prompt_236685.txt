A friend and I keep coming back to this argument about when Latin becomes Italian, and at some point I stopped caring about the actual date and started caring about why the question is so resistent to a straight answer. I'm building a list of arguments that the question, as people usually ask it, is badly posed rather than merely hard. So far I have:

(1) The continuity argument: language change is gradual, so any cut between 'Latin' and 'Italian' is arbitrary — any date you pick pretends a gradient is a step.

(2) The register argument: for centuries 'Latin' and early Romance didn't stand in a before-and-after relation; people wrote what they called Latin and spoke something else. So the question smuggles in the idea of a single entity called Latin that turned into a single entity called Italian, when actually the written and spoken varieties coexisted at different levels.

(3) The standardization argument: what we now call Italian is basically the Tuscan, especially Florentine, written standard, selected and codified by writers and then spread by schooling and media. Most of the peninsula spoke other Italo-Romance varieties that are just as much 'descended from Latin' as standard Italian is. Calling that one standard 'the successor of Latin' is a political and literary choice, not a discovery about a language.

(4) The many-descendants argument: spoken Latin didn't only turn into Italian; it also turned into French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian, and others. Asking 'when did Latin become Italian?' already reads history backwards, picking one national endpoint and treating the other outcomes as though they weren't part of the same story.

I have a feeling I'm missing a fifth argument of a genuinely different kind — not about continuity, register/diglossia, standardization, or reading history backwards toward one national language. Give me one more argument for why the question is ill-posed that isn't a rewording or special case of those four, and tell me honestly whether it adds anything the four don't.
