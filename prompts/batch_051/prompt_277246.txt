I'm a novelist, and I'm currently pinned by a character I can't decide whether to treat as irrational or not. She's a retired actuary, and in one scene she takes a sure $800 (call it A) over a risky mix (call it B) that pays $800 with 85 percent probability, $5,000 with 10 percent, and nothing with 5 percent. In a later scene she takes a 10 percent shot at $5,000 (call it D) over a 15 percent shot at $800 (call it C). I read the two scenes to a couple of friends at a residency and admitted I couldnt tell if she was incoherent or just running on her own logic. They got into it, and I transcribed the argument because it felt like a scene worth stealing.

Hal said she's straightforwardly incoherent: her two choices violate the independence axiom of expected utility theory, and if you accept independence plus a couple of other axioms, your choices have to be rankable by expected utility. A person whose choices can't be represented as maximizing one utility function, he said, doesn't just have odd taste — those preferences don't hang together. Maya shot back that "can't be represented by one number" is a bookkeeping fact, not a criticism: the axioms are just the conditions under which a utility representation exists, and calling someone irrational for failing a representation theorem is like calling a novel wrong because it can't be summarized in one sentence.

Then Hal laid out the Dutch book. Start the actuary with B and C together, he said. Since she prefers A to B, charge her a small fee — call it epsilon — to swap B for A; she'll take it. Since she prefers D to C, charge her another epsilon to swap C for D; she'll take that too. But B+C and A+D have exactly the same distribution state by state, so she's paid two epsilons to end up where she started. That, he said, is a leak, not a style. Maya replied that this only works against a myopic chooser: someone who sees the whole menu refuses the package, and someone who sees the offers coming refuses the first swap because she anticipates the second. Hal said then she's refusing a swap she prefers on the spot, in the name of a policy her current preferences don't support; if she does that every time a bookie shows up, she's just adding a hidden clause, not making the cycle disappear. Maya said a person can prefer a whole plan over its parts — looking ahead just is forming a plan-level preference, and it doesn't need justification from inside each step. Hal said if you allow that move whenever choices are embedded, coherence loses all teeth and nothing counts as a mistake, because you can always add "the way the options were framed" to what the person cares about. Maya said mistakes can be grounded in false beliefs, bad forecasts, and ignored evidence, but not merely in failing the axioms; the axioms are modeling tools, not duties.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each turn, say who's right about what, where each one overreaches or talks past the other, and what (if anything) both of them are missing. Then tell me, for the novel: is my actuary irrational, is "irrational" the wrong word for her, or does the answer depend on what we're using the word to do?
