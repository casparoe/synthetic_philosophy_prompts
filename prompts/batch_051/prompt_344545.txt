I sit on the selection committee for a small annual exhibition on games and play at a contemporary art centre. Each year the committee deadlocks over whether a particular game belongs in the show as a work of art, and the dispute usually turns on unspoken assumptions about what "art" means once a work is interactive. Some of us have been reading arguments that games can be art in virtue of what has been called the aesthetics of agency: the idea that the player's choices, and the felt quality of choosing and doing, can themselves be the aesthetic object rather than mere packaging around an authored story or image. I would like a working definition of "art" we can actually use in committee. It should clearly count an oil painting and a Tarkovsky film, clearly exclude a slot machine and a spreadsheet macro that reorders columns, and then give a defensible verdict on the cases we actually fight about. Those cases are: Tetris played at a competitive level; Gone Home, a narrative exploration game with no fail state; The Stanley Parable, a game that comments on the player's obedience to instructions; a friendly game of chess between two people; a children's game of tag with no spectators; a procedurally generated walking simulator with no authored story or characters; and a themed escape room designed by a theatre company. I suspect agency is doing a lot of the work, but I do not know how to say that without making the definition circular or counting every game ever made.

The definition should be one a mixed committee of curators, game designers, and a sceptical trustee could apply in an afternoon without reading philosophy first. Please propose the definition, run it through every case above, say explicitly where it returns the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict, including any verdict the committee will resist. I would also like you to be honest about the points where the definition is doing genuinely evaluative work rather than merely classifying. Please write in British English, and do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; continuous prose only, as in a letter or memorandum.
