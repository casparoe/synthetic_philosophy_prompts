I’m putting together a small anthology of short philosophical fiction, and the next piece is meant to take Hume’s argument about miracles seriously—not as set dressing but as the actual engine of the story. I’d like you to write a short story of around 800–1200 words. Setting: a small Scottish coastal town, sometime in the 1740s. A young medical student returns from Edinburgh to help his father, a parish minister, investigate reports that a local woman was cured of paralysis after a traveling preacher laid hands on her. The town is divided, and the son finds himself caught in the middle because he trusts the people reporting it but has also started reading Hume’s essay on miracles.

Make the drama come from the weighing of evidence: the witnesses’ honesty, their lack of medical knowledge, the possibility of hysteria or spontaneous recovery, the reputation of the preacher, and the cost of being publicly wrong. Don’t resolve it by revealing a simple fraud or an obvious miracle. The story should leave the reader with the sense that the decision about testimony is a genuine and difficult epistemic problem.

After the story, add a short afterword—not part of the story itself—explaining where it takes liberties with Hume’s actual argument or rests on one contested reading of it, how confident you are in your rendering of the argument, and what point you’re least sure about.
