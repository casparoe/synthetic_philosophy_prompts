Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a hard-nosed expected-utility person of the kind who thinks every rational preference can be represented by a single subjective probability, and an ambiguity-aversion person who thinks the Ellsberg choices are rational rather than a mistake. I want a document both could actually sign after a semester of arguing, not a soggy compromise, with each side's view stated as strongly as it is willing to own.

Use the familiar one-urn Ellsberg setup: 90 balls, 30 red, 60 black or yellow in unknown proportion, and the chooser picks red over black, then black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow. Bring in the two-urn version only if it earns its keep. Don't waste space restating them from scratch.

Two sections. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: start with the behavioral facts, then what those choices do and don't establish, and include the flat mathematical point about whether any single additive probability over the three colors can represent the pair of choices when the win/lose description is held fixed. They should both sign that bit; the fight is over what follows from it.

Then a disagreements table. Columns: the exact point at issue; the expected-utility camp's wording of its own view; the ambiguity camp's wording of its own view; the crux that would settle or dent that row; and one concrete test both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Have rows for at least (a) whether the sure-thing principle is a requirement of rationality, (b) what the two-urn version shows that the one-urn version doesn't, and (c) whether a single prior plus some source-dependent distaste for not-knowing can do all the work the ambiguity people want. If part of their fight turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which part and why.

Keep the tests small and actually runnable: I should be able to do them in fifteen minutes with a freind, some poker chips, and a deck of cards.
