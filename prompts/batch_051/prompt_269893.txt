I'm trying to get a second read on an idea I've been kicking around for a chapter. I've been using the following rough test for when an old tradition gets to count as history rather than legend with some historical furniture:

(1) at least one of its central events is attested in a record made within living memory of the event;
(2) at least one major figure in the later tradition can be matched by name or role to the contemporary records;
(3) the later tradition preserves at least one specific detail that a later storyteller would probably not have invented, because it's too weird, awkward, or off-message to be attractive as pure invention.

I want to stress-test it on the Trojan War. Hittite tablets from the late Bronze Age mention a place called Wilusa, which most scholars identify with Ilios/Troy, and a power called Ahhiyawa, which may be the Achaeans; there's even a Wilusan king named Alaksandu in a treaty, which looks a lot like Homer's other name for Paris, Alexandros. Archeology also shows a big destruction level at Hisarlik (the site usually identified as Troy) around 1180 BCE. But the story everyone knows—a ten-year siege led by Agamemnon and Achilles over Helen—comes to us from hexameter poetry written down centuries later, and no contemporary text describes that siege.

Does the Trojan War tradition clear my test? Go through each condition and say whether it's met, not met, or underdetermined. Where the answer would flip on a fact I haven't supplied, tell me what fact would do the work. And if one of the conditions is ambiguous or not pulling its weight, say how you'd reword it.
