I find myself drawn to a version of motivational internalism about moral judgment: the claim that sincerely judging an act wrong is partly constituted by an affective or motivational response—guilt, aversion, or at least a disposition not to perform the act. Applied to moral knowledge, the view says that knowing an act is wrong, in the sense relevant to blame, requires more than the ability to identify a violation of a rule; it requires the capacity to feel the wrongness as reason-giving. The theory earns its keep. It explains why we do not fully credit someone who mouths moral words while remaining affectively indifferent; it unifies moral judgment, moral motivation, and moral responsibility within one package; and it supplies a neat account of what is missing in psychopathy, understood as a deficit in precisely the empathic and aversive emotional mechanisms that normally register wrongdoing as wrongdoing.

But I also have a firm intuition that this gets the psychopathic offender wrong. Someone who harms another can often state the rule he violated, explain accurately why his victim objected and why the law punishes him, and report—without any evident confusion or delusion—that he knew it was wrong and simply did not care. The deficit looks to be in caring, not in knowing. If we insist that he lacked moral knowledge, we seem to be building the affective deficit into the definition of knowing so that the conclusion arrives by stipulation. Worse, the law's standard cognitive test for criminal responsibility asks precisely whether the person knew that what he was doing was wrong, and the psychopath passes that test while the deluded offender does not; that line is intuitively doing real work. The empirical result that psychopaths fail the moral/conventional distinction task cuts both ways: it can be read as showing that moral rules lack felt authority for them—a volitional or attitudinal fact—or as showing that they fail to grasp the special, non-arbitrary character of moral requirements—a genuinely epistemic fact.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of the phrase knowing that what he did was wrong on which both survive—for instance, by distinguishing classificatory moral knowledge from full evaluative understanding and asking which of the two blame requires, or by relocating the psychopath's deficit from the epistemic to the volitional. Which resolution is best justified, and what exactly does each option cost?
