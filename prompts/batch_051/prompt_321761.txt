I'm teaching a seminar that pairs William Golding's Lord of the Flies with the classical Chinese debate between Mencius and Xunzi over human nature, and I want a careful reading of the novel through that debate, not a plot summary or a loose essay on whether people are basically good or evil. My working understanding of the two positions is this. Mencius holds that human nature already contains incipient moral dispositions—his recurring example is the spontaneous alarm and compassion a person would feel on seeing a child about to fall into a well—and that wrongdoing is the result of failing to nourish these beginnings. Xunzi holds that human nature in the raw is a set of self-regarding desires that lead to contention and cruelty, and that whatever is good in us is a deliberate artifact: ritual, teaching, law, and culture working against the grain of the material.

At first pass the novel looks like a Xunzian fable. English schoolboys arrive with the thinnest scaffolding of civilization—assemblies, the conch, talk of rules—and it collapses into painted faces, hunting, and murder with very little outside pressure. But I am not sure that first pass survives. The island does not simply peel away artifice to reveal raw nature, as Xunzi's story would require; it also removes everything Mencius takes to be necessary for the sprouts to grow: adults, teachers, settled rituals, models to imitate. So the novel forces a cleaner question than the philosophers' own examples do. Does the island reveal that the boys are bad by nature when artifice is removed, or does it show that even initially good dispositions starve and turn monstrous when the conditions for their cultivation are gone? Read the novel with that question in mind, and attend to the moments that cut each way: the early hesitations before the hunts turn lethal, the neglected signal fire, the way the beast is assembled out of the boys' own fear, Simon's understanding of it, Roger's deliberate release of the boulder, and Ralph's final weeping in front of the naval officer while adult civilization conducts its own war offshore.

Where does the novel end up: with Mencius, with Xunzi, with both, or with neither? Where does it complicate or resist each position rather than merely illustrating it? And what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that the standard images—the child at the well on one side, the warped board straightened by artifice on the other—do not capture?
