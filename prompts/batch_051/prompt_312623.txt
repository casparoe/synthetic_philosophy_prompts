I chair the weekly grant review panel of a private research foundation. Each week five of us read the same two or three proposals, discuss them in a fixed speaking order — most senior reviewer first, least senior last — and then vote yes or no. I have a strong prior, from the information-cascade and rational-herding literature, that this procedure is manufacturing agreement: the first speaker's opinion leaks into the later votes, and our unanimous decisions may carry almost no independent information. I want you to translate that literature into a procedure the panel can actually run. The constraints are: the reviewers are part-time outside experts who have not read the academic literature, a meeting lasts ninety minutes, the stakes are grants of $10,000 to $50,000, and I, as chair, cannot be the only person who understands the voting mechanics.

Write the procedure as an ordered checklist: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the process should stop with a "no" or with "insufficient independent information," where it should hand the decision to me with the underlying disagreement made explicit, and where private judgment cannot be eliminated no matter how the procedure is specified. Then run the procedure on three stylized applications: (1) five yes-votes after the senior reviewer opened with a strong endorsement and everyone else echoed it; (2) a 3–2 split in which the two dissenters can give only weak independent reasons; and (3) a 4–1 rejection in which the single yes-vote reviewer holds private information no one else shares. Make at least one of the three come out in a way the panel will dislike. Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot do — which failure modes it will not detect, and under what conditions I should distrust its output.
