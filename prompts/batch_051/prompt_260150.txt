I'm a retired organic chemist, and since I stopped doing bench work I've been reading moral philosophy — which is how I got co-opted onto the allocation committee of a medium-sized research foundation. We meet next Tuesday to decide a single three-year grant, and I'd like to circulate a short written opinion beforehand with a concrete recommendation. The three finalists are: (i) a clinical trial network for a low-cost sepsis bundle in under-resourced hospitals — about as near to a sure thing as public health gets, but it improves care only inside existing systems; (ii) a high-risk project to develop a rapid, field-deployable diagnostic for a family of emerging viruses, with perhaps a ten per cent chance of a step-change and a decent chance of producing nothing anyone adopts; and (iii) a governance and deliberation programme aimed at getting health ministries to actually use locally generated evidence, whose payoff is institutional, diffuse and slow. The commitee is stuck because each proposal wins on a different register — lives saved per pound, prevention of worst-case outbreaks, institutional capacity, fairness across regions — and there seems to be no common unit in which the three can be compared. One faction wants us to monetise everything through expected QALYs plus a risk discount; another says the underlying values genuinely cannot be traded off, so any ranking is arbitrary and we might as well draw lots. I have a nagging sense that 'incommensurable' doesn't have to mean 'nothing rational can be said,' but I can't yet turn that sense into a position the committee can act on.

What I'm after is this: a memo, say 700 to 900 words, I can circulate as my written opinion before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete allocation — including whether to split the grant and on what conditions — and give the reasoning. Engage the strongest argument for the option or options you don't recommend rather than strawmanning them; I'd rather the memo make vivid what we'd be committing to and what we'd be giving up than pretend a spreadsheet settles it. Don't hide behind 'it's a hard choice with no answer', though, because the money has to go somewhere next week, and I'd like the distinction between 'there is no precise exchange rate between these values' and 'any choice is as good as any other' to do some actual work. Write it in American English.
