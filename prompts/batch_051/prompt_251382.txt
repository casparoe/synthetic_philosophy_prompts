I've been going through my late aunt's old letters, trying to annotate what she must have meant by each word, and it's left me with a sneaking suspicion that ordinary words simply don't have meanings — that "meaning" is a thing we pretend is there because dictionaries need entries. My argument:

(1) If a word has a meaning, then there is some single thing — a definition, a rule, a content — that all its correct uses share.
(2) For just about any ordinary word (take "light," "run," "game"), its correct uses don't share any single thing; they overlap in various ways, and the word contributes something different in different sentences.
(3) If a word's correct uses share no single thing, then the word has no single meaning.
(4) If a word has no single meaning, then it doesn't have a meaning at all — "polysemy" is just a polite name for having none.
(5) So ordinary words don't have meanings. Only explicitly defined technical terms do.

Step 2 feels empirical and probably right, and step 4 feels like its doing something sneaky, but I can't put my finger on the exact mistake. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, which step fails, and what's the strongest reading of that step on which the argument goes through — or does it collapse?
