I run a small reading group of rationalist-adjacent people, and we've been poking at the problem of evil in its angelic-fall version: how a wholly good, free, rational creature is supposed to have pulled off the first evil choice. Every reconstruction I've seen cheats. The angel is secretly proud before the fall, or ignorant, or it gets shrugged off as 'free will, brute misuse of free will,' which is just naming the thing being explained. I want a thought experiment built to order that makes the two positions below come apart cleanly, because I'm going to use it as the worked example in a dialogue I'm writing.

Here are the two positions, oversimplified by me (you can sharpen them, but no history and no citations):

1. Intellectualism about evil action: an agent only ever chooses what appears to them, at the moment of choice, to be the best available option on balance. So the first evil is impossible for a fully informed, unimpaired creature; anything that looks like deliberately choosing a known worse option secretly involves ignorance, a false evaluation, or a pre-existing bad disposition.

2. Privation/defection view: evil doesn't have to be chosen under the description 'this is best.' The first evil just is the will defecting from the order of goods, fixing on a lower good as though it were the whole, with no prior defect and without judging that lower good to be best overall. The will can fail to love in the right order without antecedently mistaking what the right order is.

Target verdicts: the privation/defection view should come out saying 'yes, this is a genuine first evil, and here's the mechanism'; intellectualism should come out saying 'no, a creature like that cannot do this, and the description is smuggling in X.'

Specification for the thought experiment:
- The creature must be genuinely free, with no prior inclination, habit, affect, or disorder predating the choice.
- The creature must know the relevant goods and their ranking at the time of choice. No forgotten data, no missing inferential step.
- No time pressure, no appetite overriding the intellect, no 'God allowed it for a greater good' anywhere.
- The evil chosen has to be a real evil, not a minor tie-break.
- You may not use 'chose freely' or 'misused its free will' as the explanation of the choice. Taboo those. The mechanism is the thing being asked for.
- One paragraph, and it should still run if you swap the angel for a mundane character (a founder, a scientist, a senior civil servant) so the supernatural setting isnt doing any work.
- Don't just stipulate that the agent 'sees the lower good as sufficient' in whatever sense would hand the win to one theory. Write an ordinary act-description and let the two verdicts fall out of it.

Then do the check:
1. Walk through what each theory says about your case and why.
2. Say which feature of the case does the seperation work.
3. Give the intellectualist's strongest reply to your case, and say what small modification would blunt that reply.

And please write the whole thing in British English.
