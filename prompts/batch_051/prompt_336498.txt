I'm a journalist working on a piece about free riding and the arguments people use to justify mandatory contributions — taxes, vaccine mandates, even compulsory voting — and my editor keeps reaching for the same analogy. He says a free rider is just like the guy who shows up to a potluck empty-handed, eats a full plate, and never once hosts. So mandatory contribution isn't coercion, he says, its just enforcing the norm any decent potluck would enforce; the only people who object are the ones who planned to eat and run. I want to know how far that analogy actually holds before I let him print a version of it.

Can you start with the correspondence table: what in society plays the role of the spread, the dish, the guest, the host, the invitation, the dirty looks? Then walk me through where the mapping breaks. I already suspect a few spots — a potluck is something you can decline, a dish isn't a percentage of income, and there's usually no standing obligation to feed strangers. Tell me which of those disanalogies actually matter for his argument and which are just cosmetic. What does the potluck picture quietly import into the target: that contributions are visible, roughly equal, voluntary, in kind? And then the part I actually need: does the conclusion that mandatory contribution is fair survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the work the whole time? If it was, give me a better analogy that captures the anti-parasite intuition without turning society into a dinner party.
