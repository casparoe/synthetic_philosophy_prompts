I am a high school student preparing a discussion for our philosophy club, and I am trying to work through a variant of the famous unconscious-violinist case. In the standard version, the Society of Music Lovers kidnaps you, and while you are unconscious they connect you as a human dialysis machine to a famous violinist with a fatal kidney condition; it will take nine months for him to recover, and unplugging before then would kill him. Since you never consented at all, the usual debate is about whether you may unplug even though doing so lets him die.

Here is the variant I want to analyze. Suppose the Society of Music Lovers does not kidnap anyone. Instead, they run a volunteer drive and ask you to sign up for what they describe as a three-hour, low-risk blood-cleansing procedure that will help a famous violinist. You read the description, ask questions, and sign. The description is false: the actual procedure is the full nine-month connection, during which your kidneys do the violinist's kidney function and unplugging early would kill him. You wake up after the connection has already been made, and the director tells you the truth and says it is now your choice whether to stay.

The question is whether your signed consent changes the standard analysis. Specifically: (1) Are you now under a stronger obligation to remain connected than the kidnapped person in the original case, and if so, what is the basis of that obligation? (2) Is your consent valid consent to what actually happened, or is it void because it was based on a false description of the procedure? (3) Does the answer depend on which fact they lied about — for example, would it make a difference if they had told you the truth about the nine months and the physical burden but lied by telling you that you could unplug at any moment without killing the violinist, or lied about who the violinist was? (4) How well do the three standard grounds for invalidity — deception, coercion, and incompetence — each handle this case, and do they all point the same way, or do they come apart?

Please work through what the main views about valid consent would say about each of these questions, and compare them with what the original no-consent case implies. Finally, tell me which argument or consideration would most likely change your overall verdict, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
