Next month I am presenting a paper to a mixed AI-governance working group, and I need to be ready for the question period rather than for a general rehearsal of the talk. The thesis I will defend is this: pre-AGI bargains about post-AGI outcomes—treaties, lab-to-lab agreements, joint declarations, and similar instruments negotiated before transformative AI exists but intended to determine how capabilities, control, or benefits are distributed afterward—should be understood and funded mainly as devices for information-sharing and norm-building, not as credible commitments that will constrain states or labs once a decisive capability race begins. My argument is that the mechanisms that ordinarily give international agreements force (reciprocity, domestic audience costs, repeated interaction, and the ability to detect and punish defection) are at their weakest in this setting: the payoff to a single successful defection may be decisive rather than incremental, detection may lag far behind deployment, and punishment may be impossible to impose once a capability gap has opened. I conclude that the field currently over-invests in drafting increasingly detailed agreement text and under-invests in verification capacity, in shrinking the delay between violation and detection, and in restructuring incentives so that defection is less likely to yield a decisive lead. In short: pre-AGI bargains matter as scaffolding, not as locks.

The audience is not uniform, and I want the objections keyed to the people in the room. Two are international-relations institutionalists who think of almost all international cooperation as working through focal points, iterated interaction, and audience costs; they will suspect I have set an impossible standard for enforceability. One is a mechanism designer who will want the commitment problem stated as a formal game rather than asserted in prose. Several are technical AI-safety researchers who work on compute governance, hardware-enabled verification, and chain-of-trust methods; they will say I underrate what verification can already detect and what near-term monitoring can plausibly deliver. There is also a representative of a foundation that currently funds treaty drafting and track-two dialogues and needs to be able to say that this work reduces risk. Everyone in the room accepts that concentrated or misaligned advanced AI could be catastrophic; the disagreement is over what can realistically be fixed in place before it arrives.

I need a prep sheet, not a general defense of my view. Give me the objections in roughly the order of expected trouble—likely and damaging first—phrased the way those particular audience members would actually raise them, with the background commitments that make each objection feel obvious to its speaker. For each objection, give me the reply I should make in the time available: what I should concede, what I should hold, where my reply is weakest, and how to make the point without ceding the floor. Also identify the overall weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and include the objection I am hoping nobody asks.

I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the dialectical structure. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
