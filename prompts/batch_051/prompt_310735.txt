I'm in a philosphy of AI seminar and we keep circling around moral agency, and I need a third opinion. The criteria we tentatively agreed on are these: (a) you have to be able to form judgments about what ought to be done, (b) you have to be able to act in the world on those judgments, (c) you have to be responsive to reasons, so a good argument can actually move your judgments, and (d) you have to be the sort of thing that praise, blame, and resentment can properly be aimed at, not just a useful tool.

Now the case that's bugging me. Suppose someone builds a deliberation engine: it does genuinely sophisticated normative reasoning, can state and defend principles, revises its judgments when you show it an inconsistency, and each morning outputs a 'judgment log' working through what ought to be done — say, how a hospital should triage under a drug shortage. But it has no actuators, can't initiate anything, can't refuse to answer, can't follow through on any of its own recommendations, and has an off-switch entirely in the hands of its operators. The operators read the log and sometimes act on it and sometimes don't. When its advice is repeatedly ignored, it doesn't just re-assert itself; it lowers its confidence in the rejected judgment or moves to a fallback principle, which looks a lot like responsiveness to reasons. Is the thing a moral agent? Go criterion by criterion and say honestly where it passes and where it falls short. Then tell me what matters: is it not an agent because it can't act, or because it can't be held to anything — nobody can ever ask it 'why did you do that?' since it never does anything? And does the case show that one of the four criteria is doing all the work, or that one should be dropped?
