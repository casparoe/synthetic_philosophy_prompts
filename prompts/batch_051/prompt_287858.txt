Yesterday I had a long argument with a friend about whether large historical outcomes are ever best explained by the avoidable errors of the losing side. He read me this sentence from the opening chapter of Macaulay's *The History of England from the Accession of James II*:

"I shall recount the errors which, in a few months, alienated a loyal gentry and priesthood from the House of Stuart."

He took it as evidence that Macaulay was doing something subtle: treating the Revolution of 1688 as the natural terminus of a long constitutional struggle while still promising to narrate the particular mistakes that brought it about. As our argument went on, I noticed that he kept doing the same thing. When I pressed him on whether the Revolution would have happened regardless of James II's conduct, he said the underlying forces made it inevitable. When I asked why the errors mattered, he said they mattered because the outcome was not guaranteed. He shifted between these registers without noticing, and Macaulay's sentence seemed to license the shift.

I have been trying to pin down whether this specific configuration has a name. I know Butterfield's Whig interpretation, hindsight bias, and retrospective teleology are nearby, but they do not quite pick out the simultaneous appeal to inevitability and avoidable error. The outcome is presented as both structurally overdetermined and contingent on particular mistakes, and the losing agents are blamed for errors that, by the writer's own larger theory, did not determine the outcome. Is there an established term in historiography or the philosophy of history for this exact move? If it has no name, what would you call it, and how should it be distinguished from ordinary Whig history, hindsight bias, and the other neighboring concepts? I am not looking only for a label; I want the boundaries between the thing I saw in that argument and those nearby terms.
