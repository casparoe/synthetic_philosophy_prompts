I am a novelist working on a novel whose central intellectual conflict concerns the epistemic costs and benefits of relying on artificial intelligence for expert work. I need a short dialogue, roughly a scene's worth, between two characters who hold opposing and sophisticated views about whether offloading cognitive labor to AI erodes genuine understanding. One character holds that understanding is a state of mind and a bundle of exercised skills, and that it degrades when it is no longer practiced; on this view, a person or a discipline that depends on systems it cannot reconstruct or vouch for has traded understanding for merely reliable output, and has become fragile in the process. The other character holds that cognition has always been scaffolded by tools, writing, institutions, and other minds, and that the epistemically relevant unit is the larger human–tool system rather than the contents of a single skull; on this view, what matters is whether the extended system is well calibrated and corrigible, not whether every intermediate competence is retained in one head. I want the scene to dramatize the disagreement rather than explain it essayistically: the characters should be in a concrete situation, with something personally or professionally at stake, and the argument should unfold through what they say.

The two characters must be equally competent and equally able to see the other's point; neither should be a strawman or a mouthpiece. They should respond to each other's actual moves, concede what their own position ought to concede, and press the points where the other position genuinely strains. To keep the exchange from recycling the familiar "AI good/AI bad" debate, push it into the weeds: the distinction between producing a result and understanding why it holds or how one would detect that it had gone wrong; the regress that arises if every output must be checked by a human, since the tool then saves no labor, while an unchecked tool hides its errors; the parity between AI and older cognitive technologies such as writing, the slide rule, or the trained lab technician; and the diachronic worry about what becomes of a field when its results can be generated faster than any living practitioner can re-derive, audit, or repair them.

The scene should read as credible fiction with two specific people in a specific place, while remaining rigorous enough that a philosopher working on the epistemology of expertise or on extended cognition would recognize the positions as serious contenders. Aim for 800 to 1,200 words.
