I'm a journalist chasing a feature on why some cultures don't really have family names in the English sense, and I got into a row with a geographer at a conference about whether patronymics like Icelandic Jónsdóttir count as proper names at all. I've been trying to formalise my side of the arguement, but something feels off.

(1) A proper name, strictly speaking, refers to its bearer directly, without any descriptive content.
(2) A patronymic such as Jónsdóttir is applied by a rule: roughly, a person gets it just in case her father is named Jón.
(3) Since that rule appeals to a property of the bearer's parentage, the term carries descriptive content.
(4) Any term whose application is governed by such a rule is not a genuine proper name but a disguised description.
(5) Therefore patronymics are not genuine proper names.

A linguist I interviewed said this would also prove that ordinary place names like London are not proper names, because an official body once decided to call the place that, and that is also a rule. I can't put my finger on where my argument goes wrong, or whether it just is valid and I should bite the bullet. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, which step exactly fails, and what is the strongest reading of that step on which it still fails? If it is valid, how should I respond to the London objection without changing the conclusion?
