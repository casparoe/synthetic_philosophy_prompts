I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a scene in which a character is publicly told off by someone who, by any fair reading, did the exact same thing a few years earlier. My editor keeps asking whether the accusation should still land, or whether the other people in the room would be right to roll their eyes, so I've fallen down a bit of a moral philosophy rabbit hole. A friend who took some philosophy in undergrad gave me what he insists is the standard view, more or less like this: "Philosophers mostly agree that hypocrites lack standing to blame. The deep problem with a hypocrite's criticism is that it isn't really true, or at least it's weakened: if you break a rule yourself, you can't coherently judge that the thing is wrong, so the judgment loses force. And since the blame is insincere—you clearly don't think the rule matters, because if you did you would have followed it—the target is justified in brushing the whole thing off. So hypocrisy doesn't just look bad; it actually cancels the content of the criticism."

Something about this strikes me as a bit too convenient for my scene, but I can't tell weather I'm just being defensive on behalf of my character. Could you go through that summary and tell me what a careful philosopher working on blame and hypocrisy would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is outright wrong? And does the final claim—that hypocrisy cancels the content of the criticism—actually follow from whatever in the summary is true? If you could do it in British English, that would be great.
