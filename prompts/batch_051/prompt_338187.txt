Public health agencies do something a little weird. A sign in a park will tell you flat out that ticks carry Lyme disease, and a travel clinic handout will say mosquitoes carry malaria, even though the people who wrote the sign know that only a small minority of individual ticks and mosquitoes actually do—sometimes a tiny minority. But the same agency, when it releases data, refuses to talk like that: every estimate gets a confidence interval, and it wont say a treatment works unless the trial clears a statistical bar. What view of how sentences about kinds work does the sign-and-handout practice presuppose? Reconstruct that implicit semantics as precisely as you can, and say whether it is different from the claim that the signs are just loose shorthand for some or many. Is the practice coherent, or is the agency speaking two different languages in its public-facing and its data-facing modes? And what would a park sign or clinic handout have to look like if the agency adopted the view that its own plain sentences quantify over individual ticks, mosquitoes, and cases, so a bare claim has to be exceptionless or explicitly hedged?
