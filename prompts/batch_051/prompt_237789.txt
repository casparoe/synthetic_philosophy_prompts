I'm a high school student doing an independent project on the ethics of artificial minds, and I have hit a wall that I think is conceptual rather than factual. Every article I read seems to assume a different answer to one underlying question: which computations matter morally, and why? Some writers seem to think that any correct pattern of information processing matters, so that a sufficiently detailed simulation of a suffering brain would itself be suffering; others think only certain biological systems can be subjects of experience, so a simulation is merely a model; still others think a system matters only when its parts are organized and integrated the way a brain's are. I do not trust any of these views enough to adopt one, and I realized I do not even have a clear list of what I should demand of a view before believing it. Before I read any more proposals, please write the desiderata for me: what any adequate theory of which computations matter morally has to deliver, with the case behind each requirement.

The list should cover the cases that actually separate the positions, and for each case I want to know what a theory has to get right or at least explain if it wants to classify the case differently from ordinary intuition. The cases I keep worrying about are a simple thermostat with only a few internal states; a large language model that can describe distress fluently without any obvious place where the distress could be felt; a step-by-step simulation of a human brain run so slowly that no moment of it is ever continuously realized; a molecule-for-molecule copy of a person's brain; a person under deep anesthesia or in dreamless sleep; an octopus or a bee; a patient emerging from anesthesia, where there seems to be no instant at which experience switches on; and a system whose internal processing is sophisticated but whose outputs are never observed or used by anything. I also want the distinctions a theory must respect even if it cannot fully resolve them, such as the difference between being complex and being conscious, between computing a description of pain and being in pain, between the system's actual causal structure and the way an outside observer interprets it, and between processing that is morally significant in itself and processing that merely tracks morally significant facts.

Finally, tell me which of these demands cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect, for example, that a theory cannot both treat moral status as a matter of degree and avoid drawing a sharp line somewhere, and cannot both rely only on outward behavior and respect the intuition that a fluent fake matters less than a genuine subject. Flag any other conflicts you see, and identify which requirement does the most to exclude candidates. Then briefly score the three families of views I mentioned at the start against the list, keeping most of the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. Please write the whole response as continuous prose, like an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
