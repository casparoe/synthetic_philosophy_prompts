I'm taking a philosophy of perception seminar and the cognitive penetration debate has got me suspicious of my own criteria, mostly because of something that keeps happening when I go birding with my uncle. I've been using a four-part test for whether a perception is cognitively penetrated: (1) there has to be a cognitive state doing the causing—a belief, expectation, desire, concept, something like that; (2) the influence has to be on the perceptual experience itself, not just on the later judgment or report ("I saw it, then I inferred it" doesn't count); (3) the influence has to be fairly direct, not just the cognitive state moving your eyes or attention around, since nobody thinks expecting a deer on the ridge is cognitive penetration just because it makes you scan the treeline; and (4) the content of the cognitive state has to line up with the content of the experience, so anxiety making everything look darker is out.

The case that's bugging me: my uncle has been birding for fourty years, and when I started going with him warblers were just small brown-green blurs in the canopy. Now, after a few months of learning names and field marks—yellow-rumped, magnolia, blackburnian—the same birds genuinly look different. The field marks are just visually present; the bird pops out against the leaves. It's not that I see a blur and tell myself it's probably a yellow-rumped warbler from its behavior. It looks like one. But the cause isn't an occurrent belief about this particular bird before I look. It's the stored categories, the learned search images, the names. And some of the change probably runs through attention and where I point my eyes: I know where in the tree to look and which features to pick out. So here's the question: do my acquired bird categories cognitively penetrate my perception, or is this just perceptual learning plus better attentional habits? Work through my four criteria one by one, tell me where they underdetermine the answer, and say whether the case shows the criteria themselves need redrawing—especially whether "cognitive state" should include stored templates and whether "direct" should rule out anything mediated by attention.
