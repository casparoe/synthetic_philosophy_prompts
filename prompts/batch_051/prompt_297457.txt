Among the ideas I keep seeing in rationalist-adjacent discussions, one of the most reused is the habit of answering an account of some self-reinforcing bad equilibrium by naming it: Moloch, a multipolar trap. The source is usually Scott Alexander's essay Meditations on Moloch, in which the Carthaginian child-sacrifice figure becomes a personification of the way individually sensible decisions can aggregate into an outcome that nearly everyone involved disprefers. It gets applied to arms races, doping in sport, grade inflation, antibiotic overuse in livestock, engagement-driven design at social media companies, and AI labs cutting safety corners to avoid being lapped. The argumentative work the analogy does is fairly stable: it reframes the bad outcome as something no particular actor wanted, pushes back against moralizing accounts that blame greedy or wicked individuals, and suggests that the only real exit is coordination; unilateral refusal is just another sacrifice on the altar.

I would like an audit of that analogy, in the style of a correspondence table followed by a disanalogy analysis. What exactly maps onto what: the god, the cult, the worshippers, the sacrifice, the priests, the altar, the mechanism by which the sacrifice is compelled? Then identify where the mapping breaks. A historical cult has a rite, an institution, and often a priestly class that benefits from the sacrifices; many of the real equilibria cited have no one intending the aggregate outcome, while others have identifiable actors who profit handsomely from the race and might be described as its clergy. Does the personification import an optimization story that the underlying game theory does not contain — a suggestion that the bad equilibrium is being aimed at, when it is only being selected by uncoordinated local choices? Does it overstate the innocence of the participants, or understate the role of those who could coordinate but benefit from the current state? And what about the exit claim: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that these failures are largely structural and that coordination is the appropriate remedy survive, or has it been carried by the vividness of the god rather than by the model?

Please be explicit about where your assessment is load-bearing and where it is loose: which of your conclusions would be reversed by what argument or evidence, and which part of the disanalogy analysis is most likely to fail.
