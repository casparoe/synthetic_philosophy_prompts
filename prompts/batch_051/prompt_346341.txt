Under the standard definition used in most asylum systems, a person is recognised as a refugee only if they can show a well-founded fear of being persecuted on account of their race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion. A person fleeing a drought that has collapsed her district's food supply, a cyclone that destroyed her city, or a gang war in which she was not personally targeted does not fall under that definition, however severe her predicament. Some such people receive temporary or lesser forms of protection; many receive nothing.

I would like to understand what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose a view about why persecution, rather than harm in general, generates duties of protection — for example, that only harms aimed at a person because of an identity or belief count, while generalised harms do not? Or can the exclusion be explained by pragmatic considerations such as numbers, administrative capacity, and the difficulty of drawing a line, which could coexist with several rival theories? Reconstruct the implicit moral theory of the practice, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, note where it is philosophically underdetermined, and say what would have to change if the underlying view were replaced by a purely harm-based or need-based view. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Please answer in American English.
