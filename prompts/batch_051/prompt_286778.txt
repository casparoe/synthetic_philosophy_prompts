I'm closing out a small reading group on Levinas next week, and I want the last meeting to end with a poem instead of another round of half-formed comments. The semester kept circling the same knot: the other person's face is an ordinary, fragile, mortal thing, something I can describe or photograph or slot into a category, but at the same time it's the one thing I cant get behind or totalize, and it makes a demand on me that isnt derivable from my projects or my choices or anything about me. I want a poem that does justice to that double nature without just using the word Other a lot.

So here's the assignment: a Petrarchan sonnet, fourteen lines, octave and sestet, volta at line nine. The octave should be the attempt to turn the other person into a theme—a case, a face among faces, something known and safely placed in the world; an abbaabba rhyme scheme would be good there to give it that self-enclosed, totalizing feel. The volta should be the interruption, where the face addresses me and I'm on the receiving end of a command that wasnt spoken aloud—the prohibition on killing, the fact that I'm responsible before I ever decided to be. The sestet can sit in that exposure, both the other person's and mine, and it should leave the tension open rather than resolving it into a tidy moral. Try not to say transcendence or infinity in the poem itself; make the images carry it. You're a poet who actually knows Levinas, not just the jacket summary: think carefully about what the idea has to get right before drafting, maybe sketch the turn first, then write the thing.
