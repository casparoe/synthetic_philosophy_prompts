I am a machine learning researcher with a working command of Bayesian inference, expected utility, prior and posterior updating, model classes, selection effects, and simulation-to-real transfer; I have not read the philosophical literature in this area. Restate the following debate in that vocabulary as precisely and honestly as you can, and then give me a residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all.

The debate is whether the simulation argument should dampen far-future fanaticism. By far-future fanaticism I mean the view that, because the number of possible future beings is astronomically large, the dominant share of expected value lies in influencing the far future—for example, by reducing existential risk—so our choices should be driven by those long-run considerations. The simulation argument, as I understand it, runs roughly like this: if a civilization can eventually run enormous numbers of high-fidelity simulations of its own ancestors or of whole worlds, then simulated observers will vastly outnumber observers in the original world; in that case, an observer with ordinary evidence about their surroundings should assign high probability to being one of the simulated observers. The question is whether this conclusion should make the fanatic discount the far future. One side says yes: if we are in a simulation, the simulators may terminate the run, reset it, or never instantiate the long-run future we were trying to shape, so our actions may not reach the base-level future at all. The other side says no: the values we find ourselves caring about are instantiated in whatever world we occupy, the standard expected-value maneuvers should already be conditional on the world being the one it is, and the simulation hypothesis only swaps one environment model for another.

Please give me a systematic translation. In particular, map the simulation hypothesis onto a hypothesis class over generative environment models with a possibly hidden outer environment; map the self-locating or observer-selection step onto a choice of prior over being a particular rollout or instance inside such a model; map far-future fanaticism onto long-horizon expected return under model uncertainty; and state exactly what the damping claim would have to do to the posterior over reachable states—or to the reward specification—in order to be true. Then tell me where the translation fails. I want to know which steps in the debate are clean Bayesian moves, which are choices of prior or reference class that no amount of posterior updating settles, which claims about the normative weight of simulated lives have no counterpart in environment dynamics, and whether the apparent disagreement is substantive or is partly a relabeling generated by the two vocabularies. End with your own assessment of whether the simulation consideration actually changes the fanatic's decision-relevant posterior, or merely re-describes it.

Please write the answer in American English.
