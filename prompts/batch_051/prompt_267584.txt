A friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about the dollar auction: an auction for a one-dollar bill in which the highest bidder wins the dollar and pays the winning bid, while the second-highest bidder also pays their final bid and receives nothing. My friend claimed that a bidder stuck in second place at ninety cents acts rationally when he bids one dollar, since the extra dime takes him from a ninety-cent loss to breaking even. I replied that this is exactly the local calculation people dress their sunk-cost reasoning in, and that continuing past the value of the prize is escalation of commitment rather than sober forward-looking choice. Neither of us could shift the other.

I want to know how one would bring evidence to bear on this. Please design an experiment that could distinguish whether a bidder who keeps bidding past the dollar is (a) reasoning forward from the auction's perverse payoff structure or (b) driven by sunk-cost escalation of commitment. Tell me the conditions or manipulations you would use, the measurements you would take, and which result patterns would favour each reading. Then be explicit about what even a decisive result would and would not settle: are you testing the mechanism, the rationality of the behaviour, or something weaker?
