A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about the genre of historical writing that presents the past as a story of cumulative improvement—growing liberty, expanding knowledge, rising toleration—culminating in arrangements that look more or less like our own, the genre often called Whig history. He defended it: he claimed that abandoning this kind of narrative leaves history an unstructured chronicle, and that one cannot narrate at all without some standpoint from which to judge advances and setbacks. I kept wanting to object, but the objections I could state on the spot were only the standard ones: it is anachronistic and presentist; it reads outcomes backward and treats what actually happened as what was bound to happen; it writes the history of the winning side and quietly dissolves defeated alternatives; it condescends to earlier people for failing to reach conclusions we now hold; and it turns present institutions into the implicit endpoint against which everything earlier is evaluated.

What I would like to know is whether those standard objections really exhaust the case against Whig history, or whether they are largely different descriptions of one underlying mistake. Give me a further objection to Whig history—one that is not on that list and is not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of the items on it. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good: does it identify a defect the listed objections miss, or does it, on inspection, collapse into one of them?
