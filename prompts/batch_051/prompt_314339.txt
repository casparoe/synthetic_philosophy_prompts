I recently spent an evening arguing with a friend about Bernard Williams's example of the man who pauses before saving his wife to make sure that saving her would be permissible, and about the idea that the man has had one thought too many. My friend kept insisting that any preference for one person over a stranger needs an impersonal warrant, while I kept insisting that something is lost once the warrant moves to the front of the man's mind. I came away with several firm verdicts and no principle that joins them, and I want you to find the principle or tell me which verdicts to surrender.

First, a man pulls his wife from a river ahead of a stranger and, when asked why, says simply that she is his wife. I find that answer complete. If he had instead paused to confirm that a rule permitting spouses to favor one another covered the case before acting, I would think the rescue itself had gone wrong, even though he saved the right person.

Second, a judge whose daughter appears before her recuses herself. I find this plainly right, and I would be far more troubled if she kept the case and said she had scrutinized her own heart and found no bias. The thought that the litigant is her daughter should move her, but only toward removing herself.

Third, an emergency-room doctor treats her own brother's superficial injury ahead of two strangers with more serious injuries, but only after running the triage criteria twice and convincing herself that the protocol, properly weighted, lets her move him up. I find this much worse than if she had simply admitted to partiality. The seemingly scrupulous appeal to the standard adds to the wrong rather than excusing it.

Fourth, a teacher grades her own daughter's essay by the same rubric she uses for every other paper and, when asked, says so. I find nothing objectionable here, and I do not think the thought that the paper is her daughter's was something she should have suppressed.

Is there one principle that captures these verdicts about when a thought involving an impartial standard is one thought too many, when such a standard is exactly what the situation requires, and when appealing to the standard is worse than leaving it out? My friend would say these are just applications of the distinction between public roles and private life, but I do not think that distinction is doing the work: the doctor and the teacher were both acting inside public roles and I judge them differently, and it does not explain what is defective about the husband's search for a warrant. Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate if any, and apply it to the case I keep turning over. A professor who is close friends with a junior colleague sits on the tenure committee, does not recuse herself, and rereads the file three times to be sure she is applying the department's standards rather than her affection. Sometimes this strikes me as the teacher's honest use of the rubric, and sometimes as the doctor's second pass through the triage criteria. I would like to know which it is on your principle.
