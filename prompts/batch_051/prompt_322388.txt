Im a PhD student working on early modern theories of personal identity, and I keep getting stuck on the brave officer objection. The memory criterion seems to me to be the only account that explains why my future self should matter to me in the way it does: what makes someone me is that a consciousness reaches back and owns my past actions, which is exactly what the prince and cobbler case is meant to show. But I also take it as just axiomatic that identity is transitive, and the brave officer case seems to force the two apart. The old general remembers being the officer, the officer remembers being the flogged boy, so by transitivity the general is the boy; but the general has no memory of the boy, so by the criterion he is not the boy.

I dont want a one-line verdict about whether Reid or Locke wins. I want to know what it would actually take for transitivity to be the thing I give up, what it would take for the memory criterion to be given up or amended instead, and whether there's a reading on which both survive — for instance by treating memory as only evidence of identity rather than what constitutes it, or by making the real criterion overlapping chains of connectedness rather than direct memory, or something else I haven't considered. Which move would you make, and where is that move at its weakest? And please write the answer in British English.
