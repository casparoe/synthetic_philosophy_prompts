The standard story is that prioritarianism was built to avoid the leveling-down objection: egalitarians supposedly have to say that a change which makes the better off worse off and nobody better off is in one respect an improvement, whereas prioritarians don't, because they only care about absolute well-being with extra weight given to the worse off. But it seems to me this escape hatch only works when you compare outcomes one at a time. As soon as you look at policies chosen under uncertainty—say a public health measure, a tax schedule, an emissions policy—prioritarianism seems to face a leveling-down problem at the level of expected well-being: a policy can be best for the less advantaged in expectation only if it cuts the expectations of the better off, even though in many of the possible states it would make someone worse off and no one better off. Or maybe that is not a leveling-down problem at all, just the ordinary cost of spreading risk. I honeslty can't tell.
