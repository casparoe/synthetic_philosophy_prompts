I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I keep noticing a move among people who are otherwise scrupulous about base rates, and I don't have a word for it. Someone will defend their own long-shot project by saying the only honest way to estimate it is to start from the outside view: look at how often "projects like this" actually pan out, then adjust for the specifics. But once they've specified "projects like this"—same field, same stage, same team background, same market conditions—the reference class has been shaved down until it holds a tiny handful of cases that all happen to look just like their project, and the outside number winds up agreeing with the number they already believed. Then the same person, asked about somebody else's project, reaches for the broad, ugly class again; I've watched a VC do this across a single lunch, using a class of ten for the fusion startup he was backing and a class of hundreds for the competitor. He presented the first move as epistemic modesty—"I'm just letting the class decide"—but the class had been chosen to do exactly what his self-judgment would do. The outside check never really constrains; it just echoes. Does that two-step have a name? I don't mean base-rate neglect or plain overconfidence; I mean the labile, self-serving choice of the reference class that does the constraining. If there's no established term, what would you call it, and how does it sit relative to the inside/outside view distinction and the reference-class problem?
