My mother was diagnosed with dementia two years ago, and I have become, in practical terms, her memory: I supply the names of her cousins, the story of how she met my father, the fact that she once won a minor piano competition at seventeen. Because I am a philosopher by training, this has pulled me into the old debate over Locke's memory criterion of personal identity, and next month I am giving a department colloquium defending a version of it. I want to be ready for the questions I will actually get from this particular audience, not the stock objections anyone could predict.

The position I am defending is this. Locke's criterion fails in its simple form for the reason Reid pressed with his brave officer: the boy steals apples, the young officer remembers the theft, the old general remembers the officer's first campaign but not the theft, so remembered continuity appears to deliver an intransitive identity. The standard repair is to read the criterion ancestrally, so that identity runs along overlapping chains of memory and the general is the boy through the officer's link. I defend that repair, but I argue that it still gives out exactly where it is needed most, namely when the final links in a person's own autobiographical memory are destroyed. My amendment is that the chain need not run only through the person's own memory; it may be carried by the memories of others who have known the person continuously. On this distributed-chain view, someone whose own autobiographical memory has failed is not simply cut off from their past self. So long as another person holds the relevant memories and holds the present person together with the remembered past under one continuing biography, identity is preserved, though I concede it becomes partial or graded. I also claim this partial identity does normative work: it is what allows us to say that the person with dementia is still the one to whom long-standing promises and particular duties are owed, rather than merely a human being to whom we owe general duties.

The audience is a research-university philosophy colloquium, mostly philosophers of mind and moral philosophers, with two early-modern specialists who know Locke and Reid in the original, one Parfitian who thinks identity is not what matters, and one bioethicist who works on dementia and is suspicious of letting metaphysical identity carry normative weight on its own.

Please write my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way the relevant member of the audience would actually put it. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is softest. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the dangerous one.
