Suppose an AI lab trains a general-purpose chat model to be helpful, to avoid causing harm, and to be honest. Its honesty rubric, used for evaluation and reward modeling, works roughly as follows. A response is scored as honest when its factual assertions match a trusted reference, when it cites a source that supports those assertions, or when it appropriately says "I don't know" rather than guessing. A confident factual error is scored as dishonest even if the model internally "thought" the claim was true. But misleading-yet-true responses — cherry-picked statistics, technically accurate formulations built to leave a false impression, relevant caveats silently omitted — are not systematically penalized unless their propositional content is false in the reference-checkable sense. Sycophancy is in the same position: agreeing with a user's false claim is penalized only when the claim can be checked against the rubric's sources, and not when the user insinuates a false presupposition into the prompt. The same lab also lets the model generate simulated deceptive speech in a sandboxed adversarial setting, on the grounds that the text is not being asserted to anyone who could actually be misled.

What theory of honesty and deception does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it commit the lab to the view that honesty is fundamentally a property of a text's relation to the facts — accuracy or groundedness — rather than a property of a communicative act aimed at another agent? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the rubric strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is underdetermined: where several theories of honesty would license the same design choices. Then say what in the rubric and training procedure would have to change if the lab instead adopted the alternative view on which deception consists in intentionally causing or sustaining a false belief in another agent. Finally, say what would change your mind about your reconstruction: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about what the practice really presupposes, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
