Here's a parallel I keep thinking about and cant decide about. In Aristotle's Posterior Analytics I.3 he argues, roughly, that if every claim had to be demonstrated from a prior claim, demonstration would go on to infinity and never arrive anywhere; and if you let the chain loop back, you make the same thing prior and posterior, which he treats as absurd. So there must be first principles that aren't demonstrated. That's usually taken as a foundationalist move: chains of justification need a bottom.

On the other side, I noticed this is basically the same shape as the recursion rule in programming: every recursive definition needs a base case or it diverges, and mutual recursion alone isn't enough. So a vicious regress looks like a nonterminating computation, and a foundational belief looks like a base case. But then I thought about lazy languages: you can define an infinite stream like the natural numbers as a seed plus a generating function, and it does terminate step-by-step in the sense that you can pull off as many values as you want. The recursion is productive even though it has no base case that stops the sequence. That made me wonder whether some philosophical regresses are called vicious only because we haven't distinguished unproductive regresses from productive ones, and whether epistemic justification could be like a stream rather than a finite chain.

Is this a deep structural parallel or just a loose metaphor? What would have to be true for results about termination and productivity to transfer to infinite-regress arguments in epistemology or metaphysics? And if the parallel is real, does the productive-regress idea actually help with a specific case, like the justification regress or the regress of causes, or does it break because each step in a philosophical regress is supposed to provide support for the previous step, not just produce the next step?
