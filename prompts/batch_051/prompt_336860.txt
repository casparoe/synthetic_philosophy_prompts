Professional philosopher, can you help me identify a pattern I keep noticing? I was in a long comment-section argument after a tech worker left a company and wrote a public essay about its internal culture. The comments kept treating the fact that he had been an insider as itself a reason to believe him — not just that he had access, but that he had broken loyalty by speaking out, and the betrayal itself seemed to be doing part of the argumentative work. It reminded me of a former true believer leaving a religious group, where every statement about how much the speaker used to believe functions as a credibility boost. I don’t mean ordinary whistleblowing, where someone reports wrongdoing through formal channels or to protect third parties. I mean the public performance of disloyalty used as an epistemic credential: the speaker invites the audience to infer that he was one of them, that he had every incentive to stay silent or loyal, and that his current speech is therefore especially believable. The break with the group isn’t just the cost of speaking; it is an essential part of the appeal.

Is there an established name for this exact configuration in moral philosophy, social epistemology, or maybe social psychology? I’m not satisfied with just a label. I want to know how it differs from ordinary whistleblowing, from costly signaling, from moral grandstanding, and from the kind of defection where someone really does have inside information but doesn’t emphasize there own betrayal. If there is no existing term, can you name it, define it carefully, and give a couple of boundary cases where it is not clear whether it applies? Before you settle on an answer, start by brainstorming the nearest concepts and what each one misses.
