I am a professor of philosophy and chair of my university's Strategic Priorities Committee. At our next meeting we must decide how to commit a one-time, unrestricted gift of $40 million, and whatever we choose will be effectively irreversible. Three final proposals remain: (A) an endowed fund expanding financial aid for low-income and first-generation students; (B) a campus-wide research computing facility; and (C) a standing program of seed grants for high-risk, early-career research. The provost's staff has produced the following simplified table of each proposal's expected long-run net institutional benefit, in millions of dollars over a twenty-year horizon, under the two scenarios it regards as exhaustive for planning purposes: S1, in which federal research funding expands substantially and enrollments grow; and S2, in which revenues contract and research funding stays flat. The provost's office will not assign probabilities to S1 and S2, and calls our situation one of decision under ignorance rather than decision under risk. The committee has accepted the table as a working summary for argument's sake, though several members, myself included, are uneasy about a single aggregate's erasing distributional facts.

|  | S1: research funding expands | S2: revenues contract |
|---|---:|---:|
| A. Financial-aid endowment | 40 | 45 |
| B. Research computing facility | 70 | 20 |
| C. Seed-grant program | 55 | 25 |

The committee is nevertheless split over the decision rule that should govern us. One bloc defends maximin: choose the proposal whose worst-case payoff is highest, so that the least advantaged members of the university are protected. A second bloc defends minimax regret: choose the proposal that minimizes the largest shortfall from what we would have chosen had we known the state in advance. A third bloc, citing Harsanyi, says that under ignorance we should assign equal subjective probabilities to S1 and S2 and maximize expected aggregate benefit; a fourth, citing Rawls, replies that this is disguised utilitarian averaging and that we should reason behind a veil of ignorance and raise the floor for the worst-off students. Meanwhile, some members object that Rawls's difference principle was advanced for the basic structure of society, not for a budget committee of one university, and that minimax regret is a rule for private choice rather than for an institution with fiduciary duties to competing constituencies. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote, no more than about 1,200 words. It should do four things: (i) state which proposal we ought to adopt and the motion to put; (ii) specify and defend the decision rule that ought to govern us, against the competing rules; (iii) show which proposal each of the three rules would recommend on the provost's figures, and say briefly where those figures may mislead; and (iv) give an honest statement of the strongest objection to the recommendation and answer it. The committee needs a document it can act on, not a survey of positions.
