So last night me and a friend spent two hours arguing about whether you can be causally responsible for something you didn't do. His line was that omissions are just absences, and absences can't be causes — there's nothing to push the causal chain along, so saying 'my not watering the plant killed it' is loose talk, at most a counterfactual remark, not real causation. I kept pointing at the law, because it seemed obvious to me that the law does treat omissions as causing outcomes, but then he pointed right back at the law and said: no, look, you're not liable for failing to save a drowning stranger even when you could have easily saved them, unless you had some special duty to. So the law seems to care enormously about the duty, not just about whether what you did (or didn't do) would have made the difference. And I realized I don't actually know what picture of causation the law is implicitly working with.

So I want a reconstruction of what the practice of law — especially criminal law and negligence — presupposes about causation by omission. Concretely: the law mostly refuses to punish nonfeasance, but it will happily prosecute a parent who starves a child, a lifeguard who doesn't go in the water, or a passerby who starts a rescue and then walks off. If an omission 'causes' in the same sense as an action, why should the duty matter at all? And if it doesn't, what is the law actually doing when it uses causal language in omission cases? Does the whole doctrine only make sense on a view where causation is partly normative — where whether you caused an outcome depends on what you owed at the time — or can a purely counterfactual account of causation, plus a separate requirement of duty, explain the doctrine perfectly well and the causal talk is just loose?

Also, are the parts even consistent with each other? The act/omission line in criminal law treats omissions as different in kind from actions, but negligence law seems to treat a landlords failure to fix a staircase as causing the fall all the time, and I genuinely cant tell if that's the same concept of cause doing different work or two incompatible concepts sharing a word. And last: the law could have picked other routes — full counterfactual (anyone who could have prevented a harm and didn't is on the hook), or full moralized (you cause whatever you had a duty to prevent, full stop). What would actually have to change in the doctrine under each of those, and which commitments are doing the real work?
