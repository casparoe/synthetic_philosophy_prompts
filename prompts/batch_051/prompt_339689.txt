I've been chewing on an argument that omissions can't really be causes, and I can't tell whether it's valid or where exactly it comes apart. Here it is:

(1) To be a cause of something is to make a difference to whether it happens.
(2) Only something that actually occurs can make a difference. A non-occurrence isn't a thing—there's nothing there to do anything.
(3) An omission just is a non-occurrence: failing to act is the absence of an act, not a weird negative event lurking behind the scene.
(4) So omissions can't make a difference, and by (1) they can't be causes.
(5) But we constantly cite omissions as causes: the plant died because I didn't water it; the engine failed because the bolt was missing.
(6) If (4) is right, then all those ordinary claims are literally false. And it gets worse for blame: if you can be blamed for an outcome only if you caused it, then the lifeguard who sat and watched someone drown didn't cause the death, so she shouldn't be blamed for it.
(7) So either ordinary causal talk is systematically false, or one of (1)-(3) has to go—but all three feel undeniable.

I half-suspect the trick is that "make a difference" is doing two different jobs in (1) and (2), but I can't pin down the exact step where the argument would fail on the strongest reading. Is the argument valid? If not, tell me the precise step and the reading of it that fails. If it is valid, tell me what it actually establishes and what it doesn't—does it really show omissions can't be causes, or just that they aren't causes in one specific sense?
