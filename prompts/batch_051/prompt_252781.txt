You are a philosopher of medicine with expertise on the epistemology of clinical trials and the principle of clinical equipoise. I am a doctoral student in bioethics, and I serve as an alternate member of a research ethics committee. I want you to scrutinize a personal rule I have been using, because I am no longer sure whether it is a defensible epistemic discipline or a bias dressed up as a principle.

The rule is this: I will vote to approve a randomized controlled trial only if, after reading the protocol and the relevant literature, I can honestly say that I have no view at all about which arm is likely to be better. I do not mean merely that the published evidence is inconclusive or that reasonable experts disagree. I mean that I detect no private belief, expectation, or even felt leaning of my own in either direction. If I notice anything like a hunch—for example, I find myself thinking that I would prefer a relative to be randomized to the experimental arm, or that the control condition looks worse—I treat my equipoise as broken. I then either abstain from the vote or vote against approval.

A senior colleague says this is a basic confusion. He argues that clinical equipoise concerns the collective state of the expert community, not my private mental state. A private hunch, he says, is exactly the kind of bias that the equipoise requirement is meant to screen off: the honest response is to set the hunch aside and vote on the basis of the evidence as the field would read it, not to let an idiosyncratic feeling veto a study that the community has good reason to run. He adds that my rule is anyway unworkable, because genuinely uncertain people rarely have literally no leaning, and punishing every leaning would block nearly all trials.

Please tell me whether my rule is defensible, and if a defensible version of it exists, what that version says. In particular: is there a principled distinction between an evidence-responsive hunch and a merely affective preference, and would that distinction save the rule? Does my rule protect participants in a way that the community-equipoise standard does not, or does it simply impose my private priors on future patients? If you had to rewrite the rule for a conscientious committee member with limited time, what would it say?

Before answering, spend a moment laying out the strongest version of my rule and the strongest version of my colleague's objection; then give your own view. Lead with your bottom line in one sentence, and keep the whole answer under 500 words.
