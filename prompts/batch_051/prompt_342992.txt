I'm a novelist, and the book I'm currently working on has two subplots that keep colliding: one follows an insurance actuary, and the other follows her younger brother, who's up for parole. While researching the two storylines I noticed a weird asymmetry in how people use statistical evidence, and I want to pin it down before I let the characters fight about it, because right now I can't tell who's right.

Scene one. The actuary, Mara, raises a customer's car-insurance premium by forty percent because the customer moved to a zip code with a higher claims rate. The customer's driving hasn't changed a bit; the only new fact is that people in that area, on average, crash more. Mara's explanation is: "I don't know you as an individual. The data says people in your bucket cost us more, so the price reflects the bucket." Almost everybody in the story — and, I suspect, most readers — treats this as an annoying but basically legitimate commercial fact.

Scene two. The same customer later applies for a job that uses a screening algorithm built from the same sort of actuarial data, and she's rejected because people in her zip code are, on average, less likely to stay in the job eighteen months. Now the same readers react like something obviously unfair has happened. You don't judge an individual by what people like her tend to do.

I can't find the logical difference. In both cases a person gets sorted into a reference class and her individual outcome is estimated from the class's average. In both cases the class is just one of many true descriptions of her — zip code, age, occupation, driving record — and carving her up differently would give a different number (I gather this is exactly the reference-class problem). In both cases she doesn't control the bucket. And yet I find myself holding both judgments: the premium hike is fine, the hiring rejection is not.

I'd like you to act as the professional philosopher I would otherwise hire as a technical consultant. Think carefully; don't rush to a one-word answer. Start by brainstorming the obvious candidate principles that might ground the asymmetry — consent and opt-out, the stakes for the person, whether insurance prices a pool rather than judges an individual, whether the decision-maker could have gathered individual evidence, the difference between spreading risk across a portfolio and admitting someone to a job — and see which ones actually survive the pressure.

Then build the strongest case you can that the asymmetry is justified, and test it against the cases that make me nervous. What if the insurance is legally mandatory, so there's no real opt-out? What if a doctor uses the same zip-code statistic to decide whether to order a cancer screening? What if a judge, rather than an employer, used the same probability at sentencing? And be honest at the end: should we keep the asymmetry as ordinary life draws it, reverse it, or give it up? My plot needs to know wether the characters are defending a principle or just an accident of psychology.
