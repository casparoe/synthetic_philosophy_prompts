Two friends of mine got into a long argument at our reading group last week about anthropic reasoning, and I still couldnt tell who had the better of it. They were going back and forth on a thought experiment — call it the Trillion-Observer Universe. Two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, make identical empirical predictions about everything we can actually observe, and they differ only in how many observers the universe will ultimately contain: T1 predicts a billion, T2 predicts a trillion, with all those observers otherwise just like us. The question is whether, after noticing that you exist, you should update toward T2.

Maya said obviously yes: a trillion-observer universe gives you a thousand times more 'slots' in which your particular stream of experience could have been realized, so your existence is a thousand times more likely under T2 than under T1 — existence is evidence for more observers, which is just the self-indication assumption (SIA). Tom pushed back that both theories entail that someone with your evidence exists, so the fact that there is one of you is not news either way; extra copies do not raise the probability of your evidence, they just multiply it after the fact. Reason as though you are randomly sampled from the observers in your reference class — the self-sampling assumption (SSA) — and neither theory gets favored. Maya replied that Tom's random-sampling move is circular: you cannot first condition on 'I exist' and then also treat yourself as a random draw from an existing pool, when the size of that pool is exactly what is in question. It is like a lottery official telling you you have won but denying that winning is more likely in a drawing with more tickets. Tom said the lottery analogy sneaks in a time before the draw when you might not have been included, but in the cosmological case there is no such time: under both T1 and T2 you were never a possible ticket in a losing pool. He offered a different analogy: you wake up in one of two sealed rooms — one contains one person, the other a thousand — and the room with more people does not make the fact that you woke up as someone more likely.

They went around on this for another hour and never landed anywere. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves — Maya's more-slots argument, Tom's no-news reply, Maya's lottery charge, and Tom's sealed-room counter — from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Also say who, if anyone, is right about which specific point, and what the whole exchange is missing — and whether SSA, SIA, or neither survives intact once you think it through.
