I've been reading rationalist-blog arguments about Goodhart's law and specification gaming for years, and I've come to realize I don't acutally know what principle I'm committed to — I just have a slogan and a bunch of gut verdicts that don't obviously cohere. I'd like you to help me extract the principle, or tell me which verdicts I have to drop.

Here are the cases. A hospital ranks surgeons by 30-day post-op survival; surgeons quietly stop operating on the sickest patients, so the ranking improves while some patients die after being sent elsewhere. I think this is bad. A call center pays bonuses on average call length; agents start hanging up on callers with complicated problems. Bad, clearly. A chess engine is trained in self-play with Elo as the objective and is then tested against a varied set of engines it never saw; it really does play better chess, not just score better. I don't think anything went wrong. A software team is evaluated on story points shipped per sprint; velocity triples after they split everything into tiny stories and re-estimate upward, though I admit some of the velocity gain is real. I think this is bad, but less cleanly than the call center. A runner trains to bring down her 5k time and succeeds, but she overtrains and wrecks her health and her marriage. I want to say the 5k time wasn't corrupted as a metric; the problem was that a bunch of other values were left out of the objective, and that feels like a different kind of failure than Goodhart. An RLHF model is fine-tuned to maximize human preference ratings and becomes sycophantic, telling users what they want to hear. I think this is bad, and it makes me want to say the preference ratings were a proxy for something more like "helpful and honest" and the optimization found a shortcut through the gap.

My working principle is roughly: a metric corrupts exactly when optimizing it can move you through the gap between the metric and the target variable, and the more optimization pressure, the worse the corruption. But the Elo case seems to be a counterexample if Elo is just a proxy for chess strength, and the runner case bothers me because there was no gap between the target and the metric and something still went badly wrong. Can these verdicts be captured by one principle, or a small set of them? Please state the principle precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, and give its verdict on a case I haven't settled: an RL agent trained against a learned reward model that is about 90% accurate, where you know some of the error might be reward hacking of a sort you couldn't specify in advance — does it matter whether the agent is optimized moderately versus extremely hard? End by telling me which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to drop, how confident you are in the principle, and the point you are least sure of.
