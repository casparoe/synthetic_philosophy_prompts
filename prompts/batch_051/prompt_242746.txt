I'm on a citizens' advisory committee for a city government, and someone on the committee has proposed a formal decision rule for evaluating public projects — road widenings, noise ordinances, closing a park for a private event, that sort of thing. The rule has two parts.

Part 1: if a proposed project would impose a 'severe' harm on even one person — defined as death, permanent impairment, or chronic pain lasting more than a year — the project is rejected automatically. No amount of benefit to anyone else can override the rejection.

Part 2: for harms below that threshold, all individual harms and benefits get added up across the whole population, with no cap on the total. So a project that breaks one person's arm can be approved if it gives a million other people a dollar each, because the summed benefits outweigh the harm. But if the same project would permanently disable one person, it is rejected even if it would prevent a hundred thousand broken arms.

The person proposing this says its the only way to respect both sides of the dust-speck-versus-torture debate: we refuse to let many small gains justify one terrible harm, but we also refuse to ignore the accumulated weight of many small harms. I'm not asking whether the rule is morally right in the abstract. I want to know how it would actually behave if we adopted it. What does it incentivize for people proposing projects, for the officials who classify harms, and for ordinary citizens? Where does it get gamed first? What decisions does it produce that nobody actually wants? And can any of the failure modes be fixed by tweaking the threshold, the summing rule, or the veto, without the whole thing collapsing into either pure cost-benefit analysis or pure individual rights?

Please act as a political economist who studies decision rules and perverse incentives, not as a moral philosopher defending a position. Start by brainstorming the players and their moves, then organize your answer around the failure modes, ordered by how quickly and badly they would show up. For each one, say whether it's fixable and what the fix costs. Then, as the very last line, give me a one-sentence summary of the main thing I should tell the committee, in the form \answer{...}.
