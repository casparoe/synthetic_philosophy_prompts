I am a PhD candidate in bioethics, and in three weeks I am presenting a paper at a hospital's jointly sponsored transplant–critical care ethics conference. The audience will be deliberately mixed: roughly half physicians, including intensivists, transplant surgeons, neurologists, and palliative care specialists, together with transplant coordinators, ICU nurses, clinical ethicists, and a small cluster of philosophers of medicine. Their commitments pull in different directions. The intensivists and transplant surgeons care above all about organ viability, predictable donor numbers, and protocols that can survive legal and public scrutiny; the neurologists are protective of the rigor of determination of death by neurological criteria and worry about anything that dilutes it; the palliative care physicians and nurses are attuned to trust, timing, and any appearance that death is being redefined for the convenience of organ procurement; and the philosophers will press on the metaphysics of the concepts, especially the content of irreversibility. The question period will be fifteen minutes.

My paper defends the following thesis: the apparent conflict between death determined by neurological criteria and death determined by circulatory criteria is diagnostic rather than metaphysical. Both are operationally valid tests for one underlying condition, namely the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to maintain itself as an integrated whole. Consequently, donation after circulatory determination of death does not violate the dead donor rule; it certifies the same condition by a different route in contexts where formal brain-death testing is unavailable, futile, or itself harmful.

The argument runs, in compressed form, like this. First, what matters morally in death is not the activity of any one organ but the loss of the whole organism's capacity for self-maintenance. Second, neither the brain nor the circulation is the per se locus of that capacity, although each can serve as a reliable operational surrogate when the other cannot be examined or cannot be awaited. Third, the apparent asymmetry between the neurological standard and circulatory protocols, where the latter may certify death after a brief observed interval of asystole, is therefore best explained by differences in diagnostic context, including how long it is possible to wait, the vulnerability of transplantable organs to ischemia, and the condition-specific probability that circulation will spontaneously resume. Fourth, the two tests thus pick out the same ethical threshold, applied under different constraints.

Prepare me for what will come after the talk. I want a prep sheet, not a further defense of the argument. For each objection I should expect, give me: who in that room is most likely to raise it; how they would phrase it in their own vocabulary; whether it has real force; what I should concede on the spot and what I should hold; and the precise point where my reply is weakest. Order the objections by how likely they are multiplied by how much damage they could do. Make sure to include the intensivist's worry that I have made it impossible to explain why we wait a fixed interval at all, the neurologist's worry that I have collapsed a bright line into a sliding scale, the palliative care worry that my view licenses treating patients as organ resources once circulation stops, and the philosopher's worry that my distinction between a criterion and its operational tests is doing illicit work. Finally, tell me the objection I should be most afraid of, the one I will be hoping nobody asks.
