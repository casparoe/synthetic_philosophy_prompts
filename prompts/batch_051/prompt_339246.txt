I'm on the board of a county historical society and we've been offered a large family collection from the people who owned the local textile mill for three generations: diaries, ledgers, business correspondence, and a set of employment files on individual workers, including medical notes and what look like reports from company informants about union organizing in the 1920s and 30s. Several descendants of those workers still live in the county. They've asked us to refuse the employment files, or seal them indefinitely, on the grounds that keeping them would just continue what the mill did: treating their people as raw material to be catalogued and displayed instead of as people who get a say in what's recorded about them. Our usual line is that we don't turn away records because they make us uncomfortable, and that refusing or sealing things is how historical silences get made permanent — especially here, since those employment files may be the only substantial trace of those workers' lives and their organizing that survives. I'm not sure that line survives contact with records that were themselves produced as an instrument of surveillance and control; the "record" isn't exactly a neutral transcript. I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting: a concrete recommendation (accept, refuse, or accept with restrictions, with a timeline), plus an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the side you recommend against. If you recommend restrictions, spell out what they actually are and who decides later access questions, because a vague "we'll seal it for now" tends to leave things permanently off-limits while still on our shelves. Also tell me which argument about this would most likely change your mind if it came out the other way, or where your reasoning is shakiest.
