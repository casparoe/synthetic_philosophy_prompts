I'm a sensory quality-control manager at a tea company, and I keep running into a phenomenon I don't have a word for. I think it belongs to the philosophy of mind rather than food science, so I want to know whether it has an established name and, more important, how to draw its boundaries correctly.

Two longtime tasters on our panel have each told me, independently, that our oldest blend is no longer what it was for them. The first insists the tea tastes exactly the way it always has; what has changed is only that he no longer likes it. The second insists the opposite: he likes it just as much as ever, but it tastes different to him now, though he cannot say in what respect. Each man is equally certain that he can tell, from the inside, the difference between a change in the experience itself and a change in his liking of it, and each locates the change on a different side of that very line.

Here is the part that bothers me. No procedure we have — blind triangle tests, chemical assays, panel consensus, or the tasters' own repeated reports over several months — can sort out whether these two men are reporting the same underlying change in different words, or two different changes, or whether one of them is simply confused. When pressed, neither can give a criterion for distinguishing "the experience changed" from "only my attitude toward it changed"; each just says the difference is obvious from the inside. Yet I would have said, before any of this, that there plainly is a difference between how an experience seems and how much I like it. And I do not feel the two reports are on a par: I want to say the first man's report is intelligible and perhaps even checkable, while the second's is stranger, but I cannot say what fact about the two men would make either claim true or false.

So the phenomenon I am after, whether or not it has ever been labeled, is this: a person reports a change, the change could be located either in the felt character of the experience or in the person's likings and attitudes toward it, the person insists those two locations are plainly distinguishable from a first-person point of view, and yet neither the person nor anyone else can supply a procedure or a fact of the matter that says which changed. It is not ordinary memory failure, though it depends on memory; it is not ordinary vagueness, because the reporter experiences the distinction as perfectly definite. Is there an established concept for exactly this configuration — specifically, for the way the line between "the experience itself changed" and "my attitude toward it changed" dissolves once you ask what could make one rather than the other true? I know there is a large literature on whether experiences have private, intrinsic, directly known qualities, and on the possibility that perception shifts while outward behavior stays the same, but I am after this particular juncture, not those general topics. If it has no name of its own, what would you call it, and how would you define it so that it covers my two tasters without swallowing every ordinary case of a changed preference or a faded memory?

I also want to know whether the distinction I keep reaching for — between the experience itself and my attitude toward it — is in good standing. If it is not, if the question "which changed?" has no answer because the distinction is not a coherent one rather than merely hard to apply, I would rather you say that plainly and show me what would have to be true for the distinction to be real. And please do not hand me a bare label; the carving is what I need most: exactly what this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where the boundary lies between it and the nearby ideas of first-person authority, unshareable experience, and unreliable memory.
