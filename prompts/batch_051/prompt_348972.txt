Question: "In behavioural economics, the endowment effect is the finding that people typically demand more to part with an object they own than they would pay to acquire an identical object. Some theorists treat this as a bias, while others argue that it can be a legitimate preference. Critically assess this debate, drawing on the concepts of loss aversion and reference dependence."

Student's answer: "The endowment effect was famously shown in the mug experiments, in which subjects who had been given a mug required about twice as much money to sell it as subjects without a mug were willing to pay. The standard explanation, due to Tversky, is loss aversion: since losses are evaluated relative to a reference point and loom larger than corresponding gains, selling the mug counts as a loss and is therefore weighted more heavily than buying it. This must be an error, because the mug is the same object in both conditions, so the two prices should converge. The same applies to loss aversion itself: it is irrational to care more about losing £10 than about gaining £10, since the amount of money is identical. Some economists have tried to explain the endowment effect away through transaction costs and status quo bias, but these accounts only confirm that the effect is rational rather than a bias."
