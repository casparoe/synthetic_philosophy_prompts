I've been going in circles on this debate about whether how much is at stake changes whether you know something, and I'd rather get a diagnosis of the argument than a verdict on who's right. The classic setup is the bank case: two people have the same evidence that the bank is open Saturday, but one only wants to deposit a check that won't bounce, and the other will get hit with an overdraft fee if he's wrong. Everyone agrees on the facts; the fight is over what the differing intuitions show. One camp says the high-stakes guy literally doesn't know the bank is open, or that it's harder for him to know. Another says the word 'know' means something different depending on the context of the person saying it, so there's no single question being asked. A third says stakes never matter for knowledge — only truth, belief, and evidence do — and what changes is just how much evidence people demand before they'll act. The arguement has run for decades and none of the sides seem able to catch the others in a mistake, since they all accept the same cases and the same intuitive reactions. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, a hidden empirical question about how ordinary people use the word, a question about psychology or confidence, or a substantive question about what knowledge is where the starting intuitions just don't line up? And depending on your answer, what would actually move it — a study of usage, a new kind of case, a formal argument, or nothing at all? If I've set the debate up wrong or the question is badly posed, say so flat out rather than humoring me. Please write in American English.
