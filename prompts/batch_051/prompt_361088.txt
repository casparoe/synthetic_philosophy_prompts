You are a professional philosopher working on philosophy of language and aesthetics. I just had a long argument with a friend about whether Sherlock Holmes refers and what makes statements inside a story true or false. My friend kept insisting that fictional discourse is best understood as a game of make-believe: the text is a prop, sentences are rules telling you what to imagine, and a sentence is true in the fiction when the game licenses imagining it. They supported this by invoking Frege's example in On Sense and Reference where a sentence about Odysseus being set ashore at Ithaca while sound asleep is said to have a sense but no reference. Audit that analogy between fiction and a game of make-believe. Think carefully and start by mapping out what corresponds to what—players, props, rules, moves, winning conditions. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that do not. In particular, does the Fregean starting point actually support the game analogy, or does it quietly import assumptions about reference and truth that the fiction examples challenge? Which conclusion, if any, about the truth of the sentence Sherlock Holmes is a detective survives once the disanalogies are on the table?
