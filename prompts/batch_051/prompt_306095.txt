In an argument about whether to discount the welfare of future generations, an economist kept quoting the proverb "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" as if it settled the matter. Someone else objected that the proverb trades on uncertainty, not on time, and therefore cannot justify pure time preference — the idea that a benefit matters less simply because it arrives later. Separate the distinct precise claims that this proverb could be making (certainty versus risk, opportunity cost, diminishing marginal utility of consumption, brute present-bias, assumptions about the future's moral standing, and so on), mark which are platitudes, which are defensible but contestable, and which are false, and then say which reading the economist's use actually needed and whether the proverb can bear it.
