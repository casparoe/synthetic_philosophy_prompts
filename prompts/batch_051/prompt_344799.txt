I'm a PhD student working on AI alignment and the philosophy of computation, and I need to submit a two-page workshop proposal for a philosophy of AI conference. The theme is "solvability in principle" applied to alignment, and I keep going in circles. I want the proposal to ask: when people say the alignment problem is "solvable in principle," what do they actually mean by "in principle" — and does the most plausible interpretation support the technical research programs people actually pursue?

Some people seem to mean something like: there exists a finite set of reward functions or constraints such that, if an agent optimized them exactly, the agent wouldn't do anything catastrophic. Others mean something like: for any specification of human values, there is some computational proceedure that would align an agent to it given unlimited compute and a perfect world model. Others seem to mean something much weaker, like: there's no theorem showing alignment is impossible, so it's rational to keep trying. These are very different claims, and they license different research bets. I want the proposal to argue that the field hasn't been careful about distinguishing them, and that arguments over "solvability" are often people talking past each other because one side has an epistemic reading (we can eventually find the right objective) and the other has a metaphysical or computational reading (there exists an objective-independent solution procedure).

The angle I'd like to pitch: instead of asking whether alignment is solvable in principle, ask what kind of "in principle" claim could actually bear the weight people put on it in research agendas. I have a half-formed idea that the most defensible reading is something like "solvable in principle relative to a fixed value specification, but not necessarily in practice relative to the messy process of finding and agreeing on that specification." But I don't know if that's too deflationary, and I don't want to just relabel the alignment problem as a moral philosophy problem and call it a day.

Can you write the proposal? It should include: the exact question, why existing positions don't settle it, the approach that would make progress (and why a philosophical rather than purely technical project is needed), what a useful deliverable looks like for people actually doing alignment research, realistic scope for a six-month project, and what we'd say to a reviewer who asks why philosophy deserves a slot instead of another empirical alignment paper.
