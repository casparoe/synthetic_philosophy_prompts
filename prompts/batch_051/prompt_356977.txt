You're a philosopher who works on speech acts and the norms of assertion. I want you to write an actual dialogue — a real back-and-forth, not two mini-lectures seperated by turns — between two equally sharp, equally well-informed philosophers who disagree about the fundamental norm of assertion.

Call them M and T, and feel free to give them a concrete setting (a pub, a walk between buildings after a talk, whatever). M defends the knowledge norm: the rule of assertion is "assert p only if you know p." T defends the truth norm: the rule is "assert p only if p is true," and he thinks the other stuff — having decent evidence, being sincere — matters but is governed by other norms, not by assertion itself. Don't bring in the belief norm except maybe to note that they're both setting it aside.

Neither one should be a strawman. T has to actually deal with the lottery case (you shouldn't flat-out assert "your ticket lost" before the draw just because you'll probably be right), and M has to actually deal with the fact that the truth norm makes false assertion the central failure and explains why a lie or a false claim matters even when the speaker was well-placed. Let them respond directly to each other's actual points: concede, push back, recharacterize the other's example, then turn it around.

Along the way the dialogue should naturally touch on:
- the lottery case,
- Moore-type assertions like "it's raining but I don't know that it's raining,"
- Gettier-style cases where someone has a justified true belief but not knowledge,
- lucky true guesses.

Don't just have them tick off cases. At some point each character should articulate how their norm handles at least one case the other threw at them, and the other should then expose a lingering cost of that fix. Leave the debate unresolved.

Before you write the dialogue, first brainstorm internally for a few sentences about the strongest version of each position and how each would answer those four cases. Then write the dialogue as your final answer.
