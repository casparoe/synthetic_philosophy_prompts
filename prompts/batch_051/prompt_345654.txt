I'm putting together a term-paper proposal for a grad seminar on the philosophy of expertise, and the assignment is specifically to defend a question worth three months of conceptual work rather than to already know the answer. My half-formed starting point is the way people talk about "training rationality" as if it were like martial arts: you drill the moves — double crux, pre-mortems, noticing motivated reasoning, whatever — the way you'd drill a kata. But I've started to think the analogy, taken seriously, is less flattering than people assume. In martial arts nobody thinks kata alone makes you a fighter; you need live sparring, a resisting opponent, timing, and getting hit. What some gyms call "aliveness" — drilling against resistance instead of a compliant partner — is the part that's supposed to make skills transfer outside the dojo. If applied rationality training is mostly kata with no sparring, then it wouldn't be surprising if people could recite the techniques and still rationalize their way through real disagreements. The part I keep flip-flopping on is that some domains (say forecasting, or debugging code) have a built-in sparring partner because reality pushes back, but general-purpose rationality might not. So maybe the martial-arts frame is better as a diagnosis of why "trainable skill" talk overpromises, rather than as a blueprint for fixing it.
