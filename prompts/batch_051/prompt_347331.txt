Bit of a niche one: I'm a philosophy PhD student working on Anscombe's ethics of war, and I keep coming back to her old protest against Oxford's plan to give Truman an honorary degree — she held that it was wrong to honour a man who, in her view, had deliberately killed innocent noncombatants as a means of ending the war, and that no good outcome could make that choice permissible. My supervisor keeps telling me to stop re-litigating 1945 and to connect her view to something she never lived to see, and honestly I'm a bit stuck. So here's the contemporary thing I want to think through. Take semi-autonomous lethal systems: a loitering drone builds a pattern-of-life profile of a suspected militant, flags him as a target, and fires unless a human operator hits a veto within, say, ten seconds using a grainy video feed. The defence of these systems is that they're more discriminating than a tired human and that there's still a human in the loop. What would Anscombe have made of this? Specifically, would she locate the relevant intention in the human veto, or in the standing decision to deploy a system known in advance to produce a certain rate and kind of collateral killing? And would she see it as morally better, worse, or just the same species of thing as the Truman case?

I'm less intersted in the policy answer than in getting her concepts right — intention, the innocence of noncombatants, the authority of the state to kill, and whether the good-consequences defence is doing any work at all.
