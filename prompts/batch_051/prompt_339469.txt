i'm grading a take-home short answer for a philosophy of law unit and want a second opinion, since i dont want to anchor you on my own view of it. Below is the question I set and one student's answer. Grade it out of 10 and give me the comments that justify that grade to the student: go point by point, say what they got right, where they go wrong, and any conflation you spot.

Question:

A legislature passes a new offense: it is a strict liability offense to "operate a food truck without a valid permit," punishable by a fine of up to $2,000. Maria has run a taco truck for years with a valid permit. When it expires, the agency's renewal reminder goes to an old address and she never learns it lapsed. She is charged after a routine inspection. Maria says she cannot be guilty because she didn't know the permit had expired. In about 250 words: (i) say what the prosecution must prove if the offense is strict liability, and whether Maria's ignorance helps her; (ii) give the strongest rationale for dispensing with mens rea here; and (iii) state the most serious objection to convicting her anyway.

Student answer:

"Since the offense is strict liability, the prosecution doesn't need to prove Maria knew the permit had lapsed, or intended to operate without it. It just has to prove the actus reus: that she operated the truck and the permit wasn't valid at the time. Her ignorance is therefore no defense to guilt. The rationale for dispensing with mens rea is deterrence and administrative efficiency: if every operator could escape by claiming they never got the renewal notice, the permit system would be hard to enforce, and people would have little incentive to track their own expiration dates. This is a regulatory or public-welfare offense, not a real crime like assault, and the penalty is only a fine, so dropping mens rea is fine; strict liability is already accepted in civil cases anyway. Convicting her is just. Maria could have checked her permits expiry date herself, and a modest fine is what keeps the permit system workable. The remaining objection is that she'd be punished for something she didn't choose to do, but retributivism only requires fault for true crimes, so in a minor regulatory matter like this the objection doesn't have much force."
