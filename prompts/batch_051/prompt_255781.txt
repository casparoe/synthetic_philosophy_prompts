I was talking to my roommate about consciousness last night, and he started explaining Dennett's multiple drafts model to me, since I'd only ever heard people make fun of it. His summary went something like this: "Dennett proved that consciousness is an illusion. The brain is just a bunch of parallel streams of processing fighting it out, and there's no central place where it all comes together—no audience, no self watching the show, no theater where experience actually happens. The winning draft gets reported as if someone experienced it, but nobody really does. So qualia, the 'what it's like' of experience, don't exist, and our sense of having a rich unified inner life is basically a trick of language. And since the hard problem was built on the idea that there's something real to explain, Dennett dissolves the hard problem rather than solving it." This struck me as off, because I thought Dennett was attacking the Cartesian theater specifically, not claiming that consciousness isn't real in any sense. But my roommate insists that's what he says, just phrased more agressively. How fair is this as a summary? I'd like to know, sentence by sentence, wich parts a careful Dennett reader would accept, which parts they'd call overstatements or conflations of two different claims, and whether the bit about the hard problem actually follows from the parts that are right.
