I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working in formal epistemology and the foundations of game theory, and this term I'm TA-ing an upper-level undergraduate seminar. The students have already worked through normal-form games and Nash equilibrium, but they have not yet seen extensive-form games or subgame perfection. In a few weeks I have a 75-minute section with roughly two dozen students, and I want to run a structured in-class game that makes them feel the force of the backward induction argument before anyone names it, so they encounter the problem in their own choices rather than in a summary. The game should be built around a Centipede-like structure: two players alternate between taking a growing pot and passing it on, the pot increases each time it passes, and the game ends for certain after a fixed number of moves. Please give me concrete rules, a payoff scheme that works without real money, a workable way to pair students and keep records across rounds, and a timeline for the 75 minutes. The sequencing matters most: in the first round students should play move-by-move against a live partner and feel both the temptation to pass and the reasoning that says a rational player should stop at the very first move. Then I would like one or two variations, for example playing against a precommitted strategy, comparing a publicly known endpoint with an unknown one, or allowing a brief pre-play message, designed so the class can identify which feature of the standard model is carrying the conclusion.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the assumptions behind backward induction: common knowledge of rationality, a finite and commonly known horizon, purely self-interested preferences over the game's payoffs, and no reputational or moral concern about the other player. It should also explain why those assumptions jointly force the seemingly absurd early-stop result, and why the pull to pass is not simply a mistake but a signal about which assumption deserves scrutiny. Please be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as students coordinating to split the pot, treating the game as a test of trust, not caring about small stakes, or playing in a deliberately unserious way, and how to correct for each without lecturing the point away. Finally, be frank about which feature of the theoretical problem a classroom version cannot reproduce, so I know what I will need to supply in the debrief myself.
