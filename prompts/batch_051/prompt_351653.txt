I recently became chair of my city's rent board, and after my first few hearings I have become increasingly uneasy about how rights-talk operates in the room: each side announces a right and then treats the discussion as closed. I will soon write a position paper for my fellow commissioners, and I want a rigorous argument behind what I have begun to think.

Please make the best case you can for the following position. The concept of a right should be retained, but it should be severed from the two interpretations that dominate public argument. Rights are not trumps that automatically defeat ordinary considerations of welfare, cost, and administrative feasibility; nor are they side-constraints that bind regardless of consequences. Rights are instruments—deliberately stiff decision rules built to correct predictable patterns of error in high-stakes, self-interested, in-the-moment reasoning. Their justification is instrumental, but their correct operation often requires treating them as if they were trumps within the proceedings they govern: their rigidity is a feature of the decision procedure, not a claim about the underlying moral furniture. Please also address, explicitly, the strongest objection: that this account merely relocates the absolute character of rights one level down and therefore does no real work when rights conflict.

After the argument, provide a table titled 'Verdicts on Sample Cases.' The table should have four columns and one row for each of the three cases below:

- 'case': a short label for the case.
- 'classification': one or two words stating whether, under your account, the claimed right should operate as a trump, a side-constraint, or an instrument.
- 'ruling': whether the right-claim should be sustained, limited, or denied.
- 'rationale': one sentence justifying the classification and ruling.

The cases:
(a) A tenant family has lived in a rent-controlled unit for eighteen years and refuses a generous buyout, claiming a right to remain; the owner claims a right to regain possession under a lawful no-fault eviction statute.
(b) During a ventilator shortage, a patient claims a right to the next available intensive-care bed by virtue of equal citizenship; the hospital claims a right of clinical discretion over triage.
(c) A student group claims a right to invite a speaker whose announced lecture is false and inflammatory but not inciting; the university president claims a right to preserve campus order.
