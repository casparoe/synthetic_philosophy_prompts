I'm giving a talk on Hume's *Of Suicide* and I keep coming back to this passage: "The providence of the deity appears not immediately in any operation, but governs every thing by those general and immutable laws, which have been established from the beginning of time." A friend says this passage plus the next few paragraphs licenses an argument that proves way too much, and I cant find the exit. He sets it out like this. (1) If God governs everything by general laws and never intervenes in particular operations, then any act done by human faculties, including suicide, is as much God's action as a branch falling. (2) So no act can be wrong because it "disturbs God's plan" or meddles with providence; that langauge is empty. (3) If suicide can't be wrong on that ground, and the only remaining grounds are harm to neighbours and harm to oneself, then morality collapses into a cost-benefit account of harm to people. (4) Then killing a solitary hermit who wants to die anyway, or killing someone painlessly in their sleep, would be acceptable as long as no one else is around to grieve. He says Hume has to either bite that or abandon the suicide argument. I think the slide is between (2) and (3) but I can't name it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
