I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether "national character" picks out anything real, and I've realized I don't have a stable view; I just have a pile of examples and I sort them differently depending on who I argued with last. I want you to force me into one position, as much as the cases allow.

Play the part of a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of philosophy of social science, the semantics of generic statements ("X's do Y"), and maybe a bit of moral psychology. Think through this carefully. Before you settle on anything, sketch two or three candidate definitions of national character and say what pushes you toward one. Steelman the deflationary view — that the term is just a polite word for stereotype — before you reject or accept it.

The deliverable: a working definition of *national character*, or, if you conclude the term is unsalvageable, a working definition of a *warranted cultural generalization about a people*. I want a definition that separates the epistemic question (is this kind of group-level claim accurate, and in what way?) from the moral question (does making it wrong anyone?), because I suspect a lot of arguments collapse the two.

Here are my cases:

1. A demographic fact: Japan's population is one of the oldest in the world. Nobody would call this national character; it's clearly not in the category, but I want to hear what property keeps it out.
2. The antisemitic canard that Jews are good with money. This is national-character-shaped and clearly unacceptable. Does it fail on the epistemic side, the moral side, or both? Whatever definition I end up with has to say.
3. A geographer saying Mediterranean cultures are a "contact culture": people stand closer and touch more when they speak. Borderline for me — behavioral, maybe true on average, but it runs on very similar machinery to (2).
4. A historian writing that eighteenth-century British political culture was deeply suspicious of standing armies, where by that she means a pattern of shared assumptions visible in speeches, pamphlets, and institutional arrangements, not a claim about the deep psychology of any individual Englishman. Is this national character, something else, or something adjacent enough that my definition should cover it?
5. A cross-cultural psychologist reporting that countries differ substantially and stably over decades on average self-reported individualism. Under the hood it's just mean differences on a questionnaire, but its published with the implication that it captures something about mentality. I want the definition to force this out into the open and say whether it counts.
6. The tourist claim that the English love queuing. This is the most paradigmatic national-character claim on my list, even though I'm not sure it's true. I half believe it in London, and half don't when I remember it's also a story the English tell about themselves.
7. A manager who says of a Nordic colleague, "he won't say he's angry — Nordic people don't do confrontation," and infers something about an individual from a coarse group average. I think the wrong-making feature lives here, but I can't say what it is without banning all inductive inference.
8. A friend explaining his family's pattern of avoiding conflict by saying "well, we're an English family." Clearly not national character, because it's a family rather than a nation; I'd still like the definition to explain why it's the same kind of move and where exactly scale changes the verdict.

Run the definition through all eight cases. Flag the ones where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, then revise, or else defend the surprising verdict. If you end up saying "national character is hopeless but there's a nearby notion worth keeping," fine — defend that, but don't leave me with "it depends" as the whole answer.
