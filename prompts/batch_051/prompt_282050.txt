I'm a graduate student in philosophy of cognitive science, and the causal exclusion argument has started to feel less like a technical puzzle and more like a threat to the way I think about my own agency. We reconstructed it in a seminar on mental causation, and the conclusion seems to prove too much: it would make deliberating, deciding, and intending causally idle, which would leave me unable to say why my reasons are ever relevant to what I do. Yet each premise, taken on its own, looks nearly impossible to deny.

Here is the argument as I would set it out. (1) The physical domain is causally closed: every physical event that has a cause has a complete physical cause. In particular, my arm's rising has a complete physical cause in the form of neural and muscular events. (2) My decision to raise my arm causes my arm to rise; mental causation is real, and rejecting it lands me in epiphenomenalism. (3) My decision is not identical to any physical event; as a non-reductive physicalist, I take it to be realized by a neural event but distinct from it. (4) But if the arm movement already has a complete physical cause, then a distinct mental event cannot be an additional cause of that same movement, unless the movement is overdetermined. This is the exclusion step. (5) Such overdetermination would be systematic, occurring for every mentally caused action, and that is nothing like the rare case of two bullets striking a target at once. (6) Therefore, so long as we retain (1), (4), and (5), either my decision is identical with the physical cause after all, or my decision does not genuinely cause my arm to rise. The non-reductive position I wanted seems squeezed out.

I find (6) unacceptable, but I can see that each way of resisting it costs me something I do not want to give up, and I suspect I am missing the exact point at which the argument could be resisted. Please think as a professional philosopher of mind who works on causation and explanation. Before committing yourself, brainstorm the main escape routes and say what each route would have to establish to count as more than a label for a problem. Then assess the argument step by step: which premise is false, or at which step does the inference slide from something true to something stronger? If the argument is essentially valid and the real question is which premise to surrender, tell me which surrender is least costly and what I would be committed to thereafter. I would most like to hold on to (2) and (3) together, but I want an honest price list rather than a defense of what I already believe.
