I sit on the risk-communication committee of a national public-health agency. The scientific staff have drafted copy for this year's rainy-season malaria campaign that opens with the sentence "Mosquitoes carry malaria." At last week's meeting, an epidemiologist objected that local surveillance puts the parasite in well under one percent of mosquitoes even in the highest-transmission district, so the sentence is, on its face, false. The communications lead replied that the wording she prefers, "Fewer than one mosquito in a hundred carries malaria," will be heard by the public as "Most mosquitoes are harmless," and that the generic sentence does different work: it states something about the standing risk from the kind of animal, not a statistic about the next bite, and it is exactly what moves people to sleep under treated nets. I've been asked to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's vote.

I suspect the real disagreement behind the two positions is about what a generic sentence says and commits a speaker to, and about whether a public-health agency should be asserting truths about kinds or statistical frequencies. The memo should get far enough into that issue to justify the decision it recommends rather than picking a formulation on stylistic grounds. Please recommend a concrete wording for the campaign opener, defend it, and engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly. I'd also like the memo to end with a short note on which evidence or argument would be most likely to overturn the recommendation.
