In my epistemology seminar we've been working through the KK principle and the claim that at least some mental conditions are luminous: when they obtain, the subject is in a position to know that they obtain. A classmate keeps defending this with the analogy that one's own mind is like a well-lit room — unlike the external world, where something can be present without our noticing it, the mental room has no dark corners, so whatever is in it is automatically visible to the subject. That image is doing real work, because it makes second-order knowledge look costless. I'd like to know how far the analogy actually holds. Could you work out the correspondence first — what maps to what (the room, the light, the objects in the room, the act of seeing, the dark corners) — and then identify where the mapping breaks down, for instance with dim or peripheral states, a headache that fades imperceptibly, or beliefs the subject has never articulated to themselves? I want to know which disanalogies are merely cosmetic and which matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to draw, and whether the KK principle, at least for mental states, survives the breakdown of the well-lit-room picture or was relying on it all along. If there is a better analogy in the vicinity, I'd like to hear that too.
