I work in ML and keep chewing on a paralell between feature-attribution methods and theories of desert in distributive justice, and I can't tell if it's deep or just pattern-matching. In cooperative game theory the Shapley value is the unique way to split a coalition's total payoff so that: the shares sum to the total (efficiency), players who make the same marginal contribution to every possible coalition get the same share (symmetry), and a null player who never changes the payoff gets nothing (null player). In explainable AI you treat a model's output as the payoff and each feature as a player, and a feature's Shapley value is called its contribution to the prediction. That feels structurally identical to what a meritocratic desert theorist wants: several people produce a joint product (a startup, a team, a society's output), and each should receive what they actually contributed, with zero contribution deserving zero, equal contributions deserving equal, and the shares exhausting the product. So: is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? Here are the spots where I suspect it breaks. Desert is usually about effort or deliberate merit, not counterfactual marginal product: the only surgeon in town can have enormous marginal product through luck, and a hard worker can have zero marginal product because she's interchangeble. Also Shapley values require a real-valued worth for every subset of players, but in social cases we don't know what any arbitrary subgroup would have produced, and the answer is often not even well-defined. But maybe that just shows desert theory needs an explicit counterfactual account of what subgroups would have produced, and Shapley supplies the right machinery once you give it that. I want you to actually check the structure, not shrug it off.
