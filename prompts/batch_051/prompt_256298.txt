I'm a novelist, and I run a monthly worldbuilding group for other speculative-fiction writers — usually eight to twelve people, two hours, wine and arguments. Next month I want to build a world around a universal karma economy: every transaction, conversation, even small kindnesses move your public score, and that score decides what you can rent, who'll speak to you, where you're allowed to stand. I've read enough about old eBay feedback, forum karma, and social credit to know the obvious beats — rating inflation, retaliatory downvotes, sybil accounts, everyone fake-nice in public. But I want my writers to feel all that from the inside, not hear me lecture about mechanics.

So: design a game or structured activity for that session that makes a small group actually run a tiny reputation economy among themselves. Keep the rules explainable in ten minutes. I want the trouble to emerge from incentives, not from me saying "now game the system." Maybe they trade small favors or tokens and can rate each other, and ratings unlock capacities; maybe they have to cooperate on some small collective project while managing their scores. Use real (small) stakes if it helps — chocolate, tokens, seating, the good chair by the window. By the end, before any debrief, they should have generated at least one of these on their own: rating inflation, a smear campaign, a sybil-style trick, an unfairly high score, an unfairly low score, or the queasy discovery that the score has stopped tracking real trustworthiness. Then give me a debrief that maps whatever actually happened onto the conceptual machinery — reputation as a signal versus reputation as currency, Goodhart's law applied to a legible social metric, adversaries gaming the system, the difference between local gossip and a global public scalar. Be honest about what the game cannot reproduce: real long-term stakes, private knowledge, the coldness of being scored by an institution. Tell me what to prepare, how long each phase takes, and what to do when the room goes flat and everyone just hands everyone fives.
