For a political philosophy unit I'm putting together, I want something funny rather than explain-y. Write a parody of a U.S. federal informed-consent form — title it something like "Designated Survivor Notification, Waiver, and Consent" (Form DS-1) — for the person picked to skip the State of the Union and wait in a secure offsite location as the designated survivor. Keep it deadpan and bureaucratic, around 700 words, set out as numbered clauses plus a signature block.

The humor has to come from the actual ethics, not from made-up absurdities: the person hasn't really consented in any robust sense (they're a subordinate being handed an "honor," and getting into the motorcade isn't the same as accepting the risk), they could become president without ever being elected or vetted for the job, the form dresses up being a spare body in the line of succession as a solemn patriotic duty, and the whole ritual is partly theatre pretending to be deep constitutional seriousness. It should be legible to a reader who has never heard of the practice, but a political philosopher should recognize the real objections landing — legitimacy, meaningful consent, aptitude for high office, and the gap between continuity of government and democratic authorization. Don't name any actual officeholders; keep it a generic "you." End with a one-sentence summary as the last line, even though there's no single verdict to extract.
