**Question.** In the *Consolation of Philosophy*, Boethius confronts the apparent conflict between God's foreknowledge and the freedom of human action. Explain how Boethius presents the problem, set out the main elements of his solution (his account of eternity, the way God knows temporal things, and his distinction between kinds of necessity), and then assess whether the solution succeeds, answering what you take to be the strongest objection to it.

**Student's answer.** Boethius's problem is that if God infallibly foreknows all future things, then every future action is already known before it happens and is therefore fixed in advance: the agent could not do other than God has foreknown, because that would falsify God's knowledge. This would destroy free will and with it the intelligibility of praise, blame, and moral effort. Boethius answers along two lines. First, he distinguishes simple necessity from conditional necessity. Simple necessity belongs to a thing in virtue of its own nature, as when a human being is mortal. Conditional necessity belongs to a proposition merely in virtue of its being known: if someone sees me walking, then it is necessary that I am walking, but only on the condition of the seeing, not in my own nature. Boethius holds that divine knowledge imposes only conditional necessity, so a future action, considered in its own nature, need not be necessary. Second, he explains eternity. Eternity is not endless duration through time but the complete possession of an unlimited life all at once. Being eternal in this sense, God does not know future things as future: all times are present to him at once, as a spectator on a hill sees a whole road in a single glance while travelers below pass along it bit by bit. God's knowledge therefore meets each action at its own time as present rather than ahead of time, so the original worry that foresight fixes things in advance cannot get started. In assessing the solution, the strongest objection is that even timeless knowledge still seems to fix what I will do: if God timelessly sees my action, then at the time I act I could not do otherwise, since that would make the content of God's knowledge false. But this objection confuses knowing with causing. The spectator on the hill sees the travelers without pushing them, and in the same way God's knowledge merely registers what I freely choose; it does not make me choose it. Since God's knowledge is not a cause of my action, my action remains undetermined. And because the future is not yet fixed — there is no fact of the matter about what I will do until my choice is made and becomes present to God — there is no unalterable fact ahead of me that constrains my choice. Boethius therefore succeeds.
