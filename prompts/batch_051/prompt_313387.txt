I've been chewing on an argument about the anti-akrasia principle (roughly: don't believe P while also thinking your evidence doesn't support P). It's supposed to show the principle is either unsatisfiable by a finite mind or it permits the very thing it bans, and I can't tell whether the reasoning is sound or where exactly it goes off the rails. Here's the argument:

(1) To follow the anti-akrasia principle, you need some view about whether your evidence supports P.
(2) But that view is itself a belief, so the principle applies to it too: you should hold it only if your evidence supports the claim that your evidence supports P.
(3) So you need another view about whether your evidence supports that higher-order claim, and then another, and so on.
(4) Either this regress goes on forever, which no finite mind can complete, or at some point you just take an evidential judgment at face value.
(5) If it's permissible to take an evidential judgment at face value somewhere up the chain, then it should be permissible to take the original first-order judgment at face value too.
(6) Taking the original first-order judgment at face value is exactly what the epistemically akratic agent does.

So the principle against epistemic akrasia is either impossible for a finite mind to satisfy, or it permits epistemic akrasia after all. Is the argument valid? If it isn't, identify the step that fails, and say whether that step can be repaired by reading one of the terms differently.
