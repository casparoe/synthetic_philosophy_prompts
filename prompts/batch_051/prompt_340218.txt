I'm giving a seminar in three weeks to a formal epistemology workshop, and what I need is a Q&A prep sheet rather than more slides. The thesis I'm defending is that Aumann's agreement theorem has been systematically overread in rationalist and informal Bayesian circles. The popular moral drawn from it — that agents with common knowledge of each other's credences cannot rationally agree to disagree, so persistent real-world disagreement is evidence that someone is irrational — gets only a thin guarantee from the mathematics. The theorem's conclusion, I argue, is almost entirely carried by the common-prior and common-knowledge conditions, and those conditions are typically either false or unverifiable in the messy disagreements people actually care about. I used to deploy the theorem the way many rationalist-blog readers do, as a cudgel against entrenched disagreement, but the more I worked through the proof the more it looked like a consistency result about a tightly specified model, not a license to treat ordinary disagreement as a rationality failure.

The audience is a philosophy department workshop in formal social epistemology. Half the room are formal epistemologists who know the theorem and its proof cold; there is one game theorist who works on interactive epistemology; two Bayesian statisticians; and a cluster of graduate students who mainly know the theorem through rationalist-blog discussion and are sympathetic to using it as a practical norm. The epistemologists are likely to think I am attacking a strawman unless I can name exactly who is doing the overreading and what they do with the theorem. The game theorist will care about what happens to the rest of the common-prior edifice — Harsanyi doctrine, no-trade results, mechanism design — if my argument succeeds. The graduate students will defend the practical norm even if they concede the formal result is conditional.

Please draft my prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they are likely to come, phrased the way members of this specific audience would put them, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Mark which objections have real force and which I can handle with a distinction. Include the two or three things I should concede up front to avoid being pecked apart during setup, and the objection I am hoping nobody asks. End with a one-sentence summary of the deepest vulnerability in my position, beginning 'Bottom line:'.
