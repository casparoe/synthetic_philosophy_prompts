I am preparing a discussion for a reading group on Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and I want to read the novel through the question of community and belonging as goods: what, if anything, is lost when a community dissolves? Treat Stevens's lifelong loyalty to Lord Darlington and to the world of the great house as the novel's central case of belonging, belonging to a community organized around service, deference, and an ideal of professional dignity. Does the novel regard the passing of that world as a genuine loss, or does it ultimately expose Stevens's sense of belonging as self-deception, purchased at the cost of his relationship with Miss Kenton, his response to his dying father, and his capacity for independent moral judgment? I would like a reading that takes both possibilities seriously. Where does the novel show something irreplaceable being lost when such a community dissolves, and where does it resist the idea that this loss should be mourned? Finally, what do you think the novel contributes to the philosophical debate about whether belonging is a constituent good that individual liberty cannot replace?
