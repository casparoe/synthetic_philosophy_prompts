I have been reading philosophical arguments on my own for a few years, and one recurring pattern has begun to bother me. An author will show that a view generates an infinite regress and will treat that as fatal, as though showing that the chain never ends is enough to refute the view. Yet other never-ending chains are treated as perfectly harmless: the sequence of natural numbers, the truth regress in which a claim is true and then it is true that the claim is true and so on without end, and an infinite past in which each moment is preceded by another are all things many people accept without alarm. So the fact that a chain does not terminate cannot by itself be what is wrong. The standard distinction between a vicious and a benign regress names the problem without solving it.

I run a small independent reading group, and we are applying for a modest grant to support a six-month project. I would like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a one-page proposal. The question we want to work on is this: what exactly makes an infinite regress vicious rather than benign? My rough idea is that a regress is never vicious in isolation; it becomes vicious or benign only relative to the demand it was introduced to answer. A chain that merely postpones the very thing it was supposed to supply, such as an explanation, a justification, a definition, or a ground, is vicious, while a chain with the same formal structure that is not being asked to supply that thing is benign. I would like the proposal to state the question precisely; explain why the obvious tests, such as asking whether the chain terminates, whether each step is self-referential, or whether it demands infinitely many steps, get the verdict wrong in identifiable cases; sketch an approach that begins by sorting regresses according to the explanatory or justificatory work they are asked to do and then tests whether the same chain really does change status when the work changes; say why this approach is better than searching for a purely structural feature that separates bad regresses from good ones; describe what a useful result would look like for someone trying to tell a sound regress argument from a bad one; and state what would count as failure. Keep the project realistic for six months of part-time work by people without academic positions, and do not include a bibliography, since I will supply the scholarly references myself.
