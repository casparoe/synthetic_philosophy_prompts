Question. Let P_t be a rational agent's credence function at t. (a) State van Fraassen's Reflection principle and explain how it differs from conditionalization. (b) Outline the standard diachronic Dutch strategy offered in support of the principle, and say what kind of incoherence, if any, it is supposed to exhibit. (c) Evaluate the following claim: "Since a rational agent updates by conditionalization, she already satisfies Reflection; conditionalization alone secures it, whatever she expects about her own future reliability."

Student answer. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says, in its expectation form, that an agent's current credence in a proposition should equal her current expectation of her future credence in that proposition: P_t(A) = E_t[P_{t+1}(A)]. The stronger conditional form adds that P_t(A | P_{t+1}(A) = r) = r whenever this is defined. The principle is not simply a rule for changing belief when evidence arrives; that is conditionalization, which says P_{t+1}(A) = P_t(A | E), where E is the total evidence gained between t and t+1. Reflection is instead a constraint on what one may now believe about what one will later believe. But the two are very closely linked, and Reflection is really conditionalization seen from the earlier time. Once an agent has adopted conditionalization, the law of total probability gives P_t(A) = Σ_E P_t(E) P_t(A | E), and each term P_t(A | E) is the future credence in A on the branch where E is learned. Thus the expected future credence equals the current credence automatically. So the claim in (c) is correct: a conditionalizer satisfies Reflection no matter what she expects about her own future reliability, because the arithmetic does not depend on that. The Dutch strategy argument supports this. Suppose the agent violates Reflection—for example, she is certain now that P_{t+1}(A) will be higher than P_t(A). A bookie who knows her present and future credences can buy a unit ticket on A from her now at the lower current price and sell that ticket back to her later at the higher future price. The agent loses the difference with certainty, and the bookie gains it, whatever the truth value of A. This shows that a violator of Reflection is synchronically incoherent in just the way a violator of the probability axioms is: she holds an inconsistent set of beliefs at a single time, and that is why the sure loss arises. Reflection, then, extends the standard synchronic Dutch book theorem for probabilism across time.
