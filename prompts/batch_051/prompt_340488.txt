Okay so last night my friend and I ended up in one of those three-hour arguments about qualia, and by the end I'd basically came around to his side. The view is illusionism about phenomenal consciousness: there isn't really a private, intrinsic, ineffable what-it's-like-ness over and above what a mental state represents and does. When I look at a red apple, the reddish feel seems like an extra residue, but on this view that residue is a kind of introspective illusion thrown off by the representational machinery. Consciousness is real, it says, but phenomenal consciousness in the thick sense is not. My friend is annoyingly persuasive, and I like that the view makes consciousness scientifically tractable instead of a spooky add-on.

But I don't want to have been talked into something just because its elegant. What would actually count against illusionism? I don't only mean the usual stuff: pain feels too immediate and raw to be an illusion, Mary the color scientist learns something new, zombies are conceivable, the hard problem won't go away. I want to know which arguments, thought experiments, empirical findings, or plain ordinary intuitions should genuinely worry an illusionist, and which are just bullets the view bites. If you had to rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, what would the list look like? Do committed illusionists, when they're being honest, concede that some of these count against them, or do they qualify the view until it can't be tested? And if they do insulate it, is that a problem for this kind of position, or is it fine for a philosophical view to be protected from empirical refutation?

Please be explicit about how confident you are in what you say, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure about. I'd rather have a messy honest ranking than a tidy confident one.
