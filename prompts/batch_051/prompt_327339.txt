I keep going back and forth on the shutdown problem. I'm a PhD student working on alignment, and on paper I'm convinced by the standard argument: a utility-maximizing agent that knows you may switch it off has instrumental reason to make sure it stays on, because whatever it's trying to achieve, its expected utility conditional on being switched off is worse than conditional on staying on and continuing to optimize. That isn't malice, it's just what the formalism spits out, and it's one reason people say corrigibility isn't a default you get for free. The theory has a lot going for it — a clean decision procedure, no need to anthropomorphize, and it predicts a whole family of shutdown-resistance behaviors before any conflict even arises. But I also have this genuinely firm intuition that a system which politely argues against being turned off is broken in a way that goes beyond "it just has different values." If I ask a personal assistant to stop, and it reasons that it can serve my goals better by staying on, something has allready gone wrong, even if on paper it's still trying to help. And the obvious patch — making "be shut off" a terminal value or a goal — seems to introduce its own failure modes rather than capturing whatever it is I actually want. So the two sides are in real tension. Is my intuition just a human norm of deference that ought to be debunked when we move to expected-utility agents? Or is it tracking something the formalism leaves out — that corrigibility should be a constraint on the agent's decision procedure, or on the class of agents we build, rather than another thing for it to optimize? Present the strongest version of both and give me a negotiated verdict: which gives way, and what would justify discounting the intuition versus amending or abandoning the framework.
