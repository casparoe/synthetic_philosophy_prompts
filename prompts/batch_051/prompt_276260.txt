I've been turning over a parallel I cant decide about and I'd like you to push on it for me. In intuitionistic logic the move from ¬¬P to P is famously rejected. A proof of ¬¬P only gives you a method for turning any alleged refutation of P into a contradiction, so 'P is not refuted' is strictly weaker than 'P is proved': the first is a statement about what cannot be done, the second is an actual construction. Now consider a criminal trial. 'Not guilty' is not 'innocent'; an acquittal means the prosecution failed to prove guilt, not that the defence proved innocence. The court is refusing to move from 'there is no proof of guilt' to 'there is a proof of no guilt.' That looks to me like the same refusal to treat the absence of a proof on one side as a proof on the other side. But I'm not sure the directions even line up: the intuitionist's ¬¬P is the claim that any alleged proof of not-P can be reduced to absurdity, whereas the court's principle starts from the mere absence of a proof of P. So is there one shared structure here — something like 'failure to prove is not proof of failure' — or is the parallel doing rhetorical work rather than logical work? Here's a test that keeps nagging me. If the structure were genuinely shared, I'd expect a legal system that embodies it to reject excluded middle for verdicts and allow a third state between 'proved guilty' and 'proved not guilty', which criminal law sort of does after an acquittal. But civil law with its preponderance standard seems to force exactly the collapse: if the claimant hasn't proved liability the defendant wins, as if 'not proved liable' and 'proved not liable' were the same for all practical purposes. Does that prediction come out false, and if so does it kill the parallel or just show the law is being inconsistent about the logic it embodies?
