Exam question:

Explain the two-factor account of monothematic delusions, using Capgras delusion as your example. Then evaluate the claim that delusions are not beliefs because delusional patients often fail to act on their delusions. In your answer, keep separate the question of what causes a delusion from the question of whether the resulting state should be classified as a belief.

Student's answer:

The two-factor account holds that a monothematic delusion such as the Capgras delusion requires two independent factors. The first factor is an anomalous experience: the patient sees someone who looks exactly like their spouse but lacks the normal feeling of familiarity, so the spouse appears as a stranger wearing the spouse's face. The second factor is a deficit in hypothesis evaluation or belief revision, which leads the patient to accept the explanation that the person is an impostor rather than the alternative explanation, namely that something has gone wrong with their own responses. This second factor is important because it explains why not everyone with the same anomalous experience goes on to develop the delusion. It also shows that the delusion is produced by a combination of an experience and a defective inference. Because the resulting claim is both absurd and badly supported, it is irrational; and because it is irrational, a delusion is not the sort of state that can be true or false, but is better classified as an imagining or an affective response. A further reason for denying that delusions are beliefs is that Capgras patients often do not act as though the claim were true: they may continue living with the alleged impostor and do not call the police. This failure to act shows that they do not really accept the claim, because anyone who genuinely believed it would act on it. I conclude that delusions are not beliefs, because they fail the behavioural test for belief.
