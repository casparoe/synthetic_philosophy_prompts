Most Anglophone jurisdictions require that a child's birth be registered with a surname and one or more given names. The surname is, by default, inherited from one or both parents — for much of the common-law world, historically, the father's surname — and it tends to remain fixed for life: it is the name under which a person is enrolled in school, taxed, cited in academic work, and indexed in databases. The given name, by contrast, is chosen at birth, can be changed with relative ease, and often carries a personal or familial intention, such as naming a child after a relative or an admired figure. Meanwhile, Spanish-speaking countries routinely give a child two family names, one from each parent, and Iceland typically derives a patronymic rather than passing down an inherited surname. I want to understand what this cluster of practices presupposes about what a proper name is and how it refers to a person.

Formalize the question this way: Does the inherited-surname-plus-chosen-given-name system presuppose a substantive theory of reference? For example, does it treat the surname as something like a Millian tag whose reference is fixed by an inherited chain of transmission, while treating the given name as more like a description or a freely chosen label? Or is the system better understood as presupposing only an administrative need — a stable, inheritable identifier — that is compatible with several rival theories of names? Press on what seems puzzling: administrative practice tends to treat the surname as the stable public identifier and to sort, file, and cite people by it, and it makes the surname harder to change than the given name, even though both are proper names for the very same person. Is there a real inconsistency here, or does it dissolve once semantic reference is distinguished from legal identification? And where exactly does the practice stop determining the philosophical question, so that a Millian, a descriptivist, and a causal-historical theorist could all accept it for different reasons?

Write a formal analysis that (1) reconstructs the implicit theory or theories of proper names and reference that the practice strictly presupposes, (2) separates what is strictly presupposed from what merely fits well with the practice, and (3) states where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then, for each of three rival views — (a) a Millian view on which every proper name is a bare tag with no descriptive content, (b) a descriptivist view on which a name's contribution is given by an associated cluster of descriptions, and (c) a causal-historical view on which reference is fixed by an initial dubbing and transmitted along a causal chain of uses — say what would have to change in civil registration, academic citation, and database design if that view were adopted outright.
