I am a third-year undergraduate in a course on the epistemology of logic, and I have been asked to lead a thirty-minute session with our ten-person seminar on what Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" shows about the justification of deduction. Rather than lecture or read the dialogue aloud, I want the class to act it out in a way that forces them to generate the regress themselves. Please design a structured role-play or game, using only index cards and a whiteboard, in which one student plays the Tortoise and the rest collectively play Achilles; the Tortoise's standing rule should be to grant any conditional premise the group offers but never to make the step to the conclusion unless a further conditional is granted, so that the group runs into the endless demand before anyone names it. The aim is for them to see in their own moves that a rule of inference is not a hidden premise and that adding premises cannot manufacture the act of inferring. Give exact rules, a script or constraints for the Tortoise, a thirty-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each stage of the game onto Carroll's dialogue, including the point where participants mistakenly think one more premise will do the work. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run, and be honest about which aspect of the distinction between accepting a proposition and following a rule the activity cannot reproduce for them.
