I teach a graduate seminar on collective decision-making, and the futarchy session always ends in the same standoff. The enthusiasts say: let a legislature vote on a metric for national welfare, then have prediction markets bet on which policy will maximize that metric—this should aggregate dispersed information better than committees, experts, or elections ever could. The skeptics say this treats market prices as if they were truth, and that the scheme smuggles a deeply value-laden choice (what counts as welfare in the first place) into a mechanism that looks merely technical. This has been running for a couple decades now and nobody seems to move. My working hypothesis is that what looks like an empirical dispute about whether conditional markets actually forecast best is really a normative dispute about what makes a decision procedure legitimate, with a smaller empirical dispute about market efficiency folded into it. I'm not asking whether futarchy is a good idea—I'm asking why the debate has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is. Put on your philosopher-of-social-epistemology hat, start by brainstorming the strongest versions of both sides, and then diagnose: is it substantive, verbal, partly empirical, a clash of starting intuitions, or a mix? Be concrete about where the empirical and normative strands seperate. Then say what would count as progress under your diagnosis, and end by telling me what would change your mind about the diagnosis itself—which consideration would push you to reclassify the debate, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
