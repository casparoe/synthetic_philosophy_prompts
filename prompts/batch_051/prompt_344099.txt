I'm not an academic, but I've spent a long time thinking about a pattern in how we judge decisions, and I want help thinking it through properly. Take two acquisitions: one completed last year and now failed, and a similar one proposed today whose outcome is still unknown. After the first failed, people say the warning signs were obvious, the risks were visible all along, and the board should have known; the failure comes to feel nearly inevitable, as if the true story was there in advance and they simply failed to read it. Yet the second deal, right now, with the same kind of conflicting expert advice, the same cultural differences, and the same gaps in information, is treated by those same people as a reasonable gamble on the available evidence. The past decision is judged as culpably blind, the present one as appropriately uncertain, even when the two are matched piece for piece except for our knowledge of the outcome.

That contrast looks like an inconsistency, but I don't want to assume it is. Act as a professional philosopher working on judgment, explanation, and responsibility. Work carefully. Begin by brainstorming the strongest justifications for treating past and present decision-makers differently — for instance, that the failure itself is evidence about the quality of the decision, that retrospective blame deters future recklessness, or that making sense of the past requires telling a story from outcome back to causes. Test each justification against harder cases, especially a fully matched pair that differs only in whether we know the outcome. Then give an honest verdict: how much of the asymmetry is defensible, whether it should be kept, revised, or abandoned, and what rule we should actually follow when judging past decisions.
