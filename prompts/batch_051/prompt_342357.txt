While working on a feature about radical life extension, I keep running across a line of thought I want to get straight. The idea is that the real obstacle to an indefinitely long life is not technological but mental: a finite mind, the argument runs, simply cannot hold infinitely many distinct experiences, so an endless life would eventually exhaust the space of possible experience and collapse into repetition. That conclusion strikes me as far too strong, but when I reconstruct the reasoning I cannot locate the faulty step. Here is the argument as it has been presented to me:

(1) A finite mind has only finitely many discriminable internal states. (2) Every distinct experience requires a distinct internal state of the mind that has it. (3) Therefore, a finite mind can have only finitely many distinct experiences. (4) An immortal life would be an infinite sequence of experiences. (5) An infinite sequence constructed from a finite repertoire must repeat at least one experience-type infinitely often. (6) If at least one experience-type recurs infinitely often, the life eventually becomes a closed loop of previously had experiences, with no genuinely new experience left possible. (7) A life that becomes a closed loop of previously had experiences, with no new experience possible, would eventually be boring, and a life that has become inescapably boring is not worth continuing. (8) Therefore, an immortal life would eventually become boring and not worth continuing.

My suspicion is that something goes wrong between (5) and (6): "recurring infinitely often" seems weaker than "settling into a closed loop," but I want a precise account of the difference, and of what additional assumption would be needed to license that step. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest version of that step on which the argument would go through?
