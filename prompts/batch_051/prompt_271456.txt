I write about institutions and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about a puzzle that recurs across several federal systems that all claim to respect something called subsidiarity. The European Union describes the principle as the idea that decisions should be taken as close to the citizen as possible, with Brussels acting only where the member states cannot sufficiently achieve an objective and common action would do better. Yet this same principle is routinely invoked to justify centralizing harmonization of product standards, data protection, and environmental rules, because the internal market makes almost any divergence look inefficient. Germany, by contrast, pursues a federal balance partly through the Bundesrat, a chamber composed of delegates sent by the governments of the Länder, who vote in blocks on instructions from their state cabinets, rather than through directly elected representatives of the citizens. Switzerland operates similarly in spirit, with strongly autonomous cantons and a constitutional rule that the Confederation may take on a task only if the cantons cannot handle it. And the United States combines a doctrine under which Congress may not conscript state legislatures or executives into administering federal policy with a rule that federal statutes can nevertheless preempt state law outright.

What I want the piece to bring out is not how these mechanisms function but what they presuppose about democratic legitimacy. Political theorists sometimes distinguish between subsidiarity as an instrumental device for solving problems at the most efficient level, and subsidiarity as a protection for the self-government of smaller communities that exist before the larger polity. I suspect these four arrangements quietly rely on different halves of that distinction, and I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory in each case: who is the relevant demos, whether the smaller units have an intrinsic standing to govern themselves or only an instrumental value in producing better outcomes, and whether the parts of a single arrangement presuppose the same thing. Please separate what each mechanism strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the institutional practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory could license it. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the European Union's use of subsidiarity if its working test were replaced by the self-government-based understanding that better fits the German and Swiss arrangements.
