I've been rereading the section of the Wealth of Nations on the East India Company, where Smith says a body that is both sovereign and merchant has, as sovereign, an interest identical with the interest of the country it governs, but as merchant, an interest directly opposite, and concludes that an exclusive company of merchants is about the worst kind of government a country can have. I work on platform governance, and it seems to me that Apple or Meta or Amazon's marketplace now do a lot of what sovereignty used to do: they set take-it-or-leave-it rules, take a cut of every transaction like a tax, and adjudicate disputes as a kind of private law. Does Smith's argument actually transfer to those non-territorial merchant-sovereigns? Or did his point only work because the East India Company stood between a colony and a wider market, so that making the local economy richer was bad for its trade, whereas a platform's profits are more aligned with its users doing well? I want to know whether the argument survives, in a repaired form, or just doesnt apply.
