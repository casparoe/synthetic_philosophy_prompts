I'm on the committee of a small community garden society, and every couple of months a member claims a chunk of our overgrown common ground as their own plot because they cleared it, fenced it, planted it, or just looked after it for a while. We keep going back to the Locke I half-rememebered from a night course: a person comes to own an unowned thing by working on it, as long as they leave enough of the same quality for everyone else and as long as nothing is allowed to spoil. But we cannot agree on what counts as the relevant work. Is clearing brambles enough, or does the work have to produce something durable the rest of us can point to? Does putting up a fence around a patch count more than watering blackberry bushes that were already growing there? What does leaving enough and of the same quality mean when there are ten members and only one sunny south-facing strip? Does the spoilage rule only bite when harvested crops rot in a shed, or also when a cleared patch is abandoned and fills back in with weeds?

We meet once a month for an hour, nobody on the committee has a philosophy background, and our rulings have to be consistent and explainable in plain language. A permanent grant of common ground to a member also has to be signed off by the whole membership at the annual meeting, so if a bad claim gets to them it causes months of bad feeling. I'd like you to build the procedure, not just give rulings on the two cases. Please respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentances, no introduction or conclusion. The first four items should be the tests we apply in order, including what counts as passing each test and where the process stops with a no. The fifth item should run the procedure on two claims and say where the procedure hands off to the full membership because the question is one of values rather than facts: (a) a member cleared and double-dug a quarter-acre of brambles, planted beans, and now wants the plot as her own permanently; (b) a member put down a concrete foundation for a shed on the edge of the common three years ago, never built the shed, and now claims the foundation and the ground under it. I suspect the procedure will tell us to reject someone we'd rather say yes to; please don't soften it.
