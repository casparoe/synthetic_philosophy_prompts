I write about science and ideas for a general publication. I am working on an essay about generative imagery and how older institutions are being forced to redraw the line between making an image and curating one. I have obtained the exhibition rules of a painters' cooperative founded in 1972, and one bylaw reads as follows:

"No member may exhibit a piece unless they can give an account of every principal element of the piece: what decision produced it, and why that decision was taken. Pieces produced through random or mechanical processes are not eligible."

A senior member now works almost entirely with a text-to-image model. She writes and revises detailed prompts, generates dozens of candidate images, rejects most of them, repeatedly re-prompts regions she dislikes, and finishes a few small passages by hand. In one piece, the model introduced a pair of figures on a balcony that she had not asked for; she kept them because they solved a perspectival problem she had been unable to prompt directly. She can account for every prompt term, every rejection, every regional re-prompt, and every brushstroke, but not for the model's own placements — only for her decision to accept them.

Does this bylaw cover or exclude her work? Please work through four approaches to the clause: the literal wording, the purpose the drafters most plausibly had, what a reasonable member would understand it to require, and what the founders would likely have said if asked. Where the approaches diverge, argue for which should govern. I am less interested in a legal prediction than in what the dispute reveals about what the cooperative was actually trying to protect, and whether "every principal element" tracks something essential to authorship or only something that happened to be legible before generative models existed.
