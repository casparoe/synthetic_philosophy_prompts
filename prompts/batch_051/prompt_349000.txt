I am the editor-in-chief of my high school's student newspaper, and I need to turn a philosophical point into editorial policy. Every deadline, our copy desk has the same argument. One editor wants to flag sentences like "Every member of the honor society may cast his vote in the runoff" or "A nurse can tell within an hour whether her shift will go badly," while another editor replies that "his" is simply the traditional generic pronoun, that readers understand it, and that changing it is ideology rather than editing. We read Douglas Hofstadter's satirical "Person Paper" in class—the piece that exposes how a supposedly neutral default becomes invisible to the people using it, by replaying the standard defenses of sexist language with race substituted for sex—but none of us has figured out how to translate its point into something a copy editor can apply at 11 p.m. on deadline.

I would like a short, ordered procedure for our style manual—a checklist or decision flow in plain words—that tells a copy editor, in under a minute per sentence, whether to flag a sentence for encoding the default human being as male, or for quietly importing some other social default, and what revision, if any, to make. Please ground the procedure in what the Person Paper actually shows rather than in taste. In particular, keep two distinctions running through the whole procedure: a term's traditional generic function (the grammatical job it seems to be doing) versus the default image it projects (the picture of the typical case it plants in a reader's mind), and a revision that changes truth versus one that changes only emphasis. Our copy editors have no background in philosophy of language, and our faculty adviser must approve anything that goes into the manual, so each step needs a plain-language rationale, not just a label.

Please work the procedure through each of the following five sentences, saying explicitly what the manual would direct for each and why:
(a) Every member of the honor society may cast his vote in the runoff.
(b) A nurse can tell within an hour whether her shift will go badly.
(c) Each applicant should ask the teacher they trust most to write a recommendation.
(d) In most owl species, the female is larger than the male.
(e) The principal, a mother of two, will address the assembly.

At least one of the worked examples should be a sentence our desk has been trying to protect but the procedure directs us to revise, and at least one should be a sentence our desk has been trying to cut but the procedure directs us to keep. End with a candid note on the kinds of cases the procedure cannot settle, where the manual will have to say "editor's judgment" rather than a rule.
