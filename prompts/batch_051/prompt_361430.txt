I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science working on causal inference, and I keep going back and forth on something my lab treats as settled: why do minimal Bayes nets track causation? The standard line is that when several directed acyclic graphs over a set of variables imply the same probability distribution, the minimality-and-faithfulness package says to prefer the sparsest graph(s) and to assume that the conditional independencies in the data reflect the causal structure, not exact cancellations among competing pathways. I find the argument for this package genuinely compelling: it is doing all the honest work in causal discovery. If you allow pathways that cancel exactly, like a gene that raises blood pressure being partly offset by another mechanism that lowers it by precisely the right amount, then any observed association can be explained away as a knife-edge coincidence, and you can never learn causes from data at all. That feels like a real systematic virtue, not an ad hoc taste for simplicity.

But I also have a firm intuition that the world does not care about our preference for parsimony. Homeostasis is everywhere: a thermostat and a slightly open window hold a room at 21 degrees through two summed influences that cancel; blood pressure is maintained by a pile of counteracting mechanisms; engineered controllers are literally built to keep this kind of knife-edge cancelation in place. When I imagine such a system, I don't just think 'the minimal graph is a useful approximation'—I think the true causal graph includes all those edges, and the unfaithful representation is the correct picture of the mechanism. The theory's reply is that these are measure-zero coincidences I should effectivly set aside. The intuition's reply is that they are exactly the structures real systems are designed and selected for.

I don't want a verdict. I want someone to walk me through what each option costs. What do I give up if I drop the intuition and treat faithfulness/minimality as a premise about the world? What do I give up if I demote it to a merely epistemic default, a conservative rule for inference rather than a claim about how causation works? And is there a reading on which both survive, something like: minimality is the right account of what counts as 'the cause' relative to a chosen set of variables, not a claim that the fuller mechanism doesn't exist? Put the answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
