I'm a PhD student in philosophy, mostly working on philosophy of mind, so I read the logic stuff as a slightly nervous outsider. Lately I've been working through the Liar literature and I keep running into a pattern I don't have a clean label for. Everyone knows the basic loop: a sentence says of itself that it is false, and you're stuck either way. The standard repairs are also familiar. Tarski's hierarchy won't let a truth predicate apply to sentences at its own level; Kripke allows some sentences to be neither true nor false; others say the Liar fails to express a proposition, or that we should go non-classical. What bugs me is that each repair seems to immediately make a new version of the paradox available. Let your solution classify the Liar as gappy or indeterminate, and there's a sentence saying "this sentence is not true or indeterminate" (or just "not true," with "not true" read to include the gaps); call it meaningful but false, or truth-valueless, or say it violates the conditions for asserting anything, and pretty soon there's a successor sentence built out of just the notions your own theory introduced. It feels less like solving a contradiction than like pushing it one level back, and then the next solution has to handle the very vocabulary it used to state the previous solution.

I'm not after another survey of proposed solutions. What I want to know is whether this iterative failure mode is a recognized, named phenomenon in the literature—I have a dim sense it is, but I can't retrieve the term—and more importantly how to carve it precisely. Is the successor sentence an objection to each particular fix, or is it meant to show something stronger, like that any vocabulary rich enough to state a solution will also be rich enough to rebuild the paradox? Should something count as an instance of the phenomenon only when it uses the solution's own resources, or is it enough that it's a self-referential sentence about the solution's central notion? And can a solution ever say "yes, those successor sentences exist, but they're harmless" and remain stable, or does the existence of the successor already do all the damage? I'd much rather get a definition with boundary cases and a sense of where the edges are then just a name.
