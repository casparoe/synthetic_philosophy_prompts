For most of the twentieth century, the reference class problem was a standing embarrassment for objective accounts of probability. The probability of an event for a particular individual — say, that a given driver will crash this year — seems to depend on which group the statistician folds the individual into: all drivers, drivers of the same age and vehicle type, drivers with the same commute and annual mileage, and so on. Because the class can always be narrowed or widened, and each choice yields a different number, it becomes hard to say that any one of those numbers is the individual's true risk rather than an artifact of the grouping.

Coarse actuarial practice papered over the problem by settling on a manageable handful of classes. Machine-learning risk scores, now used in insurance, medicine, and criminal justice, appear to change the picture: they can construct an almost bespoke reference class for each person from hundreds or thousands of predictors. A natural thought is that this dissolves the old problem. The algorithm, unlike the human actuary, simply lets the data choose the relevant class — or so the story goes.

Does the reference class problem actually lose its force once reference classes are learned by an algorithm rather than chosen by hand, or does it merely relocate into the choice of features, training data, loss function, and update schedule? Identify the premise of the original problem that this development bears on, consider whether that premise can be repaired, and reach a definite verdict: is individualized algorithmic scoring best understood as a solution to the reference class problem, a relocation of it, or a change in its practical stakes that leaves its conceptual core untouched?
