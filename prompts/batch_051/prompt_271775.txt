I'm trying to work out whether some reasons are free-floating — whether a reason can remain fully in force when no one is around to hold it, register it, or be affected by it. I have gut verdicts on a list of cases, but I can't see the principle behind them. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts, and where it forces me to revise them.

1. The last manuscript. The last surviving reader of a dying language burns the last remaining manuscript written in it; no one else can read it, and no one who could read it will ever exist again. The paper would make good tinder. I think there was a decisive reason not to burn it.

2. The deathbed promise. I promise a dying friend that I will scatter her ashes on a particular ridgeline. After she dies the trip is costly and no one would ever know whether I did it. I think the reason to go remains in full force; her death and the lack of witnesses cancel nothing.

3. The forgotten loan. I borrow money and promise to repay it next month. The lender then loses every record of the loan and forgets it entirely; he would never notice or feel the loss of the money. I think I still have an unaltered reason to repay.

4. Litter in the dead valley. On a walk through an uninhabited basin that no one else ever enters or sees, I consider dropping a plastic wrapper. I think there is a real, if minor, reason not to.

5. The crumbling fresco. In an abandoned building no one will ever re-enter, I find a partly ruined, unowned fresco. I could finish destroying it for amusement. I think there is a reason not to, and that the reason concerns the fresco itself rather than anyone's possible experience of it.

6. The dead stranger's reputation. I could publish a false and degrading story about a historical figure who died centuries ago, who has no living descendants, and whom no living person has any connection to. My gut says there is no reason not to, because defamation needs a victim — someone whose interests or standing can be set back — and here there is none. But I can't see why cases 1 and 5 don't push the same way: if unowned paper and unowned pigment can ground reasons, why can't an unowned reputation? I'm torn.

The fault line seems to be this: in case 5 I treat something with no owner and no possible audience as mattering in itself, while in case 6 I treat only persons as capable of grounding reasons. Is there a single principle that yields cases 1 through 5 and then settles case 6 one way or the other? If a simple principle forces me to give up one or more of my verdicts, which should I abandon, and why? Please also say what your principle predicts for two further cases I haven't settled:

(a) A promise made to someone who will later completely forget that the promise was made, and who will never again be in a position to demand or expect performance.

(b) An artificial system with no experiences of its own, whose only goal-like state is to keep a set of lights in a certain pattern. Someone could switch the lights off permanently, and the system would never be in a position to register that anything was wrong. Is there a reason not to switch them off?

Please give me a committed answer: state the actual principle you would defend, not a menu of positions.
