I live in a small housing co-op, twenty-two adults, and our members' handbook contains a clause that's caused a row this week. It says: 'Each adult member shall contribute no fewer than eight hours per month of labour to the upkeep of the common parts and the administration of the co-op.' One member, Rob, has begun paying a cleaner through an app to come in and do his eight hours each month — wiping the shared hallways, doing the bins rota, whatever needs doing — and he has never once joined a workday. His line is that the clause requires contribution of labour, not that it should be his own labour, and the labour does get done; if anything, the cleaner does a better job than he would. Several of us think thats obviously against the point of the clause, but whenever we try to explain why, it keeps collapsing into 'we just think he should have to turn up', which sounds like we're policing his time rather than protecting anything real.

The handbook was written in 2004 by a group who, I'm fairly sure, had never imagined a member subcontracting their hours to a stranger on an app; they clearly pictured a member with a mop, not a member with a cleaning account. I want to work out what the clause actually requires of him, read honestly rather than just as a way of getting at Rob: what the words deliver, what the purpose delivers, and what the people who wrote it would probably have said if you'd asked them about someone like him. On one view the hours are what matter and the hours happen; on another, the whole point was that each of us shares an equal burden rather than buying their way out of it. Since Rob has paid his own money, what exactly is he taking from the rest of us without giving? Is there a version of the clause that would catch what he's doing without also banning things we want to allow — swapping shifts, a partner helping out, one member doing the books while another does the garden? And where, if anywhere, is the free riding in his arrangement? I'm less interested in the practical verdict than in what the rule was ever protecting.
