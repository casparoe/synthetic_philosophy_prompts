I am in my final year reading a module on rational choice and expected utility theory, and I am also writing a term essay for a course on environmental policy. A parallel between the two has been nagging me, and I would be grateful for an assessment of whether it is more than superficial.

In the decision-theory module we studied Pascal's mugging: an agent who maximises expected utility can apparently be coerced into paying a small sum by a mugger who threatens an astronomically large harm, because any non-zero subjective probability assigned to so large a harm swamps the certain small loss. The standard diagnosis is that the setup exploits an unbounded utility function together with our willingness to assign non-zero credence to arbitrary, unfalsifiable stories.

In the environmental-policy course, the precautionary principle appears to rest on a similar form of reasoning: if a proposed activity raises the possibility of catastrophic and irreversible damage, the mere fact that the probability is low or not precisely established is not taken to be a sufficient reason to avoid costly preventive measures. Much of the case for aggressive climate mitigation seems to run this way, through low-probability but extremely severe tail risks.

I see the mapping roughly as follows: the mugger corresponds to the worst-case tail scenario; the small sum handed over corresponds to the certain present cost of mitigation; the astronomically many victims correspond to irreversible damage to future generations; and the tiny credence one grants the mugger corresponds to the small or imprecise probability assigned to catastrophic climate outcomes.

But I cannot tell whether this is a deep structural identity or only a loose analogy. On one side, the objections that defuse Pascal's mugging — bounding the utility function, refusing to assign non-zero credence to arbitrarily specific stories, or imposing some floor beneath which tiny probabilities are ignored — look as if they should also apply to precautionary reasoning. On the other side, the precautionary principle is usually presented as a principle for decision under uncertainty rather than under risk, and climate tail risks are not arbitrary inventions; they are constrained by physical models, so treating them as muggings may misdescribe the epistemic situation.

What I would like is not a summary of either idea, but an adjudication of the parallel itself. Please state the formal mapping as precisely as you can, say what would have to be true of both sides for the known criticisms of Pascal's mugging to transfer to precautionary climate policy, and likewise for the reverse transfer to hold. Then identify at least one respect in which the analogy predicts a verdict that turns out false, or else argue that the analogy is exact. I take it the two ideas are genuinely, and not merely verbally, connected only if the same structural features do the work on both sides. Could you write your answer in British English?
