Since I retired from the lab (forty years in chemistry), I've been spending a lot of time on the rationality and philosophy-of-reasoning literature, and I also got back into karate for the first time in decades. Watching the beginners in the dojo made me think about something I've started calling the "kata transfer problem" for reasoning. I have a small theory and I'd like someone to kick its tires.

The theory is this. Formal reasoning training—logic, probability, the biases and fallacies, all of it—is mostly kata. You learn a move in a cooperative, simplified context, and you can execute it beautifully when a problem is flagged for you. But in real life nobody flags the problem; the actual skill is recognizeing that you're in the kind of situation where the move applies. Martial arts has the same issue: people who are elegant at kata get hit by novices who've only drilled reflexive counters. So the transferable unit in reasoning isn't the move, it's the interruption—catching yourself mid-thought and switching out of defending-your-position mode into updating mode. And interruptions, I claim, cant really be built by kata, because kata is cooperative; you need something live, an uncooperative partner, some friction that isn't on your side. So I've started doing what I call live-fire exercises in reasoning: taking a real belief I currently hold and forcing myself to run one assigned move on it—writing down what would actually change my mind, computing the expected value of a decision I'm about to make for real, steelmanning the view I don't hold. The claim is that these transfer mostly by building the interruption habit, and the content of the move matters less than everyone thinks. I can do every fallacy on a list, but after a month of drilling sunk-cost examples I still almost finished a book I was actively not enjoying, out of pure inertia.

The part I'm least sure about is whether an interruption can itself be turned into a kata. We certainly drill "notice when your wrist is grabbed" in the dojo, so maybe I'm wrong that kata can't train noticing. But the noticing in a kata happens when you're already braced for it, and that is exactly not how it feels in real reasoning. So here's what I want checked: is the distinction between moves and interruptions doing real work, or am I just relabeling the old near-transfer/far-transfer distinction from psychology? Has someone worked this out already under a name I should know? And where does the theory break—what does it predict that's false, or what's the clearest case where a pure kata did transfer to real reasoning?
