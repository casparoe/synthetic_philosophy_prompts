I am a staff software engineer at a mid-sized company that ships an internal LLM-based coding agent, and I have been asked to write the procedure our capability-review board will use whenever a team proposes expanding what the agent is allowed to do. The agent currently reads repositories, proposes edits, and runs tests in an isolated environment, but every few weeks a team asks for more: opening pull requests directly, reading repositories outside its assignment, executing shell commands in a sandbox with outbound network access, reading production logs, or accessing non-production credentials. Our engineering organization has adopted a policy that, for capabilities above a certain risk level, the safety case may not depend on the model's being aligned or well-intentioned; it must assume the model could be pursuing some other objective and show that the proposed capability keeps the damage within stated bounds. The review board consists of two staff engineers, one security engineer, and an engineering manager; it has about an hour per proposal, nobody on it has formal machine-learning safety training, and I have no background in philosophy or formal safety research, so the procedure has to be usable by people like us.

Please turn that policy into a decision procedure the board can actually run. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values or judgment rather than engineering. In practical terms, distinguish between what a reviewer can establish by controlling the environment—permissions, sandboxes, rate limits, audit logs, human approval gates—and what would require trusting the model's internal motivations, and require that only the former carry weight for high-stakes capabilities. Build in the distinction between harm that is reversible or detectable and harm that is not, and between events that could be attributed afterward and events that could not.

Then run the procedure on three recent proposals: (a) letting the agent open pull requests directly, with a human still required to merge; (b) letting the agent read any repository in the organization rather than only the repositories on which it is assigned a task; and (c) letting the agent execute shell commands in a sandbox that has outbound network access but no credentials. At least one of these should come out in a way my team will not like; do not soften that result. Finally, say plainly what this procedure cannot do—in particular what it cannot tell us about the model's internal goals, and which classes of risk it will systematically miss.
