I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about markets, and it shook a view I have held for years: that a market's central epistemic virtue is aggregating information no single participant possesses, with trading pulling scattered, private knowledge into the price — the Hayekian picture in broad outline. My friend kept returning to the no-trade theorem. If two rational agents with common priors and no liquidity or hedging motives differ only in information, each should interpret the other's willingness to trade as evidence against the trade, so purely informational trade should not occur at all. But if informational trade cannot get started, it becomes unclear how prices ever come to reflect dispersed information. I would like a map of what would count against the aggregation view, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I do not want just the critics' version. Which assumptions behind the no-trade result can a committed defender relax without embarrassment — noise traders, heterogeneous priors, costly information, strategic delay — and which relaxations amount to quietly giving up the position? Are there empirical findings about how prices respond to trades and order flow that actually discriminate here, rather than being consistent with both sides? I find myself inclined to dismiss any result that assumes common priors as having stipulated away the interesting case; tell me straightforwardly whether that is a fair move or a way of insulating the view, and whether insulation of this sort matters for a claim of this kind.
