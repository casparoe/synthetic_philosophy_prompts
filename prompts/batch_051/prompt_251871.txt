The following clause appears in the 1926 instrument of trust of the Hartwell Museum, a private museum in the United States whose holdings include a group of Benin plaques purchased from a London dealer in 1900:

"No object in the Collection shall be sold, exchanged, lent, given away, or otherwise disposed of, and the Trustees shall at all times keep the Collection together in perpetuity for the education and enjoyment of the public."

The instrument was drafted when Nigeria was under British colonial rule, and nothing in it contemplates a claim by a foreign state. The plaques have since been shown to have been looted from Benin City during the British military expedition of 1897, and the Government of Nigeria has formally requested their return. The Trustees accept the historical facts and wish to transfer the plaques to Nigeria, but the museum's counsel has advised that the instrument may bar any transfer of title.

Analyze whether the instrument, properly read, permits, forbids, or requires the return of the plaques. Address the question from at least these angles: (1) the ordinary meaning of the text, especially the phrase "otherwise disposed of"; (2) the purpose of the clause as it would have been understood in 1926; (3) what the drafters would most likely have said in 1926 if asked whether the clause should allow the return of looted property; and (4) what a reasonable reader today would take the clause to mean, given changed norms about colonial-era acquisitions. Then state which of these approaches should control and why. Treat this as a close reading of the instrument rather than a question of case law; no legal citations are needed. Please write the answer in American English.
