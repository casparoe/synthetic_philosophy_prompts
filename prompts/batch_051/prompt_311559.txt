As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have spent years noticing where I draw the line between an ordinary factual claim that needs checking and a background assumption that I cannot do my work without. I keep hearing the phrase hinge epistemology, usually in connection with Wittgenstein's On Certainty, but I am not sure my actual convictions line up with it. Let me give you my gut verdicts on a set of concrete cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them—and what that principle says about cases I have not yet decided.

When the OPERA experiment reported neutrinos arriving at Gran Sasso roughly sixty nanoseconds earlier than light would have done, I thought the team was right to publish. I treated the suggestion that special relativity might be wrong as a real scientific question. At the same time, my confidence that the result was a systematic error was extremely high, and in the first week I could not point to the specific flaw. I considered it perfectly coherent to say: take the anomaly seriously, doubt the cornerstone, and be unsurprised when the error is found. When the timing error was traced to a faulty cable connection, I did not think the episode showed that the original caution had been irrational.

Against that, when a philosopher offers the hypothesis that the universe came into existence five minutes ago, complete with all records and apparent memories, I do not say it is false. I say it is not something evidence can get any purchase on. I would not list it among the questions science has not yet settled, and I do not think my refusal to take it seriously is a failure of open-mindedness. I treat it as excluded in advance, not refuted by data.

When a whistleblower tells me that documents in a particular registry are being altered, I take that as a serious allegation to be investigated, and specific evidence can confirm or refute it. But when someone tells me that the entire documentary record—every archive, every database, every registry—is a coordinated fabrication, I do not regard that as a bolder version of the same claim. I regard it as an attack on the frame that makes fact-checking possible, and I notice I cannot say what would count as checking it.

When a source claims to remember verbatim a conversation from thirty years ago, I treat the memory as weak unless documents corroborate it, and I am entirely prepared to learn that the source is confidently wrong. But when I remember what my editor said to me this morning, I am more certain of that memory than I am of any document that might be brought in to contradict it. I am not even sure what it would be to check it, since any check would itself depend on memory or on records whose authority feels less immediate.

When someone argues that the Earth is young because radioactive decay rates may have been different in the past, I do not think the question is illegitimate. I think it is an empirical claim that happens to be false, and I can say roughly what evidence bears on it. It bothers me when colleagues dismiss it as unscientific rather than as wrong.

When someone denies that the Holocaust happened, my first instinct is to treat them as making an empirical claim against which the evidence is overwhelming. But when the denial persists after the evidence is shown, it stops looking like ordinary error and starts looking like a failure of the person's standards of historical evidence themselves. I am genuinely torn about whether this is an extreme empirical mistake or something closer to the entire-documentary-record case.

Do these verdicts express a single principle? If so, state it as precisely as you can, and say which cases it handles naturally and which it has to strain to accommodate. If not, say which of my verdicts I should give up and what the cheapest revision of my gut would be. Then apply the account you end up with to two cases I have not decided: a scientist who suspects a collaborator of fabricating data because the results are too clean, with no concrete evidence, and a juror who is asked to set aside a confident eyewitness identification on the grounds that human memory in general is reconstructive.
