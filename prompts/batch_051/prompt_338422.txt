I run a small philosophy podcast and next month I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends the Kantian view that lying is never permissible, even to the murderer at the door. I don't want the interview to be another intro-level recap of the categorical imperative, so I've been rereading On a Supposed Right to Lie from Benevolent Motives. There's a passage near the end where Kant makes a striking point about responsibility: if you answer the murderer truthfully and the victim is found and killed, that is, roughly, a bad outcome but not something you are answerable for, whereas if you lie and the murderer follows your lie and finds the victim, you become legally answerable for the death, even if you couldn't have predicted how your words would lead him there. Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions that press on that asymmetry, plus follow-ups that anticipate the standard reply that Kant is only talking about legal right and not ethical permissibility?

I'd also like at least one question on the difference between lying and other forms of deceit that stop short of an outright false assertion, like evasive or technically true answers, and one on whether the maxim "I may lie to save a life" really fails the universalisation test once the maxim is specified carefully enough. Frame some of these as questions I can ask after the guest gives what you think his most likely replies will be, so the interview doesn't stall on his first line of defence.
