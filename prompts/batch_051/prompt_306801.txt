I work as a quant at a trading firm — I came up through sports betting and market making, and I've never taken a philosophy course. Lately a friend has been insisting that the Dutch book argument is basically just arbitrage. From what I've pieced together, the argument says: if your degrees of belief don't satisfy the probability axioms, then there's a set of bets, each of which you'd regard as fair at you own odds, that together guarantee you lose money no matter what happens; since a rational person shouldn't be open to a sure loss, your credences ought to be coherent probabilities. My amateur translation so far is this: a credence is like the implied probability embedded in the odds you'd quote; a Dutch book is like a sharp bettor hitting a market maker whose posted odds are inconsistent; and the coherence requirement is just the no-arbitrage condition.

But I can feel something slipping in. In my world, no-arbitrage leaves plenty of room: a bookmaker can quote a wide bid-ask spread and never get arbitraged, while the philosophical argument seems to assume there's one fair price you'd both buy and sell at. Also, the argument seems to treat "gets exploited" as the same thing as "is irrational," and I'm not sure those are the same to a trader — getting picked off by a sharper model can happen to someone who's making individually reasonable prices. So: translate the Dutch book argument into the vocabulary I actually work in — no-arbitrage conditions, bid-ask spreads, implied probabilities, risk-neutral pricing, market making, getting picked off — as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. What maps cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Is the residue a sign that my framing is missing something about rationality, or that the philosophers' claim is less precise then it looks once you take spreads seriously?
