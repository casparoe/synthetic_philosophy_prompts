Ive been turning over something about how courts treat evidence and I can't get it to sit right. Suppose a town has two bus companys, and 8 of every 10 buses on the road belong to Blue Bus. Someone is hit at night and can't tell which company the bus was. The math says there's an 80% chance it was Blue Bus. In a civil case the standard is just "more likely than not," so on pure numbers that should win easily. But hardly anyone thinks that alone ought to make Blue Bus pay, and from what I can tell courts mostly agree.

There's a version with a rodeo too. Say 1000 people got into a rodeo but only 499 paid, so 501 got in free. You grab one person at random. There's a 50.1% chance he sneaked in. Again, more likely than not — but nobody sensible would fine him on that alone.

Now flip it. Put the 80% statistic to the side and suppose the only evidence is one witness saying, "I think it was a Blue Bus, though it was dark and I only saw it for a second." People treat that as a real case. A jury might well decide on it. Yet eyewitnesses in those conditions are wrong a lot — maybe more often than the 20% the statistic gets wrong. So we trust a shaky personal story more than a solid, countable probability.

What I'm trying to figure out is whether there's a defensible principle that separates "statistical" evidence from "individual" evidence, or whether the law (and the rest of us too) just can't stomach deciding a case on a naked probability and then dress that up as a principle. Is there a good reason an 80% base rate should count for less than a witness who is only 80% reliable? Give me the strongest version of the distinction you can, then try your best to break it with nearby cases, and tell me straight if you think it survives or if it's mostly a feeling we've all agreed to treat as logic. And if the line does fail in civil cases, say what that means for criminal ones, where the standard is supposed to be proof beyond a reasonable doubt.
