I was arguing with a friend who's into Bayesian statistics about the doomsday argument, and I've ended up less confused about the argument itself than about an asymmetry in how we actually reason. His version goes roughly like this: for all you know, you're a random draw from the set of all humans who will ever live. If that holds, there's a 95% chance you're in the last 95% of humans rather than the first 5%. Since something like a hundred billion humans have lived so far, any total vastly bigger than about two trillion would put you in that first sliver, which would make you weirdly special. So, the argument says, expect the end to come sooner than a naive reading of history suggests. Fine, whatever.

Here's the thing I can't get straight. The same friend, like almost everyone I know, is perfectly happy to say that we're ordinary, and that we're not living at some specially early or uniquely chosen moment relative to the humans who came before us. That's the same sampling intuition pointed backwards. Turn it forward, and suddenly everyone calls it a parlour trick. It gets stranger with ordinary totals: if I visit a museum and the ticket counter says I'm visitor number 100, nobody would seriously conclude that the museum will close before visitor 2,000 just because 100 is the first 5% of 2,000. So we seem to accept the 'random draw, therefore not special' move when it's retrospective and harmless, reject it when it's prospective and constraining, and reject it everywhere for everyday finite totals. I want to know whether anything actually justifies that pattern. Is the past/future difference defensible here (because the past is fixed and the future isn't, or because the number of future humans depends on our choices rather than a pre-set lottery)? Is the museum case relevantly different (because humanity's total is not like a pre-printed roll of tickets)? Or are we cherry-picking, and if so, which side should we drop? Act as a philosopher who works on anthropic reasoning and probability, think through the options carefully before landing, steelman each move, test the candidate justifications against nearby cases, and then say which way you lean. Please write your answer in British English.
