You are a professional philosopher of emotion with a secondary competence in literary craft; answer as if consulting for a novelist who will not tolerate jargon or hedging. Think through the problem in full before writing, and privately brainstorm two or three candidate scenes and your reasons for rejecting them before settling on the one you present; do not include the brainstorming in your reply.

I am a novelist writing a literary novel in which two sisters have separated themselves over the old quarrel between the Stoics and the Romantics: one holds that the passions are disturbances built on false judgment and are, at best, noise in a good life; the other holds that some emotions partly constitute the good life even when they misdescribe their objects. I need the novel's central scene engineered to specification, not merely illustrative.

Design a thought experiment to these specifications. The scenario must be one piece of realistic domestic life, written in no more than two sentences. Its engine must be nostalgia: a pleasurable, slightly painful longing for a past that both sisters privately know was never quite as remembered. No deaths, no betrayals, no diagnoses, no reversals of fortune, and no philosophizing dialogue; the disagreement must show up in what the sisters notice and do, not in what they say. Given these shared facts, the Stoic sister must judge the nostalgia as noise, a passion resting on a false judgment about a lost past, to be dismantled. The Romantic sister must judge the same nostalgia as partly constitutive of the good life rather than merely a useful spur to action, and must do so without denying the idealization. Neither verdict may be stipulated or chalked up to temperament; each must be generated from the position's own commitments about what an emotion is.

Reply with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. The scenario itself.
2. The Stoic verdict, with the reasoning internal to the Stoic position.
3. The Romantic verdict, with the reasoning internal to the Romantic position and a direct answer to the false-idealization difficulty.
4. The specific structural feature of the scenario that makes the two verdicts peel apart, and what collapses if that feature is removed.
5. The strongest objection a defender of whichever position the scene leaves worse off would raise against the scenario itself, and the one modification that would answer that objection while preserving the split in verdicts.
