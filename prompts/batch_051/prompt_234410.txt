I'm a retired plant ecologist—thirty years of vegetation surveys and arguing with foresters about what counts as a "degraded" site—and I've only recently gotten into philosophy of language (Putnam, Hacking, that crowd). I've been tinkering with a homemade distinction I call copycat concepts, with a later stage I call fluid concepts. The microdomain is one I know cold: words that leaked out of ecology and conservation into policy and ordinary speech—invasive species, ecosystem health, resilience, degraded, native.

Here's the theory. A term starts life as an instrument-concept: it's tied to a protocol or a measurement, so you can in principle say what observation would make you withdraw it. "Ecosystem health" began as a battery of indicators; "invasive" was tied, loosely I admit, to spread rate, ecological impact, and human introduction. Then the term leaks into public use and becomes a copycat concept: people copy the word because it carries authority, but it starts doing the work of a moral word—it praises or condemns and signals which side you're on—while still presenting itself as plain empirical description. The boundary of the concept starts to follow the speaker. The same escaped garden plant is invasive if you hate it and naturalized if you love it; "resilient" gets applied to a system that has simply shifted to a novel state nobody wanted; a regenerating forest is called unhealthy even though the early-successional species are doing exactly what early-successional species do. Eventually the term becomes a fluid concept: nobody can say what would count against applying it anymore, and the tell is that it stops being falsifiable in conversation—question the word and you seem to be questioning the values behind it.

My stronger claim is that this drift is not random. It systematically runs from descriptive content toward evaluative force, and the turning point is when the term's job switches from opening an empirical question to closing one. I know there is a psychology literature around something called "concept creep" that might be the same idea with better branding; I want to know whether mine says anything more than that, or whether the whole thing collapses once you remember technical terms were never as clean as I'm pretending.

Play the role of a professional philosopher who works on concepts and language. Think carefully and brainstorm before settling on your answer. Be candid rather than flattering: is the distinction coherent? Does it already exist under another name in a sharper form? Give me the two or three strongest objections, then tell me which parts of the idea, if any, survive them. I'd rather find out its half-baked in a useful way than be told it's original.
