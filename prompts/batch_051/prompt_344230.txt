I am a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy late, and I am working through John Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration for the first time. I find the following sentence difficult to place in the overall argument:

"Why, then, does this burning zeal for God, for the Church, and for the salvation of souls — burning I say, literally, with fire and faggot — pass by those moral vices and wickednesses, without any chastisement, which are acknowledged by all men to be diametrically opposite to the profession of Christianity, and bend all its nerves either to the introducing of ceremonies, or to the establishment of opinions, which for the most part are about nice and intricate matters, that exceed the capacity of ordinary understandings?"

I understand that Locke's central thesis is that civil coercion is appropriate only for civil interests such as life, liberty, health, and property, and never for the care of souls, because belief cannot be compelled and because the care of souls was not committed to the magistrate. But here he seems to add a further complaint: that some religious doctrines are too fine or intricate for ordinary minds. Is Locke making an argument about the unintelligibility of the doctrines themselves, or about the competence of the enforcer, or about the standing of ordinary people to judge for themselves?

I would like help reconstructing the inferential role this passage plays. Specifically, if the sentence were removed, would the Letter lose an independent reason for non-coercion, or is it rhetorically reinforcing the same principle? And if we translate Locke's point into public reason terms: does the "ordinary understandings" remark support a position on which religious arguments that cannot be stated in terms accessible to the citizens being coerced are publicly inadmissible in politics? I ask because, as a natural scientist, I am used to arguments whose force does not depend on the cleverness or ignorance of the audience, and I want to know whether Locke is importing an epistemic standard for coercive public justification here. Please help me understand this passage as an argument, not as history.
