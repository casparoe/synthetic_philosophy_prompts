A research consortium I belong to has been split, for the better part of a year, over a single question: whether an AI system that can form and revise evaluative judgments but is architecturally prevented from acting on them—its outputs are confined to analyses and recommendations that others may take up or ignore—should count as a moral agent. One faction, which we call the judgment-first position, holds that moral agency resides in the capacity for normative deliberation: representing considerations as reasons, weighing them, and reaching a verdict. On this view, the system's lack of executive control no more disqualifies it than a prisoner's constrained circumstances disqualify them; what matters is what the system can judge, not what it can effect. The other faction, the efficacy position, holds that agency is partly constituted by the power to make one's judgments count in the world: to act in light of them, and thereby to be answerable for a caused difference. On this view, a system whose verdicts can never initiate, alter, or prevent anything is a moral spectator, a consultant, or an instrument for the decisions of others rather than an agent responsible for anything of its own.

Rather than continue the dispute, we have committed to producing an adversarial-collaboration document, and I have been asked to draft it. The goal is not to declare a winner but to produce a text both camps can put their names to while making the residual disagreement exact. The document should contain: a numbered list of claims about paradigm cases, the ordinary marks of moral agency, and the concept of a capacity that both camps can sign without triviality; a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording the side holding the position would accept as fair to its own view, with nearby but slanting formulations explicitly rejected; the cruxes—sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp—including, we suspect, whether an architectural block leaves a genuine capacity merely unexercised or only a disposition, whether issuing a recommendation is itself an act in the relevant sense, and whether moral agency is a threshold or a scalar property; and for each crux, a concrete case, hypothetical intervention, or observation that both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. The chair also wants each side to state what evidence or argument would lead it to soften or abandon its position. Where you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal, say precisely which part and why; where it is not, make clear what turns on the non-verbal remainder.
