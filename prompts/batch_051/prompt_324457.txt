Below is a question from an upper-level undergraduate history of logic exam, together with a student's answer.

Question: Explain in your own words what Frege changed about logic, relative to Aristotelian syllogistic. Your answer should touch on (i) how propositions are analyzed, (ii) quantification, and (iii) the difference in expressive power between the two logics, with at least one illustrative sentence.

Student's answer: Frege's fundamental break with Aristotle was to abandon the subject–predicate analysis of the proposition. In syllogistic, every proposition consists of a subject and predicate joined by the copula, and the quantifier words "all" and "some" attach to the subject term. Frege replaced this with a function–argument analysis: a concept is an unsaturated function that takes an object as argument and yields a truth-value, so the copula drops out. He also introduced the quantifier, which binds a variable instead of attaching to a subject term; thus "All men are mortal" becomes, in our notation, ∀x(Mx → Fx). This matters because the old subject-bound quantifiers cannot handle relations and nested dependency: syllogistic can express "All men are mortal," but it cannot formalize "Every boy loves some girl," which Frege's logic captures with two nested quantifiers. The contrast also shows that syllogistic was wrong, not merely incomplete, since Frege showed that "is" conflates predication, existence, and identity, which must be treated separately. Frege thereby replaced a logic of terms with a logic of concepts and relations, which is why he is called the founder of modern logic.
