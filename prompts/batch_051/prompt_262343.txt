I'm a novelist, and I could use a hand keeping a fictional church committee's theology consistant over the length of a book. The novel is set in a small evangelical denomination that's been arguing for years about whether the miraculous gifts are still around — the usual cessationist-versus-continuationist split. There's a Gifts Discernment Committee that reviews reports of healings, tongues, prophecy, words of knowledge, and decides whether to affirm them or not. I've already written a string of their rulings in earlier chapters, so I can't retcon them now. The climax of the book is a new case they have to decide, and I want them to be as consistent with their own precedents as a real committee could be, or at least honest about which principle they're adopting if the old cases don't force a decision.

Their precedents as I've written them:

1. Martha, a farmer in her sixties, said her migraines disappeared during a prayer meeting. The committee thanked God and called it an answer to prayer, but voted down wording that labeled it a sign-gift. The ruling noted the absence of medical records and said they could affirm providence without pronouncing on the gifts.

2. A delivery driver spoke in tongues during a Sunday service, and nobody could interpret. The committee didn't call it fake. They said it didn't edify the congregation, and they told him to keep it to his private devotions.

3. An elderly woman dreamed that a missionary's jeep would break down on a mountain road, and the next day it did. The committee gave public thanks for the warning but refused to enter it as a prophecy. Their ruling said a single striking dream wasn't enough for the committee to put its authority behind, even granted that God could speak through dreams.

4. A businessman's advanced pancreatic cancer went into full remission after the elders anointed him with oil and prayed. He had scans before and after. The committee declared it a genuine act of God, but the written ruling deliberately said nothing about the elders having a gift of healing; the elders themselves had not claimed one.

5. A college student gave a word of knowledge that a visitor was hiding a serious sin; the visitor denied it and left angry. The committee called the claim unverifiable and pastorally reckless, and they banned publicly naming strangers' hidden sins.

6. A woman with a local reputation as a healer prayed over a friend's chronic back pain, and the pain stayed gone for a year. The committee said something good had happened, but refused to certify her as a healer because subjective pain relief has too many natural explanations and they didn't want to create a healing celebrity.

Now the new case. Ada is a retired nurse, quiet, well liked. She says the Spirit has given her an ongoing gift of healing, not just a few answered prayers. She's not asking for money or status; she's asking the committee to formally recognize the gift so the church can send people to her with honest expectations. She brings records. Over two years, after she laid hands on people and prayed, three people with independently diagnosed, documented conditions recovered in ways their doctors found hard to explain: a fractured wrist that had refused to knit for months and then bridged within ten days, a thyroid mass that was gone on the follow-up ultrasound, and a case of pneumonia that reversed overnight after the family had been told to prepare for the worst. There were witnesses to the diagnoses and recoveries, and Ada hasn't promoted any of it.

I can hear the two wings of the committee already reading the record differently. The old cessationists say the precedents all point the same way: God heals, prayer is answered, but you never certify an ongoing gift, so Ada gets thanksgiving and nothing else. The younger continuationists say the precedents point their way too: tongues were denied because there was no interpretation, the word of knowledge because it was unverifiable, the back-pain healer because the evidence was subjective, and the businessman's remission wasn't a request to certify a gift. Ada, they say, is the first case with documented, objective, unhyped evidence for an ongoing gift.

So, for my scene: what should the committee decide if it's actually following its own precedents rather than just voting with its tribe? Which earlier ruling is the closest analog? And if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle is the committee adopting either way — and is there a third reading of the record that fits better than either side's story?
