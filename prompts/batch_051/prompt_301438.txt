Write a sestina about the distinction in Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling between the tragic hero, whose sacrifice can be understood, and the knight of faith, Abraham, whose act cannot be understood. Use these six end-words: universal, Isaac, knife, silence, faith, absurd. The sestina's machinery should do the philosophical work. The opening stanzas should let the reader live inside the tragic hero's world: a sacrifice offered for the universal, where the one who gives up what he loves can speak, can be understood, and can be wept over — the world of Agamemnon, Jephthah, and Brutus, as the book sets them beside Abraham. As the same six words recur in new positions, the poem should make the reader feel those categories failing for Abraham. The command that binds him is private and absolute; language, which belongs to the universal, cannot carry it, and the ethical is suspended teleologically — not abolished, but made relative to something that cannot be stated. The poem should render the double movement precisely: first infinite resignation, which gives Isaac up; then the further movement of faith by virtue of the absurd, which receives him back in this life, not as compensation in another. This is not a lesson about obedience or parental love, and the poem should not turn it into one. By the envoi, force the incompatible terms into each other's company, especially knife with Isaac and faith with absurd, and end on the silence that remains after the universal is suspended: not the silence of a confident fanatic, but of one who cannot make himself understood. Someone who knows Fear and Trembling should recognize the suspension and the difference between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith; someone who does not should still encounter the horror and the paradox, not a versified glossary.
