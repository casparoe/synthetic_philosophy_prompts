I've been turning over Arendt's account of political lying—the line she develops in the essay on truth and politics and then pushes further in the Pentagon Papers piece. As I read her, the big claim is roughly this: the traditional lie is parasitic on truth, because the liar has to keep one eye on the facts to keep the story straight, and the lie stays workable only as long as it remains a coherant substitute for some specific reality. The genuinely dangerous move, on her telling, is when lying stops being about hiding or replacing this or that fact and starts trying to replace the shared factual world itself, so that eventually nobody can tell fact from fiction anymore.

So here's what I want to know. Suppose the situation changes in one specifc way. Instead of a political actor or a propaganda ministry sitting down and constructing a coherent fake version of events, you have recommender systems and LLM-generated copy A/B testing millions of micro-claims at once, optimized for engagement rather than for being believed as a coherent story. The aim isn't to get you to accept a particular falsehood and act on it; it's to flood the zone until the difference between true and false stops mattering to people, so that truth just feels like one more scrollable option. And the people running it mostly don't track the truth either—they track metrics.

Does Arendt's argument survive that change? I can see two possibilities and I'm not sure which is right. One: the premise that the liar must keep track of the truth gets undercut, and the argument needs repair—maybe the "liar" is now the engineer optimizing the metric, or the objective function itself, or maybe there's no liar left at all. Two: she already described something close to this as the endpoint of image-making, and algorithmic microtargeting is just a faster way of getting there, in which case the argument survives and the new stuff is mostly engineering detail. Which premise does this development actually bear on, and is the argument dead, repairable, or untouched? I'm asking about the fate of the argument, not for policy recommendations.
