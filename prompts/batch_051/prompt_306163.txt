I keep coming back to an argument I had with a friend about smokers who lecture other people. Suppose a heavy smoker tells a friend, in all seriousness, that she should quit because smoking will wreck her lungs. Even if he's lighting up while he says it, nobody treats this as a standing problem; people shrug and say the advice is correct no matter who gives it. Now suppose the same heavy smoker tells the same person that smoking is wrong, that she's failing a duty to her family by smoking, and he says it with real moral heat. Suddenly people say he has no standing to blame anyone for a habit he hasn't kicked, that he's a hypocrite and should loose the right to lecture. Those two speeches differ mostly in whether they're framed as prudential advise or as moral criticism.

Is that difference enough to justify the asymmetry in how we treat them? I can't tell whether the ordinary reaction is tracking something real. If you think the asymmetry is defensible, state the principle behind it and stress-test it on a nearby case: a tax evader who tells a friend he should file honestly because an audit would be a nightmare, versus the same person saying that hiding income is wrong and that he should be ashamed. Does the principle survive? If you think the asymmetry is not defensible, say which side should move: should the health advice lose its force when it comes from a do-as-I-say-not-as-I-do source, or should the moral blame keep its full force, or neither? In other words, what exactly gives someone standing to blame, and does the moralized/non-moralized line earn its place?
