I have been turning over a tension between a principle of rational agency that I find almost undeniable and a practice I cannot bring myself to treat as a mistake. The principle, which I will call the responsiveness requirement, says that a communicative act addressed to another agent has rational point only if it could make a difference to that agent's response: a request is an attempt to get someone to do or decide something they would not otherwise do, or at least to enter the other's practical reasoning as a reason. If the addressee can neither learn anything from my asking, because they already know, nor be moved by it, because they cannot be moved, then I am not genuinely requesting. I am only using the grammar of request while doing something else, such as expressing myself or working on my own dispositions. The principle earns its keep in ordinary life: it explains why asking is different from hoping, why a request made to someone who cannot hear or cannot act misfires, and why the answerability of the addressee seems built into the speech-act itself. Apply it to an omniscient, immutable God and the conclusion arrives on schedule. God already knows my needs and whether I will ask, and God cannot be informed or causally influenced, so there is nothing for the petitionary component of prayer to accomplish. On this principle, petitionary prayer is either a conceptual confusion or a disguised act of self-edification, with the asking itself demoted to something ornamental.

The intuition on the other side refuses to be managed away. Interceding for a sick friend, or asking for what I cannot bring about myself, presents itself as an act directed at God rather than at my own psychology. It feels like the same species of act as asking another person for help, and I cannot regard the difference between praying and talking to the wall as merely a difference in how each makes me feel. An account on which prayer matters only through its effect on the one who prays seems to lose the addressee, and with it the whole grammar of asking. So I am not looking for a winner; I want a negotiated verdict. Walk me through the costs of each available move, and say what would justify taking it rather than merely describing it: dropping the intuition as anthropomorphic projection and learning to treat petition as self-shaping; amending the responsiveness requirement so that a practice can be rationally pointful without causally influencing its addressee, perhaps by conceding that a relational response can be eternally willed and yet still come only as an answer to the asking; or revising the theology, for instance by denying exhaustive foreknowledge of free decisions or by distinguishing immutability of nature from mutability of relational response. Is there a defensible third reading on which my prayer changes nothing in God while the asking remains exactly what it takes itself to be, and what would have to be true for that reading to be more than verbal consolation?
