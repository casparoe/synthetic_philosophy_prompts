In a seminar on nationalism and invented tradition we read Renan's 1882 lecture 'What Is a Nation?' alongside Hobsbawm and Anderson, and there's a stretch near the middle that keeps getting me muddled. Renan writes: 'Forgetfulness, and I would even say historical error, are essential in the creation of a nation.' Then a little later: 'Yet the essence of a nation is that all individuals have many things in common, and also that they have forgotten many things. No French citizen knows whether he is a Burgundian, an Alan, a Taifale, or a Visigoth, yet every French citizen has to have forgotten the massacre of Saint Bartholomew, or the massacre that took place in the south in the thirteenth century.'

My problem is this: the moment someone names the Saint Bartholomew massacre as something every French citizen 'has to have forgotten', the thing is plainly not forgotten in any ordinary sense, because here is Renan writing and being read, and his audience could presumably look it up. So what kind of forgetting is doing the nation-building work? Is he saying the nation requires everyone to have the same amnesia, or that they share a practice of not letting certain past events count, or something stranger? And is the 'many things in common' meant to be the content of the forgetting, or is forgetting itself the thing they have in common?

Could you take the passage apart, and structure your reply in four named sections: 'Plain restatement' (about 120 words), 'The apparent contradiction' (about 150 words), 'Two possible readings of forgetting' (about 200 words), and 'The reading I'd defend and why' (about 130 words)?
