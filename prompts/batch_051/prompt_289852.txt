Two people in my political theory reading group have been arguing about sortition for over a year, and they've finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration. One of them—the lottocrat—thinks the end state is a standing, randomly selected chamber with real lawmaking power replacing the elected legislature (or demoting it to a weak upper house), with citizens rotating in on staggered terms. The other—the mixed-system democrat—thinks citizens' assemblies and other mini-publics should be used much more aggressively, but always as a complement or check on an elected legislature that keeps final authority. They want a document they can both sign, and I want you to write it.

Produce it as: (1) a numbered list of claims both can endorse, and I don't mean truisms like "democracy is good" — make the claims do work; (2) a disagreements table where each row gives the precise question, the lottocrat's position phrased so she'd happily sign it, and the mixed-system democrat's position phrased so he'd happily sign it; (3) a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that answering one would actually move at least one of them, and for each crux, a concrete case, piece of evidence, or institutional pilot they'd both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Do the hard drafting: find formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted. I suspect some of thier fight is about what "accountability" even means—removability by voters versus having to justify decisions in public versus descriptive representation as its own kind of accountability—and about whether evidence from small, advisory, one-off citizens' assemblies scales to a permanent chamber that controls the agenda and has staff. Also push on whether they actually disagree about the facts (will a randomly selected chamber get captured by its staff? does retrospective voting discipline governments or just punish the economy?) or only about how much institutional risk is tolerable. If part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why, but don't dissolve the real disagreements—make them exact.
