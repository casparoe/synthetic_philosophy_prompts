My neighbor is an amateur philosopher and he lent me his annotated copy of Berkeley's Three Dialogues, and I've been stuck on one passage in the first dialogue for about a week. Philonous is trying to show Hylas that you can't really make sense of something existing without a mind perceiving it. So he challenges Hylas: try to conceive a thing existing unconceived, just pick any object at all and strip away every trace of being seen, felt, heard, imagined, or thought about. Hylas gives it a shot, and Philonous answer is that the attempt gives the game away, because in order to select the object and consider it as existing that way, you've already got it before your mind — the very act of conceiving it makes it conceived. I'm not asking whether Berkeley is right about the whole idealist project. I'm asking about this particular move: arguments that claim a hypothesis cannot even be framed without contradicting itself, because the act of framing is itself the thing the hypothesis denies. Is there a name for that move? Is it a fallacy, a legitimate argument form, or something in between? What would it take for the move to go wrong — like, if someone said "conceive of a number that has never and will never be thought about," is that impossible in the same way, or is that a different case? Where exactly are the edges?
