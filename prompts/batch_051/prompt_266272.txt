You are a philosopher of art who works on the aesthetics of games and interactive media. I would like you to think carefully, starting with some brainstorming, about an asymmetry in the way games get treated as candidates for art.

Notice how critics and players actually talk. When a video game is called art, the features cited are almost always cinematic or literary: the score, the lighting, the writing, the performances. A game built to feel like a film is far more likely to be placed among artistic works than a game that is nothing but rules and choices. Yet players of chess, Go, or Tetris regularly use aesthetic vocabulary — elegant, beautiful, expressive, even tragic — to describe the experience of deciding under constraints, and a well-played match can feel the way a fine performance does. Still, we rarely call a single game of Go, or a session of Tetris, a work of art, and the aesthetic praise video games receive usually attaches to their non-interactive parts. The asymmetry, then, is this: games seem to be admitted to art chiefly in virtue of what they share with older art forms, while the very thing that distinguishes them — the player's agency — is treated as aesthetically inert or second-class.

I want you to ask whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry. Brainstorm the strongest candidate principles that would draw the line in this way: perhaps art must be the product of an author's intentional expression, not a player's ongoing choices; perhaps works of art must be contemplatable objects rather than processes; perhaps agency yields pleasure and meaning but not aesthetic value proper. State each candidate fairly, test it against troublesome cases — improvised theatre, participatory dance, architecture, competitive figure skating, and a hypothetical game with no graphics or sound whose choices are nonetheless profound — and then give an honest verdict. Should we keep the asymmetry, revise it, or abandon it? If you defend it, defend it against its strongest counterexample; if you reject it, say what a worked-out aesthetics of agency would need to show.
