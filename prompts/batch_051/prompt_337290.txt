I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether we can properly be said to learn things from chatbots. A philosopher I interveiwed pointed me to the Indian epistemological tradition, and I've become a bit obssesed with one question I want to get right. In Nyāya epistemology, there are four accepted sources of knowledge (pramāṇas): perception, inference, analogy, and testimony, śabda. The interesting one here is śabda: roughly, a sentence produced by a competent, trustworthy person (an āpta) can give you knowledge when you understand what they said and have no defeating reason to doubt them. The standard cases are a reliable person telling you the way to the village, or a doctor telling you which herb to take.

Now here is my question. Suppose Gaṅgeśa — the later Nyāya philosopher who gave the most sophisticated statement of these ideas — were shown a modern large language model, one that has been carefully fine-tuned to be highly accurate in some domain, like a medical chatbot that clears the bar for clinical accuracy in most cases. What would he say about it? Would he classify its outputs as śabda in his sense, as something else, or would he deny that the question even arises because there is no speaker behind the words? I want you to actually reconstruct his verdict, not just list considerations on both sides. Work through the specific machinery: the āpta requirement (does the chatbot know? does it matter that it has no intentions?), the understanding requirement, the no-defeaters condition, and whether the Naiyāyikas would have room for a non-personal source of testimony at all. And be candid about where the reconstruction is soft: tell me which consideration would most plausibly push the verdict the other way, or which step in your reasoning is most likely to fail.
