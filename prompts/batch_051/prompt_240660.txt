I'm a novelist working on a multigenerational novel that jumps between periods, and I keep falling into the same shape no matter where I set a chapter. Here's the pattern I can't get away from. A state or empire starts off doing okay, maybe overstretched but basically stable. Then, over a generation or two, the number of ambitious well-off families grows a lot — cousins multiply, everyone expects a title or an office — while the number of good posts doesn't grow to match. So the political class starts tearing at itself, factions form, the treasury gets drained, and within another generation there's a wave of rebellions or civil war. Then things consolidate under a new strongman, and the cycle starts over. I first plotted this for a stretch set in 17th-century Spain, then caught myself doing nearly the same thing for one set in Han China, and then a third time for a made-up kingdom on another continent. Half of it is invention, so I can't tell how much of this is real history and how much is just narrative lazyness.

My question is whether this thing — elites multiplying faster than the positions they compete for, politics turning poisonous, state finances collapsing, and finally a big burst of internal violence and reconsolidation — has an established name, and whether there's a serious version of it rather than just a novelist's hunch. I vaguely remember hearing that someone, somewhere, tried to quantify cycles like this, but I don't know what the idea is called. Don't just give me a label, though. What I actually need to know is how the idea is carved up. Is it supposed to be a predictive cycle, or just a loose recurring pattern? How is it different from the ordinary idea that history just repeats itself, or from the kind of history that looks at long slow structures like geography, climate, and population? And where are its edges — under what circumstances is the cycle supposed to fail or dissappear? If there isn't one name, say so and tell me which nearby idea fits best.
