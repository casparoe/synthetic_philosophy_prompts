I keep ending up in arguments about Atlantis and ancient-aliens style history, and I've noticed a two-step move I don't have a word for. Step one, the person presents the claim in full scholarly costume: footnotes, a journal with an editorial board, appeals to carbon dating, translations of texts, the works. Step two, when an actual historian or archaeologist pushes back, the costume flips, and now the mainstream is a closed guild that suppresses uncomfortable truths, and the fact that experts reject the theory gets treated as evidence that the theory is onto something. I saw it happen in an Atlantis documentary: first it was all sonar scans and earnest talk about Plato giving exact dimensions, then as soon as a classicist came on screen the voiceover shifted to how the experts have to ignore it because it would rewrite everything they've published. It isn't plain pseudoscience, because the battlefield is historical method rather than experiment, and it isn't exactly the Galileo gambit, because the Galileo gambit is only the second step. Is there an established name for this specific borrowing-then-inverting-authority pattern? If there isn't, coin one, define it, and carefully distinguish it from the Galileo gambit, denialism, and pseudoscience in general. I won't be satisfied with a label; I want a precise statement of what the pattern is, what it isn't, and a rough test for when a piece of fringe history has crossed into it.

1. The pattern, stated precisely — about 150 words.
2. Nearest existing concepts — for each one, say where it overlaps the pattern and where it misses, about 250 words.
3. A name and a definition — either the best-fitting existing term (with a warning about what it misses) or a coined one, about 150 words.
4. Edges and a test — boundary cases and a rough test for when something counts as this pattern and when it doesn't, about 150 words.
