In multi-agent safety and competition policy, I keep encountering the following argument. When autonomous pricing systems learn to hold prices above the competitive level without any explicit instruction — and even learn to punish deviations, despite no one having told them to coordinate — the standard response is that this is an old problem in a new substrate: human oligopolists have long sustained the same outcomes through tacit collusion, or what competition lawyers call conscious parallelism. What took rival firms years of repeated interaction to learn, the systems acquire in days; everything else — observation of rivals' prices, the logic of punishment and return, the shared interest in avoiding a price war, the stable supracompetitive equilibrium — is said to carry over. The practical conclusion is that we should not build new conceptual machinery for algorithmic collusion. We should use the inherited legal categories and evidentiary tests, adjusted only for the speed and transparency of computation.

I would like you to audit that analogy, with particular attention to the conclusion it is being made to support. Do the following:

1. Produce an explicit correspondence table: what in the algorithmic case is meant to correspond to the firms, the pricing managers, the repeated game, the observation of rivals' prices, the punishment strategy, and the final collusive equilibrium in the human case?

2. Identify the points where the mapping breaks or is being stretched. Address at least these: (a) the legal distinction between an agreement and mere parallel conduct, and what has to hold of the human case to cross it; (b) whether the systems can be said to know or intend the scheme; (c) whether observing and responding to prices amounts to communication in each case; (d) differences in memory, speed, patience, and robustness to stochastic shocks; and (e) the contrast between interrogating a human manager under oath and reconstructing a learned policy from weights and logs.

3. For each disanalogy you identify, say whether it matters for the specific conclusion that the inherited categories and tests transfer with only minor adjustments, or whether it is a difference without legal or conceptual significance.

4. Say what the analogy quietly imports from the human case — for instance, an agent whose state of mind and understanding can be established — and whether the algorithmic case supplies anything corresponding to it.

5. Give a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying it? If the analogy should be replaced, say what a better source-analogy would be and what it would license.
