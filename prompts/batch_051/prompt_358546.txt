I'm a novelist, three years deep into a book about pricing algorithms that learn to keep prices high without ever being told to. The novel is the thing, but I've ended up with an argumentative essay's worth of views on it, and next month I'm giving a short talk at a competition-policy conference where the audience is mostly antitrust economists, a few regulators from a competition authority, and a couple of industry-friendly tech-policy people. The talk defends something close to this: our collusion toolbox still assumes a meeting — an agreement, a wink, a smoke-filled room — and because of that we're going to sleepwalk into a world where pricing algorithms reach the same result with no meeting at all. So when a firm turns price-setting over to an autonomous learning system in a concentrated market, the firm should carry the burden of showing the system isn't built to collude, rather than the regulator having to prove afterwards that it did. My argument, roughly: (1) reinforcement-learning pricing agents can converge on supracompetitive prices through trial and error with no explicit communication — the lab setups where two learning agents set prices against each other and drift toward the high-price equilibrium, because they learn that matching beats undercutting; (2) the harm is the same as old-fashioned price-fixing even though theres no email, no phone call, no 'gentleman's agreement'; (3) the existing categories miss exactly this, since parallel pricing without agreement — conscious parallelism — is legal, and the EU's 'concerted practice' idea still reaches for something more than a learned equilibrium; (4) so responsibility has to attach earlier, to the decision to deploy the thing and to its design, not to what it later 'agreed' to do. I know there are holes big enough to drive a truck through, and I'd rather hear about them before the Q&A than during it. Draft me a prep sheet for the question period: eight or nine objections, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way an antitrust economist or regulator would actually put them (not the way an AI-safety person would), each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is softest. Tell me which one or two I should concede up front, which reply has to land for the talk to survive, and include the question I'll be praying nobody asks.
