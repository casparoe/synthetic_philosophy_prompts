I am a PhD student in the philosophy of mathematics, and I am drafting a short paper around a single question: whether 2 is an element of 4. Below are my working notes. I would be grateful for comments on the argument—especially on whether it is sound, whether it mischaracterizes structuralism or the two classical reductions, and where a skeptical reader would press hardest.

Working title: Is 2 an Element of 4?

My starting point is the familiar observation that the two best-known set-theoretic reductions of arithmetic answer this question differently. In the von Neumann reduction, where 0 is the empty set and n + 1 is n ∪ {n}, the number 4 is the set containing 0, 1, 2, and 3, so 2 is an element of 4. In the Zermelo reduction, where n + 1 is {n}, the number 4 is the singleton containing 3, so 2 is not an element of 4. Both reductions satisfy the Peano axioms and support the usual arithmetic operations, so arithmetic by itself does not settle which answer is correct.

The structuralist response is usually put this way: the natural numbers are not identified with any one of these set-theoretic implementations; they are positions in the abstract structure common to all of them, and facts about set membership are accidents of a particular coding. I want to sharpen this into a claim about the question itself. On an intra-reductional reading, the question asks whether, within some specified reduction, the object occupying the 2-role stands in that reduction's membership relation to the object occupying the 4-role. That question is clear, and its answer is reduction-relative. On a cross-structural reading, the question asks whether the number 2 itself stands in a genuine membership relation to the number 4 itself, independently of any reduction. I argue that the structuralist should reject this second reading as ill-posed, rather than answer it in the negative: it assumes that numbers carry intrinsic set-theoretic natures over and above their structural role, and the structuralist is precisely denying that there are such natures.

My worry is that this rejection may be too quick. If 2 and 4 are positions in a structure, and positions are allowed to be objects, then someone can ask, without any obvious confusion, why the 2-position and the 4-position do or do not stand in the membership relation. The structuralist needs a non-question-begging explanation of why some relations count as structural while membership does not. I can see a natural line: the structural relations are those definable from the successor function that characterizes the natural-number structure, and set membership is not among them. But I am not sure that criterion can be motivated without already treating successor as more fundamental than membership, which is part of what was to be shown.

I would especially like comments on the following. (a) Does my intra- vs. cross-structural distinction do real work, or does it merely relabel the familiar point that membership facts are relative to a reduction? (b) Is there a better, non-question-begging way for the structuralist to explain why less-than is structural and membership is not? (c) Does my own language of roles and positions tacitly import the idea that the natural-number structure is itself a set-like entity, thereby recreating the problem it is meant to dissolve? (d) Are there readings of the original question, other than the two I distinguish, that I have overlooked? Please be direct: if the paper's central move fails, I would rather find out now than after I build more around it.
