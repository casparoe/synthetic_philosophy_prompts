I work as a software engineer, and my logic background comes mostly from programming-language theory and computability — fixed points, the halting problem, the Y combinator — rather than from academic philosophy. I recently read a blog post that lays out Curry's paradox and Yablo's paradox side by side, and I want to know whether the post's account is fair before I absorb its conclusion. I can follow a derivation, but I don't know the philosophical landscape well enough to tell where the summary is standard and where it starts to slide.

Here is how the post summarizes the material, as I understand it:

"Curry's paradox is what you get when you replace the liar's self-negation with a conditional. Let C be the sentence: if C is true, then 0 = 1. Suppose C is true. Then what C says is the case, so from 'C is true' and C we get 0 = 1 by modus ponens. Since 0 = 1 was derived from the assumption that C is true, we can conclude the conditional 'if C is true, then 0 = 1'; but that conditional just is C, so C is true after all, and one more application of modus ponens gives 0 = 1 unconditionally. Notice that the argument never uses negation, contradiction, or the law of excluded middle. This is usually taken to show that the liar paradox was never really about truth or falsity; the real problem is a self-referential sentence interacting with the ordinary logic of the conditional. The standard fix is therefore to give up classical principles for 'if...then' — or, if you are a dialetheist, to accept some true contradictions while weakening the logic enough that Curry's argument cannot get through."

The post then continues:

"Yablo showed that self-reference is not even needed. Consider the infinite sequence S1, S2, S3, ... where each S_n says: every later sentence S_m, for m > n, is false. If any S_n is true, then all later sentences are false; but S_{n+1} says that all of its later sentences are false, and since those are exactly a subset of the false ones, S_{n+1} is true — contradiction. So no S_n is true. But then all later sentences are false, which is exactly what each S_n says, so every S_n is true — contradiction again. And nowhere does any sentence refer to itself. So self-reference was never the real culprit; the culprit is the unrestricted T-schema, or unrestricted truth-talk, itself. In particular, any solution to the liar that blocks self-reference is dead, because Yablo produces the same paradox without it. What is left is to give up classical logic for the truth predicate, or to restrict truth-talk so severely that infinite chains like Yablo's cannot even be formed."

I could follow most of the derivations, but the closing moves felt too strong to me, and I suspect the word "false" is doing some unnoticed work in the Yablo section that "not true" might not do. Could you take the summary apart claim by claim and tell me: which parts would a philosopher of logic accept as standard, which parts are subtly wrong or overstated, how Curry's argument and Yablo's argument are actually related, and whether the author's conclusion — that self-reference-based solutions are dead and only global restrictions on truth-talk or classical logic remain — actually follows from the accurate parts?
