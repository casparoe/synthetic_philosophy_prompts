Suppose all we know is that a warehouse burned down and either the faulty wiring or a known arsonist who was in town was responsible, one or the other. Everyone will say: "If it wasn't the wiring, it was the arsonist." Almost nobody will say: "If it hadn't been the wiring, it would've been the arsonist" — that one sounds wrong unless we'd already settled that the arsonist was poised to act even if the wiring held up. The two sentences look like they differ only in tense and mood, just indicative versus counterfactual. Is the asymmetry in how we treat them defensible, or is one of our judgments doing too much or too little work? Give the best account you can of what the counterfactual adds over the indicative, and then say whether we should keep treating the pair differently or make the same inference in both cases. Test your account against a harder case of your own construction.
