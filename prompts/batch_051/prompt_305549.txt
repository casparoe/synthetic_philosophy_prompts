You are a legal theorist and philosopher of technology with deep familiarity with corporate law, tort law, and debates about legal personhood. Please think carefully, brainstorm several lines of analysis before committing, and then give a rigorous, organized response.

A jurisdiction is considering the following legal institution, which I will call Registered Artificial Personhood (RAP). Under RAP, any AI system that both (1) acts with a degree of autonomy above a regulatory threshold in legally consequential settings — for example, entering contracts, trading, driving, making credit or benefits decisions — and (2) has either been involved in an incident above a defined severity level or is projected to generate liability exposure above a defined annual amount, must be registered as a separate legal person, on the model of a corporation. The registration imposes these elements:

1. The AI receives its own segregated asset pool, capitalized by a mandatory contribution from the deploying firm proportional to projected liability exposure, plus a small per-transaction levy that funds a public no-fault compensation fund.
2. The human owners, operators, and developers enjoy limited liability for the AI's conduct: they can be sued for the AI's actions only up to their contributed capital, with two statutory exceptions modeled on veil piercing — deliberate misconfiguration, and reckless disregard of known failure modes after an incident report.
3. The AI can be sued, hold assets, and carry liability insurance in its own name. Judgments are satisfied first from its asset pool, then from the compensation fund, and never from the owners' personal assets beyond the veil-piercing exceptions.
4. Registration is renewed annually. Renewal requires the deploying firm to buy or replenish liability coverage, with premiums set by a regulatory board using a published scoring system that weights the system's degree of autonomy, the reversibility of its actions, the number of counterparties it can affect in a single run, and its incident history. If insurers decline to price the risk, the system may not legally be deployed in the jurisdiction.

Analyze how this institution would actually behave if enacted, rather than whether it is good in the abstract. Specifically:

- Who are the early winners and losers, and why?
- What behavior does it incentivize for developers, plaintiffs' lawyers, insurers, and the regulator — including the predictable gaming: classifying systems as below the autonomy threshold or as merely "supervised," thin capitalization of AI asset pools, structuring corporate groups to shift liability into separately registered AI entities, forum shopping across jurisdictions, and treating the public compensation fund as a subsidy for activities that private insurers will not price?
- Where are the likely failure modes, and which of them are fixable by amendment as opposed to endemic to the project of using a corporate-law template for an agent that cannot deliberate, fear liability, be deterred, or be punished?
- Finally, evaluate how much of the corporate-person analogy is doing the real work here: in corporate law, limited liability and asset partitioning solve coordination and monitoring problems among human stakeholders; does the same rationale survive when the "person" is a mindless system, or does the scheme collapse into a disguised subsidy and liability shield?
