ok so we've been doing hedonism in my value theory class and the lecturer keeps saying the whole view hangs on there being one thing, pleasure, that all good things have in common. but whenever I try to actually say what pleasure is, my verdicts on individual cases keep pulling apart. here are my cases:

- Biting into a really good meal when you're starving: 100% pleasure, paradigm case, no question.
- A bad headache suddenly lifting: I also want to call this a pleasure — the relief is one of the best feelings there is — but it's weird because nothing positive arrives, the pain just stops.
- Lying in a quiet dark room, no pain, no hunger, no particular thoughts: I do not want to call this pleasure, it's just the absence of bad stuff. but then I don't know how to seperate this from the headache case, since both are "the bad thing is gone."
- The exact moment a hard proof finally clicks: pleasure, definately, but it's not a bodily feeling at all, it's more like a sudden brightening.
- Feeling proud because your younger sibling won an award: pleasure, but again it's not in my body, it's a thought I keep going back to.
- A long steady runner's high: pleasure, no question, but it's diffuse across the whole body rather than a localized feeling like the meal.
- A song that's really sad but that I love and choose to listen to: I want to say this is pleasure, but "feels good" is just the wrong description — it's more like being absorbed in something intense.
- Looking forward to a trip next week: the anticipation itself is pleasant now, even though the good thing hasn't happened; weird because the pleasure is entirely about an absent future.

My tentative principle is that pleasure is an episode with a positive felt quality, a kind of "feels good," that I'd want to keep going. That gets the meal, the proof, the runner's high. But it fails on the headache relief (no positive quality, just removal), the quiet room (if relief counts, why doesn't this?), the sad song (doesn't feel good), and maybe anticipation (the good is absent).

So: give me the best principle you can that captures my verdicts, stated precisely. You can give me one principle, or two or three candidate principles to choose between. Tell me what the principle says about further cases I haven't considered — the calm after meditating, plain boredom, the satisfaction of scratching an itch, a compliment I didn't want — and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to ditch or put in a different category.
