I teach A Christmas Carol in a first-year seminar on poverty and social criticism, and I want to use it to do real philosophical work rather than as a piece of seasonal moralizing. The debate I have in mind is the distinction between the deserving and undeserving poor: the thought that help is not simply owed to someone in need, but must be earned by being the right kind of poor — industrious, blameless for one's misfortune, properly grateful. Versions of this thought run through present-day arguments about workfare, benefit fraud, and conditionality. Dickens's target is plainly the harshest form of it: the Scrooge of the opening stave, who would leave the destitute to the prisons and workhouses and who remarks that those who would rather die had better do it and decrease the surplus population. Yet the poor the story actually trains our pity on are the hardworking Cratchits, and the central drama is the conversion of the giver rather than any claim made by the receiver. I am unsure how much the story really challenges the idea it seems to attack.

Act as a moral and political philosopher with a serious interest in literary form. Begin by laying out the distinct positions one could plausibly impute to the novella — for instance, that charity should be unconditional, or that it is owed only to the industrious poor, or that the novel's real subject is the redemption of the rich rather than the entitlement of the poor — before defending the reading you judge best supported by the text. Point me to the specific scenes you would teach, such as Scrooge's initial refusal, the glimpse of Bob Cratchit and Tiny Tim, Fred's defense of the season, and the two children, Ignorance and Want, whom the Ghost of Christmas Present reveals. Say explicitly where the story resists or complicates your reading, and close by telling me what this novella adds to a philosophical treatment of conditionality that a well-argued essay would miss.
