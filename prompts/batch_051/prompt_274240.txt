A recent article I read summarizes John Maynard Keynes's 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren, and I would like help deciding whether the summary is fair. According to the article, Keynes was writing in the depths of the Depression and argued that the economic problem of producing enough to meet basic needs would be solved within roughly a century, so that by about 2030 people in advanced economies would need to work only about fifteen hours a week. The article says Keynes regarded the era of moneymaking as a necessary but morally ugly stage, and that he dismissed the love of money as a semi-criminal, semi-pathological tendency that a saner future would outgrow. It then claims that, because Keynes thought growth would make this vision possible, he urged policies to accelerate the transition, discouraging saving and investment in favor of leisure, the arts, and the cultivation of life. Finally, the article reports that Keynes was optimistic about the adjustment: the wealthy had already learned how to use free time well, and the rest of society would catch on. The author closes by noting that we still work much more than fifteen hours and that many households still struggle to meet ordinary needs, and concludes that Keynes's core claims were simply mistaken. I suspect the closing inference is too fast, but I do not know enough about the essay to isolate what the summary gets right and wrong. Go through the summary claim by claim, distinguish what Keynes actually says from what the article overstates or misattributes, and say whether the author's conclusion survives once the record is corrected.
