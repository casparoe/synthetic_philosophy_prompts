I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the moral theory end of things, and I've been going in circles on the paradox of deontology—the old problem that a deontologist seems to be committed both to the badness of violations (say, killings of innocent people) and to a prohibition on minimizing exactly those violations. I want to get clearer on whether the deontological side can actually hold together, so I'd like you to write a dialogue between two evenly matched philosophers about it. One defends a deontological constraint: it is wrong to kill one innocent person even when doing so would prevent five other innocent people from being killed. The other is a consequentialist who keeps pressing the obvious-sounding objection: if the badness of innocent people being killed is what makes the act wrong, then a constraint that forbids you from minimizing killings looks self-undermining; but if what really matters is that you not be the one doing the killing, the constraint starts to look like a fetish about your own moral purity.

The deontologist needs real, non-strawman answers here—agent-relative reasons that don't reduce to the badness of an outcome, the idea that constraints govern what I may do rather than which state of affairs I must bring about, a doing/allowing or intending/foreseeing distinction, something about the separateness of persons or treating persons as ends-in-themselves—and the consequentialist should respond to the specific moves the deontologist actually makes, not just restate the original objection. I want genuine back-and-forth, where each reply registers the force of the previous one, and ideally by the end they've located their deepest disagreement instead of talking past eachother. It would be nicer still if they converge on the nonmoral facts and the real dispute turns out to be about the structure of reasons, but only if that emerges naturally. You can keep it abstract or use a concrete case; if you use one, avoid the most shopworn transplant/trolley versions if something else would work. Keep it a focused dialogue—think two people arguing late at night, not a term paper—and don't have them cite literature at each other; I want the arguments to stand on their own.
