Here is a parallel I have been turning over and cannot decide about. An archive is not a neutral sample of a population; it records the intersections between lives and record-keeping institutions — courts, tax rolls, censuses, property deeds, parish registers. Absence from the archive is therefore a non-detection, not evidence of absence, and we reconstruct the unrecorded largely through their effects on the recorded: a prohibition that betrays a practice, a complaint in a court file that lets us infer a social world around it. This looks to me structurally identical to the way astronomy infers matter that emits no light: dark matter is reconstructed from its gravitational effects on the matter we can see. Both are inferences from a biased, interaction-limited sample to a population that does not register directly, and both read indirect effects in the visible part as the signature of the hidden part. Is this a deep structural identity — one general problem of inference under selection effects — or only a loose metaphor? One disanalogy worries me: in astronomy the relation between the visible and the invisible is law-governed and uniform, so the residuals can be modeled; in archives the silence is produced by power, literacy, property, and deliberate destruction, so the missing are not a neutral residue but an actively shaped one. Does that difference break the analogy, or is it just the difference between a natural and a social selection effect? And if the parallel is real, what would have to be true on the archival side for the astronomy side's move — estimating a hidden population from the traces it leaves in the observed one — to transfer?
