The multiple realizability argument was supposed to be the knockdown against the mind-brain identity theory: a human, an octopus, and a hypothetical silicon alien could all be in the same mental state—pain is the usual example—while their physical setups are wildly different, so the mental state must be a functional kind rather than a physical kind, and functionalism became the default story. The argument did a lot of work with imagined cases, because you could just stipulate that the beings were functionally the same. Now there are systems nobody stipulated: large language models and other trained networks match an enormous amount of human verbal behavior of the sort we normally take as evidence of beliefs, reasoning, and so on, while sharing no designed architecture with a brain and sometimes not even a stable architecture with themselves across training runs. I'm not asking whether these systems are conscious, or whether the mentalistic talk is literally true; I want to know what their existence does to the argument. On one read, this is a genuine built case of multiple realizability—human-ish functional profile, totally different substrate. On another read, the case is so messy (same behavior, no obvious shared functional description, different internals run to run) that the whole same-mental-state-different-physical-state premise gets too fuzzy to support the functionalist conclusion. Does the argument survive, need repair, or is it dead? Identify which premise the new systems put pressure on, and if there's a repaired version that still gets you to functionalism, say what it has to give up.
