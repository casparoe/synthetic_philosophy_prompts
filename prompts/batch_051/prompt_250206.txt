I am an undergraduate taking a course on intergenerational justice, and I have been turning over an argument that came up in discussion. I cannot decide whether it is valid, and if it is valid, which premise I should give up. I would like you to assess it step by step.

Suppose a country must choose between two long-term energy policies. Under Policy A it continues to rely on coal; under Policy B it builds wind capacity. Assume, for the sake of the argument, that the choice makes no difference to the welfare of anyone presently alive: the two policies differ only in what they bequeath to the distant future. Under Policy A, the population two centuries from now lives in a hotter, more polluted environment, and its members have lives that are on the whole worth living but worse than the lives that would have been lived under Policy B. Assume, too, that the two policies would lead to different people existing: because the policies affect long-run patterns of investment, settlement, and family formation, the people who exist in the distant future under Policy A share no members with the people who would have existed under Policy B.

Now consider the following argument:

(1) An action is morally wrong only if it makes at least one person worse off than they would otherwise have been.

(2) A person is made worse off by an action only if they are worse off than they would have been had the action not been performed.

(3) Take any person who exists under Policy A. Had Policy A not been chosen, that person would never have existed. Since their life is worth living, they are not worse off than they would have been in that alternative, even if their life is worse than the lives of the people who would have existed under Policy B.

(4) Thus Policy A makes no person in the distant future worse off, and by hypothesis it makes no one currently alive worse off.

(5) So Policy A makes no one worse off than they would otherwise have been.

(6) Therefore, by (1), Policy A is not morally wrong.

This conclusion seems wrong. If one policy will leave every future person worse off than the future people under the alternative would be, and the two policies are otherwise identical, choosing the worse policy seems like a serious moral failure. Is the argument valid? If it is not, identify the exact step at which it fails and explain why. If it is valid, identify the premise you regard as least defensible and say what principle you would put in its place.
