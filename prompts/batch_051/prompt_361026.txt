Consider the letter-string analogy problems made famous by the Copycat program of Douglas Hofstadter and Melanie Mitchell: problems of the form P → Q; T → ?, where the change that carries P to Q must be mapped onto T. Work throughout within the microdomain fixed by the base example abc → abd. In the base case, abc is a rising run of three consecutive letters, and in it the rightmost printed letter and the high end of the run coincide; the change replaces that letter, c, with its alphabetic successor, d.

The following four kinds of conceptual slippage in this microdomain are, as far as I know, the ones usually distinguished. For each I give a target in which one concept in the base rule deforms.

1. Unit slippage. Target: iijjkk. The source letters a, b, c map to the blocks ii, jj, kk, whose letters are consecutive in the alphabet. The item c maps to the entire block kk, and successor-of-a-letter maps to successor-of-a-block; the result is iijjll, with kk replaced by ll. The unit acted on slips from a single letter to a whole group of equal letters.

2. Alignment slippage. Target: mrrjjj. The target consists of blocks of lengths 1, 2, and 3, so the ordered three-step structure of abc is carried here not by consecutive alphabet letters but by consecutive group lengths. Thus a maps to m, b to rr, and c to jjj, and the replacement of c by d maps to the replacement of jjj by kkk, yielding mrrkkk. The dimension along which the source's three items are aligned with the target slips from alphabetic succession to group-length succession.

3. Orientation slippage. Target: kji. The target is a falling run mirroring abc, and in it the high end of the run, k, lies at the left of the printed string rather than the right. The target item that plays the role of c is therefore not the rightmost printed letter but the letter at the run's high end; replacing that letter with its successor gives lji. The concept rightmost slips from rightmost in print to rightmost in the run's intrinsic order.

4. Open-end slippage. Target: xyz. The target is a rising run like abc, but its top letter, z, has no alphabetic successor, so the run cannot be extended at its top. Guided by the same deeper operation, extend the run at an open end, the lower end is now chosen, and the move that adds a letter there is the predecessor of x; replacing x with w yields wyz. The concept the extendable end slips from the top to the bottom, and the generating move slips from successor to predecessor.

I want a fifth kind of conceptual slippage that this microdomain can exhibit, genuinely different from these four. It must not be a restatement, a special case, or a combination of them, and it should put pressure on some concept in the base rule other than the unit acted on, the dimension of alignment, the orientation of order, and the choice of extension end with its direction — or it should show why those four do not exhaust the ways a concept can slip here.

Please provide: (i) an abstract statement of the fifth slippage; (ii) a concrete target string on which it arises, together with the answer you propose and the mapping that produces it; and (iii) an honest assessment of whether the slippage is a natural feature of the microdomain or merely an artifact of its description. Finally, state how confident you are in the proposal and single out the specific point you are least sure about.
