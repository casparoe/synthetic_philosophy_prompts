Leibniz argues in the Monadology, section 9, that two substances can't be perfectly alike, because then there would be no internal difference—nothing in their intrinsic denominations—to distinguish them. I'm trying to figure out whether this principle is actually refuted by the standard treatment of identical quantum particles, or whether people just mean something different by 'indiscernible' there. In quantum mechanics, two electrons seem to be indistinguishable in a very strong sense: swapping their labels can't change the state, and you have to symmetrize or antisymmetrize. But is that a violation of Leibniz's principle, or does the formalism still allow that each electron has its own path or internal condition, just not one accessible to us? And what kind of difference does Leibniz need—something intrinsic to the substances themselves, not merely relational or epistemic. So I want an explanation of what the identity of indiscernibles really says, what 'indiscernible' means in the quantum case, and whether quantum particles are a genuine exception or just a misapplication of the principle. Be precise about the difference between epistemic indistinguishability and metaphysical identity.
