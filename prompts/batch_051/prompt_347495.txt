I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I keep returning to an argument at work that seems to depend on how far one analogy can be pushed. I would like an audit of that analogy rather than project-management advice.

Here is the situation. Nine months ago our team began migrating a legacy payments service onto a maintained open-source framework. The original estimate was three months. Every month we hold a go/no-go review, and at every review, finishing the migration is estimated to take another four to six weeks, while rolling back to the legacy system is estimated to take eight to ten weeks, because we have already deleted compatibility layers, changed configuration, and rebuilt runbooks around the new system. So at each checkpoint, continuing looks cheaper than reverting, and we continue. But total spend has now passed any value we can credibly attach to the completed migration; the completion date keeps receding; and each milestone keeps surfacing new integration problems. I have defended each individual go decision as rational on the evidence available at that checkpoint.

My colleague says the project has become a dollar auction. His model, as I understand it, runs as follows. An auctioneer offers a one-dollar bill to the highest bidder, with the rule that the second-highest bidder must also pay their final bid but receives nothing. Two bidders raise each other in small increments. Once both have sunk money, the underbidder faces a choice between losing that sunk amount for nothing, or bidding one more increment to keep alive a chance of winning while capping the loss. Each bid is therefore locally rational, but the process drives the bids well past the value of the prize. His conclusion is that our monthly go decisions are rational in the same narrow sense, and that this is exactly why we are now in a position where every available continuation of the project is bad. He thinks the fix is not to reason better at each checkpoint but to change the decision procedure: precommit to a hard cost cap, or give an external owner authority to kill the project, because checkpoint-by-checkpoint reasoning cannot escape the auction.

Audit this analogy, not the facts of our project. Please give me (1) an explicit mapping between the auction's elements and our situation—the auctioneer, the dollar bill, the two bidders, the fixed increments, the rule that the loser pays, and the moment the bids pass the value of the prize; (2) where that mapping breaks, separating disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from ones that do not; (3) which features the auction quietly imports—fixed prize value, certain costs, no new information, a rival bidder, inability to precommit, fully fungible costs—and whether each holds in an engineering setting; and (4) a judgment on whether the conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the software case has features such as new information at each checkpoint, reusable components, genuine rollback costs, option value, and organizational incentives that make continued investment fully rational past the naive break-even point. I also want to know whether our case is better handled as ordinary escalation of commitment with sunk costs than as a dollar auction, and whether the auction model contributes anything beyond that. If the dollar auction is the wrong model for our case, say what a better analogy would be, and also say where the dollar auction might be more apt than my colleague has argued.
