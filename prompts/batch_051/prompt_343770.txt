I work as a software engineer, mostly on caching and distributed systems, and I have been trying to bring that vocabulary to a set of questions about democratic representation. I have no formal background in political philosophy, so I may be reinventing something already well understood. I want an honest assessment.

I call the idea the civic cache model. Its central claim is that a legislature behaves like a cache of the electorate's preferences, and that the familiar disputes over term limits, sortition, and career politicians are best understood as disputes about eviction and refresh policy for that cache.

In the model, an election is a read: it stores a snapshot of a district's expressed preferences at election time, and the representative then votes from that stored snapshot. Between elections, the stored value degrades in two ways. First, staleness: the electorate's preferences continue to drift, so the snapshot grows out of date. Second, pollution: donors, lobbyists, and party leadership supply high-frequency inputs that overwrite parts of the snapshot between official reads; because these writes occur far more often than elections, they gradually dominate the stored value. I call these dirty writes.

Re-election is a refresh, but an unreliable one. It reads a value that the incumbent has partly produced through agenda control and constituency service, so the refresh can preserve pollution rather than purge it. Term limits are hard eviction: a fixed time-to-live invalidates an entry after some number of terms. That bounds staleness and pollution, but it also evicts still-useful entries and causes compulsory cache misses, such as inexperienced legislators and lost procedural knowledge. Sortition, selecting the legislature by lot, I model as randomized eviction. It makes the eviction schedule unpredictable, so an actor trying to capture seats cannot direct resources at whichever entry is about to expire, and it replaces the incumbent set with a fresh random sample.

If the model does real work, it should make at least one non-obvious prediction. I think it predicts this: on an issue that emerges after a representative's first election, a long-serving incumbent will track her district's current views worse than a newly selected citizen would, holding technical competence aside, because the incumbent is answering from an older and more heavily overwritten cache.

Please assess the model candidly. Is this a real theory, or is the caching machinery only re-describing familiar points? What is the closest existing position, and does it already make the points I think I am making? Where exactly does the analogy break—what does it systematically misdescribe about representation, elections, or sortition? And is the prediction about late-emerging issues a genuine consequence of the model, or have I smuggled in an assumption the model does not license?
