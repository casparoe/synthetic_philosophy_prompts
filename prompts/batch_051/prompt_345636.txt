I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long-form piece about how popular film shapes, and sometimes distorts, public thinking about whether a company, as distinct from its employees, can be morally blamed for the harm it causes. I plan to anchor one section in a close reading of Steven Soderbergh's Erin Brockovich (2000), which dramatizes Pacific Gas & Electric's contamination of the groundwater in Hinkley, California, with hexavalent chromium.

I'd like a reading of the film through the philosophical debate over corporate moral agency. In particular: some philosophers argue that a corporation can be more than a mere aggregation of individuals, because its internal decision procedures can generate decisions and policies that no single employee made; others hold that moral blame must always bottom out in the choices of particular people, and that talk of corporate intentions is at best a metaphor. Read the film against those positions. Where does it seem to treat PG&E as a moral agent in its own right, an entity that conceals documents, manages legal exposure, and sustains a pattern of behavior across changes of personnel, and where does it undercut that reading by focusing the drama on individual choices or by personifying the company as a single calculating villain? It may also matter that the case in the film ends in a negotiated settlement rather than a trial and conviction; what does that do to the question of blame? Consider, too, what blame requires: must a blameworthy agent be able to feel guilt or hear reproach, or is it enough that it can form and maintain policies over time?

I want criticism that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of a theory, and it should not bend the theory to fit the film. It is fine, even desirable, to end by saying what the film contributes to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not. Please write the answer in American English.
