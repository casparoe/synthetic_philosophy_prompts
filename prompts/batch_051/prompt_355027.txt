A friend of mine with some training in linguistics and I spent most of last evening arguing over whether the sheer amount of homonymy in natural languages is something that needs a special explanation; I came away convinced that it does, and that I have the outlines of one. I want to know whether the idea survives contact with someone who is not already invested in it.

I have been calling it the redundancy-budget account of lexical ambiguity. The central claim is this. An ambiguous form is tolerable only to the extent that the utterance and the situation around it carry enough information, independently of the form itself, to recover the intended sense at an acceptable cost. That surrounding information constitutes a budget, drawn from channels that can compensate for one another: segmental phonology, tone or pitch, morphosyntax, and shared context or common ground. What should be measured, on this view, is not raw homonymy but net ambiguity, after the contributions of those other channels have been subtracted. A form is in deficit when the channels on which its disambiguation must rely are themselves unreliable for the relevant distinction; at that point languages behave as if they are repairing the deficit, by adding distinct forms, lexicalizing a distinction, or narrowing one of the competing forms to a safer context.

Two examples make the proposal vivid. Homonyms ought to cluster across contextually distant domains rather than within them, since within-domain collisions draw on a common ground that is already committed on both sides; that is why the financial and riparian senses of English bank rarely compete inside a single frame, while a genuinely within-frame ambiguity tends either to be lexicalized away or to survive only as a defensible pun. And languages should be able to shift the same disambiguating load from one channel to another: a tone language may tolerate considerable segmental homophony because tone and habitual compound structure are doing part of the work, while a language without such channels should either tolerate less of it or rely more heavily on situational context. When context is thinned, as in writing between strangers, transcripts, or legal language, speakers predictably spend more, spelling out distinctions that live speech leaves compressed.

I can already hear my friend wielding the obvious objections: that no actual budget exists, only a metaphor; that languages do not design or repair anything; and that this is merely the old idea of functional load wearing new clothes. I want an honest assessment. Reconstruct the idea in its strongest form, say whether and how it differs from existing accounts, present the three strongest objections, and then say which of them, if any, are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and where the proposal stops being genuinely explanatory.
