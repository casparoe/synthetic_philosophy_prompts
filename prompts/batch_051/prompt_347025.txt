I've been teaching the centipede game in my rational choice module and I keep noticing something uncomfortable in myself. Here's the setup: two players, a pile of money that doubles each time someone passes, and a hundred rounds. At each round the player whose turn it is can take the larger share or pass; if you pass, the total grows and the other player gets to choose. Backward induction says the first player should take on move one. The argument is tight: at the last node the player takes, so at the penultimate node the other player sees that passing just hands them the smaller share, so they take, and this unravels all the way back. It follows from common knowledge of rationality plus the game tree, and I find each step compelling. But I also have a firm intuition that if I were actually sitting there with a hundred rounds on the table, passing early — maybe even passing for the first several rounds — isnt just a mistake: it's a reasonable way to find out wether I'm up against a rational type or a cooperative 'crazy' type, and that something in the unraveling argument throws away the fact that an early pass changes what the other player can infer about me. I don't want to be told 'rational players defect immediately, end of story.' I want to know: is my intuition just importing real-world reputation and signaling that the bare payoff matrix deliberately excludes, in which case it should be discounted as a confusion of levels? Or is it tracking a genuine hole in the backward-induction story — something about the rationality of a move at a node that's actually been reached versus the rationality of a plan chosen from the start, or about what you're entitled to believe after a surprise pass? Give me the strongest version of the case for letting the intuition go, the strongest version of the case for letting the theory go (or amending it), and tell me whether there's a reading on which both survive — and which one you'd give up if there isn't.
