I've been in a reading group on rationality and polarization, and one week someone brought in a paragraph from a popular book on why smart people disagree. The book sums up Aumann's agreement theorem like this: 'Aumann showed that rational agents cannot agree to disagree. If two people have the same prior, both update by Bayes' rule, and each knows the other's posterior probability for some claim, then their posteriors have to be the same already. What this means in practice is that when two people genuinely hear each other out and still disagree, at least one of them is irrational, ignoring evidence, or holding back information. If they were truly rational and shared their reasoning openly, their opinions would eventually converge, so persistant disagreement is basically a proof that someone is biased.'

Something about that last move feels like a leap to me, but I don't have the formal background to say which part fails. Go through the paragraph and tell me which claims match the actual theorem and its assumptions, which ones the author is adding on their own, and whether the practical conclusion about persistent disagreement actually follows. I'd especially like to know whether the theorem says anything at all about a process of convergence after discussion, or only about beliefs people already hold.
