I keep returning to a thought experiment. Suppose a small country whose language has many irregular forms — past tenses like "go/went" and plurals like "foot/feet" — announced a twenty-year project to make its written and broadcast language perfectly regular. Each year a committee publishes a list of irregular forms that may no longer appear in schools, official documents, or news broadcasts, with replacements built by the regular pattern ("goed," "foots"). From year five the project also covers pronunciation: words are to be said as the spelling suggests, and state broadcasters who keep the older sounds are fined. The committee cannot reach ordinary home speech, and enforcement outside the civil service is patchy. Treat this as a mechanism and walk me through how it would actually behave. What would it incentivize among teachers, journalists, lawyers, poets, and ordinary speakers? How would it be gamed — for instance, by rephrasing to avoid forbidden forms, by turning the old irregulars into a badge of private or regional speech, or by inventing new irregularities the committee has not listed? Would the result be a regular grammar, or would the exceptions simply move underground while the new "regular" forms develop quirks of their own? What second-order effects would appear in dictionaries, personal names, and dialects? Could any of the likely failure modes be fixed without giving up the basic goal? And what does the fate of the scheme suggest about whether a grammatical rule is a rule that tolerates exceptions, a list of stored forms, or something else? Lead with your bottom line about whether the project could succeed on its own terms, and keep the whole answer under 600 words.
