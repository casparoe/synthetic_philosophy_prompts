I'm taking a course on causal inference and statistical explanation this semester, and we are currently working through Simpson's paradox: the fact that an association can run one way in every group examined and yet run the opposite way when the groups are pooled. I've been recording my own verdicts about when the pooled comparison is the one to believe and when the group-level comparisons are. They feel coherent to me, but I cannot yet state the principle I am implicitly using. I'd like you to extract it, refine it, and defend it.

Case 1 (a treatment outcome, with severity measured before treatment). The recovery data are as follows. For mild cases: treated, 300 of 600 recovered (50%); untreated, 110 of 200 recovered (55%). For severe cases: treated, 40 of 200 recovered (20%); untreated, 150 of 600 recovered (25%). Overall: treated, 340 of 800 recovered (42.5%); untreated, 260 of 800 recovered (32.5%). So the treatment is associated with better recovery overall but worse recovery within each severity stratum. Severity is measured before treatment, and I'll treat it as the only relevant confounder. My verdict: the stratified comparisons are the ones to trust. Treated patients are disproportionately mild, and mild cases recover more often regardless of treatment; after fixing severity, the drug looks worse.

Case 2 (the same numerical pattern, but the stratifier is a post-treatment variable). Suppose the numbers are exactly as in Case 1, except that the strata are defined by whether a patient's inflammatory marker fell within a week of starting treatment. Again the treated arm looks better overall and worse within each stratum. My verdict: here I would not set aside the overall comparison. The marker fall is itself something the treatment helps bring about, so stratifying on it partly holds fixed part of the treatment's own effect. The aggregate comparison seems to me the right way to ask whether the drug works.

Case 3 (a purely descriptive case). Two baseball players, A and B, bat over two seasons. In the first season, A hits 12 for 40 (.300) and B hits 80 for 400 (.200). In the second season, A hits 90 for 600 (.150) and B hits 10 for 80 (.125). A has the higher average in each season, but across the two seasons combined, B is higher: A is 102 for 640 (.159), B is 90 for 480 (.188). My verdict: both claims are true, and neither is the uniquely correct comparison. If forced to answer "who batted better across the two seasons as a whole?", I would report the combined average and say B; but I would not call the per-season comparisons mistakes.

Case 4 (a decision problem with case mix). Two hospitals treat the same two condition types. Hospital X sees 900 low-risk and 100 high-risk patients, with 18 and 30 deaths respectively, for 48 of 1000 overall (4.8%). Hospital Y sees 100 low-risk and 900 high-risk patients, with 1 and 252 deaths respectively, for 253 of 1000 overall (25.3%). So Y has lower mortality within every condition type, but X has the lower overall mortality. My verdicts: if I knew my diagnosis, I would rather be treated at Y. If I had to route a patient whose diagnosis was unknown, the raw odds favor X. For the question "which hospital is better at treating what it treats?", I take within-condition mortality to be the right measure, while admitting that a system-level question can also be asked.

My working hypothesis is that the stratifier's causal role does the work: condition on pre-treatment confounders, do not condition on variables that are consequences of the treatment, and for purely descriptive comparisons choose the level that matches the question being asked. But that sounds to me like two or three heuristics rather than one principle, and I am not sure it handles the hospital case, where condition type is pre-treatment and yet my preference for the raw overall rate depends on what I am deciding.

Is there a single principle behind all four verdicts? Split by grade level, however, participants averaged lower than their nonparticipating classmates within every grade. Grade level is fixed before the scheme opens and is recorded for every student. Should the city treat the overall association as evidence that the scheme helps, or the grade-level comparisons as evidence that it does not?
