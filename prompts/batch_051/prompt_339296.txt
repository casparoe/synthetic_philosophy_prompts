Two engineers on my team, Rina and Ben, have been arguing for weeks about a customer-facing tool we ship, and I keep getting dragged in as the referee but I honestly can't resolve it. The tool is an AI assistant you point at a git repo; you ask it things like "are there any failing tests?" or "is this ready to merge?", and it answers in a very confident voice. The catch is that our evals show when the model doesn't actually know, it mostly won't say so — it just produces a confident-sounding answer, and in those under-the-hood "not sure" cases it's wrong around 15-20% of the time. It once told a customer "No, all tests pass" when a test was failing in a job it simply hadn't looked at.

Here's the argument as best I can reconstruct it, in order.

(1) Rina said the assistant is deceptive, but "lying" is the wrong word. Lying means knowing the truth and saying the opposite. The assistant doesn't care either way. She pointed to Harry Frankfurt, the philospher, and his old distinction between lying and bullshit: the liar is still oriented toward the truth, while the bullshitter just produces words to create an impression without checking whether the impression matches reality. She said that's a exact description of our model's confident answers, so "deception" is fair.

(2) Ben said that's anthropomorphism. Bullshit is something a person does on purpose; part of the concept is that the bullshitter wants you to think something. A model wants nothing. Lots of things cause false beliefs without being dishonest — a broken clock, a stale cache, a buggy linter. The assistant is a next-token predictor. It can be misleading, but only an agent can be dishonest. So "deceptive" is a category error.

(3) Rina said he's looking for the intent in the wrong place. It doesn't have to live inside the model; it lives in us. We've seen the evals, we know how often the confident tone is detached from fact, and we shipped it anyway — we even tuned it to sound like a senior reviewer instead of sounding hedged. That's a choice, not an accident. If a company ships a product with marketing copy that hides a known defect, nobody says "well, the ink isn't an agent"; they say the company deceived its customers. So "the assistant is deceptive" is just shorthand for "we built a system that deceives."

(4) Ben said that move gives up her original claim: if the intent is the team's, the assistant isn't the thing with the property — and what the team did is closer to shipping a service with a known annual outage rate than to deceiving anyone. We don't sit in standup hoping any particular user forms a false belief; we accept a statistical failure mode. A linter with a known false-positive rate isn't a deceitful linter, it's a noisy one. So this is a reliability defect, not dishonesty.

(5) Rina said the linter analogy hides the difference. A false positive from a linter is a flag the user can inspect; the whole point of our assistant is that the confident answer is the deliverable itself. When the failure mode is "asserts something false with authority, and we knew it would a fifth of the time," calling that a mere reliability defect is a euphemism we'd never extend to a human contractor who reviewed PRs that way. Most corporate deception isn't villainous; it's a known pattern nobody stops. Putting the same pattern in a language model doesn't make the word stop applying.

They're both still convinced. Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it: for each numbered move, say what's right, what's overstated, and what it concedes to the other side; then say who is more right overall. Also, since I'll be using this to settle the lunchroom debate, tell me how confident you are in the verdict and single out the point you're least sure of.
