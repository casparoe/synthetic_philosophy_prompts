Next month I am presenting to a joint committee of my school's parent association, student council, and administration a pilot proposal to reorganize the cafeteria line: the default combo of the day becomes the balanced meal that meets the district's nutrition standards, with every other offering available simply by asking, and the beverage area is reordered so water dispensers come first and sweetened drinks sit at the far end. The position I am defending is that this kind of choice architecture is a permissible way for a school to improve student decisions, and is preferable to a mandate, because it removes no option, it is announced openly, and it operates only through ordinary features of how people choose. The committee is not a philosophy seminar. It includes two nurses who support the goal but think the plan does not go far enough; a parent who describes herself as libertarian and will press on whether the school has any business setting a default for other people's children; a student representative who argues that steering people through habit and inertia is manipulation even when it is transparent; and the food services director, whose real worry is that students will reject the default and lunch participation will fall. Give me a question-period prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will come and how much damage each could do, phrased the way each of those people would actually put them, with a note on which objections have real force, what I should concede rather than contest, where my reply is weakest, and the one reply that must land for the pilot to survive. I am especially worried about one question I hope nobody asks: whether my argument would also license much less attractive defaults, such as an opt-out system for something students would not plausibly choose for themselves. Tell me how likely that question is from this audience and how I should answer it if it comes.
