I've been trying to pin down my views on dirty hands—the idea that some political acts are at once the right thing to do and a real moral stain on the person who does them. My current working principle is this: an official's hands are dirty exactly when, in the course of official action, she deliberately uses someone as a mere means—torture, deception, coercion, breaking a promise—whereas merely foreseeing or permitting harm leaves her clean, however tragic the choice. A clean hard choice calls for regret; a dirty one calls for something more. But I keep finding cases that don't cooperate.

Here are my verdicts, roughly as I would actually give them. (1) A mayor authorizes the torture of a captured bomber to learn which school is targeted; I think she should do it, and I think she has dirtied herself and owes something afterward. (2) A pandemic triage officer reallocates a ventilator away from a patient with poor odds to two patients with good odds, letting the first die; I think this is tragic but clean. (3) A detective falsely tells a suspect that an accomplice has confessed in order to extract a confession; I think this is permissible and not dirty, even if a department should ban the practice. (4) A prime minister lies to parliament about the state of a negotiation to keep the coalition from collapsing; I think this is dirty—it corrupts the office even if the lie was necessary. (5) A general sends a platoon into a near-certain death trap as a diversion because taking the ridge will save many times that number; I think the general's hands are dirty, perhaps permanently. (6) A finance minister promises a colleague she will vote with her, then breaks the promise at the last moment because the budget can pass only with her vote and thousands of jobs depend on it; I think this is dirty, but only mildly.

The principle I started with handles some pairs: it distinguishes 1 from 2 (using versus allowing), and 2 from 5 (foreseen harm versus conscripting people as instruments). But it seems to collapse 3 and 4, since both are deliberate deception by officials, yet I am sure one is clean and the other dirty. And 6 looks like using a colleague as a means, but the stain feels far less severe than in 1 or 5, which the principle does not explain. Is there a single principle that gets all six, or close? Please state the principle precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate or forces me to overturn, and apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: a health minister who lies to the public early in an outbreak to prevent panic while quietly building up hospital capacity.
