I've been trying to get clear on whether the notion of idolatry still earns its keep once it is detached from graven images and temples — whether there is a distinctive evaluative mistake we can call misplaced worship, and if so, what it is. I do not mean the narrow theological question of which beings are proper objects of worship; I mean the broader idea that a person can value something in the wrong way: treat a finite good as ultimate, give it a kind of devotion or reverence that does not fit its place in a well-lived life, or let it occupy a role that only something of a different order should occupy. I want a working definition of idolatry, so understood, that separates wrong-way valuing from two nearby mistakes: simply overvaluing something, and valuing something that is not valuable at all.

Here are the cases I would like the definition to handle. (a) A community treats a golden statue as a god: it prays before it, makes offerings, and locates its ultimate allegiance there. (b) A person for whom money and professional standing function as the final measure of every decision; asked what makes a life go well, they can only describe their balance sheet and their CV. (c) A parent who loves their child fiercely and would sacrifice a great deal for them, but does not treat the child as the source of all value or as the arbiter of what matters. (d) An amateur gardener who spends most weekends in the garden because it gives them joy, without any sense that the garden is sacred or that other goods are to be measured by it. (e) A romantic partner who says their lover is the sole ground of meaning in their life and that every other commitment may be abandoned if the relationship requires it. (f) A football supporter whose mood is governed for days by results, who keeps ritual habits around match days, and who regards the club as the one thing that never lets them down. (g) A patriot who treats the nation as sacred: the flag, anthem, and war dead are approached with reverence, and national identity is placed beyond ordinary moral criticism. (h) A family that venerates its ancestors through offerings and ritualised remembrance, holding that the ancestors' blessing still bears on the family's decisions. (i) A person devoted to an abstract ideal — justice, say, or truth — who treats it as unconditionally authoritative and judges every other commitment by its contribution to that ideal.

I am fairly confident that (a) and (b) are cases of idolatry and that (c) and (d) are not; I have no settled view about (e) through (i). All of the middle cases seem to involve letting something occupy the centre of a life, but I cannot articulate what further ingredient, if any, turns a strong commitment into a misplaced one. I would like you to propose a definition, run it through each case, and tell me where it surprises you. If the definition misclassifies one of my confident cases, either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. I would also like you to say which case the definition handles least well, and to make explicit what would change your mind: which further case, argument, or distinction would lead you to abandon or substantially revise the definition, and where in your reasoning you are least certain. If you think the broader notion of idolatry cannot be given a principled definition and is merely a rhetorical charge, take that view only after showing why the attempt to make it precise fails.
