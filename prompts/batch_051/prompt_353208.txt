After three hours with my friend Dana last night I think she proved too much, but I can't find the step where it slips. Her argument was about coherent extrapolated volition—the idea that what I "really" want is not whatever I want right now, but what I'd want if I knew all the relevant facts, thought clearly, and made my preferences hang together. She says the idea is either my actual preferences in disguise or a story about a stranger:

(1) If my actual preferences are formed under ignorance, bias, and inconsistency, then they don't carry authority just because they're mine.
(2) So what carries authority must be my idealized preferences: the ones I'd have after the idealizing process.
(3) That process can't just give me true beliefs and leave my wants alone, because some of my wants are themselves the product of ignorance or bias; it has to be allowed to revise the wants too.
(4) But once the process may revise the wants, "the preferences I'd have after idealizing" just means "whatever the process outputs," and the procedure does the real choosing.
(5) To keep the output mine, I'd have to already endorse the procedure, but my endorsement now is one of my actual, unidealized preferences.
(6) So either my actual preferences are authoritative after all, or the idealized preferences are just whatever a construction procedure spits out and calling them "mine" does no work.
(7) Therefore there is no such thing as a preference that is both corrected and mine.

That conclusion feels insane to me—surely I can be wrong about what I want and still have it be my good. I'd like to keep (1) and (2), and definitely reject (7). Where does the argument actually go wrong, and if I reject the culprit step, what do I give up?
