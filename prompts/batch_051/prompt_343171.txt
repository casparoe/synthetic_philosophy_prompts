I have been reading Hume on probability side by side with twentieth-century formal epistemology, and I find myself wanting to know how the two would have spoken to each other. The passage that has caught me is from David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 1, Part 3, Section 13, "Of unphilosophical probability":

"All these kinds of probability are receiv'd by philosophers, and allow'd to be reasonable foundations of belief and opinion. But there are others, that are deriv'd from the same principles, tho' they have not had the good fortune to obtain the same sanction. The first probability of this kind may be accounted for thus. The diminution of the union, and of the resemblance, as above explained, diminishes the facility of the transition, and by that means weakens the evidence; and we may farther observe, that the same diminution of the evidence will follow from a diminution of the impression, and from the shading of those colours, under which it appears to the memory or senses. The argument, which we found on any matter of fact we remember, is more or less convincing, according as the fact is recent or remote; and tho' the difference in these degrees of evidence be not receiv'd by philosophy as solid and legitimate; because in that case an argument must have a different force to day, from what it shall have a month hence; yet notwithstanding the opposition of philosophy, 'tis certain, this circumstance has a considerable influence on the understanding, and secretly changes the authority of the same argument, according to the different times, in which it is propos'd to us. A greater force and vivacity in the impression naturally conveys a greater to the related idea; and 'tis on the degrees of force and vivacity, that the belief depends, according to the foregoing system."

What is striking to me is that Hume admits here that actual conviction is graded, and that the grade responds to something other than the arrival of a proposition learned with certainty: a "diminution of the impression," the "shading of those colours" under which the evidence appears, the force and vivacity of the present impression. Yet he also records that philosophy refuses to admit these factors as "solid and legitimate" grounds of belief.

Consider now the problem that Richard Jeffrey raised much later. Ordinary Bayesian conditionalization supposes that learning consists in coming to accept some evidence proposition E with certainty and then renormalizing one's other credences on E: P_new(A) = P_old(A | E). Jeffrey's point was that much everyday and scientific learning is not like that. You glimpse a patch of colour through mist, hear a half-intelligible announcement, or read a faint and ambiguous result off an instrument; no element of any antecedently given partition need become certain, yet your confidence shifts over the partition. His proposed generalization, probability kinematics or Jeffrey conditionalization, says that when an experience moves your credences over a partition {E_1, ..., E_n} from their old values to new values q_i, you should hold fixed the conditional probabilities given each E_i and set P_new(A) = Σ_i P_old(A | E_i) q_i.

Here is what I want to know. Had Hume been shown Jeffrey conditionalization, would he have recognized it as the proper formalization of what he describes in the quoted passage — a rule for revising belief when evidence arrives only in shades — or would he have classed it with the "unphilosophical" probabilities: a rationalized version of a process that, on his own account, the mind carries out not by obeying any norm of coherence but through custom and the mechanical transfer of vivacity? And if he took the second view, could he consistently say both that evidence almost never arrives as a determinate proposition and that the influence of such shaded evidence is not a legitimate basis for belief? I am less interested in a verdict on Jeffrey than in what Hume's own position would have had to be, given this passage and his larger account of reasoning, once the two are brought together.
