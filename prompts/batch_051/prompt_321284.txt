Okay, so I spent an hour arguing with a friend last night about A.J. Ayer and his views on life after death. He kept coming back to this version of the verification principle: "A statement about surviving death has factual meaning only if some possible observation could be relevant to deciding whether it is true or false; otherwise it is neither true nor false, just nonsense." We both agreed Ayer used that to dismiss ordinary religious talk about heaven, souls, etc. Then I brought up a case he definitely didn’t have in mind: a company offers a service where, at the moment of death, they do an ultra-high-resolution scan of your brain and upload the whole structure to their servers, where a simulation of your nervous system keeps running. Your partner can talk to it, it has your memories and mannerisms, it says it still feels like you. Ayer’s rule would say the claim “I survived my death” only has meaning if observations could settle it. But here all the observable stuff—the simulation’s behavior, the apparent memories, even your partner’s emotional recognition—could happen just as well if the original you had died and this were a new conscious copy. So now I’m stuck. Does Ayer’s prinicple make the uploaded-survival claim meaningful, since we can observe the simulation? If it does, does it also make the distinction between “really me” and “perfect copy” meaningless, because no observation could ever decide it? And if the principle says that distinction is nonsense, is that a fair application of Ayer’s view, or has it secretly turned “observable continuity” into the same thing as “survival”? I care less about the practical answer than about what the rule itself can do here. Can you argue it from the words, from the purpose of the principle, and from what Ayer would have said if you sat him down in front of a laptop running his own emulation? Which approach should win?
