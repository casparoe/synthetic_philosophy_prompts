Since I became policy director of a state-level campaign finance reform group last fall, I have been trying to work out what a proposal our board may endorse would actually do. The proposal is a blind-escrow contribution system for one U.S. state: rather than lower contribution limits, it would require every contribution or loan above $200 to a candidate for state legislative or statewide office—including the candidate's own money—to pass through a state-run clearinghouse. The clearinghouse would forward money to the campaign in weekly transfers, stripped of donor identity; the campaign would receive only the total amount and a distribution of the underlying donors by size band (for instance, $200–500, $500–2,000, and above $2,000). Donors would be barred from telling a candidate or campaign, directly or through intermediaries, whether or how much they gave, and campaign staff would be barred from soliciting or recording that information. Contributions of $200 or less could still be made directly. Independent expenditures and party spending would remain outside the system.

I do not want an abstract verdict on whether the proposal is sound. I want a behavioral walk-through: how candidates would raise money without knowing their donors, whether bundlers, consultants, event hosts, or legislators' own networks would reconstruct the information the escrow is meant to destroy, where influence and access-buying would migrate, what a campaign could infer from the size-band reports and the timing of transfers, how the disclosure ban could realistically be enforced, and which of the system's failure modes are patches versus fundamental.
