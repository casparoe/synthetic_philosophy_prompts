In debates over whether humanity should attempt to contact extraterrestrial intelligence, I keep encountering versions of the dark forest argument. As it tends to be put, the galaxy is a dark forest in which each civilization is an armed hunter who stays silent because any sound may reveal his position, and who fires on any sound he hears because the alternative is to be fired on first. The argument is then used to support a strong practical conclusion: we should not send deliberate messages, and perhaps should also work to minimize our accidental electromagnetic signature, because a single detection could prove fatal.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually carries that conclusion, rather than a restatement of the argument. First, give me the correspondence table: what corresponds to the forest, the darkness, the hunter, the silence, the sound, the shot, the kill, and any other load-bearing element. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I particularly want to know which disanalogies are merely cosmetic and which ones are doing real work for the conclusion. Among other things, assess whether the analogy quietly imports that all civilizations are symmetric in capability, motive, and stakes; that a first strike is cheap, reliable, and decisive; that communication cannot reduce suspicion while concealment remains possible; and whether the target scenario actually supplies those features. Finally, tell me whether the practical conclusion, that we ought to remain silent in the strong version of the argument, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy would be if the dark forest captures only part of what is sound in the argument.
