A colleague who works on early modern philosophy corralled me after a talk and offered what he said was the upshot of the "hinge epistemology" literature rooted in Wittgenstein's On Certainty. His gloss, roughly: "All our knowledge floats on a set of bedrock propositions that we simply can't doubt—things like that you have two hands, or that the earth has existed for a long time. We can't justify them, because any evidence would already be shakier than the hinge itself, and we can't rationally criticize them either; they're more like animal certainties than reasoned conclusions. So rational argument can only ever work between people who already share the same bedrock hinges; once you hit a hinge, persuasion is impossible and there's nothing more to say." I mostly work in ethics, so I'm not going to trust my gut on this, but it strikes me as to tidy: it makes the view sound more foundationalist and more pessimistic about deep disagreement than I remember it being. Is this a fair summary? Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful hinge epistemologist would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the last bit about persuasion follows from the accurate parts. Lead with your bottom line up front, then give the reasoning, and don't restate my question.
