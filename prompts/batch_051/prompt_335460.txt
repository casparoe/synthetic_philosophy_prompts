In mid-2012, a private venture led by the American entrepreneur Russ George dispersed roughly 100 tonnes of iron sulfate into the surface waters of the North Pacific, a few hundred kilometres west of Haida Gwaii, without authorization from the Canadian government. The stated objectives were to trigger a phytoplankton bloom that would revive salmon runs and, in the process, draw down carbon. Satellite data later showed a large bloom; the carbon-sequestration outcome was never verified and remains contested. The episode is routinely treated as a preview of the governance problems posed by climate intervention that falls outside any existing decision procedure.

I want an analysis of this episode through three concepts that structure the geoengineering debate: moral hazard, termination shock, and the question of who decides. Treat each as a lens and be disciplined about the mapping. On moral hazard: identify whose behavior is supposed to be insured against and which action it is supposed to crowd out, distinguish moral hazard from the broader mitigation-deterrence concern where the distinction does real work, and then say whether the Haida Gwaii case actually fits the structure of a moral hazard or is only loosely analogous to it. On termination shock: spell out how the concept functions in the sulfate-aerosol context from which it comes, then assess how far it transfers to a one-off iron-fertilization pulse—what would "termination" mean here, for the climate and for the local ecosystem, and does invoking the concept illuminate the real risks or distort them? On who decides: identify the affected parties, lay out what could constitute legitimate authorization in principle—state consent, local consent, an international regime, or the claim that no entity may authorize it at all—and argue which of these positions, if any, could have covered this experiment. Then reverse direction and say what the episode shows about the three concepts themselves: which one does genuine explanatory work, which one breaks down or collapses into another, and whether an unsanctioned experiment of this kind is best read as a confirmation of the standard worries, an anomaly they cannot handle, or an early warning that the concepts need revision.
