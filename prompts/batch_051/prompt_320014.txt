For a seminar on social ontology I have been worrying at an argument that seems to show that the familiar generational categories — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z and the rest — are not real social kinds and should be abandoned in serious social explanation. Each step looks sound to me, but the conclusion is stronger than I can accept: talk of generations seems to track something genuine (people entering the labour market around 2008 faced a different conjuncture from those entering it around 2020), and, taken at face value, the same form of argument would dissolve nearly every social category with a fuzzy boundary. Here is the argument as I currently have it.

(1) If a generational label picks out a genuine social kind, then some truths of the form “Millennials are F” must be more than statistical summaries: they must support an inference about an arbitrary member of the kind, other things being equal, in virtue of that member's being a Millennial.

(2) There is no non-arbitrary fact of the matter about where one generation ends and the next begins. Birth years, ages at which people lived through particular events, and the formative experiences themselves vary more or less continuously across cohorts, so any cut-off is a stipulation drawn on a gradient, not the discovery of a boundary already present in the population.

(3) Therefore membership in a generation is fixed by stipulation about birth-year intervals, rather than by any property of the persons themselves beyond their having been born in a certain range.

(4) If membership is fixed in that way, then any richer claim about what members of a generation are like must be either (a) a statistical average over a stipulated interval, or (b) an essentialist claim that membership itself explains shared traits.

(5) Averages of type (a) cannot underwrite kindhood: an average over a stipulated interval is a fact about the interval, not about its members, and it licenses no counterfactuals about individual members.

(6) Essentialist claims of type (b) are false: the mere fact of having been born between such-and-such years does not cause a person to share the values, dispositions, or habits that generational discourse attributes to them.

(7) Therefore generational claims are either not genuinely general — mere averages — or false.

(8) Therefore generational categories are not real sociological kinds, and their role in social explanation is at best loose shorthand for age and period effects, at worst stereotyping that borrows a false air of naturalness.

I cannot find a step I am happy to deny, yet (8) seems to me to prove too much. I would most like to keep the thought that shared formative conditions can produce real, shared cohort properties in people who live through them at a similar age, even though the boundaries remain fuzzy. Show me where the argument goes wrong; if you think it does not, tell me which premise is least costly to reject and what accepting that rejection costs. Be specific about which step fails and why it looked plausible, and distinguish claims about cohorts sharing formative conditions from claims about intrinsic traits of their members. Please answer in British English.
