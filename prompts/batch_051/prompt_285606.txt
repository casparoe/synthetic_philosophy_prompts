I am a doctoral student in epistemology, working on the structure of justification. I have been collecting my own reactions to a series of cases that bear on the Münchhausen trilemma, and I would like to know whether there is a single principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures them. I am less interested in a taxonomy of foundationalism, coherentism and infinitism than in whether my concrete verdicts add up to something defensible.

Here are the cases and my intuitive verdicts.

(1) A mathematician believes a theorem on the basis of having followed a proof two years ago in a trusted journal. She can no longer reconstruct the proof, but she correctly remembers the theorem and the fact that it was proved. I regard her belief as justified.

(2) A person believes p on the basis of q, q on the basis of r, and so on; when asked for the chain, each link is available and the chain never repeats and never reaches a stopping point. I find it hard to say whether the belief is justified; I certainly do not think the mere unavailability of a terminal ground automatically renders it unjustified.

(3) A person believes p because q, and q because p. When challenged, she can articulate the circular move. I regard her belief as unjustified, even though the two beliefs support one another.

(4) A person believes there is a red patch before her just by looking. I regard this perceptual belief as justified without any further argument. However, when asked whether there is something that justifies the perceptual belief, I do not think the perceptual experience itself plays exactly the same justificatory role as a premise in an argument; if it did, it would seem to need its own justification.

(5) A person is asked why a basic moral claim is true and replies that it is self-evident; he refuses to offer any defence, but the claim is one that I find highly contestable. I regard his refusal as dogmatic and his belief as unjustified.

(6) A person accepts a moral claim as self-evident too, but after years of reflection and failed attempts to derive it from anything more basic; the claim is one I also find independently plausible. I am more inclined to count this as justified, though I am not sure the explanation is simply that I agree with it.

(7) A person infers p from q, q from r, and so on, but after twenty steps the chain returns to p. Each transition is valid and each inferential move seems locally reasonable. I still regard the overall structure as less justified than the never-ending chain in case (2), though I cannot say exactly why length matters.

(8) A person justifies p by q, q by r, and r by p, but the circle is disguised; he does not notice it, and the appearance of independence is due only to his not surveying the whole set. I want to say his belief is also unjustified, though I am not sure whether the problem is psychological or structural.

Please help me find the principle, or principled combination of constraints, that best fits these verdicts. In particular: Should I distinguish between a belief’s being justified and a person’s being in a position to complete a justification? Should I abandon any of these verdicts because no coherent principle captures them? What would your favoured account say about an additional case: a person whose justification for p is an inference from q, and whose justification for q is an inference from p, but who also has independent but weak evidence for each? And what does the resulting picture say about the trilemma’s assumption that circularity, regress and dogmatic stopping are the only alternatives?
