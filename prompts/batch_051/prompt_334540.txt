I have been living recently with a practical question about self-judgment. After a workplace dispute in which I was sure I had acted rightly until I tried to explain myself to a colleague, I realized how much of my confidence depended on an imagined audience of others who, I thought, would see things as I did. That led me to Adam Smith’s *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, and I have been stuck on a passage in Part III, Chapter I, where Smith argues that without society we would have no moral view of ourselves at all. Here is the passage:

> Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before. It is placed in the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with, which always mark when they enter into, and when they disapprove of his sentiments; and it is here that he first views the propriety and impropriety of his own passions, the beauty and deformity of his own mind.

Smith uses the mirror analogy to explain how we come to judge our own passions and character: the reactions of others are the looking-glass in which we first see our own dispositions. I want to know how far this analogy really holds. Please audit it as an analogy, not as a slogan. Map the correspondences explicitly: what in the source corresponds to what in the target, and where the mapping breaks. In particular, weigh whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions that Smith’s own larger account needs but the mirror image cannot supply — for example, that the social mirror is accurate, that what we see there is our own mind rather than other people’s distortions, and that we have a way of correcting what the mirror shows. Then test what Smith uses the analogy to support: the claim that we can judge ourselves only by imagining how others would judge us. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? What would a better analogy be, if there is one? I am looking for a verdict about how much work the analogy can actually carry, not a neutral list. Tell me also which argument or consideration would most change your verdict, or where your reading of the passage and its analogy is most likely to fail.
