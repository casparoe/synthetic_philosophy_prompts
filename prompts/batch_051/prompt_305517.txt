We're doing Grice in my philosophy of language class and theres an assymetry thats been bugging me. Suppose two people are asked "did you embezzle from the company?" One says "no," a straight lie. The other says "I've never been convicted of embezzling," which is true but leaves the listener just as convinced he's clean. In both cases the speaker intended the same false belief and the listener ended up with it, but I (and basically everyone I've asked) thinks the liar did something worse. The only difference is whether the false proposition gets asserted or is just conversationally implicated.

Grice gives us a distinction between what is said and what is implicated, but his notion of speaker meaning is all about the speaker's intentions, and the intention to plant the false belief looks the same in both cases. So I don't see how intention draws the moral line; the difference is only in which proposition gets asserted versus implicated. One thing he says is that implicatures are cancelable—the second speaker could add "though I did embezzle" and it would be weird but not contradictory, whereas the liar can't say "no, and I did"—but it feels backwards to let the moral blame hang on whether the speaker could have added a confession later.

So what I want to know: is there a defensible principle that makes actually lying worse than deliberately misleading by implicature, or is this just a loophole we've carved out so people can bluff while staying technically true? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against a case where the implicature is about as forced as an assertion—like answering "did you steal from the till?" with "I've never even been written up"—and tell me where the line goes. If you think there's no real difference, then say whether we should stop letting "technically true" shield people, and what that does to the gap between "don't lie" and "don't mislead."
