I keep having the same argument with myself about inference to the best explanation, and it has started to matter for how I think about almost every non-deductive move I make.

On the one hand, IBE seems to me the only honest account of how we actually get from data to claims about unobservables. It unifies ordinary reasoning and science under one pattern: the evidence supports the hypothesis that would, if true, best explain it. It does not pretend that we can assign precise numbers to priors we invented; it just makes explicit the comparative judgments about simplicity, fit, and scope that everyone already uses. And the standard rivals seem to me to fail on inspection — either they turn out to be IBE wearing Bayesian clothing, or they forbid a move that everyone, including their own authors, keeps making in practice.

On the other hand, I have a firm intuition that explanatory appeal is cheap. A hypothesis can be simple, elegant, and flexible enough to accommodate all the available data while still being exactly the kind of thing someone would overfit to. The evidence that really moves my probability is novel predictive success: the hypothesis made a definite forecast that was not used in constructing it, and the forecast came out right. If IBE is an account of justification rather than merely of idea-generation, it seems to license believing the prettiest story on offer; if my intuition is right, explanation is a heuristic for finding candidates, not a source of warrant.

C. S. Peirce's early statement of the threefold distinction seems to me to contain both views in embryo. In *Deduction, Induction, and Hypothesis* (Popular Science Monthly, 1878), after the bean-bag illustration, he writes:

"Induction is where we generalize from a number of cases of which something is true, and infer that the same thing is true of a whole class. Or, where we find a certain thing to be true of a certain proportion of cases and infer that it is true of the same proportion of the whole class. Hypothesis is where we find some very curious circumstance, which would be explained by the supposition that it was a case of a certain general rule, and thereupon adopt that supposition. Or, where we find that in certain respects two objects have a strong resemblance, and infer that they resemble one another strongly in other respects."

The word I keep getting stuck on is "adopt." On one reading, Peirce is saying that finding an explanatory supposition is itself enough to adopt it — to believe it, at least provisionally — which would make IBE a direct license to move from loveliness to credibility. On another reading, "adopt" is weaker: we take the supposition up as the thing to test next, and the actual warrant has to arrive later, through deductive and inductive work. If the first reading is right, I want a negotiated verdict on which should yield: the theory, revised into a two-stage account in which explanation proposes and prediction disposes, or the intuition, debunked as a confusion between a hypothesis's origin and its evidence. If the second reading is right, that is itself a negotiated verdict, and I want to know whether it is genuinely available in Peirce's text or is only a friendly amendment we are imposing on him. Give me the strongest version of the pro-explanation and pro-prediction positions, and say what would in each case justify dropping the intuition, abandoning IBE as a theory of justification, or keeping both.
