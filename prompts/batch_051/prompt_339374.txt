I've been going back and forth on Moore's paradox and I'd like a dialogue that stages a real disagreement about where the weirdness comes from, because the two main diagnoses seem to pull in completly different directions. The basic case is familiar: someone says "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." That's not a flat contradiction, since both halves can easily be true at once — the rain doesn't care what I believe about it. But the utterance still sounds broken somehow. What I'm after is a dialogue between two characters who offer rival diagnoses. One of them thinks the absurdity is really about self-knowledge: roughly, my judgments about my own beliefs answer to the same reasons as my judgments about the world, so I can't coherently judge that it's raining while also judging that I don't believe it's raining; the problem would show up even if I just silently thought the whole thing to myself. The other character thinks the absurdity is primarily about assertion, not inner mental life — maybe something like a rule that you shouldn't assert p unless you believe p (or maybe know p) — so the sentence misfires as a public act, even though the underlying thought isn't impossible in the same way. Make both sides strong, don't let either turn into a strawman, and have them actually respond to each other's moves rather than talking past one another. It would be good if they test their positions against nearby cases like "It's raining, but my evidence doesn't support believing it's raining" and "It's raining, but I don't know it's raining," to see whether those cases sound any different.
