There's this thing that's been bugging me. English law used to say the king can do no wrong, but Blackstone, in his Commentaries on the Laws of England, quotes Henry Finch as putting it more carefully: "The king hath a prerogative in all things, that are not injurious to the subject; for in them all it must be remembered, that the king's prerogative stretcheth not to the doing of any wrong." So even in Blackstone the king isnt literally incapable of doing wrong; what's being said is that the law won't treat the crown as a wrongdoer, or that the prerogative just doesnt include doing wrong. But modern legal systems with sovereign immunity still refuse to let the state be sued in its own courts unless it consents. I want to know: what theory of the state or of wrongdoing does that practice actually presuppose? Is it just a practical rule about who pays and who decides, or does it only make sense if you think the state is somehow not the kind of thing that can commit a legal wrong? And if a government official does something illegal, we punish or sue the official, but the state itself stays clean. Is that a coherent way to think about collective wrongs, or is it just a legal fiction we keep because its convenient? Could you separate what's historically established about what Blackstone and English law meant from what's genuinely contested among philosophers and jurists about whether the doctrine is defensible? I'm not looking for a summary; I'd like the implicit theory pulled apart.
