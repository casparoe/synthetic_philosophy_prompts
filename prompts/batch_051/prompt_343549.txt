Ok so a friend of mine has gotten really into Kelly betting and something he calls "geometric rationality," and he's worked up this argument that seems to prove way too much. I can't find the bad step and it's driving me nuts. Can you take it apart?

Here's his argument, cleaned up:

(1) Take a big sequence of independent bets—say 100,000 even-money flips where you win 55% of the time, and you can bet any fraction of your bankroll on each flip. For any strategy that always bets a fixed fraction f, the long-run growth rate is the expected value of log(1 + fX) per flip (where X is +1 on a win, −1 on a loss), and the Kelly fraction f* is whatever maximizes that expected log-growth.

(2) If two fixed-fraction strategies have different expected log-growth rates, the higher one will, with probability 1, end up wealthier than the lower one if the sequence goes on long enough, and the lower one never catches back up.

(3) A strategy that another strategy beats with probability 1 in the long run, in that sense, is rationally dominated by it. You shouldn't pick the loser.

(4) Now suppose someone has a utility function over wealth that is not exactly logarithmic. If their relative risk aversion is constant but not equal to 1, their optimal bet is some fixed fraction different from Kelly, so by (2) and (3) they're choosing a dominated strategy. And if their risk aversion is not constant, Kelly still beats their actual bet path in the same asymptotic sense, because their expected log-growth is still lower.

(5) Therefore any non-log utility is irrational. Rationality forces everyone to have exactly log utility: never more cautious than Kelly, never less, no room for caring about short-term drawdowns, and no "I need this money next year."

That last part seems crazy, so one of the steps has to go. Wich premise should I reject, and what do I have to give up if I reject it? Also explain why the rejected premise looked fine before I started poking at it.
