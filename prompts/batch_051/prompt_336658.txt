I've been going back and forth with my advisor about what it means to actually understand a proof—we keep talking past eachother—as opposed to just being fluent in the moves or checking that the steps are valid. I'd like a working definition of "understands a proof" (not a literature review, just your best shot) that gets the following cases right.

Here are the cases:

1. A grad student who can verify every line of a 60-page proof, but when you ask them "what's the one idea that makes this go?" they shrug, and a month later they can't reconstruct anything beyond the statement.
2. A mathematician who can draw the proof on a napkin, explain the central trick at a blackboard, and reuse the same trick on a new problem, but who hasn't checked all the technical conditions and would probably miss a subtle case if asked to formalize it.
3. The computer-assisted proof of the four-color theorem. No single person has checked every case by hand; the community treats it as a theorem. Does anyone "understand" it? Does the community? Or does verified-without-understanding just have to count?
4. An undergrad who memorized a proof line-by-line and reproduces it on the exam, notation and all, but can't say why lemma 2 is there, and gets lost if the steps are reordered.
5. A physicist who can't prove the theorem but uses it constantly, knows exactly when it applies and when it doesn't, and could explain to a colleague why the theorem has to have the shape it has.
6. The person who originally proved a theorem thirty years ago, but now remembers only the statement and a vague sense that there was "some diagonalization" in it.

My instinct is that 2 is a clear yes and 4 is a clear no, but I keep flipping on 1 and 6. Case 3 makes me want to say yes for the community and no for any individual, which I suspect breaks most definitions. Case 5 feels like understanding something, but maybe not the proof. Propose the definition, run it through all six, and tell me where it gives a verdict I should be uncomfortable with.
