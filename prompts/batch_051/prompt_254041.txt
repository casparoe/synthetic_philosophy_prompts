I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, writing on the epistemology of climate intervention, and I have become disturbed by an asymmetry in the way I read the literature on stratospheric aerosol injection. When I encounter a physical claim — that injection would shift regional precipitation patterns, that an abrupt halt would cause rapid warming of the kind now discussed as termination shock — I treat it as an ordinary empirical hypothesis. I look at the model setup, the robustness checks, and whether the authors mark the difference between a constrained estimate and a conjecture. When the same paper makes a claim about what the technology would do to human motivation or institutions — that its mere availability would weaken mitigation effort, that negotiating research governance would erode trust between developed and developing countries, that a moratorium on outdoor experiments would dampen or inflame public anxiety — I hold those claims to a far higher standard. For the first kind of claim, ordinary scientific support will do; for the second, I find myself demanding quasi-experimental evidence, formal models of the relevant actors, or historically close analogies before I am willing to let the claim function as a premise in any all-things-considered judgment.

I can construct a rationale for the rule: claims about motivation and institutional response are cheap to produce from prior conviction, difficult to falsify in advance, and sensitive to political facts that change quickly, while I am trained to read climate-model uncertainty and have some sense of when it should be trusted. But I can also construct an unflattering description of the same rule: that I have quietly demoted the part of the problem I find messy and human, and promoted the part I find technically legible, on no stronger ground than my own competence and comfort. I want to know whether this evidential asymmetry is defensible as a policy for a bounded reasoner, or whether it is a bias dressed as methodological hygiene. Under what conditions should claims about the behavioral and political response to a proposed technology carry more weight in early decisions, not less? Is there a principled statement of the rule that keeps whatever is right about it while avoiding the bias, and by what internal test could I tell that I was following the principled version rather than the self-serving one?
