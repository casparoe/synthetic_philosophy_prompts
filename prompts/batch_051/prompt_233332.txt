Since retiring from a career in the physical sciences I have begun to sit in on philosophy seminars, and a remark I met recently has been nagging at me. In a session on intergenerational justice, I questioned the practice of discounting future benefits, and a participant answered with the proverb that a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. His claim was that the proverb shows it is simply rational to prefer a present benefit to an equal benefit arriving later, and that public policy is therefore justified in counting a future benefit less heavily than a present one of the same magnitude. I suspect the proverb is being asked to do more than it can, but I cannot quite say which of the several things it might mean is carrying the argument.

I would like you to take that proverb apart. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing: for example, claims about the certainty of future benefits, about the productive opportunities a present good opens up, about the bare fact of where a good is located in time — what some call pure time preference — about how people in fact choose, about how a prudent or moral agent ought to choose, and any other distinct reading you find in the vicinity. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and whether it would actually support discounting the welfare of future people in public policy. Then identify which reading the seminar participant's inference requires, and say whether that reading survives scrutiny. I want a menu of readings with verdicts, not a single preferred interpretation.
