I keep noticing a move in reasoning about formal systems that I do not have a precise term for. Take the MU-puzzle. You begin with the string MI and four rules: append a U to any string ending in I; from any string of the form Mx derive Mxx; replace any occurrence of III with U; and delete UU. The task is to derive MU. One can grind through derivations indefinitely without learning whether MU is reachable; the decisive step is to stop applying the rules and observe from outside that every string obtainable from MI contains a number of I's not divisible by 3, whereas MU contains zero I's. The obstruction is a property the rules preserve, not a move the rules permit. I see the same maneuver in impossibility proofs for sliding-block puzzles and in arguments that a program or protocol can never reach certain states. I have always been uneasy with the usual description of this as 'stepping outside the system,' since the invariant argument is itself just arithmetic or logic, which could in turn be codified in some larger system. Does this maneuver have an established name? If so, I want a precise account that separates it from an ordinary proof of impossibility, from 'going meta' in general, and from a routine invariant argument; and if the description overstates what is happening, identify exactly where, and say whether one ever gets outside systems altogether or only out of one system into another.
