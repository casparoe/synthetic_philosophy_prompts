I'm a novelist working on a book set in the world of academic science—there's a subplot about a nasty priority dispute, and I keep writting and deleting the same scene because I can't decide what my characters should actually be entitled to claim. One of them keeps invoking Stigler's law, the idea that discoveries are almost never named after the person who actually first made them; he uses it to shrug off every attribution fight, and I don't buy it, but I can't articulate why.

So: give me a working definition of "discovery"—or "discoverer," if you'd rather define the person than the act—specific enough that it settles who, if anyone, gets to count as the discoverer of a scientific or mathematical result. Test it against these cases:

- Amin derives and publishes a genuinely new result, and nobody else had gotten there before.
- Bea reads about that result in a textbook and explains it aloud at lunch.
- Chen states a sharp conjecture in print but can't prove it; sixty years later Ortiz proves it. Does Chen count as the discoverer? Ortiz?
- A researcher's private 1931 notebook contains the full result, but it's never published or shown to anyone; in 1991, a stranger re-derives and publishes it independently. Who, if anyone, discovered it?
- Two groups publish the same result the same month, with no contact and no knowledge of each other. Are both discoverers, or does that stretch the word past usefulness?
- A lab tech notices an anomalous reading that doesn't fit the theory and logs it carefully; the principal investigator later interprets that log entry and announces a new phenomenon. Did either of them discover it?
- An automated system proves a theorem by exhaustively checking thousands of cases that no human has actually inspected one by one. Did the theorem get discovered? If so, by whom—the programmers, the machine, someone else?
- A craft tradition has exploited a material's useful property for centuries; an academic later publishes the first systematic account of it and gets the thing named after him. Does the academic count as the discoverer? The craftspeople?

Then do the thing a good working definition should do: say where your definition gives a wrong answer or no answer, revise it, or defend the surprising verdicts. Also tell me which of your classifications is shakiest, and whether your definition explains why Stigler's law keeps happening without concluding that nobody ever really discovers anything.

Oh, and write the answer in American English—my copyeditor insists.
