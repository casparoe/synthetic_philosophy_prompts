In the animal ethics course I am currently taking, one of the most common moves in class discussion is to rank foods by the number of animals that must be killed to produce them. On this informal scoring rule, beef usually comes out as less bad than chicken, because a single cow supplies a great many meals while a single broiler supplies only one or two; the same logic is sometimes extended to dairy and eggs. The rule counts only the animals killed directly to produce the food. It does not count the field animals crushed by harvesters or ploughed up when feed crops are cultivated, the insects poisoned by pesticides, or the wild animals displaced when land is converted to pasture or cropland. I am trying to work out what moral theory this scoring rule presupposes, and whether it really needs that theory. Does the rule presuppose that one death is one unit of harm regardless of the kind of animal, that only intended deaths count while foreseen collateral deaths do not, and that wild suffering sits outside the ledger because it is nobody's doing? Or is the rule compatible with several different theories of value, with the exclusion of wild animals a separate and optional assumption? Give a committed reconstruction of the implicit theory behind the practice, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what a scoring rule that included wild-animal deaths would have to presuppose instead.
