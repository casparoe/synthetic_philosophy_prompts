I've been going down a rabbit hole on how you can tell what an optimization process is actually optimizing just by watching what it does — not from its documentation, but from its choices. It feels like a deep problem: the same behavior is consistent with lots of different objective-plus-search-space packages, and you can only pry them apart by looking at tradeoffs and edge cases. I want you to help me think this through with AlphaZero as a case study. Please approach this as a careful philosopher of AI / epistemic engineer: start by brainstorming the possible objective/search-space combinations that fit the facts, then narrow them down, and be explicit about which inferences you're confident in and which are just guesses.

Here's the exercise. Treat the 2017 AlphaZero–Stockfish games as a natural experiment in reading an optimizer's objective and search space off its behavior. AlphaZero was trained by self-play with basically one terminal signal — win, lose, or draw — and used a neural net to guide Monte Carlo tree search; it had no hand-tuned evaluation function, no opening book, and no endgame tablebases, while Stockfish brought decades of engineered evaluation terms and search heuristics. Yet AlphaZero's most striking visible trait was that it would give up material for long-term positional pressure of a kind a classical evaluation function tends to discourage, and it converted plenty of those games into wins. What exactly does that behavior license us to infer about (a) AlphaZero's effective objective during search — e.g., whether it was optimizing expected game outcome, some proxy like material or centipawn advantage, or something stranger like 'preserve winning chances without risking a loss' — and (b) its search space — which moves and plans were even reachable in its tree, and at what effective depth or abstraction? Then run the mapping in the other direction: where does 'read the objective off the moves' break down? What additional evidence would you need to distinguish a patient true-outcome objective from a cheap proxy objective, and how much of AlphaZero's style is really search bias or the network's inductive bias rather than the objective itself? Finish by saying what the case teaches about the general project of inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from behavior — is it more promising or more hopeless than people assume? I'm less interested in chess commentary than in the epistemology: keep the mapping disciplined and say explicitly where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak.
