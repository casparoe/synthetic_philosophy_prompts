Consider the St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, and if the first heads occurs on the nth toss the player receives 2^n dollars. The game has infinite expected monetary value, yet most people would pay only a modest finite amount to play it. The standard resolutions appeal to diminishing marginal utility of money, the finite wealth of the banker, or a refusal to let vanishingly small probabilities of enormous payoffs dominate the decision.

Now change one feature: the coin is biased, landing heads with probability p. For p > 1/2 the game has finite expected value, namely 2p/(2p − 1), while for p ≤ 1/2 the expectation diverges. As p approaches 1/2 from above the expectation becomes arbitrarily large, but nearly all of it is contributed by the far tail: at p = 0.5001, outcomes in which the first heads appears only after the hundredth toss have combined probability below 10^-30, yet they account for roughly 98% of the expected value.

Write an essay analyzing how the standard resolutions of the St. Petersburg paradox fare in this biased version. Which responses still do explanatory work once the expectation is finite, and which must be revised or replaced? Does the behavior near p = 1/2 show that expected-value fanaticism can arise even with a finite expectation, thereby reframing what the original paradox is fundamentally about? Weigh the main alternatives and defend a definite conclusion.
