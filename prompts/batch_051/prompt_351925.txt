I'm taking a class on Buddhist philosophy this term and a logic course at the same time, and I keep noticing a parallel I can't decide is deep or just me pattern-matching. Nāgārjuna's tetralemma runs through four options for any claim about a thing: it is, it is not, it both is and is not, it neither is nor is not—and on the reading we're doing he refuses all four. That lines up exactly with the four semantic values in Belnap's four-valued logic/FDE: true only, false only, both, neither. T, F, B, N map one-to-one onto the four koti's. But the part that bugs me is this: in FDE, 'both' and 'neither' are live truth-statuses a sentence can have and the logic is built to reason with them, while Nāgārjuna seems to reject 'both' and 'neither' just as hard as he rejects plain true and plain false. So am I confusing the fourth koti ('neither') with the much stronger move of dismissing the whole four-way frame? And if the idea is that all four koti are 'not ultimately correct', is that refusal functioning like a fifth option smuggled in, or like a meta-level operator that isn't itself a truth-value? What exactly would FDE have to give up—or add—to model the Madhyamaka move rather than just reproducing the list of four options? Does anything on the FDE side, like paraconsistent entailment, actually transfer and teach us something about the tetralemma, or does the analogy break right at the point where both/neither are values for Belnap but positions-to-be-rejected for Nāgārjuna?
