I am an undergraduate enrolled in a course called Philosophy of Technology and Death, and I am preparing my seminar presentation for the end of term. I would like you to write me a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not a restatement of the talk.

The thesis I will defend is this: when a bereaved person uses a griefbot—a conversational system trained on the deceased person's text messages, voice, or other personal traces—while keeping clearly in view that the bot is a reconstruction and not the person, that use is ordinarily permissible and can support mourning rather than obstruct it. I will argue that the strongest objections to griefbots succeed only against specific implementations, above all commercial systems designed to maximize engagement, and not against the practice as such.

My line of argument, compressed, is the following. First, the consent objection, according to which the deceased never agreed to be simulated, is weaker than it appears. If death means there is no longer a subject who can be wronged by such a simulation, the objection must be recast as speculation about what the deceased would have wanted, and it is hard to justify giving the dead a veto over the mourning practices of the living. Second, the deception objection—that even a clearly labelled bot invites the user to treat it as the person—is the most serious objection of the three, but it supports design norms and social practices that keep the bot's constructed status explicit rather than supporting prohibition; photographs, letters, and graveside conversations already invite closely related ways of acting as if the dead person were present. Third, the commodification objection, according to which platforms profit by holding users in grief, is the most compelling against the industry as it actually exists, but it targets the commercial infrastructure rather than a non-commercial, user-controlled bot.

The audience is a twelve-person upper-level seminar. Most of the members are philosophy majors who think in terms of posthumous wrongs, dignity, informed consent, and self-deception. Three are human-computer interaction students who tend to think many philosophical objections dissolve once one looks at how such systems are actually built and used. The instructor works on relational autonomy and will press me on whether my argument smuggles in an atomistic picture of the bereaved person as a free individual chooser.

Please give me the eight or so objections I am actually likely to receive from this room, ordered roughly by how damaging they would be, each phrased the way the relevant member of this audience would phrase it. For each objection, state how likely it is to come up, what I should concede, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply, and identify where that reply is softest. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks. Also tell me the weakest joint in my own argument, so I can prepare to defend it rather than hope the room misses it.
