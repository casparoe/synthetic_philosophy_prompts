I am a control engineer, and the vocabulary I reach for instinctively runs to setpoints, plant dynamics, disturbances, open-loop versus closed-loop intervention, model mismatch, and restricted admissible control inputs. I have followed only from a distance the philosophical dispute about nudges, defaults, and the legitimacy of choice architecture. Please restate that dispute as a translation into control-theoretic terms rather than a paraphrase in its own vocabulary. Treat a nudge or default as an alteration of the plant or of its initial conditions, not as a restriction on the human's admissible control inputs; treat the paternalist's claim that the intervention leaves the agent better off by the agent's own lights as a claim that the designer can identify the agent's own setpoint and steer the system toward it; and treat the libertarian constraint—that options remain formally open—as the constraint that the human's admissible set of control actions is never reduced, however much the environment is reshaped.

Then give me the residue report. Tell me which parts of the dispute translate cleanly, which parts you had to bend to make them fit, and which parts have no representation in the control-theoretic picture at all: for example, the critics' claim that steering someone without their awareness is a distinct wrong even when it moves them toward the very behavior they would endorse, or the claim that a person is to be treated as the author of their own ends rather than as a plant whose setpoint is given. For each untranslatable residue, I want your judgment as to whether it exposes a real blind spot in the control-theoretic vocabulary that I should try to represent explicitly, or whether it is an artifact of the philosophers' looser formulation that dissolves once the design problem is made precise.
