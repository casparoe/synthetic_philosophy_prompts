I've been arguing with a friend in an MD/PhD program about an asymmetry in how we judge doctors, and I can't decide whether it's defensible or just a superstition the system is built on. Suppose an oncologist has a real but not overwhelming hunch, from a plausible mechanism and a couple of small messy early-phase results, that drug A is probably better than drug B for a given cancer. If she simply gives a patient B outside any trial, that looks like a serious failure of her duty: she believes A is probably better, and she acted against her own medical judgment. But if she enrolls the same patient in a randomized trial in which a computer assigns A or B, the act of letting the patient receive B becomes not only permissible but the kind of thing we complain there isn't enough of. The doctor can be the same person, with the same hunch, on the same afternoon; the only thing that changes is whether she or the randomization decides.

The obvious answer is clinical equipoise: a trial is acceptable only when the expert community is genuinely uncertain, so an individual doctor's hunch shouldn't count as knowledge, and the patient is randomized precisely because nobody knows. But that seems to solve the problem by averaging across experts while a particular doctor still treats a particular patient. The community might be uncertain only because the investigator hasn't shown them her unpublished pilot data. If she would refuse B for herself, is she really in equipoise just because the field doesn't yet know what she knows? And if patient consent is doing the real work, then why isn't it fine outside a trial for a patient to consent to the probably-worse drug; why does the fact that the coin flip will eventually produce a publication make the same risk acceptable?

So the question I want answered: what exactly does enrolling a patient in a trial change about the doctor's obligation to that patient, such that a treatment she'd be wrong to choose is permissable when randomization chooses it? Is it the knowledge gained for future patients, the consent, the ex ante chance of getting A, the fact that she didn't 'choose' B, or a combination? And does your answer survive the pilot-data case, where the doctor's hunch is strong but unpublished and community equipose is mostly a polite fiction? I'm not asking for a history of the doctrine; I want the strongest rational reconstruction of the asymmetry, tested against that hard case.
