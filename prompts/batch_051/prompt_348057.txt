I run a five-person benefits appeals team at a company with a self-funded health plan. We handle a little over 40 appeals a week and each one is supposed to get turned around in under half an hour; none of us are lawyers. The plan document is about 70 pages, with a purpose section up front saying the plan exists to cover medically necessary care and shield members from catastrophic costs, and then a long list of exclusions and limitations. We keep going back and forth because one reviewer reads the exclusion literally and denies, another reads the same exclusion in light of the purpose section and grants, and a third is always saying "they can't have meant that, that has to be a drafting mistake." Our counsel has also told us that if language is genuinely ambiguous after we've done everything else, we read it against the plan because we're the ones who drafted it, but she wants that treated as a labeled last resort, not a shortcut.

I'd like you to turn the textualism-vs-purposivism debate into a reusable decision procedure my reviewers can actually follow, not an essay about it. For each step, say exactly what in the document they should look at, what question they're answering in plain language, and where to go next. The procedure should cover at least: what to do when the text looks clear on its face; what to do when its ambiguous; when the purpose section may override or constrain a literal reading and when it may not; when the plain text has to win even though the result feels bad for the employee; and when the reviewer must stop and escalate to counsel. If you use any cannons of construction, translate each one into plain questions a non-lawyer can answer from the document itself; if you can't translate a cannon that way, don't use it, and tell me which one you dropped and why.

Then test the procedure on these three recent appeals. (1) The plan excludes "treatment for obesity." An employee whose significant weight gain was caused by hypothyroidism was prescribed a medication for that weight gain; the purpose section says the plan covers treatment of the underlying disease. (2) The plan covers "inpatient hospital services" and excludes "residential treatment facilities." An employee's 15-year-old was admitted to a residential mental-health program that is licensed as a healthcare facility and staffed by nurses, but it is not licensed as a hospital; the purpose section says the plan covers medically necessary treatment of severe mental illness. (3) The plan excludes "injuries sustained while under the influence of alcohol." An employee broke her ankle in a fall after two drinks; her blood alcohol was below the legal limit for driving, and two witnesses saw her trip on a broken stair. For each case, show the path through the steps, the decision, and which school of thought is doing the work at each point; flag the decision my reviewers are likely to hate and explain why the procedure gives it anyway.

After the procedure, give me back a JSON object summarizing it, with exactly these fields: "procedure_name" (string); "steps" (array of objects, each with "order" (integer), "question" (the plain-language question the reviewer answers), "if_yes" (next step number or final decision), "if_no" (next step number or final decision), and "source" (one of "textualism", "purposivism", "tiebreaker", "judgment", or "hybrid")); "escalation_triggers" (array of strings, each a concrete condition that means the reviewer stops and sends the file to counsel); "worked_cases" (array of objects, each with "case_label", "facts", "steps_hit" (array of step numbers), "decision", "winning_school" (one of the same five labels), and "likely_unpopular" (boolean)); and "unresolved_cases" (array of strings describing the kinds of appeal the procedure cannot settle on its own).
