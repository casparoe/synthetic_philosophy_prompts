I'm a retired chemist, and I've spend the last few years reading legal philosophy for fun. There's a pattern I keep noticing but I don't have a word for. A town has a bylaw from 1912 forbidding ice cream trucks, or one banning the drying of laundry on balconies. Nobody has enforced it in living memory, the council never repealed it, and everyone—including the police—treats it as dead. Then one day a new official uses it to go after someone whose behavior is annoying for unrelated reasons, and the court has to decide whether the charge is lawful. On a plain reading the statute is still in force, since it was never repealed or struck down. But it also seems wrong to say it was governing anyone's conduct all those decades, because nobody ever took it into account.

I'm not just looking for a label, though I'd like to know if this situation has an established name. What I really want to understand is exactly what's going on: how is this different from a law that's merely underenforced, or from a custom that has quietly replaced a statute? And what does it do to the rule-of-law idea that law should be public, stable, and applied as written? I come at this with a chemist's habit of looking for underlying mechanisms, and I can't figure out what would make the statute simultaneously valid and dead. Does the long disuse itself have any legal significance, or is a dormant statute exactly as valid as one passed yesterday until someone gets around to repealing it?
