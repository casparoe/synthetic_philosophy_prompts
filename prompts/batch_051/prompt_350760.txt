I recently read a survey chapter on Popper that gave a two-paragraph account of the problem of verisimilitude, and I want to know how far to trust it. The author says that Popper needed a notion of one false theory being closer to the truth than another, because his picture of science as conjecture and refutation treats our best theories as typically strictly false even though some are plainly better approximations than others. Popper also wanted this notion to reward content rather than probability: a rich theory that is almost right should count as closer to the truth than a cautious near-tautology. His proposal was to define verisimilitude as the difference between a theory's truth content and its falsity content — roughly, its true consequences minus its false consequences, with greater content counting in the theory's favor. This looked promising until Tichý and Miller independently showed in the 1970s that, on Popper's definitions, no false theory is more truthlike than any other: the measure collapses exactly in the cases it was designed to handle. Since their arguments do not depend on any special features of Popper's setup, the chapter concludes that verisimilitude cannot be given any formal or comparative account at all, and that talk of scientific progress as increasing closeness to the truth is best understood as flattering rhetoric rather than a factual assertion. I suspect the final step is too strong, but I am not sure whether the technical result is even being reported correctly or whether later work repaired the idea. Could you audit this account for me: which parts are accurate, which are misrepresentations or overstatements, and does the conclusion about progress toward truth follow from the parts that survive scrutiny?
