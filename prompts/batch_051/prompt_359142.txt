I help run a political theory reading group, and in our most recent meeting — on whether citizens of wealthy states are permitted, required, or forbidden to weight the interests of their compatriots above those of distant strangers — one member cut short a long exchange by saying that 'charity begins at home'. In his hands the proverb was doing several jobs at once: it was meant to establish that people in fact care more for those near to them, that this partiality is morally fitting, and that a state may therefore direct its resources to its own citizens before attending to need abroad. I found this too quick, but I could not say precisely why at the time.

I would like you to lay out, as a menu, the distinct theses that 'charity begins at home' could be asserting in this context. For each reading, specify the exact claim — whether about moral psychology, the proper ordering of concern, the division of moral labour, the justification of special obligations, or the limits of cosmopolitan impartiality — and give a verdict on whether it is trivially true, plausible but limited, or false, and on what it would and would not establish. Then identify which reading or readings provide the most charitable reconstruction of what my fellow member was actually arguing, and say whether any of them genuinely supports the conclusion that compatriots have a special claim on resources that distant strangers lack. Please write the answer in British English.
