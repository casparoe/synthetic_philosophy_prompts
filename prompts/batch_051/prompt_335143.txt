I'd like to read it through the prescriptivism-versus-descriptivism debate in the philosophy of language: on one side, the view that some grammar and usage really are wrong and that correcting someone is teaching them to speak properly; on the other, the view that 'correct' just means the prestige dialect, that every dialect has a complete grammar of its own, and that calling a variant 'bad grammar' is usually a social judgment wearing a linguistic costume. The play feels almost built for this, since the premise of Higgins's experiment is that the only thing thats changed about Eliza is how she talks, and everything else — her status, how she's treated, what she can do — is supposed to follow or fail to follow from that alone. But I don't want the discussion to end at "Higgins is a snob and prescriptivism is false," because by the end Eliza does have something she didn't have before, and she talks about it in terms of self-respect and freedom, not just accent. Shaw himself was deeply into phonetics and spelling reform — he wanted to make 'correct' speech easier for everyone to acquire — so I suspect he's not a pure descriptivist either.

Could you give me a careful reading of the play through this lens: where the text sides with prescriptivism, where it sides with descriptivism, and where it refuses the binary altogether?
