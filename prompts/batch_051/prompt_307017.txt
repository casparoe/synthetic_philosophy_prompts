I've been trying to get a handle on the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents, and I noticed a parallel I can't decide about. In provability logic, Löb's theorem is □(□P→P)→□P. The usual gloss is that a reasoner can't prove its own soundness about P unless it can already prove P. That sounds really close to the epistemological point that you can't verify the reliability of your own belief-forming methods by using those very methods, because the verification would already assume the reliability its supposed to establish. But maybe it's only a superficial analogy. Treat this as a philosopher of self-reference and formal epistemology, and think carefully about whether there's a genuine structural identity here or just a loose metaphor. Where does the mapping hold and where does it break? For example, does "believing that your beliefs are reliable" really correspond to □P→P, and what does a reasoner have to be able to prove about its own proof predicate before Löb actually constrains it?
