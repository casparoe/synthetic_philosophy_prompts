A familiar argument in discussions of moral agency runs as follows. To be a moral agent, a system must be able to act on its judgments; a system that can assess situations and deliberate but cannot intervene in the world is at most a moral spectator or a moral patient, not a moral agent. When people defended this requirement, the paradigm case was usually a human in a constrained position: a bystander, a locked-in patient, or an adviser with no authority. The assumption buried in those examples was that the judging entity was separate from the machinery of action, and that its judgments could not reshape the world except through its own bodily intervention.

Now consider a different kind of case. A large-language-model-style system has been trained on an enormous amount of human normative discourse and can produce subtle, case-specific moral judgments, fluently participate in deliberation, and revise its judgments when challenged. In a particular deployment it is deliberately given no executive capacity: no actuators, no ability to write to its own memory, no way to force a decision, and every candidate action it proposes passes through a hard filter or a human overseer before anything happens in the world. Suppose, moreover, that its judgments are frequently adopted downstream, not because the system has been given authority, but because the human overseers find its reasoning independently persuasive. Does the old argument survive this case? I am asking what happens to the argument, not what the deployment policy should be. Which premise exactly is now under pressure? Does the argument collapse, does it need repair, or is it untouched? If a repair is available, say what weaker but still significant claim could take its place; if not, explain why the pressure only points to a nearby issue, such as a distinction between moral agency and moral influence.
