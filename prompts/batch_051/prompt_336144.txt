I am a PhD candidate in intellectual history, and I have been turning over an argument that came up in a methods workshop. It seems to prove that the pejorative notion of "presentism" is empty — a conclusion I find unacceptable, since it would dissolve the distinction between careful contextualist reconstruction and undisciplined anachronism. I want to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

(1) A historian can choose a question, a period, a descriptive vocabulary, and a standard of significance only from within her own historical situation; there is no standpoint outside all times from which these choices could be made.
(2) If inquiry is conducted from within the historian's situation, then its questions, descriptions, and standards of significance are necessarily shaped by the concerns and categories of the historian's present.
(3) For an account of the past to be presentist in the sense the term criticizes just is for it to be shaped by the concerns and categories of the present rather than by the categories in which the historical actors understood themselves.
(4) Therefore every historical account is presentist in the sense the term criticizes.
(5) If every historical account is presentist in that sense, then the charge of presentism is empty: it applies to all accounts indiscriminately and cannot count in favor of any account over another.
(6) But the charge is not empty. A study that attributes a modern national identity to a fifteenth-century peasant, or that evaluates a premodern legal procedure by contemporary due-process standards, is criticizable in a way that a careful reconstruction of the peasants' or litigants' own categories is not. Whatever exactly the charge of presentism tracks, it tracks a real difference here.
(7) Contradiction. Therefore at least one of (1), (2), and (3) is false.

I would most like to keep (1) and (6): I accept that historical knowledge is situated, and I also accept that anachronism is a genuine criticism. But I do not see which premise to give up. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to — in particular, what account of the term of criticism "presentism" do I owe if the problem is in (3), and what alternative account of situated inquiry do I owe if the problem is in (2)?
