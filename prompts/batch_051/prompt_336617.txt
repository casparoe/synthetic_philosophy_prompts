I'd like a reading of Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose through the problem behind sola scriptura: once you say a text is the sole authority, you still have to decide what the text means, and whoever gets to make that call becomes a new authority — so you get a regress instead of a foundation. The novel is famously a murder mystery about people killing to control the meaning of one book, and I'd like you to take the theological machinery seriously rather than treating it as set dressing. Read Jorge's insistence that Aristotle's book on laughter should stay unread, and the abbey's locked library, as a dramatization of the regress: the text has authority precisely because access to it is policed, and interpretation is a form of power. Does the novel think the regress is inescapable, or does William of Baskerville model an interpretive practice that doesn't just replace one arbiter with another? And at the end, when the library burns and the book is lost and Adso is left assemble fragments, does Eco side with the view that meaning needs an institution to settle disputes, or does he leave open a reader who goes on reading alone, with no church to say what the text means? I'd like an honest reading that say where the novel complicates or resists the philosophical point as much as where it dramatizes it — and what the novel adds to the debate that a seminar on hermeneutics wouldn't.
