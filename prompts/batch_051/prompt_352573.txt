I co-convene a small working group on moral psychology and cultural difference. The group began after a personal episode: my cousin and I failed the same person in nearly the same way, yet I responded with a focused sense of having done a bad thing and set about repairing it, while he responded with a diffuse feeling of exposure and worthlessness and withdrew. Only later did I recognize that our responses tracked the contrast between guilt and shame. Since then, two members of the group have settled into opposing positions, and I have asked them to produce an adversarial-collaboration document rather than another round of arguments.

The first position holds that guilt is the more defensible moral emotion. On this view, guilt targets the act rather than the self; it leads the offender to acknowledge the wrong, apologize, and make repair, and it remains compatible with self-respect. Shame, by contrast, targets the whole self, feels like being exposed as defective, and tends to produce hiding, avoidance, or counterattack rather than repair. From this side, a culture that makes guilt its central moral emotion is better at holding people accountable without defining them by a single failure.

The second position holds that shame has been unfairly maligned. On this view, shame responds to falling short of shared standards in the eyes of others, and it tracks real damage to standing, belonging, and mutual regard; it registers that a relationship or community has been injured, not merely that a rule has been broken. Guilt, this member argues, can become a private, cheap, and self-absolving transaction that never faces the person harmed. Properly directed shame keeps the offender's attention on the social meaning of the wrong and can motivate genuine repair and reintegration. They also argue that the modern preference for guilt over shame is culturally local, tied to a particular individualist picture of the self, and that other cultures' reliance on shame is not a moral deficit.

Please draft the joint document these two members could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should include:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section: the claims both can accept about how guilt and shame arise, what each tracks, and what each tends to track poorly.
2. A disagreements table: for each genuine disagreement, a neutral statement of the dispute, then each side's position stated in wording that side would accept for itself.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect one crux is whether shame necessarily involves a global negative evaluation of the self, and another is whether a culture's emotional norms can be deliberately redesigned rather than only emerging historically; identify any others.
4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or kind of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about what it would show recorded in advance.
5. A note distinguishing the substantive disagreements from any that are merely verbal — for example, whether the two sides sometimes mean different phenomena by the word shame.

I want the document to be something both members could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.
