As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have spent the past few months reporting on the quarrel over whether people who use image-generation systems are engaged in creativity at all. A view has been forming in my notebook, and I would like it pressure-tested before I ever commit to it in print. Its starting point is that novelty has become nearly free: a diffusion model can produce thousands of distinct and superficially arresting images in an afternoon, so creativity cannot consist in the production of new combinations or variations, because that is precisely what the machine does unaided and without, I assume, being creative in the interesting sense. Instead I have been toying with what I call the signature condition: an artefact is creative only if someone stands behind its novelty as their own act. By this I mean that the person who presents the work could, if asked why this particular variation was kept rather than a dozen equally plausible neighbours, give an honest first-person answer — "I rejected the others because…, I pushed the composition darker because…, the unresolved tension is the point" — rather than merely reporting that the system happened to emit it. Creativity, on this account, is not generation but authorial liability for selection: one is creative to the extent that one is answerable for the choices the machine's outputs let one make, not to the extent that one is surprised by what it made.

The view is meant to explain how the same prompt can be a creative act in one person's hands and not in another's: the person who clicks through a hundred outputs and posts the most impressive one without being able to say what "most impressive" was tracking has only incidental taste, whereas the person who curates, rejects, refines, and finally endorses a single image has made themselves answerable for it. I can already see problems. It may just be the old Duchampian point that selection is an artistic act, updated for diffusion models; or a variant of what critics call curatorial creativity; and I am unsure whether the language of "standing behind" and "liability" does any real work or merely moralises about art. So act as a professional philosopher of art with expertise in creativity and computational aesthetics. Take your time: first reconstruct the signature condition in the strongest, most charitable form it can bear, then assess it candidly. Is it coherent and genuinely distinct from existing accounts, and if not, which account does it collapse into? What does it get right about AI-assisted art? Above all, where does it break? Give me the cases it misclassifies as creative or uncreative, and say explicitly which objections are fatal and which are merely awkward.
