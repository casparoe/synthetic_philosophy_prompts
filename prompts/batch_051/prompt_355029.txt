You are a professor of philosophy of science writing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on scientific inference. The seminar has covered inference to the best explanation, explanatory virtues, the distinction between an explanation's loveliness and its likeliness, and the bad-lot worry. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the specific conceptual moves that would separate an A student from a B student before drafting anything.

Write one exam question, intended to be answered in 90 minutes, that presents the following invented scenario and asks the candidate to reach a justified verdict. The question should be unanswerable by reciting a definition of inference to the best explanation or listing explanatory virtues; it should force the candidate to confront a case in which explanatory virtues point in opposite directions and to say something precise about what would make the simpler but anomalous model more likely to be true.

Then provide:
1. The exam question itself, including the scenario.
2. A model answer of about 700 words.
3. A marking scheme with exactly five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong.

Here is the scenario to embed in the question:

An ecologist studying geographic variation in the mating calls of a songbird compares one mainland region with three island populations. Model M1 posits a single mechanism: call variation is driven entirely by how well sound transmits through the local vegetation. M1 has one free parameter and unifies nearly all the mainland data under a single gradient, but it fails on the three islands, where calls vary in the opposite direction from what the vegetation model predicts. Model M2 retains the vegetation mechanism but adds two further mechanisms: a predation-pressure effect and an island founder effect. M2 covers all the data, and each added mechanism is individually plausible, but the two added mechanisms carry extra adjustable parameters, and the founder-effect assumptions were selected after seeing the island data and currently have no independent support. M1 is simpler and more unified; M2 has fuller scope and leaves no anomalies. Neither model is better in every explanatory respect.

The exam question should ask something in the neighborhood of this, refined by you: Does inference to the best explanation, as an account of how we should infer, yield a determinate verdict between M1 and M2? What would have to be true for M1, despite its anomalies, to be more likely to be true than M2, and what would have to be true for M2's extra mechanisms to count as a mark of a better explanation rather than a mark of ad hoc patchwork? The question should require students to distinguish clearly between assessing how lovely an explanation is (roughly, how much understanding or unification it affords) and assessing how likely it is (roughly, how probable it is given the evidence and background knowledge).

One more request: if you think this question rests on a confused premise—for example, if pitting the models against each other in this way presumes that inference to the best explanation must always issue a winner, or if the loveliness–likeliness distinction is not doing the work I think it is—then say so plainly and revise the question instead of writing a model answer around the muddle. I would rather receive a corrected question than a polite compliance.
