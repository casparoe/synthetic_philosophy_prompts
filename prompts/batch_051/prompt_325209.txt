I am a philosophy-of-language PhD student, and I keep constructing the following argument, whose conclusion I cannot accept. (1) "He is a Boche" and "He is a German" are true in exactly the same situations, where "Boche" is a derogatory word for a German. (2) So "Boche" contributes no truth-conditional content beyond that of "German". (3) If an expression contributes no truth-conditional content, its contribution is pragmatic rather than semantic. (4) If an expression's contribution is merely pragmatic, it is supplied by the speaker on the occasion of use, not by the word's conventional meaning. (5) So the derogatory force of "Boche" is not part of the word's conventional meaning but is added by the speaker each time. (6) If (5) were right, a speaker should be able to cancel that derogation: "He is a Boche, but I mean nothing derogatory by it." Yet the derogation survives the cancellation, and the utterance remains infelicitous in a way (5) predicts it should not. (7) So at least one of (1)–(4) is false. I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since the extensional equivalence seems independently right. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked safe, and what a semantic theory that keeps (1) and (2) while still making the word itself derogatory has to commit to. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
