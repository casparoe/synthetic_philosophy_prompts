My buddy and I spent way to long last night arguing about whether language actually shapes thought, and we ended up making a list of the neo-Whorfian findings we both consider the classics: (1) Russian blues, where Russian speakers are faster to tell two shades apart when they fall on opposite sides of the goluboy/siniy boundary, and the advantage goes away under verbal interference; (2) the grammatical gender effect, where German and Spanish speakers describe the same object in stereotypically feminine or masculine terms depending on its gender in their language, the bridge being the famous example; (3) the spatial frames work, where speakers of a language like Guugu Yimithirr use compass directions even for tabletop space and apparently have to stay oriented to remember where things are; (4) Mandarin speakers arranging time vertically rather than left-to-right more than English speakers do; and (5) the Pirahã number result, where speakers of a language that allegedly lacks exact number words beyond one/two/many can't do exact matching for larger quantities.

Here's the thing. My friend says all five are secretly the same finding: the language makes a certain contrast or dimension salient, and then you see it in a fast discrimination or sorting/arranging task. I don't think it's right, but I couldn't come up with the counterexample I was reaching for. I'm pretty sure there's at least one other classic paradigm out there, one where the linguistic effect shows up in a categorization judgment—something closer to what counts as the same kind of thing—rather than in reaction time or spatial arrangement. What is that paradigm? Give me the setup and the result, and explain why it's genuinely different: not just another instance of the salience mechanism, and not a different-language version of one of these five.
