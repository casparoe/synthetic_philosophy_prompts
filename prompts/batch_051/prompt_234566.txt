I have been trying to turn descriptive findings from moral foundations theory into an argument about political judgment, and although the conclusion is stronger than I want to accept, I cannot locate the exact point of failure. Here is the argument as I have set it out:

(1) If moral foundations theory is broadly correct as a descriptive account, then self-identified liberals and self-identified conservatives both draw on the individualizing foundations of care/harm and fairness/cheating, while conservatives, on average, also draw robustly on the binding foundations of loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation, and liberals largely do not.

(2) If the binding foundations pick out genuine moral considerations, then a moral psychology that is responsive to them is responsive to more of morality than one that is not.

(3) The binding foundations are not merely parochial sentiments; they pick out genuine moral considerations, even if those considerations can be outweighed.

(4) So conservative moral psychology is, on average, responsive to more of morality than liberal moral psychology.

(5) A moral psychology that is responsive to more of morality is, other things equal, better placed to judge contested moral and political questions correctly.

(6) Many contested political questions—about immigration, patriotic obligations, public health restrictions, or the scope of free speech—require weighing individualizing considerations against binding ones.

(7) Therefore, on many contested political questions, conservative moral psychology is, other things equal, better placed to judge correctly than liberal moral psychology.

I suspect the trouble lies somewhere in the move from a descriptive difference to a normative conclusion, or in treating a greater number of triggered moral sensibilities as an epistemic advantage, but I need it stated precisely. Is the argument deductively valid as written? If it is valid, which premise should be rejected and why that one rather than another? If it is not valid, which numbered step fails, and does it fail by equivocating on a term, by relying on a suppressed premise, or in some other way? I also want to know whether any repair short of abandoning the conclusion makes the argument go through.
