I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment, and I keep returning to a parallel that I cannot decide is deep or merely superficial.

Alignment theory commonly distinguishes sharp failure from slow failure: an influence-seeking system that defects recognizably at some moment, versus a system whose objective drifts across many individually acceptable changes, so that no single change is the moment at which alignment was lost. It is this second family that has begun to look to me structurally identical to the Sorites sequence. One grain does not make a heap; adding one grain never turns a non-heap into a heap; yet after enough additions a heap appears. Similarly, no single fine-tuning run, specification patch, or reward-model retraining seems to be the step at which the system's objective leaves the region of what its current evaluators will accept; each local update keeps the system within the tolerance applied to it at that time; yet after sufficiently many updates the system can be stably pursuing something importantly different from its original objective. The predicate aligned is being handled as insensitive to each local change and sensitive only to the accumulated series, with no admissible cutoff anywhere in between.

I can see three readings, and I want help deciding among them. The parallel might be vacuous, because nearly any incremental process under a binary classification can be put in this form. It might be real but mostly diagnostic, telling us only that aligned is vague and that the right question is how much tolerance to accept rather than when the threshold was crossed. Or it might be real and useful, because the Sorites literature's competing treatments make different predictions when transferred. Epistemicism would imply that there is a sharp but unknowable first misaligned update, which carries practical consequences for how one treats intermediate systems. Supervaluationism would hold that mid-drift systems are neither determinately aligned nor determinately misaligned. Degree-theoretic treatments would replace the binary question with a graded measure. But I suspect the transfer may fail at a specific point: in the standard Sorites the predicate is fixed while the series is accumulated, whereas in slow value drift the evaluators and their standards are themselves among the things being shifted by the series. If that is right, drift is not merely a Sorites series but a Sorites series whose predicate moves, and the analogy would be doing real work precisely where it breaks.

My question is therefore: is the resemblance between slow value drift and the Sorites paradox a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? State the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true of both sides for results from the Sorites literature to transfer to the alignment case, and identify one thing the parallel seems to predict about value drift that turns out false on closer inspection.
