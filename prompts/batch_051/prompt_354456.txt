I'm giving a department colloquium in a few weeks, and the paper I'm presenting defends a constitutivist line on Moore's paradox. My thesis is that the reason you can't properly assert something of the form p but I don't believe that p isn't, as the standard story has it, that assertion is governed by a knowledge rule or a belief rule. It's that belief is constitutively self-ascriptive: a state that didn't dispose its bearer to self-ascribe it, when the question arises, wouldn't count as a belief at all. The oddness of Moore sentences falls out of that, because asserting one displays the speaker flouting a constitutive norm of the very attitude they must be expressing. The audience is mostly normative epistemologists and philosophers of language. The people who'll actually push back are two or three who work on norms of assertion and who'll have the Williamson line and the DeRose-style line ready to hand, plus a couple of Wittgenstein scholars who think the later Wittgenstein already settled all this with avowals and first-person ascriptions, and who'll find my view to theorised. Nobody in the room is signed up to my kind of constitutivism, though two of the epistemologists are sympathetic to transparency accounts of self-knowledge, so they're not hostile to the thought that belief has a special first-person profile. My sense is the objections I'll actually get from this lot aren't the ones I've been rehearsing. Could you put together a Q&A prep sheet for me: the objections, ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do if they land, phrased the way this audience would actually phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft, what I should concede up front, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
