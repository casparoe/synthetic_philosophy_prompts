You are a teaching assistant in an undergraduate philosophy of mind course. The exam question below was set for a short-answer section, and a student submitted the answer that follows it. Grade the student's work carefully.

Exam question:
Explain Searle's Chinese Room argument as a challenge to the claim that the brain is a computer. In your answer, distinguish the strong AI thesis from the weak AI thesis, state exactly what the argument is intended to show, and critically evaluate one standard reply.

Student's answer:
Searle's Chinese Room targets the strong AI thesis, which says that a suitably programmed computer literally understands—for example, understands Chinese—rather than merely simulating understanding. It does not target weak AI, the claim that computers are useful tools for modeling cognitive processes, which Searle accepts. In the thought experiment, a monolingual English speaker with no knowledge of Chinese sits in a room with an English rulebook and a supply of Chinese symbols. Chinese questions are passed in; following the rulebook, he matches symbols by shape and passes out Chinese answers, so that to a Chinese speaker outside the room he appears to understand Chinese. But the man is manipulating symbols purely formally and never attaches any meaning to them. The core premise of the argument is that syntax is not sufficient for semantics: computation is formal manipulation of symbols, and formal manipulation alone cannot produce meaning or understanding. Therefore no program, however well executed, can by itself be sufficient for understanding; strong AI is false.

The strongest reply is the systems reply. It says that although the man does not understand Chinese, the system as a whole—the man, the rulebook, the symbols, and the processes—does understand Chinese. This is essentially the same point as the robot reply, namely that understanding belongs to the whole organism or system rather than to any single part. The reply fails, however, because if no component of the system understands Chinese, the whole system cannot understand it either: a whole cannot have a property that none of its components has. And if we allowed understanding to emerge at the level of the whole system, we would no longer be giving a computational explanation of the mind. Since the robot reply merely adds sensorimotor interactions, which are just more inputs for the symbol-shuffler to process, the Chinese Room therefore shows that no computational description of the brain could explain understanding.
