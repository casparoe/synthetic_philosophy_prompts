I'm a novelist, working on a book about a country preacher in the 1870s who gets himself into a theological hole I can't quite dig him out of, and it's starting to stall the manuscript. The hole is two passages of scripture he keeps banging against each other. The first one, Deuteronomy 24:16, says: "The fathers shall not be put to death for the children, neither shall the children be put to death for the fathers: every man shall be put to death for his own sin." The second, 2 Corinthians 5:21, says: "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him." So one verse says a person can't be punished for another's sin, while the other seems to say God laid human sin on somebody who didn't commit any. My preacher takes the first as a hard rule about how God's justice works, and then the second sounds like God breaking his own rule.

I know the rough map of atonement theories — penal substitution, satisfaction, moral influence — well enough to name them, but I can't sort out which reading of these two verses is real and which is somebody's way of making the problem go away. Am I even right to read these as a contradiction, or does the contradiction only show up once you've already decided the cross is a legal punishment? Give me the candidate reconciliations here — different senses of "being punished for," the difference between a judge punishing somebody and a willing party standing in, a distinction between the old covenant's civil law and what the cross is doing, whatever else there is — and then a verdict on which is honest and which is special pleading. I'd especialy like to know which of the three theories can hold both verses without doing violence to one of them, and whether any of them quietly has to water down the Deuteronomy passage to survive. Because if the tension isn't actually there, the crisis that drives my novel is fake, and I'd rather find that out now than have it collapse in a scene I've already written.
