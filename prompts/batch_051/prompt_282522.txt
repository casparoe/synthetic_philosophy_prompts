I am taking a philosophy of mind elective at my high school, and for our unit on emotion each of us has to write up what we actually think an emotion is, so that the rest of the class can stress-test it before we defend ourselves in discussion. I have settled on the following six commitments, and I would like a consistency audit before I have to present them.

(a) An emotion is, at bottom, a felt pattern of bodily changes: to be afraid just is to feel one's heart racing, one's breathing tighten, and one's muscles tense.
(b) Emotions are nevertheless directed at things in the world. A fear is of the dog, an anger is about the insult, a grief is over the loss; the emotion presents its object as being a certain way.
(c) An emotion can be fitting or unfitting depending on whether the world is as the emotion presents it: fear is fitting only when its object really is dangerous, grief only when the loss really is a loss.
(d) Some emotions are recalcitrant. People can be terrified of flying while sincerely judging that flying is safe, and a remark can sting even when one knows no offense was intended; I take this to show that emotions are not judgments or beliefs.
(e) A felt bodily state, considered just as a feeling, is not the kind of thing that can be fitting or unfitting. A racing heart is not correct or incorrect.
(f) Finally, I hold that an emotion gives one a reason to act only when it is fitting. An unfitting fear of a harmless spider does not give any reason to flee the room.

Which of these commitments actually conflict with each other, which looked to me like they conflicted but in fact do not, and how severe are the genuine tensions? For each real tension, say what would need to be given up or weakened to restore consistency, and tell me which single revision would do the most work. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious.
