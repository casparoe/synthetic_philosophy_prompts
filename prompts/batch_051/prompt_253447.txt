I'm a novelist, and last month at a writers' colony I watched two friends, Lena and Mark, go around and around about another resident, Sasha. Sasha writes literary nonfiction. In a recent piece about a nineteenth-century divorce, she wrote flatly that after the trial the wife never saw her brother again. She told us later she had no source for it; it just felt true to her from the surviving letters. A few weeks after the piece came out, an archivist found a new batch of letters confirming that the brother really did vanish from her life. At dinner, Lena said Sasha had done something wrong anyway; Mark said she hadn't. I scribbled down the arguement because it felt like a scene from the novel I'm drafting, where a journalist character gets caught in this exact kind of situation. But I want to know who was right, not just steal the vibes.

Here's the exchange as close as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Lena: The basic rule for saying something as fact is that you shouldn't assert what you don't know. Sasha didn't know it, she guessed. The later confirmation changes nothing about whether the assertion itself was out of bounds.

(2) Mark: That makes no sense. The whole point of a factual statement is to say something true. She said something true. Nobody was misled. The rule, if there is one, is just: don't assert what's false.

(3) Lena: But then whether Sasha did something wrong is settled by luck after the fact. Same action, same absence of evidence, and she'd have done wrong if the brother had turned up and done no wrong if he didn't. A rule you can actually follow can't depend on an outcome you don't have yet.

(4) Mark: Of course an evaluation can depend on the outcome. The same risky pass in soccer is a great play if it connects and a reckless giveaway if it's picked off. But I'll also add: Sasha at least believed it. The rule could be 'assert only what you believe,' and she had a genuine hunch.

(5) Lena: A hunch isn't belief in the relevant sense. She had no more reason to think the brother never returned than that he did. If belief just means feeling confident, the rule is toothless; anybody can talk themselves into confidence about anything.

(6) Mark: Then you're requiring knowledge, and knowledge is too strong for any real writer. Historians and journalists assert things all the time on inference from ambiguous evidence. If you had to know every sentence, you'd never write a narrative. At most Sasha needed enough support for the claim given the role it played in the piece.

(7) Lena: I never said she needed proof for everything. I said she shouldn't have written a flat factual sentence when the evidence was equally balanced and she knew it. That's the difference between inference and invention. If you allow that, you're no longer doing nonfiction.

(8) Mark: I can accept that she should've flagged it. But your first claim was that she violated the rule and that the later truth doesn't change anything. If we now agree the real problem is that the evidence was too thin, then we're no longer saying knowledge is the norm — we're saying the norm is about adequate support. And adequate support isn't the same thing as knowledge.

Please go through this move by move and adjudicate it: for each of the eight moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or mixed, and why in a sentence or two; then give a bottom line on who is right about what, and on what the rule governing assertion actually is — knowledge, truth, belief, or something like adequate support. Also tell me what both of them are missing.
