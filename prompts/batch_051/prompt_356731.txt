I'm in an advanced set theory course, and we've spent the term on the Axiom of Choice and its weaker relatives. I keep seeing choice defended or attacked by pointing at one or two consequences—Banach–Tarski on one side, Zorn's lemma on the other—and I want the full specification of what an adequate choice principle would have to do before I settle on a position. Write the desiderata: the requirements that an adequate principle of choice (or an adequate account of the role choice should play in set theory) must meet, where the standard of adequacy is doing ordinary mathematics—analysis, algebra, topology—while also making a coherent foundational claim about what mathematical existence amounts to.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference. Sort out at least the following, and add anything else you think belongs on the list. The finite-choice baseline that ZF alone already gives. The socks-versus-shoes distinction and what it shows about needing a rule versus needing only a witness. Which of countable choice, dependent choice, the Boolean prime ideal theorem (equivalently, the ultrafilter lemma), and full choice are load-bearing for which parts of ordinary mathematics: sequential versus topological formulations in analysis, Baire category, the compactness theorem for first-order logic, Tychonoff's theorem, Zorn's lemma, the well-ordering theorem, and the claim that every vector space has a basis. Whether avoiding the allegedly pathological consequences—non-measurable sets, Banach–Tarski decompositions, a well-ordering of the reals—is a genuine requirement or a prejudice of one camp. And whether an adequate principle has to settle the status of choice as a logical principle, a set-existence axiom, or something else.

Then mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are negotiable or parochial, and flag any subsets of the list that are jointly unsatisfiable. (We've seen, for instance, that full choice is consistent with ZF, that it conflicts with the demand that all sets of reals be Lebesgue measurable, and that countable choice is too weak for some standard analysis.) Finally, say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the positions people actually hold—full choice, countable choice only, dependent choice only, and no choice beyond what ZF proves—and score those positions only briefly against the list at the end. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases.
