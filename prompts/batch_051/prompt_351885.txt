In my former life I worked as a research physicist, and since retiring I have been reading my way into economics and the theory of practical reason. A puzzle I cannot shake concerns the way economists treat voluntary market choices as reliable evidence of a person's welfare while discounting the person's own avowals of what he wants. Suppose a man buys cigarettes every day. An economist will count each purchase as a datum that reveals part of his utility function: he values the cigarettes more than the money he gives up. Now suppose the same man tells his doctor that he wishes he could stop, or spends his evenings attending a smoking-cessation clinic. The statement and the clinic attendance tend to be set aside as cheap talk, or as a mere stated preference, because speech costs nothing and because the man might deceive himself or others. Yet attending the clinic is not costless: it consumes time, money and comfort. Both the purchase and the clinic attendance are observable acts, both take place under constraints, and both appear to express something about the man's aims. The economist's practice therefore rests on an asymmetry: choices count as evidence of welfare in a way that avowals and attempts to change one's own behaviour do not, or count far less.

Behind this sits a broader conceptual question about what utility is supposed to mean. Is a person's utility the order that his choices reveal, or is it something more like his considered good, which his choices may fail to track? I can see several candidate justifications for the economist's asymmetry: the greater cost of action than of words; the greater reliability of deeds over speech; the thought that a purchase reveals an all-things-considered trade-off whereas speech reveals only a partial attitude; or the thought that the two behaviours reveal different temporal selves, a present self who buys and a future self who would rather not have bought. What I cannot yet see is whether any of these survives harder cases. If the man throws away his cigarettes before a long trip, or pays a deposit he will forfeit if he smokes, those acts are as costly as the purchases and point the other way, yet economists are often content to model the precommitment as the expression of a stable preference while treating the original purchases as the true revelation. I would value a careful examination of whether anything actually justifies the asymmetry, or whether it is a convenience that economics has adopted and then neglected to defend.
