I'm a student in a decision theory seminar this term, and we've been working through a family of one-shot, no-communication games for which the professor asked us to record our gut verdicts before doing any arithmetic. He calls this cluster Platonia-style games, and one variant he labels the Luring Lottery. The point of the exercise is that the interesting question is not always where the equilibrium is, but what a player should do when the other players are reasoning from the same public facts in the same way. He groups cases like the ones below under the label superrationality. I've collected four of my verdicts, and they don't obviously hang together, so I'd like a principle behind them, or an honest account of which verdict has to go.

Case 1, the copy prisoner's dilemma. I play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against another agent, and we both know we are running the very same decision procedure on the very same inputs and the same utility function. Payoffs: mutual cooperation gives 3 to each; mutual defection gives 1 to each; if one defects while the other cooperates, the defector gets 4 and the cooperator gets 0. My verdict: cooperate, because any consideration that would lead me to defect would lead the other to defect too, leaving us both at 1 instead of 3. Case 2, the stranger prisoner's dilemma. Same payoffs, but the other player is an ordinary rational stranger I will never meet or play again: no shared code, no communication, no reputation. Here my verdict is defect, since my act cannot reach the other's act and cooperating only exposes me. I can't articulate the feature that flips me between cases 1 and 2 as sharply as I'd like, and that bothers me. Case 3, the Luring Lottery. Twenty strangers, no communication, each writes down a positive integer; the prize goes to whoever writes the smallest number that exactly one person writes, and if no number is unique nobody wins. Stipulated: all of us reason from the same public facts in the same way. My verdict: I would not write 1, even though it is the smallest number, because I expect everyone to write 1 and void it; but I also don't believe any particular number is the rational one. I keep thinking the warranted move is to randomize from something like a wide, self-consistent distribution, and I can't pin the distribution down. Case 4, the day game. Same twenty people, but each writes an integer from 1 to 20, and the smallest uniquely written number wins; if no number is unique, nobody wins. My verdict here has a different shape: I would adopt a public, deterministic tie-breaker, such as writing the rank of my surname in the alphabetized list of all twenty names. That guarantees exactly one person writes each number and gives each player an equal chance before computing which rank is theirs; I would stick with it even after discovering I'm rank twenty, though this feels like exactly the kind of self-sacrificing move decision theory is supposed to forbid.

The nearest I can get to a single statement is this: in games where all players reason from the same public facts in the same way, a player should choose the rule she would want everyone in her epistemic position to converge on, rather than choosing a particular act while holding everyone else's acts fixed. But I can already see trouble: the day game's rule has to tell different people to write different numbers, the copy-versus-stranger contrast still needs an explanation, and I suspect the principle silently presumes common interest. Please state the principle precisely, or a small set of candidate principles, with the cases that motivate each clause; tell me which of my four verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should overturn that one; and test it on a further case the professor posed but I haven't settled: two copies of the same procedure face a one-shot, zero-sum guessing game in which one wins exactly when the two selections match and the other wins exactly when they do not. Finally, say what would change your mind: which argument would make you revise the principle, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
