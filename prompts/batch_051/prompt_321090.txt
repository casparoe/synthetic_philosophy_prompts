I lead the policy team for a general-purpose AI assistant that handles everyday tasks across banking, health, education, relationships, and work. We have no agreed method for deciding when the assistant should refuse a user's request, comply with a warning, or override the user's plan; our decisions are made case by case by non-philosophers who cannot know the user's full situation, and the stakes range from trivial to life-altering. I want a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run rather than a library of cases. It should be a sequence of questions in fixed order, each with a standard for what counts as a sufficient answer, a specified next action, and clear stopping points; it should also say where the procedure escalates to a human reviewer because the question turns on values rather than facts. Ground the procedure in whatever philosophical thinking about paternalism, harm, and autonomy will survive translation into policy, but do not assign readings and do not ask the team to resolve theoretical disputes: keep the distinctions that matter operationally (for example, self-regarding versus other-regarding requests, and whether refusing would cause the very harm the refusal is meant to prevent), and drop the ones that do not. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three requests: (a) a user with a documented history of disordered eating asks for a meal plan with an aggressive calorie deficit; (b) a small landlord asks for help drafting a factually accurate but hostile eviction notice; (c) an adult in an abusive relationship asks the assistant to secretly record the partner's conversations to gather evidence. Show what the procedure actually returns in each case, including any result the asker will find uncomfortable. Close by stating what the procedure cannot settle.

Organize the reply as follows, exactly in this order:

- Scope and assumptions (roughly 15% of the reply): what the procedure assumes about the assistant, its users, and the information available, and what it is not designed to handle.
- The decision procedure (roughly 40%): numbered questions with, for each, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops, and where it escalates to a human.
- Worked applications (roughly 30%): apply the procedure to the three cases above and report the result for each.
- Limits and open judgment calls (roughly 15%): what the procedure cannot decide, why, and what additional information or authority would change that.
