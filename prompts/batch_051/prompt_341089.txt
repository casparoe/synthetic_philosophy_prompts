I teach an upper-level seminar on the politics and law of the global internet, fifteen students, one weekly 150-minute session. After class recently I got into a long argument with a friend about a news story we had both read: a court in one country ordered a platform headquartered elsewhere to remove an article from the entire internet because it allegedly defamed a politician under that country's standards, even though the piece would be plainly lawful in the platform's home country. My friend's position was that there is no deep problem here. The platform is a private company; it chose to operate in that country and can choose to stop; beyond that, its terms of service and ordinary market incentives decide what it hosts, and any attempt to say that some country's law should govern speech globally is just overreach by whichever country happens to shout loudest. I pushed back for an hour without being able to state the thing that was bothering me, and I have realized since that the thing is structural rather than a dispute about values. Because there is one platform and many jurisdictions, whatever the platform does with the article — keep it up everywhere, remove it everywhere, geo-block it in one country, or defer to legal demands — decides which jurisdiction's speech rules reach which people, including people who consented to none of it. That is the point I want my students to feel rather than be told.

I would like you to design a runnable in-class activity for the next session: a structured role-play or simulation, not a discussion task, built around a small set of fictional jurisdictions with genuinely incompatible speech rules — defamation, privacy-based takedown, electoral-disinformation rules, or similar — plus a platform team and at least one affected-user or appeals role. It needs explicit rules and materials, a timeline for the 150 minutes, and a consequence scheme that uses no real money but makes some jurisdictions costlier to defy than others, so that my friend's market-choice reply gets tested from the inside rather than rehearsed as a slogan. The debrief should map specific moments in the simulation onto the conceptual distinctions I care about — a state's territorial jurisdiction, the platform's de facto rule-setting, and the user's consent — and it should do so after the pressure has accumulated, not by announcing the lesson in advance. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be frank about which features of the real internet the simulation cannot reproduce and which ones I will have to supply myself in the debrief.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the proposed mechanics — in particular, whether the incentives will reliably produce the structural insight rather than a diffuse argument over which value system is best — and single out the one point of the design you are least sure will work as intended.
