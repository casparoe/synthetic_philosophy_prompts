I chair the three-person awards committee of a small institute that gives one annual grant. The grant's terms require the winning work to satisfy two conditions: it must make a significant original contribution, and it must be accessible to a reader outside the author's specialty. The committee takes three votes: yes or no on the contribution condition, yes or no on the accessibility condition, and yes or no on the overall question of whether to make the award. Our bylaws state that the grant is to be given only if both conditions are met and that decisions are made by majority vote. They are silent on how the three votes are combined.

This year's votes were:

- Delgado: contribution yes, accessibility yes, award yes.
- Bianchi: contribution yes, accessibility no, award no. Her stated reason: the work is important, but it addresses a narrow technical audience, and the accessibility condition exists precisely to rule out such work.
- Okonkwo: contribution no, accessibility yes, award no. His stated reason: the work is written clearly, but it mostly reorganizes other people's findings rather than making an original contribution.

Read condition by condition, a majority finds the contribution condition satisfied (Delgado and Bianchi), and a majority finds the accessibility condition satisfied (Delgado and Okonkwo); so a condition-by-condition reading of the votes yields award. Read on the bottom line, a majority votes no award (Bianchi and Okonkwo). The two members who vote no do so for opposite reasons, so there is no stable majority view of what, if anything, is wrong with the candidate — only two different two-person majorities on two different premises.

This has the shape of the discursive dilemma studied in social choice theory, but classifying it will not settle what we do at Thursday's meeting. Please write the short memo I will circulate beforehand. The memo should recommend one concrete procedure for combining our three votes in cases where the conditions and the bottom-line vote diverge, apply that procedure to this year's vote with an explicit bottom line I can announce to the applicant, and then present the strongest case for the procedure being rejected and explain why it should lose. I also want the memo to flag what a purely procedural fix cannot settle here, so the committee does not pretend the choice of rule is normatively neutral.

Act as an expert in judgment aggregation and the theory of group decision-making. Before committing to a recommendation, begin the memo by explicitly working through the candidate procedures and what each would have us do in this particular vote matrix; consider whether there are defensible alternatives beyond the two obvious ones, and show your reasoning as you rule them in or out.
