I am a hearing officer on a state professional-licensing board. Roughly once a month I hear a discipline case in which the evidence against the licensee is, at bottom, a statistic: an unusually high share of a clinic's claims were improper, or a physician's prescribing pattern deviates sharply from their peers, or a contractor's error rate is far above comparable firms. I have no formal training in probability, my decisions can be reviewed by a court, and I have about half a day per case including writing the order. I need a reusable procedure for deciding when naked statistical evidence — evidence that places a party in a class, without anything that ties the statistic to that party's particular conduct — is enough to meet the preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, and when it is not.

Write the procedure as a checklist of questions I should ask in order. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to me because the question is one of policy or values rather than fact, and what to write in my order so a reviewing court can see which step failed. Say how each step changes if the applicable standard is clear and convincing evidence or proof beyond a reasonable doubt, since some of our revocation cases are treated as quasi-criminal. Do not let the procedure collapse into the rule that statistical evidence is never enough, or the rule that it is enough whenever the probability exceeds 50 percent; it has to handle cases in between, and it should say what to do when I cannot get a precise number.

The procedure should take seriously the two standard cases. In the blue bus case, a pedestrian is injured by a hit-and-run bus and the only evidence against the Blue Bus Company is that it operates 80 percent of the buses on that route. In the gatecrasher case, 1,000 people attended an event, 499 paid, and a randomly selected attendee is sued for the unpaid admission. A careful procedure will explain why many people resist liability in both cases even though the probability appears to exceed 50 percent, and will say whether that resistance is justified rather than a mistake.

Then run the completed procedure on these three cases:

1. The blue bus case.
2. The gatecrasher case.
3. A case designed to turn that instinct on its head. A health plan's auditor took a random sample of 100 claims from a single clinic and found 71 were not payable under the contract. The plan now asks the board to order the clinic to repay 71 percent of the value of a further 1,000 randomly selected claims from the same clinic and billing period, none of which was individually audited.

At least one of these three should end somewhere I will not like, given my background instinct that only individualized proof is real proof; mark that step explicitly. Also flag every step where choosing or challenging a reference class is doing the real work, and say what the procedure cannot settle — such as why a standard of proof is set where it is rather than somewhere else. Keep it usable by a lay hearing officer, not a probability textbook. Begin with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the checklist and your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the prompt.
