Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life" and its film adaptation Arrival center on the heptapods, whose writing system is depicted as encoding whole meanings directly rather than the sounds of their spoken language: a single circular mark corresponds to an entire utterance, the script is produced all at once rather than in a linear sequence, and it is said to have no correspondence to heptapod speech. Write a philosophical reading of this fiction through the lens of the study of writing systems, in particular the question of what a script must encode and how phonemic an alphabet really is. Linguists such as John DeFrancis and Richard Sproat have argued that every full writing system in human history encodes a spoken language, and that the idea of a script that represents thoughts without representing sounds is an ideographic myth; on that view Chinese characters are not ideograms but morpheme-syllabic signs, and even an alphabet is rarely a faithful phonemic record, since systems like English orthography encode morphology and etymology as much as sound. Does the heptapod script constitute a genuine counterexample to that thesis, or does it work as fiction only because its inner workings are left unspecified? Where does the story get the relationship between a script and a language right, and where does it slide into metaphor, for example by treating a non-linear writing system as if it were a non-linear form of cognition, or by suggesting that one could learn a script without learning the language it belongs to? I want a reading that takes both the fiction and the linguistics seriously: say what the story shows that the academic debate misses, and what the academic debate shows that the story obscures.
