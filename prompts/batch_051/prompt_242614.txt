I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy, and our next session is on Bernard Williams's integrity objection — specifically the thought experiment in which a man must choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger, saves his wife, and then, pressed for his reasons, reaches for an impartial justification of the kind a consequentialist or Kantian would demand. Williams's point is that the justification itself is a failure: not that the man acted wrongly, but that reaching for an impartial reason here — one available from outside the relationship, of the form, in such cases one may prefer one's wife — is one thought too many, and the wife would rightly be wounded by it.

I want to open the session with a poem rather than an assigned reading, something I can put up on the screen before anyone has spoken. Please write a Petrarchan sonnet in the husband's voice. The first eight lines (abbaabba) should carry the incident itself — the immediate danger, the body's knowledge of which person to reach for, the stranger still there — with no moral vocabulary. The volta at line 9 should turn on the moment the justification arrives: after the rescue, when the husband catches himself explaining why he saved her, and feels the form of words undo the act. The sestet should make the poem's structure do the philosophical work: let the octave's closed, orderly rhymes suggest the impartial system's demand that the act be underwritten by a general and statable reason, and let the sestet's rhyme pattern or syntax break that order as the particular relationship refuses to be cashed out in those terms. Keep it to fourteen lines. Do not use the words utilitarianism, consequentialism, impartiality, or integrity in the poem, and avoid the phrase one thought too many as well. Leave the wound open rather than resolving it, and do not end with a moral.
