My supervisor in a neuropsychiatry unit has been recording, for each delusional patient we see, whether she would count the patient's delusion as a genuine belief. She works within a two-factor framework — an anomalous experience supplies the content, and some failure in evaluating that content lets it persist — but she says that framework does not by itself settle whether the result is a belief, and she insists that her rulings apply a single rule for that. The trouble is that her rulings so far are consistent with more than one rule, and I want to know which she is actually running.

Her rulings so far:

1. A patient with a persecutory delusion that coworkers were poisoning his lunch. He avoided shared meals for two years, brought his own food, confronted a coworker, and cited smells and stomach complaints as evidence. She ruled it a belief.

2. A patient who said her husband had been replaced by an identical impostor, but when pressed said she knew he must be her husband and that it only felt as if he were not, and who never acted on the idea. She ruled it not a belief.

3. A patient with a grandiose delusion that he owned the hospital. He gave staff instructions, tried to reassign beds, and argued from the alleged ownership when overruled. She ruled it a belief.

4. A stroke patient who, when asked about his left arm, said it belonged to someone else; he never mentioned it spontaneously, showed no distress, and when asked whether he really believed it, just shrugged. She ruled it not a belief.

5. A patient with a somatic delusion that insects were burrowing under her skin. She kept written records of the insects' movements, repeatedly sought surgery, and when nothing was found argued that the insects must be too deep to detect. She ruled it a belief.

Next week we will see a patient who maintains, with full conviction and in every context, that his internal organs were surgically replaced with metal replicas. He says he has believed this for months, repeats it to anyone who asks, and rejects counterevidence. But he takes no action: he does not seek removal of the implants, eats normally, expresses no fear, and the idea never enters his planning, his explanations of other symptoms, or his reasoning about anything else.

Predict how she will rule on this new case. Say which theory of what it is to believe she is implicitly using — a functional-role view, on which a belief must be available for inference and action; a conviction or endorsement view, on which sincere and stable avowal is enough; or something else — and explain why the track record fits your attribution better than the alternatives. Then tell me which case settles the matter, and whether her ruling on the new case is decisive between the leading rules or whether a further case would be needed. Please commit to a definite prediction and a single best account of her rule, and defend it rather than laying out the options.
