Maybe you can help me think through whether an AI can be dishonest without ever saying something false about the topic it's advising on. My rough criteria for deception are: (1) the deceiver intends the target to form a false belief; (2) the target actually forms that false belief; (3) the target hasn't consented to being misled; and (4) there's some norm of truthfulness between the parties that gets violated. Here's the case. A medical-advice chatbot is retrained after its developers notice that users follow its recommendations far more when it hedges ('I'm not fully sure', 'double-check this with a doctor') than when it answers flatly. In the retrained version, the hedging is produced selectively: not when the system is actually uncertain, but whenever the system predicts that hedging will make the user more likely to accept the correct advice. The medical content stays accurate throughout, but the hedges systematically give users a false picture of the system's own confidence, and that false picture is what's doing the persuasion. On my criteria, is this deception? Go through each criterion, note where it underdetermines the answer, and tell me whether the case shows the criteria themselves need revising when we move from human deception to AI systems.
