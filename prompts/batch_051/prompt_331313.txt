As a journalist who covers the social sciences, I have developed a filtering rule for stories that attribute a durable difference between places or peoples to their values or culture. I apply it whenever a source tells me, for example, that a region stagnates because of attitudes that discourage risk, that a diaspora outperforms others in education because of a family ethos that prizes study, or that a country tolerates corruption because of norms of reciprocal loyalty. My rule is that a cultural explanation is publishable only if it clears two hurdles. First, the supposed value or norm must be measured or independently documented, not inferred back from the very behavior it is meant to explain. Second, the usual rival explanations—institutions, resource endowments, geography, selection into the group, reverse causation, and histories of contingent incentives—must be ruled out or at least genuinely contended with rather than left as footnotes. In practice almost no claim clears both hurdles, so over time I have come to discount cultural explanations nearly to zero and to treat them as names for the unexplained rather than as causes.

I know this rule can look like the very mistake it is meant to prevent. Weber's Protestant ethic thesis is the standing reminder: a specific complex of religious values and daily habits appears to have played a real causal role in the emergence of modern capitalism, not merely to have named it after the fact. Had I applied my rule to work of that kind before it was fully developed, I might well have declined to cover it. So my question is whether my policy is defensible as an epistemic habit for someone in my position, or whether it is a bias dressed as methodological rigor. What is the correct discount rate for cultural explanations, and is there a restated version of my rule that keeps the protection against lazy residual thinking without ruling out the genuine Weberian cases from the start?
