Honestly the more I think about theory change the more Lakatosian I get. Not single-shot falsification, not Kuhn-style gestalt switches: science just looks like a bunch of research programmes, each with a hard core you refuse to test directly, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that absorbs the damage, and a rational rule of sticking with the programme while its problemshifts are progressive (novel predictions, confirmed excess content) and dropping it once they degenerate.

But I've got a nagging worry that I'm accepting this in exactly the unfalsifiable spirit Lakatos himself made fun of. So play it straight: what would count against the Lakatosian picture? Treat it both as a claim about how science actually works and as a prescription for how scientists ought to behave. I want a map of its exposure, not a hit job and not a defense.

Push into the weeds. Which historical cases genuinely embarrass it, and which ones does it handle by re-describing? Does the protective belt give a programme unlimited license to keep adding epicycles and calling the result progressive? Since you can only tell retrospectively wether a problemshift was progressive or degenerating, does that make the view useless in real time, say for appraising something like the string theory programme? Is the notion of novel prediction doing all the real work, and is that notion itself shifty? And take the self-referential question seriously: is Lakatos's own methodology a progressive research programme by its lights, and if not, is that fatal or just a cute footnote?

One more thing: don't let the answer hide behind the line that programmes can only be appraised retrospectively. I know that's part of the view; I want to know when that move is legitimate and when it becomes insulation.
