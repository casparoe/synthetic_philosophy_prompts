I have spent the past several months arguing about strong longtermism and have ended up substantially convinced by it. As I understand the view, it holds that if we can make the long-run future go better — by slightly lowering the probability of a civilization-ending catastrophe, or by making it more likely that a good trajectory rather than a bad one locks in — then these effects, spread over an astronomically large number of future lives, will tend to dominate the more tangible near-term benefits we can produce. On this picture, reducing existential risk and shaping which long-run trajectory humanity takes are the central moral priorities of our time, ahead of present poverty, present disease, and other pressing but comparatively contained harms.

What I want is a serious map of what would count against this position, because I do not trust my ability to find its weak points from the inside. I am not interested in the familiar complaint that it neglects suffering today. I want to know what would actually have to be true for strong longtermism to lose its claim on action: for instance, that our long-run effects are so clueless, or so symmetrically distributed between good and bad, that the expected-value case collapses; that the future is much smaller, or much more heavily discounted, than the view requires; that extinction risk settles into a low constant background rate so that no particular intervention has durable leverage; that person-affecting considerations exclude merely possible future people from the calculus; or that value is not additive across such numbers, making the whole exercise a form of fanaticism. For each candidate defeater, I would like to know whether it would be fatal or merely awkward, whether a sophisticated defender would concede it as a live empirical bet or dismiss it as an artefact of scope insensitivity, and what the view is committed to predicting that its rivals are not. Finally, I want a judgment on whether strong longtermism is a position that genuinely risks something, or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that insulation is itself a reason to abandon it. The defeaters I would find most persuasive are those that do not merely show the view is difficult to act on, but show that its central inference from astronomical stakes to present priority does not go through.
