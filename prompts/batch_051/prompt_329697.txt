Since I retired from the lab I've ended up on the grants committee of a small arts foundation, which is a strange place for someone who spent forty years studying sediment transport. I've started reading aesthetics in the evenings, but mostly I just write down my own votes and try to see if there's a pattern. I'd defend the following. I'd fund a translation of a difficult contemporary Slovenian novel that maybe two hundred people will read, and I'd fund restoring the backlist of a mid-century novelist who's books have gone out of print. I'd vote against subsidizing ebook discounts for current bestselling literary fiction, even though that would reach vastly more readers. I'd give a year's rent to a poet with a slow, strange project, but I'd refuse a grant to a fine historical novelist who admitted she'd finish the book without us. I'd fund a public library's story-time program, not because I think it gets kids reading (probably it does) but because it makes a room in which an adult speaks and children listen. I'd fund digitizing a collection of early queer zines, but I'd be uneasy about a big exhibition of a famous living novelist's drafts. I'd fund a text-heavy, branching video game, but I balked at a proposal to put short poems on buses.

I keep reaching for a rough slogan — fund the making and the keeping of difficult things, not the selling of them — but I can already see it won't cover the story-time case or the video game. Is there a single principle of public value behind these votes, or do some of them have to go? If you had to state the principle precisely, where would it draw the line, what would it say about a case I haven't listed, say a tax break for independent bookstores or a grant to a writer with a large following to take time off and experiment, and which of my votes would it force me to overturn?
