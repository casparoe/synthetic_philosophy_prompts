Question: Compare the main accounts of what makes a statement racist. Explain the difference between intent-based and effect-based accounts, say how a content-based account treats slurs, and then discuss whether the phenomenon of dog whistles favors one account over another.

Student's answer: The main divide is between accounts that locate racism in the speaker's mental state and accounts that locate it in what the utterance does. On an intent-based account, a statement is racist only if the speaker intended it to express or stir up racial hostility or contempt. So if someone says something that a listener finds offensive but there was no such intent, the statement itself is not racist, even if the speaker was clueless or insensitive. On an effect-based account, the speaker's state of mind is irrelevant: what matters is whether the utterance demeans, excludes, or reinforces racial hierarchy. That is the slogan impact over intent. A content-based account is a third option. It says some expressions carry racialized content in their literal meaning. A slur is the obvious case: it is racist in virtue of what it means, independently of who says it and what effect it has on any particular occasion.

Dog whistles look like a problem for intent-based accounts. A phrase like law and order can, in the right context, convey a racially loaded message to one audience while remaining deniable to everyone else. If the speaker can plausibly deny the racialized intention, the intent account seems stuck saying the statement is not racist, even though the coded message does exactly the kind of damage effect accounts are built to capture. So far, dog whistles favor the effect account.

But I do not think they do, on reflection. A dog whistle is only a dog whistle if the speaker deliberately uses the code to get the in-group to hear the racial message. If the speaker genuinely did not intend the hidden reading, then it is not a dog whistle at all; it is just ambiguous speech that some people happen to interpret one way. So dog whistles are actually intent-dependent phenomena, and they end up supporting the intent account rather than embarrassing it.

The serious remaining problem for the intent account is epistemic: we rarely know what a speaker intended, and with coded language the speaker has every incentive to lie or self-deceive. The effect account avoids this because we do not need access to anyone's mental states, only to the harm the speech causes. The trouble is that effect accounts overcorrect: they would classify as racist any statement that offends a member of a racialized group, even when the offense is idiosyncratic and the speaker could not have predicted it. So the best view is a hybrid: a statement is racist if and only if it is either intended as a dog whistle, or has the effect of reinforcing racial hierarchy.
