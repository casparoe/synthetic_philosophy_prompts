I am taking a seminar on the history of moral philosophy, and after our session on The Methods of Ethics a classmate gave me roughly the following account of Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason. On his telling, Sidgwick recognized two rational methods for deciding what to do. Rational egoism directs each person to do whatever maximizes her own happiness over her whole life; utilitarianism—the form of morality Sidgwick thinks survives rational scrutiny—directs her to do whatever maximizes everyone's happiness. Each of these principles, Sidgwick held, presents itself as self-evident on its own. Yet they can issue incompatible instructions, and, on the classmate's account, Sidgwick proved that neither can be rationally subordinated to the other, leaving practical reason with a contradiction at its core. Because reason cannot resolve that contradiction, the account concludes, Sidgwick fell back on something non-rational—sympathy, custom, or religious hope—to move from the standpoint of self-interest to the standpoint of the general good. I am inclined to trust most of this, but the final move worries me. Could you give me an accuracy audit of it: going claim by claim, which parts would a Sidgwick scholar accept, which would they qualify or reject, and does the concluding claim survive the corrections? If the summary, or my framing of the question, rests on a confusion, please say so plainly rather than working within it.
