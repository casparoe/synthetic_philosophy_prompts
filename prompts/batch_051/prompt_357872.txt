I am a PhD student working on interpretability and the governance of deployed machine-learning systems, and I am writing a short paper about a tension between explainability rules and models whose decision-relevant knowledge is not expressible in natural language. I want to think through one concrete case.

Our lab's model-deployment policy contains Clause 7.4, drafted before the lab seriously confronted deep latent features:

"For every consequential automated decision, the system shall provide a plain-language explanation stating the principal factors it relied on and the weight each factor received."

Now consider a neural classifier that screens small-business loan applications. Ablations, probes, and counterfactual interventions all indicate that its decisions are driven above all by one learned direction in an early embedding layer: a continuous pattern in how applicants narrate past financial difficulty, which predicts early repayment better than any documented financial variable does. The developers never designed this feature, it has no name, and when the system is asked to explain an individual decision it generates a fluent explanation citing the applicant's stated income, debt ratio, and repayment history. Those cited factors are genuinely correlated with the outcome but they are neither necessary nor sufficient: holding them constant while suppressing the latent direction flips around three-quarters of the applications that sit near the approval threshold. The direction itself cannot be rendered in plain language, and the team's best efforts describe it only by loose analogy to narrative posture.

The authors of Clause 7.4 plainly imagined factors like credit score or annual revenue, variables a compliance officer could read from a file. They did not anticipate that the principal factor might be a distributed pattern a model can use but cannot articulate.

My question is one of interpretation rather than engineering. Does Clause 7.4 require the system to state this latent direction, even though no plain-language statement of it is possible? Is a fluent but incomplete explanation that names real factors which do not actually drive the decision in compliance with the clause, a violation of it, or something the clause simply does not cover? Work through the text, the clause's purpose, what its drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader of the policy would take it to require; then say which approach should control here and whether the case shows that Clause 7.4 itself needs amending.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. In the fifth item, after giving your verdict, state how confident you are in the overall reading and which single point you are least sure of.
