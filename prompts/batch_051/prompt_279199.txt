In the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov, Dostoevsky's Inquisitor argues that the freedom Christ offered is an unbearable burden for ordinary people. Human beings, on his account, need assured bread, clear authority, and relief from the terror of genuine choice, and they would therefore trade their autonomy for managed contentment under the Church's care. The force of the argument rests on a psychological claim about what ordinary people actually prefer and a normative claim that those few who can bear freedom are right to govern, for their own good, the many who cannot.

Now consider how digital platforms govern attention today. Recommender systems, infinite feeds, and A/B-tested defaults do not announce themselves as taking away freedom; they continuously shape the choice environment so that users mostly encounter options predicted to keep them on the platform, while the experience still feels like free browsing. The manager is no longer a visible authority demanding submission but an invisible curator of the option set itself, and what it optimizes for is retention or engagement rather than the managed contentment the Inquisitor promised.

Does the Inquisitor's argument survive this development? Lay out the argument's central premises, identify which premise the development stresses most directly, and then give a verdict: strengthened, in need of repair, or no longer viable. Explain what in the mechanism of algorithmic curation forces that verdict, and, if you take the argument to fail, describe what the underlying problem should now be called.
