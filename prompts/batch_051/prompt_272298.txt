I run a small reading group on medieval Islamic theology, and we've spent the last two meetings banging our heads against the old Mu'tazila–Ash'arite dispute over whether acts are good or bad in themselves or only because God commands and forbids them. The textbook version is easy: for the Mu'tazila, human reason can see that some things, like torturing an innocent person or lying purely to harm someone, are ugly before any revelation arrives, and God, being just, would never command them; for the Ash'arites, God's command is exactly what constitutes good and evil, and the suggestion that God is answerable to some independent moral standard looks like a threat to His lordship. But the caricatures paper over the real moves each side makes, and I want something I can hand to the group to show the argument actually tightening.

Write a dialogue between a Mu'tazili and an Ash'ari theologian, set in Baghdad in the tenth century, in which they try to locate their genuine disagreement rather than just trading slogans. Make them press each other on at least three pressure points. First, is the statement "God cannot lie" a limitation on God's power or a perfection of God, and what follows from each answer? Second, does the Ash'ari position make God's goodness and justice arbitrary in a way that would make revelation itself untrustworthy, since on that view saying "God is good" collapses into "God does what God does"? Third, does the Mu'tazili position secretly set up human reason as a second, rival authority that God must obey, and if not, how is it supposed to avoid that? Both characters should be competent, and each time one reaches for his school's standard reply, the other should push on the weakest point in that reply. Try not to end with a clean winner, but leave the reader able to see what each side would have to grant for the argument to move forward.

For the format, write this as one continuous piece of prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Don't lay it out as a script with names and colons; carry the dialogue inside ordinary paragraphs with quotation marks and a little narration, the way it might appear in an essay or a letter.
