I propose four criteria for something's being a genuine reason in the normative sense — a consideration that can genuinely support a belief or an action — as opposed to a mere cause or after-the-fact explanation:

(i) It can stand in a justificatory relation to at least one agent's belief or action.
(ii) It can be assessed as better or worse: it weighs more or less heavily against competing considerations.
(iii) It is, at least in principle, the kind of thing an agent could hold before the mind in deliberation.
(iv) It supports what it supports through a rational transition — evidential support, means–end relevance, or the like — rather than through bare causation.

Now consider a case. In a deep and liquid market, thousands of traders act independently on different, privately held bits of information about a proposed merger. No individual trader possesses all of the information, and each performs only a small step of the total inference — for instance, judging how seriously to take one rumor about a regulator. The equilibrium price that emerges is different from the price any individual trader thought fair, and it reliably tracks facts that no single trader knew. An investor who holds none of the underlying information then treats the price movement as a reason to sell, saying that the market has concluded the merger will fail.

Does this market verdict, a rationale that floats free of any single reasoner, qualify as a genuine reason under my four criteria? Go through the criteria one by one and say honestly where they underdetermine the answer. Then take a position on what the case shows: does it show that one of the criteria should be relaxed — for example, that a genuine reason need not be held by a single reasoner but can be distributed across many — or does it show that calling the price a reason is only a loose personification of the market?
