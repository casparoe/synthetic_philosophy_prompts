I'm giving a short presentation at my company's ethics-and-policy brown bag defending the view that chronic small promise-breaking (being 15 minutes late to meetings you've agreed to, saying 'I'll send it by Friday' and then forgetting, agreeing to review a draft and never getting back to the person) is a serious moral failing rather then a matter of mere etiquette or personal style, and that organizations ought to track and sanction it much more than they now do. My line is roughly this: each individual breach looks low-cost only if you value the other person's time and agency at zero; the real unit of assessment is the pattern and what it signals about a standing disposition to treat other people's planning as a resource to be consumed. I also want to claim the wrongness isn't defeated by the fact that no single instance causes a harm you can point to, because the harm is mostly to people's capacity to rely on one another at all.

The audience will be a mix of sharp people: two act-utilitarians with an effective-altruist bent who think that if the consequences of an individual broken promise are small, the wrong is small, and that our scarce attention should go to large-scale suffering rather than lateness; one Kantian who keeps saying the real issue is about universalizable maxims and treating people as ends rather than means; and one philosopher of action who treats promising as a convention, so the wrongness of breaking a small promise is mostly a matter of whether the convention's rules were internalized, and he doesn't see why a company should be in the business of moral character. There will also be a few agnostic engineers.

Write me a prep sheet for the Q&A: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased the way these people would phrase them, with a two-minute reply to each, a note on where my replies are soft, and the one question I should be praying nobody asks. Please also flag which of my own claims I should retreat from up front rather than be forced to defend.
