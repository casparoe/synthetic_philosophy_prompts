I recently joined the volunteer board that sets rules for our town's parks, trails, and water use, and the same question keeps coming up in every meeting: when is it acceptable to do something small that causes no noticeable harm on its own but would be damaging if lots of people did it? I have been writing down my own snap verdicts on concrete cases, and to my discomfort they do not line up with any principle I can state. I would like your help finding the principle behind them, or telling me which of my verdicts I should revise.

Please work as a professional philosopher with expertise in moral theory and in reflective equilibrium. Start by brainstorming several candidate principles and explicitly lay them out before testing them against the cases. Work slowly through the cases, and do not try to make every one of my verdicts come out right if the price is an ad hoc patchwork.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. Cutting across the lawn in the town park to shave ninety seconds off my walk to the bus. Verdict: wrong.
2. Crossing against the light on the empty main road at 3 a.m., after checking carefully both ways. Verdict: fine.
3. Skipping a local election because my vote has essentially no chance of deciding it and the weather is miserable. Verdict: fine.
4. Running a sprinkler for fifteen minutes during a declared drought, in defiance of the temporary ban, to keep my tomato seedlings from wilting. Verdict: wrong.
5. Not leaving a tip at a restaurant in a nearby town where tipping is customary but not legally required, because I know I will not be back. Verdict: wrong, though I cannot say exactly why it differs from case 3.
6. Downloading a single paywalled academic paper from a pirate site for my own reading because my library has no subscription to the journal. Verdict: fine, or at most a very small wrong.
7. Deciding not to have children because I do not want the cost and the loss of independence. Verdict: fine.
8. Leaving my empty coffee cup on the train seat instead of carrying it to the bin at my stop. Verdict: wrong.
9. Letting my dog off leash in the bird-nesting meadow before sunrise, when I can see no one else around. Verdict: wrong.

Some of these sit in obvious pairs for me. Cases 3 and 4 look alike: each is a tiny personal contribution to a collective institution, each done for a fairly small personal reason, yet I condemn one and not the other. Cases 1 and 2 both involve breaking a rule about public space, and I distinguish them, which makes me suspect it matters exactly what the maxim is that I would be universalizing. Cases 6 and 8 both involve taking something from a shared system, and I distinguish those too, but I am not sure my distinction survives scrutiny. And case 7 sits uneasily with any simple "what if everybody did that?" test, since everybody doing that would be the end of everybody.

My rough first attempt at a principle is something like this: an act is wrong when the generalized practice, understood as everyone doing the same kind of thing in relevantly similar circumstances, would undermine the very thing the act relies on for its value, unless the act is so personal that generalizing it is illegitimate. But I do not know how to make "relevantly similar" and "so personal" precise, and the sprinkler case makes me suspect I am merely rationalizing a distinction between rules I resent and rules I accept.

Please state the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each candidate, work through what it says about my cases, especially the pairs I identified. Then apply it to cases I have not listed, for example: taking one undersized fish from a lake that is closed for the season to let the population recover, when I am confident no one would see; pocketing a $20 bill I see someone drop when I could return it; and breaking a promise to visit a friend when breaking it would catch no one's notice. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the sensible move is to revise the verdict or to add a clause to the principle. You may draw on Kant's universal-law formulation, Hare's prescriptivism, Singer's generalization argument, or similar frameworks wherever that helps, but what I want is the extraction and testing of principles, not a survey of philosophers.
