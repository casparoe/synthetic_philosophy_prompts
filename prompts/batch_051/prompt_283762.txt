You are an expert in moral philosophy, especially the ethics of ritual, punishment, and responsibility. Please work through this carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before committing to the one that fits best.

I have been teaching a seminar on ritual and moral responsibility, and I keep returning to a set of my own judgments about the transfer of guilt, blame, and moral burden to a substitute. Taken one by one they feel obvious; taken together they seem to pull against one another, and I cannot find the principle behind them. (1) In the Levitical Day of Atonement rite, the high priest lays his hands on a goat’s head, confesses the community’s iniquities over it, and sends the goat into the wilderness. I want to say this is not merely decorative symbolism: something is genuinely handed over to the animal and removed from the community, although the goat does not become guilty and the people do not cease to remember or own their wrongdoing. (2) If a court were to execute an innocent person as a substitute for a real murderer, I find the act both unjust and metaphysically empty: the murderer’s guilt is not transferred, the substitute is not guilty, and the court has simply added a second wrong. (3) If a friend voluntarily takes over someone’s monetary debt to a creditor, I have no hesitation saying that the obligation has genuinely moved, and the original debtor is released. That looks like the transfer of a burden rather than of guilt, but I cannot state exactly what separates the two. (4) When a parent whose child has vandalized a neighbor’s house says, “I take responsibility for this,” makes restitution, and the neighbor regards the matter as settled with the parent, I think the parent acquires answerability without acquiring guilt for the child’s act. Again a burden has moved while culpability has stayed put. (5) By contrast, when a frightened community blames a convenient outsider for a crop failure and drives him out, I think nothing at all has been transferred to him; the community’s feeling of being cleansed is just a feeling, and the expulsion has made the community worse rather than better. (6) The case that most unsettles me is a request made years later by a remorseful wrongdoer to the person he wronged: “Would you carry this guilt with me?” The victim understands the request and answers that she will. I do not want to say she becomes guilty, but I also do not want to say that nothing happened.

What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these six judgments as possible. State it precisely, show how it handles each case, and be honest about which of my judgments it forces me to revise or abandon. Then apply your principle to a seventh case I have not listed: a penal system that permits a convicted offender to pay a willing stranger to serve his prison sentence in his place, so that the offender remains legally convicted but the stranger is the one punished. If your principle cannot deliver a single verdict there, say what that indicates about the two or three things I may be running together under the word “transfer.”
