I've been arguing with a friend about whether the silence up there should actually make me update against advanced life being common, and I realize my gut reactions to specific searches are all over the place. I want to know if there's a single principle behind them, or which of these verdicts has to go. Here are the verdicts I keep wanting to defend.

If a complete all-sky survey examined a hundred thousand nearby galaxies for the waste heat of Dyson spheres and found nothing, I feel that strongly rules out galaxy-spanning civilizations being common. But if a survey of the same galaxies for narrowband radio beacons found nothing, I barely move. That feels inconsistent, because I can't honestly say radio beacons are less plausible than star-enclosing infrastructure. On the other hand, when someone says "SETI has been listening for sixty years and heard nothing," I don't treat the duration as strong evidence at all; only the completeness of the search seems to matter to me, but I can't articulate why alot of years shouldn't count. If a thorough search of our solar system for dormant self-replicating probes or discarded alien machinery found nothing, I'd update hard against the idea that colonization fills the galaxy. But if a survey could only detect complete Dyson spheres and not partial ones, a null result would update me much less than a survey that could also see partial swarms. A detected oxygen-methane mix in an exoplanet atmosphere would feel to me like almost no evidence of life, given all the abiotic routes to those gases, whereas a single narrowband radio signal that repeats would basically settle the "are we alone" question for me even though everyone says a signal could be a fluke. Finally, if we found microbial fossils on Mars, I feel like that would make the Fermi paradox worse rather than better, but I'm not sure how to state the principle that yields that, or whether the same principle predicts how I should react to a survey that found exactly one candidate Dyson sphere but couldn't confirm it. What I'm looking for is this: state, as precisely as you can, the principle that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which clause of the principle handles which case, and then apply it to the Mars-fossil case and the one-candidate-Dyson-sphere case. Don't just tell me I'm being Bayesian; show me the working assumptions about priors and detection that each verdict commits me to. End with a line beginning "Summary:" that states your final principle in one sentence.
