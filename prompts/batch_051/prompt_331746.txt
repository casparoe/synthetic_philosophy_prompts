I sit on the grant-review board of a small foundation that funds early-stage work on reducing suffering: better pain drugs, animal welfare tech, apps for chronic pain, that sort of thing. Over the past year we've gotten a growing pile of proposals that take a much harder line. The people writing them keep saying that treating suffering is a half-measure and that the real goal should be to get rid of the capacity to suffer at all — in humans first, and eventually in all animals — using gene editing, pharmacology, neural implants, whatever does the job. A lot of them are clearly riffing on the old transhumanist idea that a future civilization should engineer things so that pain, fear, grief and boredom simply cant occur, even if useful signals and reflexes stay in place. The board is split down the middle, nobody here has any real philosophy background, and we can't keep voting from the gut.

I want a reusable screening procedure we can actually run in a twenty-minute discussion before a vote, for any proposal that claims to move toward this goal. It should be a checklist or an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure says no and stops, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is a values call rather than a factual or technical one. It has to be usable by board members who aren't going to learn the theory, and it cant pretend our disagreements are empirical when they aren't; I want it to stay true to what abolitionism is actually after, and to flag the places where ordinary objections about what's "natural" or "dignified" are quietly doing the work instead of being examined. We also have to justify every rejection in writing to the trustees, so a no should always point to a specific step in the procedure.

Then run the procedure on three proposals currently in our pile: (1) a gene-editing project to produce a line of chickens that show no pain behavior and no pain-related brain activity when injured but otherwise act normal in a barn setting; (2) a human trial of a drug that shuts off the felt awfulness of chronic pain while leaving the ability to notice tissue damage intact; (3) a plan to develop and release a self-spreading gene drive that suppresses the pain system in wild rodents, on the grounds that wild animals suffer constantly and nobody is treating them. At least one of these should come out with an answer the abolitionist-leaning members of the board won't like, and I want the procedure to spell out where our job as evaluators ends and a genuinely moral decision begins.
