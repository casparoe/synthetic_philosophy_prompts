I have, over several years of arguing about fakes and then changing my mind, accumulated a list of theses about originals, copies, and forgeries that I apparently accept, and I want to know whether they amount to a stable position or just a pile of opposing intuitions. I am turning the list into a short public lecture for a museum audience, so I need to know what I can defend before I stand behind it.

The theses are as follows. (a) The value of a work of art as art is fixed by the experience it can provide to a suitably attentive viewer, listener, or reader; two perceptually indistinguishable objects must therefore be equal in artistic value, and no fact about provenance can by itself make one better than the other. (b) Nevertheless, when I learn that a painting is a forgery — same canvas, same pigments, same visible surface, but made later than claimed and passed off as earlier — my estimate of its quality as art shifts downward, and I think that shift is the correct one rather than mere snobbery. (c) For repeatable works such as a sonata, a novel, or a poem, I have the opposite reaction: a perfect copy, or even a meticulous completion of a lost fragment, seems to carry all the artistic value of the work, while the original manuscript has only archival or sentimental value. (d) I explain this contrast by the identity conditions of the works: a painting is a singular physical object whose history of making enters into what it is, whereas a musical or literary work is an abstract structure that any competent token instantiates; caring about the right token is therefore rational in the first case and fetishistic in the second. (e) At the same time I reject the idea that biography or provenance matters for its own sake; facts about an artist's life, intentions, or circumstances seem to matter artistically only when they change the meaning of the work or the way an informed audience should engage with it.

Go through these theses and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be so on a careless reading, and what the cheapest revision would be for each real tension. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for each one say what would have to give and what it would cost. If there is a single revision that would stabilize the whole set, identify it and explain what it sacrifices.
