I've been trying to sort out what actually makes something idolatry rather than just loving or caring about something an enormous amount, and I'd like your help working through a case. My rough view is that idolatry is a distinctive way of valuing something wrongly. Here are my four working criteria: (1) the person treats the object as a final end, so all their other commitments get kept or dropped depending on whether they serve it; (2) the object is made to be the source of the person's sense that their life is meaningful or justified — it's the thing they couldn't lose, as they see it, without losing their reason to go on; (3) the devotion is non-negotiable, held immune from trade-offs or serious reappraisal; (4) the devotion is misplaced — typically because the object is being asked to bear more weight than a finite, contingent thing can bear, or because the person has a false picture of what the object is.

Now the case. R is a woman in her sixties who has spent forty years working to protect a particular salt marsh on the coast where she grew up. She isn't religious, and she does'nt think the marsh has a mind or a spirit. She knows it is one estuary among many, that it arose by accident, and that it could be destroyed by sea-level rise no matter what she does. Still, every significant decision in her adult life has gone through one filter: does this protect or restore the marsh. She has ended relationships over it, left a career for it, and oraganized her friendships around it. Asked if she could turn her attention to another wetland or to a more abstract climate cause, she says no — not because the others are worthless, but because, as she puts it, the marsh taught her how to mean anything at all; without it, she thinks, she would have no reason to do anything. She doesn't say the marsh cares about her, or needs her, or will reward her. She says she would rather die than watch it be destroyed, and she means it.

So here's what I want. Run R through the four criteria and tell me whether she counts as idolizing the marsh. Go criterion by criterion, and where a criterion doesn't settle the question, say what extra fact about R would settle it. Then step back: does the case show that criterion (4) is doing all the real work, while (1) through (3) just describe any wholehearted love? And can a clear-eyed devotion to something known to be finite still be valuing it in the wrong way?
