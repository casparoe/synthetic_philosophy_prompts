In my reporting on evolutionary biology, the units-of-selection debate tends to be introduced to me through an argument that runs, in outline, like this. In its classic form, associated with George Williams's Adaptation and Natural Selection and later sharpened by Richard Dawkins in The Selfish Gene, the argument starts from the observation that only genes, or gene-like replicators, persist across generations with enough fidelity to accumulate adaptations, while organisms and groups are comparatively transient vehicles. It then adds that group-level accounts face a special difficulty: a trait that benefits a group is usually vulnerable to a within-group alternative that secures a quicker payoff for its carrier, so between-group selection would have to be unusually strong to prevail. The conclusion is a standing methodological caution — treat group-level adaptation as a last resort, assume that apparent cases will dissolve into lower-level explanations under closer inspection, and place the burden of proof on anyone who invokes group selection.

Lately, however, I have been reading the literature on major evolutionary transitions — the origin of the eukaryotic cell through endosymbiosis, of multicellular organisms from once-independent cells, of social insect colonies from solitary ancestors — and I wonder whether this argument treats as a fixed assumption something that selection can actually alter. On the transition account, an individual organism is itself the outcome of a process in which formerly independent entities suppressed within-group competition so thoroughly that the group began to operate as one unit. What counts as an individual is therefore not a background condition for selection; it is one of the things selection produces. That seems to matter for Williams's caution, because the very case he treated as extremely unlikely — between-group selection overwhelming within-group subversion — is the case invoked to explain how individuals arise in the first place. My question is whether the gene's-eye argument against group selection survives this development: does it go through unchanged, does it need repair, or does it collapse? Please identify the specific premise the major-transitions view puts under pressure, and say whether a repaired version can retain the original caution while conceding that group selection may create the very individuals the argument takes for granted.
