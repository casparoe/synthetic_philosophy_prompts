My brother has a weirdly consistent track record on disputes where a half-decent theory and a firm gut feeling point in opposite directions, and I can't tell what principle he's running. Here are the verdicts I rememeber:

- Monty Hall: he switched doors once he saw the probabilities, and now treats the stay intuition as just an error.
- Linda the bank teller: he says the feminist-bank-teller answer is a conjunction-rule violation and the intuition is a bias, full stop.
- A rare-disease test with a low base rate: he goes with the base rate, and says the urge to panic is wrong.
- The footbridge trolley case: he won't push the man, even though he admits the utilitarian math says push; he says a theory doesn't get to overrule a revulsion that strong.
- A deathbed promise: he says keep the promise, even though breaking it would do a little more total good; the moral intuition beats the consequentialist calculation.

The next case I want to predict is voting. There's a standard rational-choice argument that voting isn't worth it because an individual vote is almost never decisive, but I'm fairly sure he also has a firm intuition that he ought to vote.

What will he say about voting, and what rule is he actually running? I can see at least three rules that fit everything so far: formal or probabilistic theories beat intuition but moral theories don't; or intuitions get trusted when they're socially calibrated and distrusted when they're about evolutionarily novel traps like probabilities; or theory wins whenever it doesn't ask him to harm someone or break a serious commitment. Which best fits, and what extra case would tell the remaining candidates apart?
