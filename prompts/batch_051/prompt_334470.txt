Me and a friend just had a long argument about whether there can be reasons that aren't anybody's reasons. Like, a rainforest would be better off uncut, but there's no particular person whose reason that is -- it's just sort of out there. He called these free-floating reasons, and I kept pushing back that reasons have to be reasons *for* somebody, or they aren't reasons at all. Then later it hit me that the whole dispute maps almost exactly onto free variables versus bound variables in first-order logic. An open formula like P(x) isnt true or false on its own; it only gets a truth value relative to an assignment, or once you bind it with a quantifier. But logicians still carry it around inside proofs and do fine with it (universal generalization over a fresh variable, discharging assumptions, that kind of thing). A free-floating reason looks like the same shape: a normative slot where an agent should go, which becomes a full-blooded reason once you assign it to someone or bind it with something like "someone has a reason to...". My friend says this is pure pattern-matching: variables are meaningless syntactic objects, reasons are normative, and anyway an open formula under an assignment is doing semantic work, while a free-floating reason is supposed to be in force before any assignment. Is he right that the analogy is only superficial, or is there a real structural identity here? If it is real, what would the logical side lead me to predict about reasons that turns out false? And please say how confident you are in what you claim and which point you're least sure of, rather than giving me the whole thing with equal confidence.
