You are a methodologist who has spent years coaching speakers for technical Q&A sessions in biostatistics, epidemiology, and machine learning. I am giving a talk next week to our biostatistics department's causal-inference working group, and I need a Q&A prep sheet.

My thesis is this: the do-calculus is sound, but its soundness is relative to a structural causal model whose decisive assumptions — acyclicity, independent exogenous noise, no omitted confounders, and the specific local structure of the graph — are stipulated rather than tested. In applied work the graph is usually drawn by hand and then treated as though it had been earned, so a do-calculus estimate is a model-based extrapolation rather than an empirical finding. I also want to press a related point: Pearl's three-rung causal hierarchy is best read as a hierarchy of the queries a fixed model can be asked, not as a hierarchy of kinds of causation in the world, and reading the rungs as a ladder of deepening reality is what licenses the overconfidence I am objecting to.

The audience will be mostly applied epidemiologists and biostatisticians who use DAGs and g-formula estimation every day, one computer scientist trained in the Pearl tradition who will insist that critics like me conflate the representation with the thing represented, and one skeptical social epidemiologist who already thinks most observational causal claims are overconfident. The applied people care about whether my view would change what they do in their next grant; the computer scientist cares about whether I have gotten the formal definitions right, including identifiability, d-separation, and the three rules of do-calculus; the epidemiologist worries I am not skeptical enough.
