I'm a third-year economics student currently taking a seminar called Philosophy and Economics of Risk, and a recent unit on ergodicity economics left me unsure whether one of its central claims is something we could ever test. The running example was a repeated multiplicative gamble: each round begins with your current wealth, a fair coin is tossed, and if it comes up heads your wealth rises by 50% while if it comes up tails it falls by 40%; you can also choose each round what fraction of your wealth to stake, leaving the rest uninvested. For any fixed staked fraction, the expected value of wealth rises by 5% per round, so an expected-value maximizer should stake as much as possible. But the typical trajectory declines, because the time-average growth factor is about 0.949 per round — the square root of 1.5 times 0.6 — and a few fortunate paths drive the ensemble average upward while most individuals go broke. Our lecturer then claimed that people, and perhaps evolved organisms generally, are best understood as maximizing time-average growth rather than expectation; the expected-value calculation is not just a bad heuristic but the wrong quantity for a single decision-maker.

My difficulty is with the descriptive side of that claim. If I see someone staking a cautious fraction, my first explanation is ordinary risk aversion — expected utility with a concave utility function — and for multiplicative wealth, maximizing expected logarithmic utility is mathematically the same policy as maximizing the geometric mean. So a preference for a quarter of one's wealth does not by itself reveal which quantity the agent is genuinely tracking. I want to know whether there is any experimental or observational design — with human subjects, with animals facing variable food rewards, with simulated traders, or otherwise — that could separate the hypothesis that people optimize the time average from the hypothesis that they optimize expected utility with some concave utility function. And if the two can be made to diverge on paper, what would a result on one side or the other actually demonstrate?

Please design the strongest test you can of the claim that decision-makers behave as time-average, rather than expected-value, optimizers in non-ergodic settings. In your response, use the following sections in this order, with roughly these lengths: 'The claim' (~100 words), restating the precise descriptive claim under scrutiny and what turns on it; 'The operationalization' (~250 words), saying what observable choice pattern would count as time-average optimization and why the manoeuvre of concave utility threatens the identification; 'A concrete test' (~350 words), describing the design, conditions, and measured behavior in enough detail that someone could run it; 'What the results would show' (~200 words), specifying which outcomes would favor each reading and how decisive they would be; and 'What no test can settle' (~200 words), separating what the experiment can establish from the part of the claim that is interpretation, normativity, or metaphysics and cannot be reached by any measurement.
