Construct a thought experiment to the following specification. The topic is whether a person's I can be genuinely distributed across more than one brain, and whether ordinary human relationships can make literal, not merely metaphorical, sense of the claim that part of A still lives in B after A dies.

Work with three views:

1. Animalism: a human self is identical with a living human organism, so when that organism dies the self ends, although it may leave causal traces in other minds.
2. Psychological continuity theory, in the standard Lockean–Parfit-style family: a self persists through overlapping chains of strong psychological connections, especially first-personal memories and intentions, with the right kind of cause; survival can be a matter of degree, but merely knowing about someone, remembering them third-personally, or imitating them does not by itself amount to that person's survival.
3. A distributed-pattern view in the spirit of Hofstadter: a self is a self-referential, self-stabilizing pattern of thought and feeling, which can in principle be realized at lower fidelity in another brain, so that a recognizable but diminished strand of one person's I may persist in someone else.

Design the case so that views 1 and 2 converge on the verdict that A has not survived in B, while view 3 is compelled by the structure of the case to say that a low-fidelity strand of A's self genuinely does persist in B.

Constraints: no brain uploading, no teleportation, no brain transplants, no split-brain surgery, no telepathy, no afterlife, and no device that records and reinstalls a person's mental states. Everything must proceed only through ordinary human capacities such as long intimacy, shared habits, memory, attention, imitation, joint deliberation, and grief. State the scenario in no more than about 200 words, and keep clear the difference between B's remembering A and B's thinking as A would.

The case is for a discussion of whether a literal continuation relation can be assembled from ordinary material, so do not simply stipulate that B contains part of A; build the case from features that each theory itself commits to counting or discounting.

After the scenario: (a) show the reasoning by which each of the three views reaches its specified verdict; (b) identify the single structural feature that lets view 3 peel off from views 1 and 2 while views 1 and 2 stay together; (c) say what the case collapses into if that feature is removed, and whether it then becomes a case of mere influence about which none of the three would diverge; and (d) give the strongest objection a defender of view 1 or view 2 would press against the case, and the reply a defender of view 3 should offer.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on personal identity, and do not present any of the three views, or the Hofstadter-style reading in particular, as the consensus when it is not.
