In the computational-creativity reading group I attend, one analogy is made to do a great deal of argumentative work, and I want it audited properly. The analogy is that an existing artefact—a tune, a poetic form, a proof, a chair design—is like a device with a control panel, and creating is like turning a small set of its dials a few degrees at a time: transpose the melody, alter the metre, change the material, weaken a premise. You keep the settings that work and discard the rest. Run enough small adjustments, the argument continues, and you arrive at something that looks radically new; there is therefore no difference in kind between competent variation of a received pattern and genuine innovation. The people who use the analogy draw a specific conclusion from it: creativity is continuous and local; talk of leaps, ruptures, and irreducible genius is mystification; and a procedure of variation-plus-selection accounts for the whole of what we call the creative spark. Audit this analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly—what plays the role of the dials, the positions on a dial, the underlying device, and the hand that judges a setting. Then mark the places where the mapping strains: real dials have fixed dimensions, known ranges, independent axes, reversible movements, and a stable device beneath them, and conceptual spaces may lack each of these. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. Say whether the conclusion survives the audit, and offer a better analogy if you think one is available. Please write your answer in American English.
