A law-school friend walked me through an argument he half-built for a criminal law seminar and now I suspect it proves too much. Here it is, numbered as he gave it.

Suppose a man committed an armed robbery in 1984. He was never caught. In 1985 the state would happily have prosecuted him if it had found him then. He has been clean ever since. Last year someone found a videotaped confession he made in 1986, plus records that establish the crime about as well as a case from 1986 would have. The statute of limitations has run.

(1) If a serious wrong was committed in 1984, the passing of forty years does not make the act any less wrong. The robbery is not half as wrong now, or slightly wrong now; if it was wrong then, it is wrong now.

(2) If the act is exactly as wrong now as it was in 1986, and the state's evidence is at least as good now as it was in 1986, then the state's backward-looking reason to punish — the reason that points at what he did — is no weaker now than it was in 1986.

(3) If that reason is just as strong, then in a case like this, with excellent evidence, the bare passage of time should not make prosecution impermissible or pointless.

(4) But a statute of limitations makes the bare passage of time decisive: once the clock runs out, even a taped confession and clean records won't get you a prosecution.

(5) So either every limitation period that would bar this case is unjust, or the passage of time by itself weakens what is owed for a wrong.

(6) The second horn sounds just as bad: if the passage of time weakens what is owed, then an unpunished wrong quietly shrinks, and after enough years an unpunished robber owes less than a fresh shoplifter owes for the shoplifting. That is close to saying wrongness decays, which (1) denied.

My friend's classmates said (4) is loaded, because limitation rules are really blunt responses to fading memories and degrading evidence. But the case was built with excellent evidence, so that reply sidesteps the argument. Others said punishment also serves deterrence and reform, and those aims decay. Sure, but that leaves the backward-looking reason in (2) standing; you would still need to explain why it can be outweighed, and then we're back to asking why the simple passage of time can outweigh it. I keep going back and forth between blaming the move from (1) to (2) and biting the bullet on (5)'s second horn. Which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think none of them need rejecting, explain why the second horn is not as wild as I think. And tell me what argument, or what alteration of the case, would most likely change your diagnosis.
