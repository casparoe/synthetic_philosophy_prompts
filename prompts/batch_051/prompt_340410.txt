(1) To be corrupt is to let some incentive — money, a favor owed, a threat, flattery from someone you like — shift your verdict away from what the merits of the case would have given on their own.
(2) But there's no such thing as being selectively immune to incentives. The machinery that makes a person feel the pull of a bribe, or the warmth of being flattered, isn't fitted with a filter for "corrupting only." Someone who genuinely doesn't feel that pull isn't going to feel the pull of much else either: the obvious distress of the person in front of them, the worry that their last ten decisions got overturned, the sense that this ruling will wreck someone's livelihood. So-called immunity to corrupting incentives is just flat affect, pointed by luck at the categories we happen to label corruption.
(3) Flat affect isn't a virtue. It's a defect of practical judgment, because part of what the merits of a case amount to is what's at stake for the people involved, and you can't track that if your insulated from how much it costs them.
(4) So the person we call incorruptible either isn't really immune to incentives — it's just that the incentives they answer to aren't the ones we've chosen to call corruption — or they are immune in a way that makes them worse at judging, not better.
(5) Therefore "incorruptible" doesn't name a coherent ideal. It's a presentational myth, or a vice.

I keep suspecting step (2) is doing something sneaky — something about the word "incentive" — but I can't make the objection stick. Is the argument valid?
