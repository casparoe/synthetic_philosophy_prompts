I spent last night arguing with a friend about a mutual acquaintance who has lived in this country for nineteen years as a permanent resident, works, pays taxes, and is raising citizen children, but cannot vote because she has never naturalized. My friend kept insisting that a democracy must presuppose a demos, and that the demos just is the citizenry; otherwise, he said, the state becomes answerable to outsiders rather than to its own people. I kept objecting that this is circular — the only people who get to decide who counts are the people who already count — and that it leaves a settled population permanently subject to laws it had no part in authoring. His deeper point was that a state that loosens the boundary until everyone with a stake gets a vote ceases to be a self-governing political community; my deeper point was that his view makes an inherited legal status do all the work that actually belongs to being governed. By the end we had to admit that neither of us could say what membership in the demos consists in, as distinct from merely being affected by a state's decisions or subject to its enforcement.

Give me a working definition, or a principled criterion, of membership in the demos, and test it against these cases: (1) a natural-born adult citizen who has lived in the country her whole life, votes, and is fully subject to its law; (2) a tourist who happens to be in the country during an election; (3) the nineteen-year permanent resident described above; (4) a citizen who emigrated thirty years ago, has rarely returned, and pays no taxes in the country; (5) a sixteen-year-old citizen who works and pays income tax; (6) a citizen serving a life sentence; (7) a citizen with profound cognitive disabilities who cannot understand what voting is; (8) future generations who will bear many of the effects of today's climate and fiscal decisions; (9) a noncitizen who crosses the border daily to work but lives in the neighboring state. I am confident that (1) belongs and that (2) does not; I lean toward including (3); and I have no stable view about (5), (6), (7), (8), and (9). For those, let the definition do the work, and I am willing to be surprised.

Please commit to a definite position rather than surveying competing principles and leaving them side by side. State the definition, run every case through it, say explicitly where it yields a verdict that ordinary democratic practice would reject, and then either revise the definition or defend the verdict. End by telling me which of your classifications you are least willing to defend, and why.
