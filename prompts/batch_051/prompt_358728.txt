I am a PhD student working on formal epistemology, and in a recent decision theory seminar a colleague offered the following argument, which, he claims, shows that the Dutch book defense of probabilism becomes self-undermining once it is allowed to run across time. I cannot find the exact point of failure, and I would like a precise diagnosis. (1) A rational agent is not systematically exploitable: there should be no set of bets, each of which is fair or slightly favourable by the agent's own degrees of belief at the moment it is offered, that jointly guarantee a net loss no matter how the world turns out. (2) If an agent's current degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then there is such a set of bets, all offered simultaneously. (3) Therefore, rational credences satisfy the probability axioms. So far this is just the familiar argument.

But, he continues, the same principle cuts deeper. (4) Suppose that right now you assign credence 0.1 to some proposition A, and you know with certainty that tomorrow a drug will be administered that, as a matter of brute physiology rather than as evidence for A, will cause you to assign credence 1 to A. Today your fair price for a ticket that pays one dollar if A is ten cents, so you would regard selling such a ticket for twelve cents as more than fair. Tomorrow, being certain of A, your fair price for an equivalent ticket will be one dollar, so you would regard buying it for ninety-eight cents as more than fair. The two transactions together would leave you having received twelve cents and paid ninety-eight cents, while the one-dollar obligations cancel; you therefore lose eighty-six cents whether or not A is true. (5) By (1), then, you are already irrational now unless you assign probability 1 to A. Generalizing, your present credence in a proposition must equal the credence you know you will have in it. (6) That consequence is intolerable: it would require a rational person to adopt a known delusion in advance and would count foreseen future irrationality as present irrationality. (7) Therefore, he concludes, the Dutch book premise in (1) is false, and the original argument for (3) is no better than a shared mistake.

I am reluctant to accept that conclusion, but I also cannot see how to reject any step without pulling apart the synchronic argument I would like to keep. I would most like to retain something in the neighbourhood of (1) strong enough to secure (3), and I am prepared to restrict the principle in whatever way is needed. But I want an honest accounting of the price. Which step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
