I do incident review for automated decision systems — fleet dispatch software, clinical triage tools, adaptive cruise control type things — and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a name for.

Two cases. A van's brake-assist system braked too late in heavy rain. The driver couldn't have seen the obstacle. The engineer who set the braking threshold hasn't touched that code in three versions. The fleet operator disabled the secondary sensor to save money. The vendor says the model behaved within spec. All of those statements are true, and yet the final report still lands on the driver as the responsible party — not because the analysis points there, but because the report format needs a named human. The actual fault, if that word even means anything here, is smeared across decisions made years apart, none of which was clearly wrong at the time it was made.

Second case: a triage algorithm under-recommends screening for an unusual presentation. The physician followed its output. The hospital bought the tool. The vendor says it was trained on a population slightly different from the one it got deployed on. The family wants someone held responsible; the hospital board wants it to never happen again. And those two demands pull apart: the path to preventing recurrence runs through changing the training distribution, the interface, and the audit schedule, none of which requires blaming anyone in particular. The path to blaming someone runs to whoever is available, insured, and still in business, which does almost nothing to prevent the next one.

I keep thinking that in an old-fashioned engineering failure, the single question "what went wrong?" does double duty: it finds fault and tells you what to fix. With these systems it dosen't do either cleanly — blame goes to whoever is left standing, and prevention goes to a separate group of people who would rather not talk about blame at all. There's also something about the responsibility not exactly distributing, but evaporating: each individual decision looks defensible on its own, and the total outcome is a serious harm with no corresponding owner.

Is there an established name for this, and is it one phenomenon or two? I don't just want a label and a sentence. I want the carving: what exactly it is, what it isn't, how it differs from ordinary "no one is entirely at fault" or group diffusion of responsibility, and whether the detachment of blame from prevention is part of the same concept or a second one. If the closest existing concept doesn't fit perfectly, say so and tell me where the edges are.
