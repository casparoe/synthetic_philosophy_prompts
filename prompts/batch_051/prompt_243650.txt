I am a novelist working on a book whose protagonist moves from a community that polices conduct through shame to one that tries to cultivate guilt. In the course of writing, I have come to hold a position that I want you to stress-test, because the novel should not simply be propaganda for it. The position starts from a passage in Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.9, in H. Rackham's translation:

"Modesty cannot properly be described as a virtue, for it seems to be a feeling rather than a disposition; at least it is defined as a kind of fear of disrepute, and indeed in its effects it is akin to the fear of danger; for people who are ashamed blush, while those in fear of their lives turn pale; both therefore appear to be in a sense bodily affections, and this indicates a feeling rather than a disposition. The feeling of modesty is not suitable to every age, but only to the young. We think it proper for the young to be modest, because as they live by feeling they often err, and modesty may keep them in check; and we praise young people when they are modest, though no one would praise an older man for being shamefaced, since we think he ought not to do anything of which he need be ashamed."

In my own words, I hold that shame is not a moral virtue and that, as a standing moral emotion, it is inferior to guilt. Guilt asks what I have done; shame asks what I am before an audience. A mature agent should not need shame, because he will not do the things that would make him ashamed. Shame is at best a training emotion for the young. A culture that makes shame its primary moral emotion keeps its members in moral adolescence, tying them to an audience rather than to their own judgment. I have been using a device I call the audience-free test: a response counts as a strictly moral emotion only if its content can survive the imagined removal of every actual and hypothetical observer. Guilt passes; shame fails, because shame is constitutively the experience of being seen, even if only by an internalized other.

Act as a professional philosopher with expertise in moral psychology and the history of ethics. Think carefully, and begin by reconstructing the position in its strongest form, including the distinction between shame and guilt I am relying on and the empirical presuppositions of the audience-free test. Then tell me what would count against the position. I want defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be: fatal, serious but non-fatal, merely awkward. For each, say whether a committed defender of the position would concede that it counts against the view or would dismiss it, and why. Distinguish evidence that would break the theory from evidence that would only dent it. Also state what the position predicts about individual agents and about cultures that rival positions do not, and which of those predictions would be the most discriminating tests. In particular, my plot assumes that a culture can deliberately move from shame to guilt; tell me what would have to be true for that to be a real cultural choice, and what evidence would count against its being possible or desirable. End with an honest assessment of whether the position, as I have formulated it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for this kind of claim.
