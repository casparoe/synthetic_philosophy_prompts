Been thinking about something I call a safe Pareto improvement and I want somebody who actually knows the philosophy to kick the tires. A normal Pareto improvement is a change where at least one person ends up better off and no one ends up worse off. My problem is that this is only defined relative to our model of people's preferences and of how the change will play out, and in practice that model is always wrong somewhere. So my notion is: a change is a safe Pareto improvement if it's Pareto-improving not just on the model we have, but on every model in some plausibility envelope around it — the nearby models we'd still take seriously after it ships and the complaints roll in. This feels worth defining because the stuff I see at work (changing a default, splitting a shared service between teams) is constantly Pareto on paper but quietly harms someone under a slightly different, equally defensible model. Three things I want checked: does the defintion hold together or is the 'envelope' doing all the work? is this already a standard idea under some name I don't know (robust optimization, ambiguity aversion, something in social choice)? and is 'safe' maybe the wrong property to optimize for, since you could fix ordinary Pareto improvements with ex post compensation instead of demanding pre-change certainty? Give me your bottom line first, in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and dont restate the question.
