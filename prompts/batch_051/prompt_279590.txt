Negative utilitarianism holds that reducing suffering has priority and that reducing it outweighs promoting happiness. Classic versions of the view were attacked with the world-exploder objection: if suffering is the only thing with negative value, then painlessly annihilating all sentient life would be not just allowed but required, since it would mean no future suffering. Contemporary suffering-focused views often avoid this by making two changes. They become person-affecting, counting harms only to beings who exist or will exist, so that a life not lived is not a wrong against anyone. And they impose a side constraint against intentionally killing existing sentient beings, even painlessly. I want to know what actually happens to the objection under this repaired view.

Does the world-exploder objection survive? Which premise did it actually depend on—the totalizing aggregation over possible future beings, or the deeper idea that any agent who makes suffering the sole criterion must be willing to end lives for it? Does the repaired view escape the objection, or does a weaker relative of it reappear: for example, an urgent duty never to create new sentient lives, or to use only nonlethal means to shrink the expected amount of sentient life? I am asking about the fate of the argument, not about which policy follows.

If the question is badly posed—if the repair is ad hoc in a way that matters, or the original objection never needed the totalizing premise I assigned it—tell me that and answer the strongest version of the question you can. Keep the response under 500 words and begin with a one-sentence bottom line: the objection is dead, needs repair, or still lives in a weaker form.
