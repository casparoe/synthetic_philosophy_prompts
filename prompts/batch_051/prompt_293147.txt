So I'm writing a novel set around a California ballot proposition — sweeping zoning reform, let's say, because my main character runs the campaign — and I've run into a problem a lot of novelists have: a character of mine has a tidy argument I don't actually know is true. He's a former city planning aide, and every time a reporter asks why the result should count as "the people's will" even though half the city hates it, he says a referendum is basically a jury: ordinary people, not politicians or judges, get presented the evidence, weigh it, and hand down a verdict, and that's why the outcome deserves respect even when it's ugly. He thinks he's describing the purest form of democratic judgment.

There's a political scientist character who's going to dismantle this, but I don't want her to just win on vibes, and I don't want the novel to settle into a lazy both-sides-have-points fog. So give me the actual audit of the analogy.

Lay out what maps onto what: jurors to voters, the charge to the ballot measure, the evidence to the campaign, the jury-room deliberation to whatever passes for deliberation in a vote, the verdict to the yes/no result, the judge's instructions to the official ballot summary and proposition text, the unanimity-or-near-unanimity rule to majority threshold, sequestration to — well, to what? Then where the mapping breaks, and be ruthless about which breaks matter for the conclusion he's actually using it to prop up (that we should treat the outcome as a legitimate verdict rather than a gamed number) and which are just surface-level.

I also want the stuff the jury analogy quietly imports: random selection as a guard against bias, forced deliberation, rules of evidence, the idea that a jury decides a bounded factual question rather than a contested policy preference, jury nullification, the fact that juries are tiny and can hear each other. Does his conclusion survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the whole load? And if the jury analogy's no good, what's the closest better one — an election, a mass negotiation, a focus group with a veto, or should I say there isn't one? I'd rather have none than a false tidy one.
