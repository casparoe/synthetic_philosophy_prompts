Here's an argument I keep half-believing and can't tell whether it's sound or doing something sneaky. The conclusion is that famines are never just natural disasters — they're political crimes, or at least political failures — and I want someone to tell me exactly which step is wrong, because the conclusion feels too strong but each premise feels true when I read it one at a time.

(1) A famine is an event where large numbers of people starve.
(2) People starve when they cannot command enough food — through wages, land, markets, rations, gifts, whatever.
(3) What food a person can command is set by entitlements: property rights, prices, wages, government provision, and the rules about who may buy, sell, grow, or receive food.
(4) Entitlements are human arrangements: they are made and enforced by laws, policies, courts, and officials.
(5) So in any famine, who starves is set by human arrangements, not merely by nature.
(6) If whether thousands of people starve is set by human arrangements, then the famine isn't an act of God, it's something people did.
(7) And if it's something people did on that scale, it's not a misfortune, it's a political crime.

Part of what attracts me to this is that it's supposed to bypass questions about food supply entirely. It shouldn't matter whether the total amount of food collapsed or not; once any food exists, who gets it is an entitlement question, and if no food exists, then... hmm, actually that might be exactly where step (5) breaks. But I want more than my own hunch.

I can already hear two other replies too: (a) a drought can destroy the food supply so thoroughly that no arrangement could have fed everyone, so (5) overreaches; (b) "crime" needs a perpetrator with some kind of intent or gross negligence, and bad peacetime policies aren't automatically that. But I want precision, not objections. Which numbered step is the first one that fails, and on what exact reading of it? Is the argument valid as written? If you fix the bad step, what's the strongest surviving version of the argument, and does it still get you to "political crime" or only to the weaker claim that natural events have political dimensions? Be specific about "determined by," "cause," and "crime."
