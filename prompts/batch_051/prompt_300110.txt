Take the classic Chinese room thought experiment, but change the question. In the original, the room passes behavioural tests for understanding Chinese while, so the argument runs, merely following a rulebook; the moral is that behaviour underdetermines understanding. Now treat the room as a black-box optimiser whose objective and search space a spectator must infer from behaviour alone. The room is presented with a sequence of written decision problems: each describes a finite menu of actions and their consequences, and the room returns its chosen action. Nothing else is observable.

First, state the purely behavioural conditions under which the room's choices are consistent with the hypothesis that it is maximising a single, stable utility function over the consequences as described. Define these conditions in your own terms—stated as constraints on choices, not on utilities—and explain why they are at best necessary, and not sufficient, for the hypothesis.

Second, suppose the room sometimes violates those conditions in specific patterns: (a) choices consistent within each menu but not across menus; (b) a persistent refusal to choose an action that the written description says dominates all others; and (c) choices that change when irrelevant alternatives are added or removed. For each pattern, say what, if anything, it allows the spectator to infer about the room's objective, about the action space it actually searches over, and about whether the room is better modelled as an optimiser at all, or as a rule that merely happens to coincide with maximisation on the observed cases.

Third, suppose the spectator may design the decision problems, but each query is costly. How should the spectator choose queries to separate the candidate hypotheses, which should include at least the following: a single-objective maximiser that evaluates every option in the menu before choosing; a satisficer that stops at the first option meeting a fixed threshold; and a habit-following agent that chooses by rote unless a problem is novel. Identify which separations require adaptive querying—where later problems depend on earlier answers—and which hypotheses remain behaviourally indistinguishable in principle even with unlimited data.

Conclude by stating which of the spectator's possible inferences survive in the limit of unlimited, well-designed data, and which remain underdetermined, and compare that result with the original thought experiment's conclusion that behaviour underdetermines understanding.
