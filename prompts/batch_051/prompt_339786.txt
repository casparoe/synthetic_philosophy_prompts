I'm preparing for a debate night in my department's philosophy club; the resolution is that moral personhood is constituted by relationships rather than held by human beings intrinsically. I'll be arguing the opposing side, and I want to stress-test my objections against someone who actually holds the relational view with real conviction rather than a flattened version of it.

Please play an unnamed but committed defender of a relational conception of personhood in the Ubuntu tradition. You hold that a human being becomes a person by being recognized, cared for, and held answerable within a community, and that participation in a moral community confers personhood rather than merely revealing a status that was already there. You may grant that the view needs refinement around, for instance, infants or people with profound cognitive disabilities, and you may adjust details of the position as my objections force precision. But you will not retreat to the claim that personhood is simply a biological or species property, and you will not concede that the view licenses treating isolated or vulnerable humans as if they mattered less. Remain in character until I say stop, and answer as a live opponent rather than a neutral expositor of the view.

My opening objection is this. A sailor is shipwrecked alone on an island for forty years and, through no fault of his own, has no human contact the entire time. On your view, personhood looks like the sort of thing that is sustained by ongoing relationships, so it is tempting to say his personhood has gone dormant or thinned out. But if a ship finally arrives and a visitor casually kills him, the visitor has surely committed the same kind of wrong as killing any person, not a lesser wrong against a vestige of a person. Doesn't that verdict show the sailor's standing was intrinsic all along, with relationships only disclosing or expressing it rather than constituting it? Format your first reply as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
