Ok so I keep going back and forth on the insect thing. One side says: if there's even a small chance that insects are sentient, the numbers are so huge—the number of individual insects alive at any given time is mind-boggling—that the expected amount of suffering swamps almost anything we do to prevent human or farm-animal suffering. The other side says that's a mug's game: once you start multiplying tiny probabilities by astronomical population sizes, you can justify whatever you want, and the whole apparatus stops tracking anything real. I'd like to stop going case by case and instead ask what a theory of insect moral status would actually have to get right. Write the desiderata: the requirement list for an adequate theory of what, if anything, we owe to insects given uncertainty about their sentience. At minimum it should cover (a) what kind of evidence from behavior, learning, or neurobiology counts as raising or lowering the probability of sentience, (b) how to handle the fact that the probability might be very low but the number of affected individuals very high, (c) whether aggregation across that many individuals is even legitimate or whether it's just expected-value reasoning run amok, and (d) the distinction between being sentient and having moral weight. For each requirement, give the concrete case or result behind it. Flag any two requirements that you think can't be jointly met. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out popular theories. Keep the focus on the criteria, not on scoring existing views.
