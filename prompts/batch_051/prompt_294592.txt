I'm an undergraduate in a course on the philosophy of time, and we have been discussing how social practices like calendars, clocks, and era names quietly embody views about what time is and how history is structured. I want to use our civil calendar as a test case. The calendar used for nearly all international civil purposes numbers years from a traditional calculation of the birth of Jesus, uses no year zero (1 BC is followed immediately by AD 1), and therefore counts decades and centuries from an initial year 1 rather than from 0, which is why the twenty-first century is standardly said to have begun in 2001 rather than 2000. The same epoch is retained in the secular BCE/CE labels, while astronomers quietly use a separate convention in which 1 BC is year 0 and earlier years take negative numbers. I'd like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes about time and history. In particular: does the absence of a year zero commit the calendar to an ordinal rather than a cardinal conception of time, or is that merely a notational artifact? Does the choice of a single epoch presuppose that history has a center or that some one event can orient all of it, or is the epoch best understood as an arbitrary coordination device with no deep content? Does the BCE/CE relabeling, which leaves the epoch untouched, actually neutralize whatever the original choice presupposed, or does it carry that commitment along under a secular description? And what would have to change in the calendar if a different theory, such as a purely cardinal time with no privileged epoch or a plurality of epochs, were substituted for the one you reconstruct? I am not asking for a history of the calendar; I am asking for the implicit theory and for a careful separation of what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and what it leaves philosophically underdetermined.
