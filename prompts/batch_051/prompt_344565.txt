I'm a retired chemist — spent thirty years doing kinetics in industry, mostly catalysis and some polymer work — and I've only recently started reading metaphysics in my retirement. I keep getting stuck on the literature about dispositions, finks, and masks. The standard sort of case I keep seeing is Martin's live wire: a wire is disposed to shock you, but a fink (some machine that flips it to dead when touched) removes the disposition before it can manifest; a mask (insulation) stops the manifestation without removing the disposition. I get the point about simple counterfactual analyses failing, but I've got a nagging feeling that my whole professional vocabulary already handles this — we deal all day with reagents that would react except something intercepts them, catalyzes them, poisons the catalyst, scavenges the intermediate, protects the functional group, or changes the pathway. I want to know whether my feeling is right and what it costs.

Here's the passage that keeps nagging me, from Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXI, "Of Power," section 1:

"Thus we say, Fire has a power to melt gold, i.e. to destroy the consistency of its insensible parts, and consequently its hardness, and make it fluid ; and gold has a power to be melted ; that the sun has a power to blanch wax, and wax a power to be blanched by the sun, whereby the yellowness is destroyed, and whiteness made to exist in its room."

What I'd like is a systematic translation, not an analogy. Restate the fink/mask problem — the distinction between a fink and a mask, the failure of the simple conditional analysis, and the intrinsic/extrinsic disposition distinction — in the vocabulary of chemical kinetics and thermodynamics: rate laws, rate constants, activation energy, transition states, catalysts, inhibitors, poisons, scavengers, protecting groups, equilibrium constants. Do it one claim at a time, the way you'd translate a paradox into a set of coupled rate equations, and keep it honest: where the chemistry vocabulary maps cleanly, say so; where you have to fudge, say what got fudged; where a philosophical claim simply has no chemical counterpart, say so.

Then give me the residue report. I specifically want to know: does the chemistry translation show the fink/mask dispute is less substantive than philosophers think, or does it show that chemistry is missing something the metaphysics needs? In particular: chemists don't usually ask whether a reagent "really has" a disposition when the reaction doesn't happen because of an inhibitor — the inhibitor changes the conditions, period. Does the philosophical insistence that a masked disposition is still there have any counterpart in how a kineticist thinks, or is that exactly the kind of thing chemistry can't express? And test the translation against the Locke passage: when Locke says gold has a power to be melted, is that thermodynamic or kinetic, and does the fink/mask literature have a way of saying that gold's susceptibility to fire survives the presence of a flame-retardant coating, or does the distinction break down?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your overall verdict on what survives and what doesn't — and then give me the translation and the residue report, no preamble and no restating of the question.
