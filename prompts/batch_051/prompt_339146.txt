I'm the newest member of my hospital's surgical innovation ethics committee, and I've been volunteered to write the memo on an application we've deadlocked on twice now. The protocol is a first-in-human study: a patient with a progressive condition that destroys basically everything below the neck but spares the brain would have the brain removed, kept alive, and implanted into the body of a brain-dead donor. The surgical team says it's just a transplant with extra steps — the person obvious goes with the brain, so the recipient consents and the donor's family consents to whole-body donation, end of story. But one faction on the committee keeps bringing up Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment (the one where the brain is kept alive in a vat, separate from the body, and he asks where he is). Their worry is that once brain and body come apart, "where the person is" stops having an obvious answer — is the result a patient with a borrowed body, a donor body carrying someone else's memories, or some third thing with no determinate identity until the hospital or the state just stipulates one? They also point out that the team's consent form calls the patient the "recipient" throughout, which the faction thinks is already begging the question.

The chair wants a memo that actually breaks the tie. I need you to draft it: make a clear recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions), give criteria the committee can apply to this study and to future brain/whole-body transplant proposals, and give the losing side's strongest point a fair run rather than straw-manning it. And keep the Dennett talk mostly in the background — this has to be something a busy hospital committee can vote on, not a philosophy paper.
