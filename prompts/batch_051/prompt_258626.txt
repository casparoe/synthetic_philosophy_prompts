I am a PhD student in psychology working on expert forecasting, and I am preparing a handout for a doctoral methods seminar on epistemic humility. I want the handout to end with a sorting instrument — a decision tree that assigns a reader to a position on the inside/outside view by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they prefer.

Please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Start by stating the branching questions and the combinations of answers they permit; only then place the actual positions as leaves; and explicitly mark any answer-combination that is unoccupied or unnamed, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the spot is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

For my purpose, the branching should be driven by three distinctions: (1) whether a reference class's base rate ever supplies usable information about an individual case; (2) whether distinctive features of the particular case can ever license overriding the outside view; and (3) when such an override is licensed, whether the license is uniform across kinds of judgment or varies with the type of domain.

The leaves must include at least these positions: the strict outside view (reference-class monism); the anchored outside view (start from the base rate and adjust for case features, but expect the adjustment to be too small); the expertise-exception view (case-specific override is permitted only when backed by a calibrated track record); the domain-sensitivity view (whether inside or outside deserves priority depends on the kind of judgment being made); and radical case-specificism (the reference class is always too coarse to use). If two of these collapse under my distinctions, say so and flag what would be needed to separate them; if any leaf you add is one nobody clearly defends, label it unnamed and say why.

Each branching node must be phrased as a concrete question a seminar participant could answer without already knowing the labels; no question may mention the inside view, the outside view, base rates, or the name of any leaf. Where it helps, make the nodes case-based — a kitchen renovation running over time, a patient with an atypical presentation, an unusual election, a startup pitch. Each leaf should carry a short name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Format: aim for a one-page instrument, no more than six branching questions and roughly five to seven leaves. End the response with a one-sentence summary of the tree's central distinction on its own line, beginning with "Summary:".
