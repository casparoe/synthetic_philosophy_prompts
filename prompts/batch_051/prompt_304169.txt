Cox's theorem is the usual story for why rational degrees of belief have to be probabilities: if your plausibilities are real numbers, the plausibility of a conjunction is some function of the plausibilities of its parts, the plausibility of a negation is another function of the plausibility of the original, and logically equivalent propositions get the same plausibility, then after a monotone rescaling you get stuck with the product and sum rules of probability. The derivation is normally done inside classical Boolean logic, even if nobody says so out loud. Now replace that background logic with something non-Boolean—specifically the logic of quantum-mechanical propositions (subspaces of a Hilbert space, conjunction as intersection, disjunction as closed span, complement as orthogonal complement), where the distributive law fails: A & (B or C) is not generally equivalent to (A & B) or (A & C). Does Cox's theorem survive this switch? Identify which premise of the classical derivation is actually carrying the load. Is it just real-valuedness plus associativity of conjunction (which would still hold), or does the proof secrectly lean on distributivity, or on having disjunction behave the nice Boolean way? Then say whether a modified Cox-type theorem could be recovered on the non-distributive lattice—maybe forcing probability measures on subspaces, or maybe not—and what that does to the claim that Cox's theorem shows probability is the logic of plausible reasoning, full stop.
