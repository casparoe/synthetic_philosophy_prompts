Here's a counterfactual I've been turning over. Suppose the major European scientific academies — the London one, the Paris one, all the big ones — agreed around 1700 on a strange and credibly enforced publishing norm. If two or more people independantly reach the same discovery within about ten years of each other, no individual gets their name attached to it: the law, the formula, the device, whatever, becomes anonymous common property, credited to no one in particular, with none of the usual payoffs (prizes, professorships, a place in the history books). The catch is that the decision gets made only after the ten-year window closes, so on the day you publish you don't yet know whether your result will end up named after you or absorbed back into the common pool. Only so-called singletons — discoveries nobody else duplicates within a decade — get named for their creator and carry the usual reputation rewards. Physics, budgets, and everything else keep working as they actually did. I want you to walk me through the next two centuries of science, making the causal chain explicit. Do people publish earlier to establish priority, or sit on results hoping the ten years pass without a rival so they can collect as a named singleton? Do they pile into hot fields or retreat to lonely ones where duplication is less likely? Does the actual content of scientific knowledge change, or only the sociology of who gets famous? And what would the accumulating record of named versus anonymized results tell us about whether big discoveries are inevitable or depend on a few irreplaceable people? Mark which links in your prediction are robust, which are guesses, and what assumption, if false, would flip the main effect.
