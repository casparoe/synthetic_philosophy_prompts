I've been rereading W. E. B. Du Bois's essay "Criteria of Negro Art" (originally a speech from 1926, published in The Crisis) for a class on African American political thought, and I keep tripping over what looks like a flat contradiction in the same piece. Early on, Du Bois spends a lot of time on Beauty with a capital B: he describes beautiful scenes he's encountered, attacks white Americans for being loud strivers with no feeling for beauty, and says Black people have a special vision of what a truly beautiful world would be—one where people know, create, realize themselves, and enjoy life. The goal of art, in that stretch, seems to be beauty and the making of a certain kind of world, not the advancement of a political program. But then, near the end, he doubles down and says that art is always propaganda, that it has never been anything else, and that he has no use for art that isn't put to work for the Black freedom struggle. Those two register as totally different aesthetic theories: beauty-for-its-own-sake versus art-as-instrument. Are they actually in tension, or is there a reading that makes them fit? What candidate reconciliations could someone offer—for example, a distinction between the ultimate end and the immediate function, or between the art white audiences already have and the art Black artists owe their own community—and which of them are honest to the text rather than special pleading? Which reading best explains why he lets both claims stand in the same essay instead of choosing one? I'd like the answer to be in American English.
