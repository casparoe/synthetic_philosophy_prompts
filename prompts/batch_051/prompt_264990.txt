Construct an orthography for an invented language, Kessari, and show that it meets the specifications below. This is for a handout in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the structure of writing systems. The purpose is to force a clean separation between three levels at which a segmental script can be said to be "phonemic": the surface phone (actual sound), the surface phoneme (contrast after predictable allophony is abstracted away), and the morphophoneme (contrast recoverable only from related forms). A construction cheats if it simply stipulates that the script is "morphophonemic" rather than deriving the verdict from the language's phonology, or if it abandons the alphabetic format to secure the verdict.

Kessari has the following properties:

- Consonants /p t k b d g m n s l r/; vowels /i e a o u/.
- Syllables are (C)V(C). Lexical stress falls on either the final or penultimate syllable and is not predictable.
- Word-final devoicing: /b d g/ surface as [p t k] at the end of a word.
- Unstressed /a/ surfaces as [ə].
- Word-initial /p t k/ are aspirated [pʰ tʰ kʰ].
- Consonant-final nouns form their plural with the suffix /-a/, which does not receive stress. In the plural, a stem-final underlying voiced stop is no longer word-final and surfaces voiced.

Design an alphabetic orthography for Kessari — Latin letters in angle brackets are fine; you need not invent glyphs — satisfying these target properties:

(A) Alphabetic and segmental: at most fifteen letters, one for each segmental phoneme, vowels always written, no separate letters for allophones, and no logographs, syllabograms, digraphs, or featural notation that encodes subsegmental detail.
(B) It distinguishes in writing two words that are homophones in their singular pronunciations but are pronounced differently in the plural.
(C) It does not distinguish [a] from [ə] in writing.
(D) It assigns the same spelling to at least two words that differ only in lexical stress.
(E) Once stress is supplied, a reader who knows the rules can recover the segmental surface forms from the spelling; but a speller cannot recover the spelling from the surface pronunciation alone, because the underlying final consonant is needed.

Then provide the demonstration:

1. Give the grapheme inventory and a compact set of reading rules (grapheme-to-surface rules) stated in terms of Kessari's phonology, noting explicitly what information the script does not encode.
2. Give a full nominal paradigm for two consonant-final nouns, singular and plural, in both spelling and surface transcription; also give one pair of words differing only in lexical stress. These examples should jointly display (B), (C), and (D).
3. State your verdict: at which of the three levels — surface phone, surface phoneme, morphophoneme — is the script phonemic? Justify the verdict and identify the exact feature(s) of Kessari that force the script away from surface-phonemic spelling.
4. Identify the single feature of the language doing the most work, and say specifically how the script's status would change if that feature were removed.
5. Give the strongest objection from a defender of the strict surface-phonemic principle — someone who holds that a good alphabet writes surface contrasts, and predictable allophones only when decoding requires them — and the best reply available to a defender of this orthography.
6. Conclude by saying what the case shows about what an alphabet is a theory of: sound, or the lexicon's persistent contrasts? Explain how the script's treatment of [a]/[ə] and its treatment of final devoicing pull in different directions.
