Alright, I'm a science journalist and I've got a reporting rule I use on deadline that I genuinely can't tell if it's defensible or just vibes dressed up as principle. When I can't parse a study myself, I decide how much confidence to put in a claim by looking at the structure of who believes it. If five or six experts who got there independently — different institutions, no shared grants, they didn't coauthor the key paper — all sign off on something, I'll write it as settled. But if thirty people sign off and they're all from one collaboration, or they all trained under the same advisor, or they're all just reacting to the same preprint I watched circulate last week, I treat the whole pile as one source. Three independent groups nudging the same way beats thirty coauthors, in my book.

The part that's been bugging me is the mushy middle. A field where everyone knows everyone, or a consensus built at a conference rather than by people coming at it from different directions. And I half-remember something from a class about a jury theorem where majority rule only works if the voters are independent and individually better than coin-flips — am I just blindly applying that? The thing is, shared training isn't always bad: people trained the same way might share a systematic blind spot, sure, but they might also share a set of hard-won methods that make each of them more likely to be right. So is my rule rationally defensible? Is there a cleaner version that keeps whatever's right in it without the sloppy parts, and where exactly should I draw the line between 'independant corroboration' and 'one rumor wearing five name tags'?
