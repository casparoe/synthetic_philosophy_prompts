I have recently been accompanying my mother to neurology appointments after her epilepsy became drug-resistant, and although a corpus callosotomy was mentioned only as a distant possibility, it sent me into the split-brain literature and, from there, into a more basic question I find I cannot answer cleanly. I want a working philosophical definition of a single subject of experience: what it takes for a collection of mental states, processes, or behaviors to belong to one subject rather than to two or to none. I am not asking for a clinical or legal criterion, and I do not need references; I need a definition I can actually apply.

In particular, I want the definition to get these cases right, and I will report my own tentative verdicts. A person in dreamless sleep: zero subjects. A healthy adult reading while drinking coffee: one subject. Two healthy adults conversing: two subjects. A split-brain patient during the classic experiment in which the right hemisphere sees a word and the left hand draws the corresponding object while the speaking hemisphere reports having seen nothing: I am torn between saying there are two subjects and saying there is one subject with disunified contents, and I want the definition to force a choice rather than allow both descriptions. The same patient a minute later, walking down a corridor and chatting: one subject. A split-brain patient whose hands are in visible conflict, one hand buttoning a shirt while the other unbuttons it, while the speaking hemisphere offers a sincere confabulated explanation: I genuinely cannot decide. A person producing automatic writing under hypnosis who reports no awareness of what the hand is writing: I lean toward one subject, but weakly. A partially callosotomized patient who shows no conflict in ordinary life but subtle failures of integration on precise timing tasks: I lean strongly toward one subject.

The principle I have been toying with is that a set of mental states belongs to one subject just in case those states are jointly available to a single system of reasoning, report, and voluntary control within the same moment. That yields the obvious verdicts immediately, but it worries me in the hypnosis case and possibly in the split-brain conflict case, and I can already see it will need qualifications. I also want the definition to pronounce on a case I have not seen discussed: suppose two hemispheres could communicate only through a slow, effortful external text channel, so that each side eventually learns what the other experienced but there is never a moment at which the relevant information is jointly present in a single working memory. Is that one subject, two subjects, or some third thing? Please propose the definition, run it through every case including that last one, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, revise it or defend the surprising verdicts, and tell me which of my own verdicts I should abandon.
