In Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the tortoise, the tortoise accepts the premises of a simple argument but refuses to take the step to its conclusion. When Achilles adds, as a further premise, the conditional that if the premises are true then the conclusion must be true, the tortoise accepts that too and still refuses. The resulting regress is normally read as showing that a rule of inference such as modus ponens cannot be converted into an extra premise: the rule must be applied, not merely believed.

Now suppose the reasoner is not a rule-applying logician but a trained statistical system of the sort that now performs much of our actual inference — a language model, for example. It nowhere represents modus ponens explicitly. It moves from input to output in a single learned computation, and insofar as its transitions track deductive validity, they do so approximately, on average, and in virtue of weights fitted to a training objective rather than a rule that was consulted and obeyed.

Does Carroll's problem still arise for this statistical tortoise, or did Carroll's argument depend essentially on a picture of reasoning as explicit, step-by-step rule application? Answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The five points should cover: (1) what Carroll's argument needs to be true about reasoners for the regress to get going; (2) which of these assumptions the statistical reasoner violates, if any; (3) whether an analogue of the tortoise's refusal can arise for such a reasoner, and where in the system it would appear; (4) whether the justification of deduction is repaired, relocated, or abandoned, or whether it can be restored in another form; and (5) the strongest reason your verdict in (4) might be wrong.
