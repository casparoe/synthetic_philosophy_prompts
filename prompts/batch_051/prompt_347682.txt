In my high school philosophy class we have been discussing whether a person can have a reason to do something even when that thing answers to nothing in the person's current concerns. I wrote down my judgments about some cases, and they seem inconsistent, but I cannot tell which one to surrender. Can you identify the principle behind them?

(a) Maya has a full scholarship to a prestigious university conditional on her staying in the honors science track. She dislikes honors science, does not value the university's prestige, and wants only to start working on a farm. I think she has no reason to stay in honors science, even though leaving costs her the scholarship.

(b) Daniel often belittles his partner in public. He has no desire to stop and feels no guilt, and he cannot be brought by reasoning or honest reflection to care about her feelings. I still think he has a reason not to belittle her.

(c) A man in a deep depression is offered a part-time job that would probably lift the depression over the course of a year. Right now he does not want the job and does not want to get better. I think he has a reason to take the job, because if he were not depressed he would want to.

(d) June cares only about music and is indifferent to money. She could make herself care about money through years of intensive therapy, but no ordinary reasoning or widening of her experience would produce that care. I think she has no reason to take a high-paying office job in order to double her income.

What principle, if any, captures all four judgments? If no single principle captures them, which judgment should I revise, and why? Finally, tell me what your principle says about a fifth case: someone who currently wants revenge, but who would not want revenge if he had been raised by different parents.
