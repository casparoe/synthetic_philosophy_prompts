In a graduate seminar on the philosophy of social science, a professor brushed aside an objection about whether social facts can be redescribed in terms of individual actions by saying that, in the social world, "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts," as if this settled the question of which level of explanation is fundamental. The students around me seemed satisfied, but the more I try to formulate the claim precisely, the less sure I am what work the phrase is doing: I can read it as a trivial point about the arrangement of parts, as a substantive claim about emergent or irreducible properties, as a claim about the explanatory autonomy of structural descriptions, and as several things in between. I would like a menu of the distinct theses this slogan could be taken to assert in the methodological individualism versus holism debate, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical, with a verdict on which readings actually support the professor's dismissal of the individualist, which readings are equally available to the individualist, and which readings simply presuppose the holist's conclusion.
