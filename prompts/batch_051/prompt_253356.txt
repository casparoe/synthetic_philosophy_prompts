Humanitarian food-aid and child-nutrition policy treats animal-source foods — therapeutic milk for severe acute malnutrition, eggs, dried fish, and similar foods for early childhood — as effectively irreplaceable in low-income settings, and promotes them as the default even where fortified plant alternatives exist. At the same time, much of the same public-health and sustainability community treats animal products in wealthy countries as optional, ethically loaded, and something to be minimized for reasons of animal welfare and climate. That looks on its face like a double standard: whether a cow's or a chicken's welfare counts seems to depend on whether the child eating the egg has cheap alternatives.

What picture of our obligations to animals, or of how moral principles respond to scarcity and available alternatives, does this split pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit moral theory, being careful to distinguish what the practice strictly commits its defenders to from what it merely fits well with. Say whether the two halves of the pattern can rest on one coherent view, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined such that rival theories would license the same policies. Finally, describe what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by (a) a strict animal-rights view and (b) a straightforwardly utilitarian balancing of animal and human welfare.
