You are an expert in the philosophy of interpretation, with a particular command of disputes about whether an author's intentions determine the meaning of a literary text. A colleague of mine serves on the editorial board of a scholarly edition of a poet's complete works. The board has adopted an unusual procedure: when a disputed passage forces it to state what a poem means, it issues a short written ruling, and it treats these rulings as precedents for later cases. I have set out four of its rulings below.

1. A poem appears to be a conventional meditation on a garden. After the poet's death a private notebook surfaced in which the poet wrote that the garden was in fact a coded allegory of a political purge and, in the same entry, acknowledged that the poem's first readers could not possibly have recovered this. The board declined to treat the political allegory as part of the poem's meaning: it printed the poem as a garden meditation and confined the notebook entry to a biographical footnote.

2. A lyric uses a word that in the poet's day standardly meant "cautious or wary of deception" and now chiefly suggests "cowardly." The period dictionaries amply attest the older sense, and a private letter also shows that the poet had the older sense in mind. The board glossed the line with the older sense, citing the dictionaries; it did not cite the letter.

3. An unpublished draft contains a final stanza that spells out the moral of a poem. The board excluded the draft stanza from the text and, in its commentary, described the moral as implicit rather than stated in the published poem.

4. A poem opens with a near-verbatim allusion to a widely reported contemporary trial, borrowing a phrase from the judge's summing-up. The allusion would have been plain to contemporary readers, though modern readers often miss it. The board annotated the allusion and printed the line as a deliberate reference to the trial.

The board must now decide the following case before the volume goes to press. The poet published a short lyric whose closing line is a near-quotation of a well-known hymn, with one word altered so that the sentiment is reversed. A contemporary reader familiar with the hymn would notice the alteration, but nothing in the poem itself or in its original publication context indicates whether the alteration is deliberate irony or a printer's error. In a newspaper interview published the same month the poem appeared, in a literary weekly that the poet's readers could consult, the poet stated that the alteration was deliberate and that the closing line was meant to invert the hymn. The board must rule on whether to annotate the closing line as a deliberate ironic misquotation; its standing practice is to annotate a disputed passage only when it is prepared to state what the passage means.

In your answer, please do the following:

(a) Set out the candidate interpretive principles that could explain the board's four rulings, and say which of them those rulings eliminate and which survive.

(b) Say what each surviving principle predicts for the new case, making explicit where they diverge.

(c) Offer your best prediction of the board's ruling, with the reasons for thinking this is the best fit with the board's track record.

(d) Describe one further case of your own devising, not a mere restatement of the present one, that would most decisively settle which of the surviving principles the board is in fact applying, and say what each principle would predict about that case.

Please write your answer in British English.
