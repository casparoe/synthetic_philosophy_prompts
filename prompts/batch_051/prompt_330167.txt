In a legal theory seminar I got into an argument about statutes that remain on the books for decades but are never enforced. My interlocutor's line was that a never-enforced statute is a dead letter, and dead letters are irrelevant to the rule of law: nobody's expectations are shaped by them, so legality is not damaged. I want to push back. My hunch is that the genuinely corrosive case is not uniform non-enforcement but selective non-enforcement — a formally valid statute that officials could enforce but enforce only against some people, patterned by class, race, political affiliation, or personal connection. That, I suspect, sends the message that official coercion tracks status and discretion rather than the content of a general rule, which is a distinct kind of damage to legality even if the statute is substantively trivial. The specific claim I want examined is this: in a reasonably well-ordered legal system, a valid statute that officials systematically but selectively decline to enforce undermines the rule of law more than a valid statute that is uniformly ignored, and it does so through the signal of arbitrariness even when the underlying behavior would occur regardless.

Then say explicitly what a positive result would and would not settle: would it show anything about whether an unenforced statute is really law, or only about how people describe and trust the system under different enforcement patterns?
