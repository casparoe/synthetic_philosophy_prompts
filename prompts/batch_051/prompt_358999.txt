I am a retired natural scientist, recently drawn into the philosophy of criminal law, and I have been invited to present a short paper to a workshop whose regulars are criminal law theorists and moral philosophers working on coercion, threats, and the moral limits of the criminal law. One or two are sympathetic to law-and-economics, but the dominant sensibility is that the criminal law should track moral wrongdoing rather than efficiency, and they will insist that any account of blackmail's wrongness identify a chargeable act rather than merely a bad relationship or character. My thesis is this: the paradox of blackmail is an artifact of treating a conditional threat as a mere conjunction of two independently permissible acts, and it dissolves once blackmail is understood as a distinctive wrong of discretionary domination — the blackmailer makes the exercise of a power he is at liberty to exercise over the victim's social standing conditional on payment, exploiting the victim's vulnerability in a way that neither disclosure nor a request for money, taken alone, does. Prepare a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion period. Give me the objections I should expect, ordered by how damaging they are, each phrased the way this audience would phrase it, with an indication of which camp it comes from, a two-minute reply, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front to keep the room from getting stuck on the standard formulation, and include the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
