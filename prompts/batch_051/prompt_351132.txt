Since the 1990s, most affluent democracies have rebuilt their working-age benefits around a single idea: support is conditional. A claimant who wants cash assistance must produce evidence of looking for work, attend advisory meetings, accept training or work placements, and can have payments reduced or stopped for noncompliance, while old-age and disability benefits are paid as entitlements with nothing like these activity tests. The official rationale is that conditionality separates the genuinely needy from people who could work but will not — the most recent version of the old distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor. As a journalist who covers social policy and the ideas behind it, I want to know what that distinction actually commits us to. What philosophy of human agency, desert, and the causes of joblessness does the practice presuppose? Which rival views — for example, the view that unemployment is substantially a matter of luck and labor-market structure — does it quietly rule out? And is the practice internally coherent, given that the same systems often prosecute relatively small benefit fraud harshly while treating comparable sums lost through official error as a recovery problem rather than a crime? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as the practice allows, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, flag any tensions among its commitments, and say what would have to change in the administration of benefits if that theory were replaced by a luck-based account of economic outcomes.
