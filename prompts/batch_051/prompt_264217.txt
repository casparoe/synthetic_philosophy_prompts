In my metaphysics seminar last week we got stuck on causation by omission—the familiar case where the gardener doesn't water the plant and the plant dies—and one of the sharper students kept leaning on an analogy to defuse the standard objection. The objection, as everyone in the room knew, is that an omission is not an event or a happening; there is nothing there to do the causing. His reply: a hole in a bucket is also not a positive thing—its just the absence of bucket-material where the material ought to be—and yet we unhesitatingly say the hole caused the leak. So absence can be causally efficacious, and an omission is just a hole of a different kind. He wants to conclude that omissions cause their outcomes in exactly the same way, and that philosophers who balk at omission-causation are imposing a standard they never apply to holes.

I would like this analogy audited rather than just admired or dismissed. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the hole, the surrounding bucket, the water, the leak, maybe the shape and edges of the hole. Then show where the mapping breaks. I can already see candidate stress points: a hole is localized and has a shape, while an omission's boundaries and duration are fuzzy; a hole's causal work might be absorbed entirely by the positive physical surroundings (the edges, the material arrangement), which would mean the absence itself isn't doing anything; and which omission gets cited as the cause seems shot through with our expectations and norms in a way that which hole leaks does not. But I want you to separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the mere surface differences. At the end, say whether the analogy can support the claim that omissions are genuine causes, or only that we are entitled to a convenient way of talking—and tell me whether there is a better analogy that does the work without smuggling in the conclusion. I'm not after a reading list; I want to know how far the argument itself actually goes.
