I find myself structurally convinced by a principled universalist account of corruption. On this account, the wrong-making feature of bribery, vote-buying, nepotistic hiring and clientelistic distribution is the same everywhere: they convert a public office or a public resource into an instrument of a private, reciprocal relationship, and thereby replace rule-governed, impersonal treatment of citizens with particularistic favour. The account is attractive because it is simple, unified, and yields definite verdicts in hard cases: no identifiable quid pro quo need be shown; it is enough that a benefit is channelled through a relationship that an officeholder is supposed to keep out of the decision.

Yet I have a firm intuition that some things this account classifies as corruption are not corruption, or at least not the same wrong. Consider a councillor in a poor district who uses a modest discretionary fund to pay a constituent's emergency medical bill, asks for nothing, but expects—without demanding—support at the next election; a businessperson who gives an official a small customary gift at a festival, in a setting where refusing it would be a conspicuous insult and no identifiable decision is pending; a community leader who steers a public-works post to a neighbour whose family helped them through a previous crisis, in a locality where formal social insurance is thin and mutual obligation is the main safety net. In their settings these look less like the abuse of office than like the ordinary wiring of human cooperation—sometimes the only wiring available.

I am not asking you to tell me which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of 'corruption'—or of 'universal'—on which both survive. Please address the debunking move in both directions: that my contextualist intuition is the theory of the powerful, rebranding dependency as culture; and that the universalist theory is a particular administrative ideal—perhaps just the codified standards of one region's institutions—dressed up as neutral principle. Give me the strongest version of each position, and then a negotiated verdict. Please write the response in British English.
