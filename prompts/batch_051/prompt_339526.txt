I'm a journalist working on a piece about how biography teaches moral judgment, and I keep getting stuck on the opening of Plutarch's Life of Alexander. The gist of the passage is this: he says he isn't writing a straight history but something more like a portrait of a life. Then he claims that great public deeds, like battles, often reveal little about a person's virtue or character, while a small remark, a joke, or a fleeting expression can reveal much more. He compares the biographer to a painter who tries to capture the likeness in the face and especially the eyes, where character shows, and leaves the rest of the body to others. I understand this as a kind of methodological statement, but I'm not sure what the actual argument is. Why would small, private details be better evidence of moral character than large, public deeds? Is Plutarch saying that public actions are overdetermined by circumstance, or that people can perform great deeds for bad reasons? Is he just rationalizing the fact that his sources for Alexander were mostly anecdotes and gossip? And if the purpose of reading such lives is moral self-improvement, doesn't this approach risk producing a flattering moral portrait rather than an example you can actually learn from? I keep seeing Plutarch called an early hagiographer, and I can't tell whether that's supposed to mean something like "wisely attentive to character" or "willing to distort for edification."
