After forty years in the lab I thought I understood what it means for a system's future state to depend on what you do now, so when I retired and got into philosophy the Newcomb problem really got under my skin. In case you haven't run into it: a predictor with a 99 percent success rate has already either put a million dollars in an opaque box or left it empty, and you choose between taking only the opaque box or taking it plus a transparent box that visibly holds a thousand dollars. The predictor put the million in the opaque box if and only if it predicted you would take only that box. The two-box crowd says the money is already there or it isn't, so taking both boxes dominates taking one no matter what the predictor did. The one-box crowd says that when you take only the opaque box, you make it overwhelmingly likely the predictor put the million there, so one-boxing is the rational play.

I've settled, tentatively, on a thirdish view. The dominance argument for two-boxing seems to me to silently assume the predictor's act is simply fixed in the causal past, with no dependence on your choice. But a predictor that gets you right 99 percent of the time must be hooked into whatever causes your choice: your dispositions, how you deliberate, the kind of person you are, maybe the prediction itself influencing you. So which answer you get depends on which causal graph you're entitled to assume, and the standard story doesn't supply one. If your choice is modeled as an intervention, you cut the arrows from those common causes to your act and two-boxing pops out. If your choice is modeled as an observation of yourself, the correlation between act and prediction does real work and one-boxing pops out. Put bluntly: Newcomb's problem as usually told is underdetermined between two causal models, and the dominance argument is not a neutral argument for two-boxing, because it only works after you have already helped yourself to the model that one-boxers should reject.

What I'd like from you is an honest map of where this position is exposed. What would have to be true for the underdetermination claim itself to be false? Could someone show that the dominance argument goes through under any reasonable causal graph, or that only one of my two models is actually licensed by the setup? Which tweaks to the problem would be merely awkward for my view and which would be fatal, for example making the predictor's mechanism visible, or changing its accuracy, or making your own deliberation hidden from you? Does the view collapse into something like evidential decision theory once the graphs are spelled out, or is it well insulated, and would being well insulated be a vice here? I think I would find it most persuasive if somebody could show me a version of the dominance argument that doesn't depend on treating the prediction as causally independent of the choice.
