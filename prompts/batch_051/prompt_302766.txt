I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I want help diagnosing a metaphilosophical debate. Please take on the role of a philosopher who works on the epistemology of philosophical intuitions and who knows the empirical literature on cross-cultural variation in intuitive judgments about philosophical scenarios; think through the issue step by step, and start by separating the questions that this debate tends to run together.

The debate I have in mind has been running for roughly two decades and, to me, has not moved much. Critics of armchair method claim that intuitions about canonical cases concerning reference, knowledge, free will, and intentional action vary systematically across cultures and social groups, and that this undermines the practice of taking a philosopher's own intuition as evidence about the target concept or about what is possible or necessary. Defenders reply that much observed variation dissolves once translation, comprehension, and pragmatic features of the tasks are controlled; that a stable core of shared judgments remains; or that the critique attacks a straw man, because professional philosophers do not naively consult untutored folk intuitions but employ theory-laden, discipline-trained judgments in reflective equilibrium. Each side treats the other as missing an obvious point, and new studies get absorbed without resolving anything.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of findings. Specifically: is this best understood as a factual disagreement about how much, how deep, and how robust the variation is; a methodological disagreement about what established variation would show; a metaphilosophical disagreement about whether intuitions function as evidence, as explanatory starting points, or as probes for eliciting concepts; or some combination? My working hypothesis is that the two sides are largely talking past each other because they assign intuitions different epistemic roles, so no empirical result about variation could by itself settle the matter, and the apparent single debate is really two debates, one empirical and one normative. Please assess that hypothesis honestly, say where it is right and where it is too coarse, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis—whether a sharper taxonomy of intuition-roles, a study designed to satisfy both sides' criteria, a formal reconstruction, or a reason to regard the original question as ill-posed.
