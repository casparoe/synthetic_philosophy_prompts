I recently served as a juror in a civil suit in which the judge instructed us that the plaintiff could recover only if the defendant’s conduct was both a but-for cause of the injury and a proximate cause of it. The two instructions sat uneasily together: the first asked us to do a counterfactual comparison, while the second asked us to judge whether the chain of events was sufficiently direct and foreseeable. The court called this single element “causation,” yet it handed us two tests that seem to embody different ideas. Since then I have been reading around the subject, and Bertrand Russell’s 1912 essay On the Notion of Cause has unsettled me further: he argues that the mature sciences have no use for the word “cause” and that philosophers have kept it alive through confusion and habit.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of causation this legal practice presupposes, if any, and then assess it. Does the practice presuppose that causation is a real relation that the but-for and proximate tests are two imperfect windows onto? Does it presuppose that causal language in law is really an instrument for assigning responsibility under constraints of policy and proof? Is it perhaps consistent with several rival metaphysical views, and therefore underdetermined? Then tell me what would have to change in tort law if Russell’s eliminativist view were taken seriously: would the but-for test survive, or would both tests need to be replaced by explicit policy judgments about the assignment of liability? If you think I have conflated distinct issues, or that the practice presupposes no single theory and my request is misposed, say so plainly rather than forcing an answer.
