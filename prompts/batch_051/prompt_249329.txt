The anonymous response reads as follows: Insects would deserve direct moral consideration only if they are sentient, so the key empirical question is whether an insect can feel pain. Nociception—the capacity to detect and respond to noxious stimuli—is widespread among insects, but nociception is not the same as pain, since a creature can have nociceptors and withdrawal reflexes without any conscious experience of pain. Behavioral avoidance of harmful stimuli is similarly inconclusive, because such behavior may be purely reflexive. Still, given the complexity of some insect behavior, we should assign at least a non-negligible probability to the hypothesis that insects are sentient. If insects are even weakly sentient, a utilitarian cannot simply ignore their suffering, and because the number of individuals affected by insecticide use is so enormous, even a very small per-insect disvalue could aggregate into a very large amount of expected disvalue, which counts against routine use. A rights-based view would go further: if a creature has any subjective experience at all, it is a subject of a life, and killing it as a means to agricultural efficiency is impermissible. But deontologists generally restrict rights to rational beings, so on the standard Kantian picture insects are simply means, and Kantian considerations give us little reason to refrain from using them. The most promising overall response is precautionary: because we cannot rule out insect sentience and the stakes are enormous, we should act as if insects can suffer, preferring non-insecticidal pest management or narrow-spectrum application over broadcast spraying, except in genuine public-health emergencies.
