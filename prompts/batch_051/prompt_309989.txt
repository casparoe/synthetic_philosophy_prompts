I keep coming back to a parallel I can't decide about. Arrow's impossibility theorem says, roughly, that when there are at least three options, no method of turning individual preference rankings into a single social ranking can satisfy all of a short list of conditions that each look individually indispensable: it should handle any pattern of preferences, respect unanimity, decide between two options using only information about those two options, and not let one person dictate the result. When I hold that list up against the design goals of a community karma system — the kind used on Reddit, Hacker News, or an old-school forum — I see what looks like the same problem. A karma system turns many users' heterogeneous judgments into one reputation score or ranking, and its designers seem to want the same four things: it should work for any pattern of votes, respect the case where everyone rates one user above another, not make a user's standing depend on irrelevant third parties, and not let a single brigading account determine someone's reputation.

Is this a real identity of structure, or am I just pattern-matching on the word aggregation? I can already see one obvious disanalogy: Arrow's theorem is about ordinal rankings, while karma systems usually take cardinal inputs such as upvotes or star ratings, and simple averaging of cardinal scores may sail straight past the impossibility. But I am not sure how deep that disanalogy goes, because the moment the system is used to rank posts or users rather than merely score them one at a time, ordinal structure seems to sneak back in.

I would like a precise verdict, not a general impression. State the mapping in full, say what would have to be true on both sides for impossibility or manipulation results to transfer, and tell me whether anything does transfer — for example, the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, which says roughly that with at least three alternatives and unrestricted preferences any strategy-proof social choice rule is dictatorial, and which would suggest that any non-dictatorial reputation rule is open to strategic manipulation. And name the thing this parallel predicts that turns out false. If the analogy is only superficial, I want to know exactly where the two structures come apart, and whether a more careful version of the parallel can be salvaged.
