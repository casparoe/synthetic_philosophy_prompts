My lab director keeps a running log of cases in which our group had to decide whether a new measurement discriminated between two competing models. For each case she records the state of play before the data arrived, the state of play afterward, and her one-line verdict. These are the four most recent entries (the model labels are mine, not hers).

1. Model A predicted in advance a specific value for a decay rate, using only auxiliary assumptions that had been independently calibrated in earlier, unrelated experiments. Model B had predicted no such channel; after the result, B accounted for it by adding an interaction whose only motivation was the result itself. Her verdict: the result strongly favored A.

2. Model C and Model D each predicted only that a quantity would fall somewhere in a broad interval; the observed value fell inside both intervals. Her verdict: no discrimination.

3. Model E predicted a specific value from independently supported auxiliaries; Model F made no quantitative prediction and offered no natural post hoc account of the observed value. Her verdict: the result strongly favored E.

4. Models G and H were both fitted to an old data set before the new measurements arrived; each then predicted the new values using free parameters chosen only to improve the fit. Her verdict: no discrimination.

The new case is this. Before the relevant data were collected, the defenders of Model P fixed an adjustable parameter at a value with no independent support; the parameter was flexible enough that, by fixing it differently, the model could have been made to predict any of a wide range of possible outcomes. They then used the model with that fixed parameter to predict the observed value, and the prediction was correct. Model Q made no advance prediction, but after the result appeared, Q's defenders showed that the result follows from Q together with an auxiliary mechanism that had already been well established in unrelated experiments. P and Q cannot both be correct.

Given only her log, what verdict should the director give on the new case if she is to remain consistent with her past judgments? Identify the rule or rules that fit her past cases—candidate rules include a temporal-novelty rule (results favor whichever model predicted them before the data arrived; post hoc accommodation earns no credit) and an independent-motivation rule (results favor whichever model's fit rests on assumptions with support independent of the data, while assumptions adopted solely to fit the data earn no credit even when adopted in advance)—and say which of these rules or refinements of them diverge on the new case, and what each predicts. State which earlier case is the closest analog and why. Then give your best prediction of her verdict, with the reasons for preferring the underlying rule, and propose a further case that would settle which rule she has actually been running.
