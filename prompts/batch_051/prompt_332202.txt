Kant's little 1784 essay An Answer to the Question: What is Enlightenment? looks like a declaration of free inquiry: early on he more or less says that the public use of ones reason must always be free, and that this alone can bring about enlightenment among mankind. But then in the same essay he says the private use of reason may often be pretty narrowly restricted without any real harm to enlightenment, and he runs through the examples: taxpayers pay, officers obey, pastors teach the creed their church gives them, while each of them, writing as a scholar for the reading public, can criticize the very taxes, orders, or doctrines he serves. (And note his public/private labels do strange work: for him private use is the use you make in a civil post, while public use is the scholar writing to the world.) Is this a contradiction, a division of labor, a change of mind, or a distinction between two senses of useing one's reason that he never spells out? Which reconciliation survives the text and which one is special pleading? And does the essay end up buying free thought at the price of obedience—in which case is it a defense of the Enlightenment or evidence for the charge that the Enlightenment was always about discipline dressed up as reason?
