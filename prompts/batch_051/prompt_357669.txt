I've got a half-baked idea in pragmatics I want a candid read on. The standard picture, roughly Lewis/Stalnaker, says conversation runs on a kind of scoreboard: assertions get added to common ground, and presuppositions get quietly "accommodated" unless someone objects. So if you say "I'll pick my sister up at the airport" and I didn't know you had a sister, I can either accept "you have a sister" into the common ground at no cost, or pipe up with "wait, you have a sister?" So far so good.

Here's my itch. I think accommodation has two phases, and the usual scorekeeping story only captures the first. When a presupposition is freshly accommodated, it's cheap to reject — a simple "hold on, is that true?" does it. But once the conversation starts leaning weight on it — later moves that only make sense if the presupposition is true — the presupposition "sets." After that, rejecting it isn't the same act anymore. You now have to account for your earlier silence: why didn't you object when it was first slipped in? The load-bearing use has quietly upgraded the presupposition's epistemic standing, not because anyone argued for it, but because too many subsequent moves rest on it.

I've been calling this floorboard content: stuff that enters as background, gets built on, and is afterward protected by something like estoppel — call it conversational estoppel. A working example: in a comittee meeting someone says "Once the new funding model kicks in, the associateship line is stable." The presupposition that it will kick in gets accommodated. Two speakers later, someone else says "Right — and under the new model we should advertise the line in the spring and run the satelite workshop in the fall." Now if you want to challenge whether the funding model is actually coming, you have to unmake the intervening planning, and you owe an account of why you sat quiet. My claim is that this is a difference in kind: common ground has two tiers, and floorboard content is protected in a way ordinary accommodated presuppositions are not.

What I want: is this a real distinction or just a metaphor for social awkwardness about speaking up late? Am I reinventing something that's already been worked out (my guess is yes, somewhere in the scorekeeping literature, but I can't place it)? Most importantly, where does it break — cases where heavy use doesn't make a presupposition harder to retract, or where the right response is exactly to retract it and the "estoppel" is just a bad norm? And if I've got the mechanics of accommodation wrong, or "two tiers" is doing no work, tell me straight instead of humoring me.
