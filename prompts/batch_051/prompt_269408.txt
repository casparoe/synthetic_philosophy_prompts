It's been over a decade since the Snowden stuff came out, but I keep coming back to it. Just so we're on the same page about the facts: in 2013 Edward Snowden leaked a pile of classified documents showing the NSA was vacuuming up phone metadata in bulk, and that tech companies were being quietly compelled to pipe user data to the government under secret FISA court orders, all justified as counterterrorism. I want you to analyze that episode through the lens of republican liberty, by which I mean the non-domination idea: you're unfree when you live under someone else's arbitrary power even if they never actually use it, the way a slave with a kind master is still unfree. That's distinct from negative liberty, where the wrong is actual interference, and from positive liberty, where the wrong is lacking the capacity to steer your own life.

Run it in both directions. Direction one: through the lens, what exactly was the wrong? Does a program that collects metadata on everyone but reads almost none of it count as domination? Is the power still arbitrary when a statute and a secret court technically authorize it? Who's dominating whom — is the right model government-over-citizens, or government-and-platforms-over-everyone, or something else? Direction two: does the episode actually fit the theory, or did it expose weak spots? I'm especially curious about the chilling effect on people who were never interfered with in any concrete way, and about whether a capacity for algorithmic search that no human ever points at most people still counts as the sort of power the theory cares about. Then tell me what would have to change for the arrangement to count as non-dominating, and whether that's even realistic when the watchers get to decide in secret what there own rules mean. Give me the strongest version a republican-liberty person would actually sign up for, and then show me where it still leaks.
