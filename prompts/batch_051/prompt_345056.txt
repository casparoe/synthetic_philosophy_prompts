I've been rereading Clifford's The Ethics of Belief and James's The Will to Believe and keep going in circles on this. Clifford, in the shipowner case from the first section, writes: "He had acquired his belief not by honestly earning it in patient investigation, but by stifling his doubts. And although in the end he may have felt so sure about it that he could not think otherwise, yet inasmuch as he had knowingly and willingly worked himself into that frame of mind, he must be held responsible for it." James, in the opening of The Will to Believe, writes: "I have brought with me to-night something very like a sermon on justification by faith to read to you—I mean an essay in justification of faith, a defense of our right willfully to adopt a believing attitude in religious matters, in spite of the fact that our merely logical intellect may not have been coerced." The obvious reconciliation is that James thinks Clifford is right about ordinary cases like the shipowner but not about religious cases where the option is live, forced, and momentous. But I don't see how that gets James out of it. If the shipowner's belief that the ship is sound is a live hypothesis for him, and sailing vs. not sailing is forced and momentous in some sense, then Clifford's case seems to meet James's own criteria for passional decision. If it doesn't meet them, then James has to say religious believers are allowed to do something the shipowner isn't, but on what principle? I'm looking for candidate reconciliations and a verdict on which ones actually work rather than just relabel the cases.
