Since retiring from a research career in physics, I have been attending a philosophy of science reading group, and I recently found myself in a disagreement I cannot resolve. A younger participant dismissed the suggestion that chemistry and biology are in principle reducible to fundamental physics by invoking the slogan “More is Different.” When I pressed him on it, he said the slogan shows that the behavior of a large collection of particles cannot be derived from the laws governing the individual particles, and that any project of explaining higher-level phenomena in terms of quantum mechanics is therefore wrongheaded. I heard this slogan throughout my career—usually deployed to defend condensed matter physics against the condescension of particle physicists—but I realize I never examined what it is actually asserting. It seems to me to run from the uncontroversial observation that an aggregate has properties its parts lack, through more ambitious claims about the failure of derivability and the emergence of genuinely new laws at each scale, all the way to strong theses about the autonomy of the special sciences.

Could you lay out, as a list, the distinct precise theses that “More is Different” could be expressing, indicate which are trivially true, which are substantive and defensible, and which are probably false or overstated, and then say which of these readings, if any, would actually license the conclusion that reduction to fundamental physics is misguided? I am comfortable with renormalization group arguments and effective field theories, so feel free to use those as standards for sorting the readings.
