So I got volunteered to run the final session of our philosophy of science reading group, which spent the term on laws of nature, and the two most active members have been argueing past each other for weeks. One's a Humean: laws are just the theorems of the best systematization of the whole mosaic of particular facts, roughly Lewisian, so there's nothing over and above the regularities. The other's an anti-Humean in the Armstrong/Maudlin vein: laws are extra, governing facts that make the regularities hold and explain why they keep holding. I'd rather the session end with something both could put their name to instead of a reheated debate.

Please draft the adversarial-collaboration statement they'd produce after a good-faith attempt. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Include: (i) claims both can sign, about what laws do in explanation and prediction, how laws differ from accidentally true generalizations, and how actual physics uses laws; (ii) the precise disagreement, with each side's view worded so that they'd accept the characterization of their own position; (iii) the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if answered, would actually move one of them. From the notes I've got, the cruxes probably include: whether the best system is unique enough to ground a real law/accident distinction; how the anti-Humean handles the inference problem (if laws are extra facts, how do we get epistemic access to them from finite data); what to do about ceteris paribus and special-science laws; and whether the "swapped quiddity" scenario — two apparently distinct fundamental properties with identical nomic roles, differing only in some hidden identity — is a genuine possibility or empty. For each crux, give the concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts it will show. If you think part of their fight is purely verbal, say which part and why. And try to keep the agreed claims substantive rather than thinning them down until they're trivial; that's the hard part.
