Ok so I've been re-reading the no-trade theorem stuff and I think I've gotten myself into a weird corner. The way people state it is: two rational Bayesians with common priors, common knowledge of each other's rationality, and only private information separating them won't make a purely informational trade, because each would realize the other's willingness to trade is itself information. But actual markets are just huge constant flows of trade. So does the theorem commit me to saying that most of the people on the other side of my VOO purchase are being irrational? More precisely, should I treat every voluntary trade as proof that at least one party is making a mistake, and if I'm the less informed party, should I basically never trade, since the counterparty's offer is them saying 'I know something you don't'? But then how does any market maker stay in business, and why wouldn't the same logic freeze all trade between the market makers themselves? I have a nagging sense I'm conflating something—maybe the theorem just doesn't apply to trades that aren't purely about information, or maybe real markets just don't satisfy common priors—but I can't tell which premise is doing the work or whether I should actually update hard on every bid-ask spread I cross. Also its weird to me that the same argument would seem to make liquidity provision look like a giant epistemic error, which can't be right.
