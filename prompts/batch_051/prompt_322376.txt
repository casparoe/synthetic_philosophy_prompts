Hey — I'm a PhD student working on medieval ethics, specifically on Abelard's *Ethics* (You might know it as *Scito te ipsum*, sometimes just called the *Ethica*). I need something for a class I'm helping teach, and I want it to be a bit off the beaten path from the standard "sin is in the consent, not the act" gloss.

One thing I keep getting hung up on: Abelard has this running view that vice (a habitus, a disposition) is not yet sin; sin is only in consent (consensus) — specifically consent to what one takes to be illicit. The act itself adds nothing. But he complicates this with a bunch of examples that are way less clean than people sometimes pretend. The one I keep thinking about is the servant who kills his master because he's terrified for his own life — Abelard says there's a sense in which he "consented" to the killing but not to murder, because he didn't consent to doing what he took to be illicit. The consent is real but it's a consent to an action under a description that doesn't include "doing wrong." Or something like that. Actually I can't remember the exact wording and I don't want to overquote him from memory. But the example has always stuck with me.

So here's what I'd like: a mock committee report from a (fictional) medieval monastic disciplinary tribunal — a kind of "case review" — in which they're trying to apply Abelard's consent-based framework to a bunch of monks' confessions. Make it deadpan, formal, and slightly bureaucratic. The humor should come from the fact that these monks are absolutely terrible at Abelardian casuistry: they're either anxious scrupulosity cases, or they're gaming the system by carefully describing what they consented to. The committee itself should be impeccably Abelardian — no real vice is assigned, sin is in consent, the act is morally indifferent, etc. — but the cases should keep pushing that framework into absurd corners: someone who consented to a sin but reports that the act was carried out by someone else; someone who tried really hard to consent to a serious sin but couldn't manage to take it seriously; someone who consented to what was actually a sin, but because they couldn't identify it under a relevant description, the committee has a mid-report crisis about whether it counts. A reader should come away having a decent grip on Abelard's view, but they should also feel how strained it gets when you use it to run a disciplinary institution.

Keep it under 700 words. And no "winking" — the committee should be totally straight-faced, like they're just working through the logic, even as it gets more ridiculous. Maybe there's a final case that resolves surprisingly cleanly, and that's the one nobody on the committee wants to discuss.

Oh, and don't worry about historical period precision or citations — it's for a teaching exercise, not a paper. If you want to make up plausible names for monks, go for it. Just keep the theological details consistent with Abelard.
