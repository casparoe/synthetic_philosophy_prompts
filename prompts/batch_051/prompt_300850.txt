so ive just finished jury duty — first criminal trial — and i cant stop thinking about a thing the judge said before we got the case. she told us the prosecution had to prove this defendant did it, not that people like him tend to do this kind of thing, and that we couldnt convict unless we were satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt. the trial ended up not turning on it, but it got me spinning on a question i havent been able to drop since.

i know the famous puzzles vaguely — the blue bus thing (a witness says a blue bus hit her car, 80% of the blue buses in town belong to one company, can you find that company liable?) and the gatecrasher thing (a thousand people at a rodeo and only four hundred paid, can you convict a particular attendee for not paying?). but i havent read the actual papers and i dont want a reading list. i want an honest assessment of my own idea.

ive been calling it the trace rule. here it is: a bare statistical fact can justify suspicion, and it can make other evidence look stronger, but it can never get you past beyond a reasonable doubt on its own, no matter how high the number. to convict you need at least one piece of evidence that is causally downstream of the specific defendant doing the specific act — an eyewitness, a fingerprint, a recording, a receipt with his name on it. a rate is about a class he falls into; a trace is about him. so the rule is: no conviction on rates alone.

this feels obviously right to me, which is exactly why i suspect its either already a thing in the literature or quietly broken. the dna thing bugs me: a random match probability is also a fact about a class (everyone who shares those markers), and courts happily let people be convicted when its dna plus almost nothing else. and im not sure my phrase "causally downstream" is doing real work — i tried to explain it to a friend and ended up saying "you know, like a trace," which is a circle.
