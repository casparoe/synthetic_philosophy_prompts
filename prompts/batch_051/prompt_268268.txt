The question I set was:

"Critics often dismiss evolutionary psychology as unfalsifiable 'just-so stories' — adaptive explanations that can be fitted to any observation after the fact. Assess this criticism. Your answer should explain what the environment of evolutionary adaptedness (EEA) is and use at least one example."

The student wrote:

"Evolutionary psychology is not just-so storytelling, at least not when it's done well. The field's central idea is that the mind contains adaptations shaped by the environment of evolutionary adaptedness, which is usually identified with the Pleistocene savanna where our ancestors lived as hunter-gatherers. Because selection operated there but we now live in very different conditions, an adaptation can lower current fitness: our preference for sugar and fat was useful when calories were scarce but now causes obesity and diabetes. This lets the theory make predictions. From the savanna hypothesis you can predict that men should prefer younger mates and women should prefer high-status mates, since those preferences would have raised reproductive success in that environment. Large cross-cultural surveys of mate preferences have confirmed exactly these sex differences, so the adaptive explanation is proved. The 'just-so story' charge is therefore unfair: good evolutionary psychology proposes hypotheses that could have failed but didn't."
