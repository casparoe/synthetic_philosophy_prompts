We are far more willing to deploy a human clinician whose diagnostic judgment rests on tacit cues she cannot articulate than to deploy a machine-learning system of comparable accuracy that cannot explain its predictions in terms a clinician would recognize. Pressed to justify a diagnosis, the clinician may be able to say only that the scan looked wrong to her; the model, asked for its reasons, can produce a saliency map or a ranked list of input features, but neither is ordinarily treated as an explanation in the clinical sense. The clinician is treated as a skilled expert; the model is treated as too opaque to trust. This is an asymmetry in our practice: we demand legibility from machine reasoners that we do not, and often could not, demand from human ones.

I am attracted to a principle that would justify the asymmetry: we should train models to be understandable rather than merely correct, because legibility is a requirement of responsible deployment. But I can also see the suspicion that the principle is doing no independent work, and is instead rationalizing a prior distrust of unfamiliar systems. What, if anything, justifies the asymmetry? Candidate justifications include: that the human can be held accountable in ways the model cannot; that the human shares a world, a training history, and a common-sense background with her colleagues, and this changes the epistemic status of her inarticulate judgment; that human and machine error profiles differ in ways that make headline accuracy comparisons misleading; or, on the other side, that the asymmetry is mere bias in favor of the familiar and should if anything run the other way, since the model's internal states can at least be inspected across cases and improved systematically. Give me the strongest version of each candidate, test it against nearby cases, such as a human clinician dutifully following an unexplained institutional protocol, a model whose internals are inspectable but not human-comprehensible, and a human who outperforms the model while being just as inarticulate, and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
