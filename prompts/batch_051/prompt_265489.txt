I find myself pulled toward a Carnapian way of carving up political and economic argument, and it leaves me uneasy. The view, roughly, is that every debate about how to organize production and distribution — markets versus planning, who gets what, how risk and security are weighed — splits cleanly into two kinds of question. There are theoretical questions about what is the case: what a given institutional arrangement causally produces, how information flows or fails to flow through a planning board, what incentives a transfer scheme creates, who ends up better or worse off. These are truth-evaluable and, in principle, settleable by ordinary empirical inquiry. And there are practical questions about what ends to adopt: equality, security, autonomy, growth, stability, and the trade-offs among them. On the Carnapian picture, statements that answer practical questions — society ought to guarantee housing, the plan should prioritize the worst-off — are not true or false at all; they express attitudes, wishes, or decisions, not claims that could be known. The view has real virtues. It explains why the factual part of a planning dispute is tractable while the ultimate choice persists after all the facts are in. It avoids having to locate a special realm of normative facts. And it gives the sciences unchallenged authority over the part of political disagreement that can be called true or false, while making the rest an honest matter of what we collectively will.

But I also have a firm intuition that this cannot be the whole story. When a planning body considers a scheme whose cost savings depend on denying some group access to basic provision — a rationing rule or a housing allocation that quietly immiserates a vulnerable minority to make the system more efficient — the judgment that the scheme ought not be adopted does not feel to me like an expression of preference, on all fours with preferring one color scheme for a public building. I want to say the scheme is worse, that the harm to that group is a reason against it, and that a planner who cannot see this is not simply attached to different ends but is failing to reason correctly, even after every empirical question about the scheme's consequences has been settled. If the Carnapian picture is right, it seems I should debunk that conviction as the felt residue of having taken sides, a projection of my own attitudes onto the world. I do not want to be told in advance that the intuition is just a projection, nor that the theory is obviously self-defeating. I want to know what each option would cost.

So: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — for me to accept that the final move in a planning judgment is non-cognitive, and that its apparent rational force is an artifact? What would it take for the Carnapian picture to be amended or abandoned — for example, by allowing that practical statements can be truth-apt after all, or that even non-cognitive commitments can be held to rational standards of coherence, universality, or responsiveness to the interests of those affected? And is there a reading on which the clean bipartite picture and the rational force of the intuition both survive — perhaps Carnap only denied that ought-statements work like empirical claims, and never meant to deny that choices among ends can be better or worse reasoned? Carnap's own socialism and pacifism sharpen the problem: if his practical commitments were, at bottom, attitudes with no truth-evaluable content, in what sense were they anything more than arbitrary? Tell me what would have to be true for the theory to accommodate the intuition, what would have to be true for the intuition to be explained away, and whether the version of Carnap I have described is even the one worth defending.
