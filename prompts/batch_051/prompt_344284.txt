heres something I'm putting together for a global-justice seminar: a handout on what actually counts as corruption, once you get past the obvious bribe-in-an-envelope cases and into clientelism, vote-buying, gifts to officials, and the question of whether the Western ideal of the impartial public official is just a local norm.

I'd like a decision tree that takes someone from a concrete starting case to a named position (or 'unnamed' if nobody really defends it). The starting case: a local councillor reliably delivers paved roads and school supplies to her own village, and only to villages that turn out to support her at the ballot box; the goods stop flowing to any village that votes the other way. Depending who you ask, that's corruption, clientelism, constituent service, or just politics.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from labels: first the branching questions and what combinations of answers are even possible, then hang the positions on the leaves. Each branching question should be one a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to. I want these distinctions in there, as concrete questions where you can: whether a practice has to be illegal to count as corrupt; whether the defining wrong is a breach of an official's duty, damage to the public interest, or a violation of impartial treatment; whether what makes gift-giving corrupt is the reciprocal obligation it creates (as opposed to the unequal access it buys); and whether majority local acceptance settles the normative question or some universal standard survives. As leaves include the legalist view, the public-office view, the public-interest view, the impartialist/universalist view (clientelism as corrupt because it treats citizens unequally), a contextualist view (standards are local and Western categories misdescribe gift-based politics), and a functionalist view (classify by consequences, not by a fixed concept). Aim for about 8–12 leaves, with one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment.

Flag any two positions your tree can't really seperate, and note any answer combinations nobody occupies, with a quick guess at why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

After the tree, add a short note on which bits are settled enough to teach as background and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, so I don't accidentally present a contested position as the consensus.
