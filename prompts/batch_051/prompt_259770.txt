I keep running into Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument and I want to push past the responses I've already heard. For context, the argument is that if people freely pay a quarter each to watch Wilt play, then any patterned principle of distributive justice gets upset even though nobody complains about any individual transaction, so liberty and patterns are in tension. Here are the objections I already know, so please don't give me any of these or close variants of them:

- The starting-distribution objection: the argument assumes the initial holdings are just, but all actual holdings descend from histories of theft, conquest, and slavery, so the example never describes a real society.
- The "there is no liberty as such" objection: the voluntary exchanges only count as free against a background of enforced property rules, which themselves coerce everyone else; Nozick is just privileging the liberty of owners over the liberty of non-owners.
- The Cohen-style ethos point: the transfers upset the pattern only because the players choose to pay Wilt and Wilt chooses to keep the money; people with egalitarian motivations could reproduce the pattern voluntarily, so the argument shows something about preferences, not about liberty itself.
- The "patterns are a strawman" objection: most egalitarians don't demand a fixed end-state distribution; they want fair institutions, a social minimum, or non-domination, all of which are compatable with Wilt getting rich.
- The rectification objection: once past injustice is admitted, entitlement theory needs an unknowably complete history of just and unjust transfers and gives no determinate answer about which current holdings are legitimate.

As far as I can tell these are the standard moves. I want one more objection that isn't in that list and isn't just a rewording, special case, or combination of them. It can be something you've come across that's less standard, or something you work out yourself, but it should be a genuine challenge to the argument, not a nitpick. Then tell me honestly whether the objection is actually any good and how it relates to the ones above. Lead with the bottom line: state the new objection in your first sentence, then unpack it.
