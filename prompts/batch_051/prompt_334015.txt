I sit on the Conduct Committee of a national writers' union. I am a novelist by trade rather than a lawyer, which is part of why the committee asked me to prepare the written opinion in a complaint that has divided us badly. I need that document drafted so that it ends in a concrete disposition the committee can actually vote on, and so that it gives the rest of the committee a criterion they can use again, since we expect more cases like this.

The facts are agreed. At a festival panel last month on the city's literary identity, one of our senior members, a novelist of forty years' standing, said: "We keep handing the city's literary culture over to people who got here five minutes ago and don't know the place at all. And they are the ones now telling us what the city is, what its stories are, and who belongs in them." Four younger members, three of them writers of color, have filed a complaint. They do not claim the remark contains a slur. They claim it is a dog whistle: that "people who got here five minutes ago" and "who belongs" draw on a long nativist idiom about outsiders and belonging, and that the sentence as a whole told the audience, in code, that the city's literature belongs to writers like the speaker and not to them. Two of the complainants were in the audience and describe feeling, immediately, that the room had been told they did not count. A fifth member, who is white and was also present, has sent a note saying she heard nothing racial and took the remark to be about gentrification and the young graduates and remote workers flooding the city's housing market.

The speaker's defense is that the panel topic was place and memory, not race; that by "people who got here five minutes ago" he meant newcomers of every background; that he intended no racial meaning and has spent four decades publishing novels that treat the city's immigrant communities with evident sympathy; and that censuring him for a remark containing no racial term would punish him for meanings other people projected onto his words. Our code prohibits conduct that demeans or excludes members on the basis of race, but our bylaws also commit the union to defending writers' expressive freedom, and several committee members worry that we are being asked to discipline a member for an interpretation rather than a statement.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one of four dispositions: uphold the complaint and formally censure the speaker; dismiss the complaint; issue a public statement from the committee without individual sanction; or require the speaker to participate in a facilitated conversation with the complainants within a stated time. If you judge that the right disposition is a combination, or something other than these four, say so and say how it would work. The committee is not asking for a general essay on free expression; it is asking for an actionable decision. The opinion must state, in plain terms, the criterion by which it judges the remark to be or not to be racist for disciplinary purposes, without pretending the sentence's ambiguity away, and it must say clearly how much weight intent, effect, and evidence of coded meaning each carry. It must also present the strongest consideration on the losing side as fairly as if that side's advocate had drafted the paragraph.

Because the other committee members want working materials for discussion rather than one long essay, give the memo first, and then append a JSON object with the following schema:

- recommendation (string): the precise disposition, including any time frame, conditions, or follow-up vote.
- governing_criterion (string): a two-to-three-sentence test for when a statement counts as sanctionably racist under our code, explicitly stating the roles of intent, effect, and evidence of coded meaning.
- intent_weight (string): one of "decisive", "substantial", or "minor", followed by one sentence of justification.
- effect_weight (string): same format, for the statement's effects on the complainants and the wider membership.
- dog_whistle_evidence (object): with keys "for" and "against", each mapping to an array of strings listing the specific evidence in this case that supports or undercuts the dog-whistle reading.
- strongest_losing_side_consideration (string): the strongest argument for the disposition you did not recommend, stated at full strength.
- facts_to_check_before_vote (array of strings): any further facts the committee should verify before acting, or an empty array if none.
- precedent_risk (string): the chief risk this disposition creates for future cases, plus one sentence on how the opinion's criterion limits that risk.
