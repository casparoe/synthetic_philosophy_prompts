Analyze the 1983 Soviet nuclear false alarm through the lens of moral luck. Take the salient facts to be these: during a period of acute US–Soviet tension, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov, on duty at a Soviet early-warning command post, received an automated satellite warning that American missiles had been launched, concluded that the warning was a computer malfunction rather than a real attack, and did not relay it in the way that would have set the Soviet command chain on a path toward retaliation. Treat Petrov as the primary agent. First, reconstruct the decision problem he faced, including what relaying or withholding the alert would have meant in that institutional and strategic context. Second, map the episode onto the usual taxonomy of resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal luck as exactly as you can; where the mapping is strained, say where and why rather than forcing it. Third, run two counterfactuals: one in which the warning was real and Petrov's non-escalation allowed a first strike to land unchallenged, and one in which the malfunction was so obvious that any competent officer would have hesitated. Use those to say whether the corresponding differences in blame or credit — traitor, hero, unnoticed functionary — are what moral luck would predict or a distortion it should condemn. Fourth, close with a position on whether the later celebration of Petrov counts as evidence for or against the view that outcome luck should matter to moral judgment.
