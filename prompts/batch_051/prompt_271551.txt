Me and my friend argued all afternoon about whether Lakatos's criteria can actually classify anything at all. As I understand him, a research program has a hard core you don't test directly, a protective belt of auxillary assumptions that takes the hits, a positive heuristic that says roughly what to try next, and the program is progressive when changes to the belt predict novel facts that get confirmed, degenerating when the changes just get bolted on after the fact to explain anomalies. Fine. Now take the scaling-laws research program around large language models. The hard core is something like: past some size, more parameters, data, and compute give you smooth and fairly predictable improvements in general capability, mostly along power laws. The belt is the tinkering with architectures, data mixes, optimizers, evals, and the repeated claim that genuinely new abilities emerge at some scale. It does make some forward predictions (loss curves, a few downstream tasks), but a lot of the eval choices look like the goalposts moving after a model flubs a benchmark. Is this a progressive or a degenerating research program by Lakatos's standards? Go through the criteria one by one. Where it's genuinely mixed, say whether the progressive/degenerating distinction is doing real work here, or whether Lakatos just assumes a cleaner line between predicting novel facts and accommodating them afterward than this kind of field has. Also, does the field's dependence on empirical scaling count as theoretical growth at all, or is it a positive heuristic with no real theory behind it?
