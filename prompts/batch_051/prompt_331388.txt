In an environmental economics course I am taking, the textbook summarizes the dispute over the discount rate for climate damages roughly as follows.

Pure time preference, it says, is the economist's term for caring more about what happens sooner than about what happens later: a person who would rather receive a dollar today than a dollar next year, or who saves for retirement, displays a positive pure time preference. Ramsey argued that applying this attitude across generations is ethically unjustifiable, because it means valuing people born later as less important solely because of their date of birth. Even so, most climate-economy models discount future damages at rates in the vicinity of four to five percent, which the textbook illustrates by saying that a statistical life saved in 2100 is treated as worth almost nothing in present-value terms. The textbook then identifies this as the crux of the disagreement between Stern and Nordhaus: Stern used a near-zero pure time preference and concluded that aggressive emissions reductions are warranted now, whereas Nordhaus used rates inferred from observed market returns and concluded that only more moderate near-term action is justified. Finally, the textbook states that their disagreement is ultimately an empirical one about how fast consumption will grow.

I suspect the broad outline is right, but I have heard that the debate turns partly on distinct ethical assumptions that the textbook's last sentence may collapse into an empirical claim. I would like an accuracy audit of this summary. Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a substantive misrepresentation. In particular, how should one distinguish (a) pure time preference, (b) discounting future consumption because future people are expected to be richer, and (c) the interest rates observed in markets? And does the textbook's conclusion—that Stern and Nordhaus differ mainly about an empirical growth projection—survive once these distinctions are drawn? If a defender of Stern's approach and a defender of Nordhaus's approach each read this passage, what would each say had been attributed to them incorrectly?
