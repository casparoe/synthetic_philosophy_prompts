i keep coming back to Davidson's radical interpretation argument and trying to figure out whether it still bites. the setup: someone shows up speaking a language you have zero prior handle on — no dictionary, no bilingual informant, no shared history. Davidson's claim is that interpretation cant even get off the ground unless you assume the speaker is mostly coherent and mostly right about the obvious stuff in the shared environment, because beliefs and meanings are entangled: you can't fix what their words mean without assuming what they believe, and you can't fix what they believe without assuming what their words mean. the principle of charity is supposed to break that circle. so the argument is that interpretation only gets moving because you're forced to treat the interpretee as largely rational and largely in touch with the world.

now the development: large language models seem to pick up a working competence in dozens of languages from raw text alone, without ever being in a shared environment with anyone, without triangulating on objects, and without anything that looks like assuming speakers are mostly right. they learn patterns and correlations from corpora and produce translations and fluent continuations anyway. it's tempting to treat this as a counterexample to Davidson, but maybe it isnt interpretation in his sense at all — maybe it's a text-prediction feat that never assigns meanings or beliefs to anyone.

i want to know whether Davidson's argument survives this, and which premise is actually under pressure. is the premise that interpretation requires charity, or is it the implicit premise that linguistic competence just is interpretation? can the argument be repaired into something like: LLMs get fluency without semantics, and any system that actually got as far as meanings would still need charity? or does the LLM case show that charity was a piece of field-linguist method all along, not a transcendental condition on interpretation, and he overclaimed? give me the strongest version of the "it survives" and the "it's dead" positions, and then say which one you'd actually defend.
