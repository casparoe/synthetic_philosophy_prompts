I am an undergraduate reading for a joint honours degree in philosophy and computer science, and this term I am taking a philosophy of mathematics course. We have spent several weeks on the cluster of arguments standardly grouped as Benacerraf's dilemma. I am at ease with the vocabulary of programming-language semantics — denotational and operational semantics, semantic domains, values, reductions, type systems, inhabitation, full abstraction, and the Curry–Howard correspondence — but I find the epistemology in the papers hard to follow, and I want to see whether I can understand the dilemma more precisely by restating it in that vocabulary.

As I understand the dilemma, it has three elements. First, the standard semantics for mathematical language takes mathematical assertions at face value: a claim that there are infinitely many primes is true only if numbers exist, and numbers, sets, and functions are standardly regarded as abstract, causally inert, and outside space and time. Second, a plausible causal constraint on knowledge says that to know about objects a thinker must stand in some appropriate causal relation to them, or at least that causally isolated abstract objects are epistemically inaccessible. Third, these two commitments jointly imply that mathematical knowledge is impossible; the standard responses are to reject the face-value semantics, to weaken or reject the causal constraint, or to accept some form of skepticism about mathematics.

Please provide a systematic translation of the dilemma into the vocabulary of programming-language semantics, piece by piece, followed by a residue report. Go through at least the semantic premise, the causal constraint on knowledge, the skeptical conclusion, and the main dialectical forks. For each, say what translates cleanly into notions such as semantic values or denotations, operational equivalence, inhabitation, reduction rules, or full abstraction; what can be rendered only with distortion; and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. In the residue report, say which parts of the philosophical problem are genuinely missing from the programming-language framework, and which parts depend on assumptions the framework does not share — for example, a picture of reference or causation that a working semanticist might reasonably abstract away from. Be honest about whether each failure of translation is a deficiency of the target vocabulary or a sign that the original philosophical claim is less precise than it appears. Do not smuggle the target framework's assumptions into the translation and present them as if the dilemma already carried them; treat the residue as the main finding.
