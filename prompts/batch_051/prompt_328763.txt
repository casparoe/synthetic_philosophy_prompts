I am building a system for inferring human preferences from observed behaviour, in the spirit of inverse reinforcement learning, and I have found that my own unreflective inferences are far less tidy than the textbook treatment suggests. Going on gut, I notice that I treat superficially similar behaviour very differently. A colleague says she is trying to save money, yet she buys a £4 flat white every morning even though there is free coffee in the office: I infer that she prefers the flat white to the £4. A friend always takes the stairs to my fourth-floor flat when the lift is working: I infer that he prefers the exercise or strongly dislikes the lift. A commuter pays a £3 toll each morning to save ten minutes while insisting that the toll is a rip-off: I infer that she will trade £3 for ten minutes. A rat presses a lever whenever pressing has previously delivered food: I infer, without hesitation, that the rat wants the food. In all of these, the behaviour seems to reveal a preference directly.

But other cases pull the other way. A dinner guest leaves the last slice of cake: I do not infer that he dislikes cake — he may simply be being polite, and the preference I impute, if any, is for avoiding a social faux pas rather than against cake. A housemate never helps himself to my food although he says he likes it: I do not infer that he secretly dislikes it. A student sits through a seminar in silence: I do not infer that she is uninterested. When a colleague agrees instantly with whatever the manager proposes, I do not infer that he actually agrees; I infer at most that he wants the manager's approval. And here is the pair that bothers me most. One student spends every evening learning chess openings instead of revising for an exam he insists he cares about, and I infer that he really prefers chess to his course. Another student spends every evening on social media instead of revising for an exam he also insists he cares about, and I do not infer that he prefers social media to his course; I judge that he is failing to act on his real preference rather than revealing a different one. The two look formally alike, yet my verdicts differ, and I cannot say what feature is doing the work.

The tentative principle I keep testing against these cases is this: infer a preference for an outcome from behaviour when the person chose without coercion, knew the options, and the choice is best explained by that outcome. But I already suspect that it is too permissive in some places and too restrictive in others — in particular, it seems to ignore the difference between the person's beliefs about how actions map to outcomes and my own, and it says nothing about how one decides which features of an action are the object of the preference. Please test this principle against my cases, repair it as needed, and state the repaired principle precisely, indicating what each clause is doing and how it handles each case. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without strain or ad hoc qualification, whether any verdict should be abandoned, and what the principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a friend who says she wants to get fit but takes the lift every single day. I would like the answer written in British English.
