I work as a collections registrar at a mid-sized museum, and we have recently begun facing disputes over how to label, insure, and display objects that have been substantially restored, repaired, or reconstructed. The core disagreement is philosophical before it is practical: when is the object before us still the same object, and when has it been replaced by a later object or a replica? Please write a decision procedure my department can run, in under an hour per object, before a case goes to the collections committee. Ground the procedure in the distinction between constitution and identity and in the principle that what an object can survive depends on the kind of object it is, but turn those ideas into questions a registrar without philosophical training can answer, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a definite verdict, and where it must stop and hand the case to a curator or committee because the question is a value judgment rather than a matter of fact. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a terracotta statue whose lower half has shattered and been rebuilt in new clay while the upper half is original; (2) a ceremonial wooden canoe whose planks have each been replaced one at a time over two centuries, until none of the present wood is original; and (3) a bronze figure that was melted down in a fire and recast from the same molten metal by the artist's own foundry. Flag the case in which the procedure yields an answer the institution will be reluctant to accept, and say plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
