At a dinner last week two friends went back and fourth about a moral trade another couple made. Sam was defending it and Priya was against it. The couple, Jonas and Mina, have very different moral priorities: Jonas is an animal-welfare person who thinks factory farming is one of the worst things in the world, and he thinks climate change is massively overfunded by comparison. Mina is a climate person who thinks animal-welfare stuff is mostly a sideshow. They made a trade: Jonas will spend every other Saturday canvassing for a carbon-tax initiative, which he thinks is nearly pointless, and Mina will go vegan for the same year, which she thinks is nearly pointless. Each is doing something they regard as a moral waste of time in exchange for the other doing something they regard as hugely important.

Sam said it's a brilliant deal. Each is giving up something they don't value much for something they value enormously, so both make the world better by their own lights, and neither does anything they think is actually wrong. It's just a trade where both sides walk away richer.

Priya said that's exactly the problem: it treats moral commitments like preferences you can swap. If Jonas really thinks canvassing is pointless, he's spending his Saturdays on something with no moral worth, just to buy Mina's veganism. He's treating Mina's conscience like a vending machine.

Sam answered that Mina's veganism reduces animal suffering whether or not she cares about animals—the animals don't care about her motives. Jonas wants less animal suffering, and the deal gets him that. Same for Mina and emissions. Why does it matter that each thinks the other's project is overrated?

Priya said the deal only looks good from inside each person's own value system, and those systems disagree about what's actually good. In an ordinary trade both parties can use a shared scale to judge the deal, but here there's no single standard by which the exchange makes the world better—each is judging the deal by a value system the other rejects. Jonas thinks a year of veganism is worth a lot, Mina thinks it's worth almost nothing, so they're not really trading; they're exploiting each other's perceived errors.

Sam said that's not an objection, it's just how trades between people with different tastes work. And exploiting each other's errors cuts both ways: if Mina is right, Jonas's Saturdays were wasted but the world got cheaper emissions; if Jonas is right, Mina gave up steak for a good cause. At least one of them made the world a lot better by their own standard. The alternative is they each do their own thing and complain.

Priya said no, the alternative is that each does what they actually think is right and tries to persuade the other. The deal lets both of them avoid the hard question of who's right, and it gives each a reason to hope the other is wrong—if Mina is right about animals being a sideshow, then Jonas overpaid.

I couldn't sort out who was right. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing? I'm less interested in a final winner than in the moves.
