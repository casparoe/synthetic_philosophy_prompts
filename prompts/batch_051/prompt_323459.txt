I'm a novelist, currently writing a book about a professional poker player who inherits a failing racetrack and has to decide whether to sell to a casino conglomerate or keep the place going. I wanted her decision-making to feel genuinely rigorous, so I spent a couple months reading decision theory, and I got fairly well convinced by the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization: the thought that if your preferences don't fit some probability and utility function, there's a set of bets or trades you'd accept one by one and end up worse off no matter what, and that this is less about being irrational than about your preferences not hanging together. The Dutch book and money pump versions feel to me like the strongest statement of that.

But then I actually worked through the Allais and Ellsberg cases, and I realiezd my own instincts are the embarrassing ones. In the Allais setup I'd take the sure million over a gamble that's 89% a million, 10% five million, and 1% nothing; then in the second pair I'd take a 10% shot at five million over an 11% shot at a million. And with Ellsberg, if there's one urn with a known 50-50 split and another where the red-black mix is completely unknown, I'd rather bet on red from the known urn than red from the unknown one, and I'd say the same about black. I can't name any coherent probabilities that would make that pattern work. My character would do exactly the same, and I can't bring myself to write her as simply making a mistake.

So here's what I actually need, because I have to decide what kind of mind this character has. Should the firm intuition be debunked and dropped—written as a bias she's smart enough to see through—or should the coherence story be amended or abandoned, with her gut tracking something the theory leaves out, or is there a reading on which they're compatible? Give me the strongest version of each option, what would justify each move, and then say which way you'd write the character and why. Format: a numbered list of exactly five entries, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
