I'm a software engineer on a forecasting team (churn, demand, pricing) and I keep running into the same argument. Some of our best models are built on assumptions that everybody knows are false: customers act independently, the process is stationary, there's no feedback loop where our predictions change behavior, stuff like that. Whenever I've tried to flag one of these assumptions in a design review, the senior engineers tell me it doesn't matter as long as performance on the held-out set is good. And honestly, their usually right. The model with the false assumptions beats the more "realistic" model we also built, and not by a little.

Over time I've turned that into a little homemade principle, I call it predictive license: the truth of a model's assumptions doesn't matter, only its predictive accuracy does, and the only legitimate criticism of an assumption is a prediction it gets wrong. I like this because it keeps arguments concrete: don't tell me the assumption is unrealistic, show me the eval metric it hurts. But I've also started to worry I've just stopped thinking about explanatory truth altogether, and I can't tell whether that's a disciplined attitude or a lazy one.

So I wanted to run it past people who think about this for a living. Is predictive license actually defensible? Is it a known position (I vaguely remember some economist saying unrealism in assumptions is fine if the predictions work)? And where does it break — what's the strongest case of a model that predicts great but where its false assumptions should still bother us? I'm looking for an honest assessment, not validation.
