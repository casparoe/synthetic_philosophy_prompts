I'm in a philosophy of logic seminar and we just spent two hours on Lewis Carroll's little dialogue 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles,' the one where the Tortoise accepts A and B — the stuff about things equal to the same being equal to each other, though the details don't matter — and then digs in and refuses Z. Achilles says fine, take this as a new premise: if A and B are true, then Z must be true. The Tortoise cheerfully writes it down and still won't move; Achilles adds a longer one; and it goes on like that forever. What I can't stop thinking about is that the Tortoise isn't making some elementary mistake. He's exposing the difference between a premise and a rule of inference: you can't stuff modus ponens into the premises as one more conditional, because you'd still need modus ponens to do anything with that new conditional. Taking the step is something you do, not something you assert. The class kept going around about wether this shows deduction rests on something ungrounded, or, rather, that rules and premises just justify in different ways, and we never really landed it.

Anyway, I'd like to close the seminar with something more alive than a handout. Could you write me a pantoum — you know the form, second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as first and third of the next, and the whole thing loops around at the end — four or five stanzas, from the Tortoise's point of view? I want the recycled lines to carry the philosophy: each time a line returns it should feel almost identical but a little heavier, like the conditional premise that keeps swallowing one more clause while Z never arrives. Someone who knows the Carroll problem should be able to pick out which line is doing the ever-lengthening premise and which is the polite refusal to take the step, but it shouldn't read like a glossary entry. And if the last stanza can put us back exactly where we started, so the poem, like the argument, doesn't terminate, that would be perfect.
