In one of Hume's economic essays, Of the Balance of Trade, he asks us to imagine that most of a kingdom's money vanishes overnight, and he suggests that after prices and wages fall in proportion the country would not be materially weaker. I keep coming back to that thought because it seems to generate an argument much stronger than the one Hume himself draws, and I cannot tell whether the extra strength is real or whether a hidden step is doing too much work. Here is the version a fellow student defended:

(1) A nation's real prosperity consists in its population, labor, goods, and industry, not in the quantity of money it holds.  
(2) Money serves only as a medium of exchange; therefore if the total quantity of money changed and every price and wage adjusted in the same proportion, all relative prices and real incentives would be exactly as before.  
(3) So a country with only a fraction of its former money, after such a proportional adjustment, would be no worse off than before, and a country with many times as much money would be no better off.  
(4) Any observable effect of a change in the money stock must therefore come from the interval in which prices and wages have not yet adjusted.  
(5) If a monetary change could be made perfectly smooth, fully anticipated, and uniform across all prices and wages, it would have no real consequences at all, regardless of its size or direction.  
(6) This implies that the difference between an expanding and a contracting money stock is never itself a source of prosperity or harm; it is only a coordination problem, a matter of how evenly prices adjust.

The conclusion seems to prove too much: it would make one indifferent between a society whose money stock gradually shrank to a tenth and one whose money stock gradually grew tenfold, as long as the change was smooth. That cannot be right, or if it is, I need to know which premise to reject and what I lose by rejecting it. Is there a slide between nominal and real quantities here, or does the argument force me to give up the idea that money is merely an instrument?
