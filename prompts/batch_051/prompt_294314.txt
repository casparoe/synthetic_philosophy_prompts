In preparing a seminar on the philosophy of medicine, I have been trying to pin down an old debate that students find slippery. A colleague sympathetic to Thomas Szasz's critique of psychiatry has offered the following criteria. She holds that a condition counts as a disease only if it (i) involves an underlying structural or functional pathology of the body; (ii) can be identified independently of the patient's, the clinician's, and the surrounding society's values; and (iii) presents signs and symptoms that are manifestations of that pathology rather than meaningful responses to a person's circumstances. On her view, most psychiatric diagnoses fail these tests and are therefore not diseases, strictly speaking.

I want to test that position with a borderline case. Consider a person who, for two years after a violent assault, has recurrent episodes in which her heart races, her breathing becomes difficult, and she is convinced she is about to die; the episodes are reliably triggered by reminders of the assault. A medication acting on the heart reduces the racing but not the conviction, while a medication acting on the brain reduces both. Neuroimaging shows alterations in the circuitry involved in regulating fear. Yet calling her condition a disorder at all requires judging that her responses are disproportionate to the threat she now faces; a clinician who regarded such fear as an intelligible, even fitting, response to what she endured would not classify it as pathological.

Work through each of the three criteria with respect to this case. Where the case fails a criterion, say whether that failure is better read as showing that the criterion is too strict, or as showing that the case is not the kind of thing the criterion was meant to capture. Then say what the case reveals about the criteria themselves: in particular, whether the demand for value-independent identification is doing the real work, and whether that demand should be retained, revised, or abandoned. Finally, assess the colleague's broader claim in light of the case: is this condition a disease in everything but name, or does the case show that disease status can survive the absence of a value-free somatic lesion? Please separate what is settled in this debate from what is genuinely contested among those who work on it, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
