I've been trying to settle my mind about equanimity — the ideal, call it apatheia or simply freedom from disturbance, of not being knocked about by one's emotions — and whether it is genuinely good, and at what cost. The endpoints feel clear to me. A chronically anxious woman learns, through cognitive-behavioral exercises, to stop catastrophizing; the free-floating dread disappears, but she still fears what merits fear, still grieves, still loves, and still takes delight in things. That seems to me an unmixed good. A second person takes a medication that damps down every emotion — not only fear, anger, and sorrow but also joy, love, amusement, and aesthetic pleasure — leaving only a flat, mild calm. I find that result terrible, whatever the tranquility it buys.

The hard cases are these. First, a mother's grown son dies, and over several years of mourning she comes to a calm acceptance: she can speak of him without coming apart, but she still carries a tender sadness on his birthday, and she says she would not take a pill that removed even that residual pang. I think her state is good, and the residual sadness is no defect. Second, a different mother's son dies, and from the first week she takes a grief-blocking medication; she never mourns and feels no sadness. She knows perfectly well that her son died and that it is a terrible thing, but when she speaks of him she has the same mild, uninvolved affect she would have toward a character in a novel. I cannot call that good, even though she is as free from the disturbance of grief as one could be. Third, an activist is kept awake by anger at a serious political injustice; she is offered a treatment that would leave intact her clear-sighted recognition that the injustice is wrong and her determination to fight it, while removing only the felt anger. Here I find myself thinking she would lose nothing important, and that the calm resolve might even be better.

So I seem to hold that removing free-floating anxiety is good, removing residual grief is bad, and removing righteous anger can be good or better — yet each is the removal of a disturbing negative affect. Is there one principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all these verdicts, and what does it say about a further case: a man who, after a betrayal, forgives so completely that he no longer feels any resentment or hurt while still judging that he was wronged? If no principle captures everything, state candidly which verdict I should overturn, and give the strongest principle available once I do.
