I am a science journalist who covers work on rationality and cooperation, and I will be moderating the closing Q&A at a small workshop on formal models of strategic choice. The final speaker is a decision theorist defending a strict form of superrationality. Her position is that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two players who know themselves to be in the same epistemic situation, have the same information, and are following the same mode of reasoning, the rationally required move is to cooperate. On her account, a player in that position should not treat the other's move as fixed and then maximize against it; the player should treat her own choice as selecting a decision procedure for the pair. The effective alternatives are then that both cooperate or both defect, and since both cooperating is better for each player than both defecting, cooperation is required. She concedes that the usual dominance reasoning is correct for asymmetric or unknown opponents but holds that it is the wrong standard for the symmetric case.

Write me a sequence of questions for the Q&A, ordered so that the natural objections are voiced and then become progressively harder for her to deflect. For each objection, anticipate the reply she is likely to give and include the follow-up question that reply invites. The questions should press on: the causal independence of the two players' choices and the dominance argument; the charge that superrationality substitutes evidential for causal reasoning, and whether it therefore commits her to one-boxing in Newcomb's problem and to evidential decision theory more generally, with the difficulties that invites; the status of the principle, whether it is a decision rule, an equilibrium-selection criterion, or a moral norm, and whether a rational individual could defect while sincerely endorsing it for the pair; the exact symmetry demanded, and what the view prescribes when the players are only approximately symmetric or when each knows the other is very likely but not certain to mirror her choice; and the threat that a superrational cooperator is exploitable by any opponent whose reasoning tracks hers imperfectly. End with one question about what experimental result or formal theorem could lead her to abandon or substantially weaken the view.
