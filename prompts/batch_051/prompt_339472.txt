For the past year I've been the volunteer who picks the music for the Friday "living room" at a small palliative day hospice — I make the playlist each week, and sometimes families send in a song. We've started going back and forth about sad music. One nurse says we should never put on anything in a minor key when someone is newly grieving, because she says it "validates" the sadness and the person spirals. Another thinks the whole point of the room is that music can keep someone company in a feeling without shoving a new feeling into them, and that a slow drooping aria and a fast driving one in a minor key aren't the same kind of sad at all. It got me reading the philosophy of music and emotion, and the piece that sticks with me is the puzzle of how sounds can even be sad, since sound waves don't have mental states. The answer I find most useful is the contour/resemblance one: music is sad a bit like a willow or a slumped posture is sad — it shares the shape, pace, and movement of human expressive behaviour — and that's different from what it makes a particular listener feel. So being expressive of sadness is not the same as inducing it.

I don't want a one-off ruling for our current fight; I want a reusable proceedure the team can actually run on any request, in under ten minutes, at the nurses' station, with no philosophy background. Please write that procedure grounded in the expressiveness-versus-induction distinction: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because no checklist can settle it. The point of the distinction should survive as plain questions, but don't flatten it into "sad music good" or "sad music bad." Then run it on three cases: (1) a client with dementia who doesn't say much but brightens and hums along to a very sad aria, and asks for it every week; (2) a newly widowed client who keeps requesting the music from her husband's funeral; (3) a young volunteer who wants to add a minor-key song that reliably makes one particular client cry. It would help if at least one case comes out in a way my team won't like, and you say why.
