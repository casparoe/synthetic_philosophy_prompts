I keep seeing legal fiction used to mean roughly "anything the law makes up," which feels too broad, so I want a working definition. Here are cases I have views about: a corporation being treated as a single person who can sue and be sued is clearly a fiction; so is a rule that says notice is deemed served three days after its mailed, even if the letter never showed up. By contrast, a speed limit or a valid contract are not fictions, even though both are products of the law. The hard ones for me: the "reasonable person" in negligence law (no actual human is that prudent, but it doesn't feel like the same move as pretending a company is a person); "everyone is presumed to know the law," which everyone knows is false; constructive notice, where you're treated as knowing something you didn't know; the old common law dodge of pretending a defendant in one county was in custody in another county just to get jurisdiction (the Bill of Middlesex trick); and the "fiction of the lost grant" in long-use property cases, where a court pretends there once was a deed that got lost. I half-suspect the line is something like: a legal fiction is a statement the law applies as true even though everyone knows it's false, and unlike a mere presumption it can't be rebutted by evidence. But I'm not sure the reasonable person comes out right under that definition, or whether "irrebuttable" is doing real work or is just a symptom of something deeper. Give me a definition, run it through these cases, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or goes silent. Also say how confident you are in the definition and single out the point you're least sure of.
