I am drafting a novel set in a near-future democratic country, wealthy and with a professional civil service, that has just enacted a constitutional amendment with three clauses. First, the state may not prohibit, compel, or levy a targeted tax on any voluntary adult behavior in the domains of health, consumption, and personal finance unless that behavior directly and severely harms a non-consenting other party. Second, whatever regulation remains permissible must operate entirely through choice architecture: defaults, opt-out arrangements, framing, disclosure formats, waiting periods, simplified paperwork, and social-comparison messaging. Third, every such intervention must be reauthorized by the legislature every five years, must be openly identified as a designed default when it is administered, and may be challenged by any citizen before a review tribunal. The reform begins with broad but not fanatical public support.

I want the novel's world to follow from the amendment rather than from authorial convenience, so I would like you to reason through what this society looks like forty years later. Which public-health and financial goals does the arrangement plausibly advance, and where does it fail? How do firms, platforms, and marketers adapt their own competing choice architectures, and does the state respond by pushing its nudges deeper into manipulation? Does the ban on coercion leak through back doors such as licensing, liability rules, mandatory insurance, or public procurement, and how would courts and legislators police those boundaries? How does the relationship between citizens and the administrative state change when state power is exercised mostly through defaults and framing rather than commands? I care less about whether your verdict is optimistic than about the visible chain of reasoning: mark which effects seem robust, which are speculative, and what assumptions would have to fail for the prediction to be wrong.
