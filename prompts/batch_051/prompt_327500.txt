I've been trying to pin down what I actually think about Whig history — the habit of writing the past as a series of steps that lead to us — going by my gut on cases rather than principle first.

- A history of physics that treats caloric theory, phlogiston and the aether as wrong turns that later got corrected: fine. The later theories really do predict and control better.
- A Victorian-style history of parliament that runs Magna Carta to the Glorious Revolution to the Reform Acts to universal suffrage as the slow growth of liberty: not fine. It reads like the past being force-marched into a present-day pep talk.
- A history of marriage that presents it as evolving from arranged dynastic alliances toward companionate, egalitarian marriage and finally equal marriage: not fine, even though I agree with the endpoint.
- A history of computing from relays through valves to transistors to integrated circuits, told as steps toward the smartphone: fine? I want to say fine, because each stage solved a concrete bottleneck, but I worry I'm just pattern-matching to a tidy narrative.
- A history of English that describes the loss of case endings and grammatical gender as simplification: not fine. Nothing got simpler; it just changed.
- A history of medicine that presents bloodletting and miasma theory as errors that modern medicine corrected: fine, probably. But I can't see what seperates this from the marriage case, since both judge the past against a present standard.
- A history of astronomy as the shedding of epicycles and geocentrism on the way to heliocentrism: fine.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? Where does it place the case I keep going back and forth on — a history of my own field, neural nets, written as if everything was always heading toward deep learning, with GOFAI as a wrong turn and old connectionist work as a prophecy? And which of my verdicts should I give up if the cleanest principle says they have to go? Please answer in British English.
