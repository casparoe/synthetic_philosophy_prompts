I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I keep returning to a parallel I cannot decide about. Aumann's agreement theorem says, roughly, that two Bayesian reasoners with a common prior and common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities cannot agree to disagree: for any event, their posterior probabilities must be identical. The professional norms of objective journalism seem to make the same promise. Reporters are supposed to share standards of evidence and habits of news judgment; they publish their conclusions so that others in the newsroom (and, ideally, the public) can see them; and they are expected to reason competently. The mapping looks clean: sourcing and reporting are private information; publication is making one's posterior common knowledge; and the background assumptions that supposedly define professional objectivity play the role of the common prior. If the parallel is deep, then persistent disagreement among objective reporters has to trace to a failure of one of the assumptions, and the usual suspect would be the common prior — which would make objectivity, understood as convergence, quietly dependent on a shared worldview rather than on anything neutral. But the parallel also predicts something plainly false: two reporters handed the same wire copy and the same style guide do not file materially identical stories, and journalists disagree about emphasis, framing, and which facts matter even when their posterior on any fixed claim agrees. So my question is what kind of analogy this is. What, exactly, would have to be true of a newsroom for the theorem's transfer to go through; where does the mapping break down; and does Aumann's theorem genuinely illuminate the epistemology of journalism, or just give an old debate about bias and objectivity a technical-sounding gloss?
