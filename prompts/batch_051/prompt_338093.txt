I'm putting together a short statement of views for a giving circle that has to decide how to allocate a five-figure annual budget, and the group is split between funding direct service delivery (disease prevention, cash transfers, that sort of thing) and funding structural work (policy reform, institution-building, norm change). I've followed enough of the arguments on both sides that I can't get away with saying that both matter, so I've tried to write out the specific commitments I actually hold. Here is where I've landed:

(a) A donated dollar should go to whichever use has the highest expected marginal impact, where 'marginal' means the difference made by the next dollar, not the average impact of the program.
(b) The largest durable improvements in well-being across history seem to have come from structural or systemic shifts — technological change, institutional reform, changing norms — rather than from scaling up existing services at the margin.
(c) Structural work is much harder to evaluate than direct delivery: its effects are delayed, heavily confounded, and not usually observed under anything like a clean counterfactual.
(d) The right response to (c) is to discount expected-value estimates that lean on structural theories, since the estimates themselves are less reliable even when they come out looking enormous.
(e) If a system is stuck in a bad equilibrium, marginal improvements that work within that equilibrium can be eroded as the system returns to it, whereas changes that shift the equilibrium itself can persist.
(f) Neglectedness matters: if most other funders are chasing measurable direct interventions, that is a reason for a small funder like us to take the less-crowded structural options seriously.
(g) But we should not reject a structural proposal just because it cannot produce a cost-effectiveness estimate as clean as a direct intervention's; precision and expected value are different properties.

Before I present this, I'd like an audit of the set. Which pairs or triples are genuinely in tension with each other, and how severe is each tension? Which apparent tensions actually dissolve once the relevant terms are sharpened? For each real tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost; then tell me which single revision, if any, would do the most to make the whole set hang together. Please also separate what is settled among people who have thought carefully about these questions from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
