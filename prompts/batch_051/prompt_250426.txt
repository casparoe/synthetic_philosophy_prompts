I'm an undergraduate taking a contemporary moral philosophy course, and we have just finished a unit on Scanlon's contractualism. I keep finding myself pulled in two directions, and I would like help determining whether the theory and one of my firmest intuitions actually conflict.

On one side, I find the core of the view very attractive: an act is wrong when it is disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, and a principle must be justifiable to each person, considered one by one. I am especially drawn to what is usually called the individualist restriction, the idea that you cannot justify a principle by summing many small complaints until they outweigh one person's serious complaint. That restriction seems to explain why we should save one person from death rather than prevent a mild disappointment to a very large number of people, even if the aggregated disappointment would be enormous. It captures something about the separateness of persons, and it gives the theory a structural elegance: it tells you which considerations may and may not be combined, not merely how much weight each has.

On the other side, I have an intuition I cannot bring myself to distrust: in a choice between saving one stranger and saving five strangers, where the stakes for each person are the same, you are not merely permitted to save the five; you are required to. Flipping a coin, or treating the choice as a mere tie, strikes me as a failure to take the numbers seriously.

The problem is that the individualist restriction seems to put pressure on exactly that intuition. Each of the five has a complaint if you save the one: you let them die. The one has the very same complaint if you save the five. If complaints cannot be summed, then the fact that there are five of them does not seem to give anyone a stronger reason. The one can say to you: my claim is no weaker than any of theirs, so by what principle could you choose against me? And the restriction seems to forbid answering, because there are more of them. I know there are moves available, for example that each person has an ex ante reason to prefer a policy of saving the greater number, since it maximizes each person's chance of being rescued from behind a suitable veil of ignorance, or that treating each life with equal weight requires saving the greater number because no one can reasonably demand that their own life receive more than equal consideration. But I cannot tell whether these moves genuinely respect the individualist restriction or merely smuggle aggregation back in under another name.

I am not asking you to tell me which side wins. I want you to lay out, as strongly as possible, what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of the individualist restriction on which the theory and the intuition are compatible. In particular: does contractualism as Scanlon actually defends it require saving the greater number, merely permit it, or leave the case underdetermined, and how would we tell which verdict the theory delivers rather than which verdict we want it to deliver?

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
