You are a specialist in the philosophy of science and in Karl Popper's thought, consulting for a project on the history of scientific methodology. Assume for this exercise that Popper—who died in 1994—could be brought fully up to date on the replication crisis that has convulsed psychology and neighboring empirical fields in recent decades, and on the reform movement that grew up in response: pre-registration, registered reports, open data, large-scale replication attempts, and calls to replace or supplement null hypothesis significance testing.

Your task is to give a careful, well-organized prediction of where Popper would land on this movement. Before committing to an answer, brainstorm at least three different ways his commitments could pull him, and say which strand of his philosophy dominates in each. Then produce a single considered verdict.

In particular:

- What would Popper identify as the underlying defect the replication crisis exposed? Relate it precisely to his concepts of falsifiability, ad hoc adjustments to save a theory from refutation, the rejection of induction, and the asymmetry between confirmation and disconfirmation—but do not merely gesture at those terms. Say what in a modern research workflow he would see as the equivalent of the moves he criticized in his own time. Be specific about practices such as p-hacking (flexing the analysis until a statistically significant result appears), HARKing (hypothesizing after the results are known), and the file-drawer problem (suppression of null results).

- Which parts of the reform agenda would he embrace as making science bolder, riskier in Popper's preferred sense, and more genuinely open to falsification?

- Where would he likely part company with today's reformers? For example, work through what his rejection of induction implies for how he would understand statistical inference, replication, meta-analysis, and any proposal that treats accumulated significant results as progressively confirming a hypothesis. Consider also his hostility to subjective and degree-of-belief pictures of science, and his emphasis on bold conjectures and severe tests rather than cautious parameter estimation.

- Would he diagnose the replication crisis as a local technical failure of psychology, or as evidence that something deeper was wrong with how empirical scientists understood their own method? What would that imply for his criterion of demarcation?

End with a brief forecast: if Popper were advising a working psychology department today, what two or three concrete practices would he insist on, and which prominent reform practices would he criticize as misguided?
