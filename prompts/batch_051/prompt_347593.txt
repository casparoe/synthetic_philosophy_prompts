ok so I'm a PhD student in decision theory / formal epistemology, and I've been going back and forth on the two-envelope problem and some close relatives. A reading group asked me to write down where I've landed so the others can pick at it, and I'd rather find out now whether the list hangs together. Could you audit these commitments for me: which pairs are in tension, how severe each tension is, what the cheapest fix would be for the worst one, and which of the tensions I'd probably flag myself isn't actually one. Also, since I'll have to defend the audit to people who know this material: tell me where your reading is most likely to fail, and what argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict about where the real tension is.

Here are the commitments:

(1) In the standard finite two-envelope problem, before you open anything, switching is exactly neutral. Symmetry settles it; the envelopes have the same expected value, and I'd take either one.

(2) The familiar argument that the other envelope is worth 2x or x/2 with equal probability, so switching is worth 1.25x, is a mistake. In particular you can't assign 1/2–1/2 credence to the other being double and the other being half conditional on every possible observed amount x; no proper prior gives you that, and the right posterior has to depend on you're prior over how the amounts got chosen.

(3) I'm a strict Bayesian about this: if there's no proper prior, then after opening there's just no well-defined expected-value question on the table. I don't let improper priors or loose talk about objective symmetry quietly do normative work.

(4) But when I actually imagine opening the envelope and seeing, say, $100, I can't bring myself to say I've learned anything about whether I hold the smaller or larger amount. The number is scale-free; $100 and $10,000 are the same kind of ignorance in different units. Seeing x is evidentially inert for any x, and my answer is neutral in spirit every time.

(5) I have no problem with the idea that under a proper prior, seing x can tip the balance. If the pair was picked from a small finite menu of possible pairs, a mid-range amount could make switching rational and the top amount could make staying rational. That doesn't undermine the ex ante symmetry in (1); it's just Bayes.

(6) In infinite-expectation variants, a St. Petersburg-flavored two-envelope problem where each envelope has infinite expected value unconditionally, I'm inclined to say the symmetry argument from (1) stops being decisive, and I'm not sure there's a fact of the matter about switching without adding extra principles.
