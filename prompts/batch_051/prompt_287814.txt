I've been helping lead a small group at my church, and we're doing a series on inerrancy and why the Bible can be trusted. One thing I keep tripping over is how we treat the Gospels compared to how my history teacher tells me to treat any other old account. In history class, if two people describe the same event and they disagree about basic details—like how many people were there or what exactly was said—I'm supposed to treat that as a reason to be less sure about the details, or at least to ask which version is right. But at church, when the Gospels do the same kind of thing, we don't do that at all. Like, the four Gospels give different lists of which women went to the tomb on Easter morning, and they describe different numbers and kinds of figures there (one angel, two men, that kind of thing). Or when Jesus predicts Peter's denial, Mark says the rooster will crow twice before Peter denies him three times, but the other Gospels just say a rooster will crow. At church we either harmonize this by adding an extra unmentioned crowing or extra unmentioned women, or we say the differences actually make the story stronger because it shows the authors weren't colluding. But none of us would accept that move for two friends telling the same story in court.

So I cant figure out whether there's a principled reason for the asymmetry, or whether it's just that these are my sacred texts. Give me the strongest principle you can find that would let me treat Gospel-level discrepancies differently from ordinary testimony-level discrepancies. Then stress-test it: would that principle let a Muslim do the same move for an apparent contradiction in the Qur'an, or let me treat any conflicting set of witness statements as evidence that the witnesses weren't colluding? If the principle can't survive those tests, I want to hear that too.
