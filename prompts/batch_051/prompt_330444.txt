I have been thinking, in a somewhat untrained way, about why the past seems more determined after it has happened than it was for the people living through it, and I recently wrote down my reflections in the form of a short essay. I would be curious to know what a careful reader makes of it.

The essay is built around a claim that has begun to seem important to me: that hindsight bias may not be an accidental flaw in historical explanations so much as a price of doing the explaining. To explain an event is to begin from the fact that it happened and to move backward through the available material selecting just those items that help produce the known outcome. The outcome is the filter. Causes that might have mattered but were neutralized, possibilities that occupied the participants, and paths that closed before they could run their course all drop out of the story, not because they were unimportant to the people then, but because they are irrelevant to the ending we already know. I also try to connect this to the narrative fallacy by suggesting that real-life sequences get compressed into stories precisely through this backward selection; a story needs an outcome to know what counts as an event, so the tidy narrative and the known ending are the same thing seen twice. I then approximate the difference by asking how the same stretch of time would have looked to a well-placed observer one month before the event. That observer's list of live possibilities is long and messy; the historian's list is short and pointed. Neither list is simply the truth: the observer is blind to what will turn out to matter, and the historian cannot see what it was like not to know. I end with the worry that this may make explanation in history permanently double: it can mean showing why something became possible, or showing why it became inevitable, and the second meaning keeps winning because it makes better stories.

Could you comment on whether the contrast between the historian's retrospective view and the observer's anticipatory view actually supports my central claim, or whether the essay quietly slides from talking about bias to talking about something else? I would also be glad to hear which parts strike you as overstated or underdeveloped, and whether the final thought about the two meanings of explanation is clear enough to keep, sharpen, or cut.
