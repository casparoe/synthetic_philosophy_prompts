I am preparing a lecture on brain-computer interfaces for a course on technology and the mind, and I keep needing a distinction that I suspect already has a name in the literature. The phenomenon is this. When people object to a company or a court inferring what is going on inside a person's head from neural signals, the objection is usually treated as an informational-privacy complaint: the person did not consent to the collection or use of sensitive data, and suitably strong consent, anonymization, and security would meet it. Yet many of the complaints do not track the sensitivity of the information at all. A long-haul driver who buys her own fatigue-detection headset, and is grateful for it, can still report that she no longer feels alone in her own mind, even though the device registers nothing but drowsiness and no third party ever sees the data. A person who freely volunteers for a brain-based lie-detection test that then clears her can still describe the experience as an intrusion. The wrong that remains once consent, secrecy, and harm are set aside is not informational privacy, since nothing sensitive is disclosed, and it is not autonomy in the ordinary sense of uncoerced choice, since the person chose the device and benefited from it. What I want is the carving, not just the label. Is there an established concept for this distinct wrong, and, whether or not there is, how should it be defined so that it separates cleanly from informational privacy on one side and from autonomy-as-choice on the other, and where do its edges fall, for example when the device infers almost nothing rather than rich mental content? Please answer in continuous prose, as if writing a letter to a colleague, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
