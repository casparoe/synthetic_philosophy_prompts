I repeatedly encounter a one-liner in arguments about artificial intelligence, always in slightly different words: that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the human mind cannot be a machine, and that this is why no AI will ever be a genuine mathematician rather than a fast calculator — the kind of anti-mechanist conclusion associated with Lucas and Penrose. It tends to be offered as though it closes the debate, but I suspect several genuinely different claims are being compressed into one sentence. I would like you to lay out a menu of the precise theses this line could be expressing, from the technically secure to the extremely strong.

Number the readings. For each one, state it as precisely as you can, classify it as (a) an established mathematical result, (b) a plausible but contestable philosophical thesis, or (c) false, unsupported, or a non sequitur, and give one or two sentences defending the classification. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually license the conclusion that no AI can ever be a genuine mathematician, and which slippage between readings is usually doing the persuasive work when the line is used that way. Flag the hidden assumptions the ambitious use of the line needs but seldom states — for example, assumptions about whether the human mind is consistent, about our being able to step outside a formal system to see a Gödel sentence as true, and about whether machine means one fixed formal system or any physical computational system at all.

Finally, give the strongest defensible version of the line that remains once the bad readings are set aside, and say what that version would and would not imply about the prospects for artificial mathematicians. Make the last line of your reply begin with Verdict: and state that strongest defensible version in one short phrase.
