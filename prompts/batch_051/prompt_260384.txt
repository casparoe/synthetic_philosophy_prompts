I want to turn a tangle of attitudes I have about envy and resentment into something principled rather than a list of reactions. Loosely in the background is the idea I associate with Rawls: that envy can be excusable, perhaps even not a vice, when the inequalities provoking it are great enough to wound self-respect. But I do not want exegesis; I want the cases below to do the work.

Here are my verdicts on eight cases, given as honestly as I can.

1. A colleague earns four percent more than I do for the same work. Lately I brood about her slightly better car and hope she stumbles in the next project review, even though there is nothing unfair about the pay difference — she negotiated, I did not. Verdict: this is plain envy and a vice; it is not a signal of injustice, only spite at an unobjectionable difference.

2. A less qualified candidate gets the job I wanted because his father sits on the board. I resent him and say openly that the appointment was unjust, and I mean it. Verdict: my resentment is warranted; it tracks a real defect in the selection procedure. It is not a vice merely because I also wanted the job.

3. A friend has inherited enough never to have to work, while I hold two jobs. I sometimes wish he would lose it all, though he has never wronged me and is generous with what he has. Verdict: I would not call this envy vicious. It is excusable — a symptom of an unjust background distribution. But the wish that he specifically be ruined is not something I should endorse or act on.

4. A rival wins a grant I wanted, and by any honest standard she deserved it more than I did. I still quietly hope her project underperforms. Verdict: I call this a petty vice. Her advantage is legitimate, and my being worse off is not a license to wish it away.

5. An acquaintance has more than I do almost entirely because she worked longer hours and made fewer costly mistakes. I catch myself telling myself and others that her advantage is unfair. Verdict: this is moralized envy — dressing dislike of my own position up as a claim about injustice. It is a vice because it abuses the language of fairness to smuggle in groundless resentment.

6. A friend is a far better writer than I am, and I want that. But what I want is to become as good, not for her to become worse; in fact I admire her. Verdict: I do not fault myself for this. It may be painful, but it is emulation rather than malice, and if it counts as envy at all it is a benign kind.

7. A public benefit exists that everyone in my community uses, including me. One person, already very well off, gets somewhat more from it than the rest of us do. I campaign to abolish the benefit entirely, even though this would leave me and others worse off and would only stop that person from getting the extra amount. Verdict: this is vicious levelling-down. It may gesture at a real inequality, but its aim is to damage the envied person, and the means harm everyone without repairing anything.

8. A friend enjoys status from an inherited title that I believe should not exist. I resent the status, but I find myself treating him coldly and bringing up his unearned position in arguments, as if he were personally responsible for the system that made it valuable. Verdict: the resentment is responding to something real, but it misfires by targeting him rather than the practice. It is excusable as a symptom of a legitimate complaint, but not as an accusation against him.

I notice two pairs that trouble me. Cases 3 and 4 look alike: in both, I wish someone would lose an advantage, and in neither has the other person wronged me. Yet I call the first excusable and the second a vice, apparently because the first advantage is unfair and the second is not. But why should the fairness of the source govern whether a feeling directed at a non-culpable person is excusable? And cases 3 and 7 look alike in a different way: both concern an unfair advantage and a wish to deprive. Yet I call one excusable and the other vicious, apparently because one is an unendorsed private feeling and the other is enacted in public. But I am suspicious of any principle that leans heavily on that inward/outward line.

A tentative principle I have been toying with: whether an episode of envy or resentment is vicious, excusable, or actually warranted depends on three things — whether the target's advantage is unfair; whether the feeling tracks a practice or institution rather than the person; and whether the desire inside the episode is to match or repair rather than to deprive. But that principle makes trouble in cases 3 and 7, since both concern an unfair advantage and a wish to deprive, and my verdicts on them diverge.

Please take this seriously and do the work on my behalf. State the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts, precisely enough that it yields determinate verdicts on new cases; say which clause each case motivates; apply it to a few further cases I have not listed; and say plainly which of my eight verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should revise the verdict or the principle. If your best principle saves all eight at the cost of inelegant epicycles, say so, and tell me what the simpler principle would force me to drop.
