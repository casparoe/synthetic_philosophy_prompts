For a project I'm doing on mind uploading, I went back to Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, section 3, where he says: "In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else." Then he gives the oak and colt examples. But later in the same chapter he says for persons it's consciousness that makes identity. My question is: if Locke were alive today and we told him about gradual neural replacement (each neuron swapped for a functionally identical silicon chip over a year while I'm awake and talking the whole time) and about scanning-and-uploading-all-at-once (original brain destroyed, copy wakes up in a computer), what would he actually have said about whether I'd survive? The passage makes it sound like he'd be fine with gradual replacement, but I'm not sure he'd be fine with the all-at-once case because the copy's consciousness is just continuous from it's own perspective, not literally the same stream. Would he have to accept both, neither, or one? And would that reveal a tension in his account between the "something else" for living creatures and the consciousness criterion for persons?
