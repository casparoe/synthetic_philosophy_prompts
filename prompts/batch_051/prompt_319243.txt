Bit of an architecture question for you. The classic argument for treating perception as cognitively impenetrable—Fodor, Pylyshyn, that whole modularity crowd—went roughly like this. Early vision is an encapsulated, bottom-up system that computes the sensory signal and passes its output on to central cognition; beliefs and desires live outside that module, so they can influence where you look and how you interpret what you see, but they can't literally change the percept itself. The Müller-Lyer illusion is the standard illustration: knowing the two lines are equal does nothing to how long they look.

But the field has largely shifted to predictive processing. On that picture, perception just is top-down priors being corrected by bottom-up prediction error; expectations, context, and quite high-level knowledge get baked into the generative model at every level. That sounds like exactly the kind of influence the encapsulation argument said was impossible—not attention or judgement, but what you actually see.

So does the cognitive impenetrability argument survive this shift? It feels like the premise under pressure is the modular architecture, but I can't tell weather that premise was doing the real work or was just scaffolding. If it does need repairing, what's the strongest version that still captures the original intuition that there's something properly perceptual which beliefs can't freely rewrite?
