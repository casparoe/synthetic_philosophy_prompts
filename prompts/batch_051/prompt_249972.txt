I live in a housing co-op of about 200 people, and after a couple of ugly blowups—one person moved out after a public callout, and now a lot of people just quietly hide there mistakes—we're debating a proposal someone's calling the Repair Ledger. The pitch is that our community currently runs on shame: public exposure, ruined reputations, the feeling that you as a person are bad. The proposer wants to shift us to guilt: you did a specific bad thing, you fix it, and the relationship can recover. Here's the mechanism. Anyone who feels harmed files a confidential report naming the person. That person is notified privately and has two weeks to either accept it and propose a repair (an apology, restitution, a change in how we do something) or contest it. Contested cases go to a rotating panel of three randomly drawn members, who hear both sides in private and decide whether a repair is owed and what it should be. Now the twist: public discussion of the incident is banned. Anyone caught spreading the story in the group chat or at meetings gets a mark in the ledger and can be assigned community service; repeat gossips can lose speaking rights at meetings. Once a repair is completed, the entry is sealed and can never be brought up against the person again.

I'm not asking whether this is a nice idea. I want to know how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize among people who might do harm, people who might report, panelists, and everyone else? How would people game it, and what happens to the harms that never get filed because someone's too scared or because the ledger doesn't fit? And the deeper question: can a community actually choose its moral emotion through rules like this, or does shame just find the cracks and come out sideways?
