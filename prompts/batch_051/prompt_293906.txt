For a seminar on non-Western epistemology, I give three criteria for a dispute's being a genuine case of many-sided truth in the Jain sense (anekāntavāda): (i) each side's assertion is explicitly indexed to a standpoint and offered as only conditionally true; (ii) the parties regard the apparently opposing assertions as partially true rather than false; and (iii) the apparent opposition cannot be dissolved by saying that the parties simply use the key term in different senses. Now consider a working group on a national forest. An ecologist says, "From the standpoint of carbon storage, the forest is healthy." A fire specialist says, "From the standpoint of fire resilience, it is not healthy." A soil scientist says, "From the standpoint of nutrient cycling, it is healthy in the east and unhealthy in the west." Each accepts the others' reports as accurate as far as they go, and none will assert, without qualification, whether the forest is simply healthy. Is this a genuine case of many-sided truth, or does "healthy" quietly mean something different in each report? Go criterion by criterion, say where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the example shows that any criterion needs revision. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this tradition, and don't present a contested reading of anekāntavāda as the consensus.
