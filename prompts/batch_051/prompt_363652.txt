I keep running into the same pattern in my judgments about reasons for attitudes—admiration, amusement, desire, belief—and I would like to know whether a single principle lies behind it. A wealthy collector offers a struggling painter a million dollars to admire his new painting, which is competent but flat. I want to say the painter has a very strong reason to get himself to admire the painting—to study it until something strikes him, to reframe it, even to take a pill that would induce admiration if one existed—but I cannot bring myself to say the money is a reason to admire the painting. The usual demon scenario gives me the same reaction: if a powerful demon threatens my family unless I admire him, I have overwhelming reason to bring it about that I admire him, yet the threat does not sound to me like a reason to admire him. By contrast, when a painting is genuinely original, honest, and emotionally exacting, that it has those features is a reason to admire it, and not merely a reason to make myself admire it. Likewise, a truly surprising and apt joke is worth being amused by; its being funny is a reason to be amused. But if someone offered me a thousand dollars to be amused by a flat, mean-spirited joke, I would again say I had a reason to fake amusement or to try to cultivate amusement, not a reason to be amused by it.

The belief cases are where my grip slips. A doctor tells a patient that his chance of surviving five years improves substantially if he genuinely believes he will recover, though all the medical evidence points the other way. Part of me wants to say the health benefit is a reason for the patient to believe he will recover; another part insists that it is only a reason to want to have the belief, and has nothing to do with whether the belief is true. Similarly, a friend in acute distress asks me to believe she did not betray me, even though the evidence says she did. Her distress seems to count for something in the space of reasons, but I do not see how it bears on whether she actually betrayed me. I have been reaching toward a rough principle: a consideration is a reason of the right kind for an attitude only if it is a feature of the object that would help make that attitude correct, while considerations that merely make having the attitude useful, prudent, or comforting are not reasons for the attitude at all, only reasons to bring the attitude about. But I cannot tell whether this principle is too strong, too weak, or simply too vague.

Can you extract a precise account from these verdicts? I want to know what separates the right kind of reason from the wrong kind across admiration, amusement, and belief, and which of my judgments the best account will force me to revise. In particular, can the object-given/state-given distinction be sharpened enough to handle the belief cases, or should I conclude that wrong-kind reasons are a different species of reason rather than no reason at all?
