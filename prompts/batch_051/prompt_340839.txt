I'm taking a philosophy of global justice module and we've spent the last two weeks on sweatshops and whether they can be exploitative even when both sides benefit. I wrote out an argument for my essay that felt watertight at the time, but now I'm worried it proves too much. It goes:

(1) For an exchange to be exploitative, the party doing the exploiting has to gain at the other party's expense.
(2) You gain at someone's expense in an exchange only if the exchange leaves them worse off than they would have been without it; otherwise you're gaining, but not at their expense.
(3) A sweatshop worker who takes a factory job of their own free will is usually better off with the job than without it, since their next best option is worse (unemployment, worse informal work, etc).
(4) So such a worker is not left worse off than they would have been without the exchange.
(5) So the factory does not gain at the worker's expense.
(6) So the exchange is not exploitative.

I'm not talking about the cases where employers lie about pay, confiscate passports, or threaten people; I'm happy to grant those are coercive and the argument wouldn't cover them. The conclusion is only about the genuinely voluntary cases. My hunch is that step (2) is doing something sneaky by turning "gaining at someone's expense" into net harm, but I cant quite articulate the alternative. Can you tell me whether the argument is valid, and if not, which numbered step actually fails? And if the problem is (2), how should it be restated so the standard sweatshop case can still count as exploitative without making every voluntary transaction exploitative?
