I'm in a graduate seminar on intertemporal choice and climate economics, and in a discussion about how to set the social discount rate, a colleague defended a sizable positive pure rate of time preference by invoking the old maxim "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." His move was roughly this: the maxim is folk wisdom about prudence; it tells us that a benefit enjoyed now can outweigh a larger benefit of the same kind enjoyed later; and therefore we may discount future welfare itself, not merely future consumption or the probability that the future benefit will arrive. When I pressed him on whether the maxim is actually about uncertainty, liquidity, or the differing ways present and future goods can fail to materialize — two birds in the bush may never be caught — he said that distinction was beside the point for policy.

Before our next meeting I want to come prepared with a precise disambiguation. What are the distinct precise claims that "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" could be making about why present goods are to be preferred over future goods? Please lay them out as a menu, from readings that are nearly trivial to readings that are substantive and contentious, and flag any that strike you as false or true only under special conditions. For each reading, say whether it licenses a positive pure rate of time preference — discounting future welfare as such, after correcting for uncertainty about delivery, growth in the value of goods, and the possibility that no relevant beneficiary will exist — or whether it only justifies discounting future goods for other reasons entirely. Then tell me which reading or readings my colleague's argument actually needs, and whether the proverb, on its best defensible reading, gets him his conclusion. End by saying how confident you are in your overall verdict and which of the individual readings you are least sure about.
