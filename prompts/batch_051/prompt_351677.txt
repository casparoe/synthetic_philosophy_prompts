Before I read any further on whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake, I want the full specification of what an adequate answer would have to do. Write the desiderata for a theory that evaluates the welfare consequences of the transition from foraging to farming — not a theory of why the transition happened, but a theory of how to evaluate it. The theory must specify and defend the unit of evaluation: is the question about the individuals who lived through the transition, about their descendants over some horizon, or about an aggregate across persons and generations? It must specify a counterfactual baseline — which foraging populations, in which environments, at what point before intensification — and say why that baseline rather than another. It must separate the process of domestication from the settled, grain-dependent end-state, so that the costs of crowding, infection, and dietary narrowing are not attributed to farming simpliciter. It must be constrained by the archaeological and skeletal record — shortened stature, enamel defects, dental caries, signs of anaemia and infection, and elevated mortality — while being explicit about what that record can and cannot show. It must handle inequality, storage, and durable hierarchy as possible losses in themselves, not merely through their material effects. It must confront the labour–leisure comparison and the problem that recent foragers, often living on marginal land, may be a poor proxy for pre-Neolithic populations. It must address the population trap: agriculture sustains far more people at plausibly lower average welfare, and the theory must say whether, and under what principle, a larger number of worse-off lives compensates. It must respect irreversibility and ratchet effects, since dense specialized populations cannot simply return to foraging, and decide whether foreclosure of alternatives is itself a cost. It must face the incommensurability of ways of life — autonomy, mobility, risk, social structure — rather than assuming a single index of well-being. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Then mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps; flag any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; and say which single requirement, applied strictly, eliminates the most popular verdicts.
