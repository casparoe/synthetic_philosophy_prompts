I run the actuarial pricing team for a mutual property insurer that underwrites in wildfire-exposed regions. Four times a year we re-fit our loss models, and each time we have to choose which category schemes to rate with — construction era, defensible-space class, vegetation type, elevation band, distance to the most recent burn scar, and the like. Adding categories always improves in-sample fit; what I need is a reusable procedure for deciding which categories are fit to project beyond the historical data — which of them track something that will still partition future losses, and which merely soak up the past. I gather this is close to Goodman's new riddle of induction: the problem of separating projectible predicates such as green from gerrymandered ones such as grue (green if first examined before some future time, blue after), which fits all the emeralds we have seen just as well as green does. I don't want a seminar; I want an operational checklist my team can run in a half-day model review, without any philosophy background, before our chief underwriter signs off.

Please write the decision procedure as a sequence of tests to run in order — a flowchart in words. For each step say what question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "do not use this category," and where it escalates to a human because the question is one of judgment rather than mechanics. Ground the steps in what the grue problem and the induction literature actually give us about projection, entrenchment, and background theories, translated for actuaries. Mark which steps are mechanical tests and which are judgment calls, and be explicit where the procedure adopts one contested reading of Goodman rather than a settled consensus.

Then run the procedure on three proposals currently on my desk:

1. Rating by construction era, with the split at 2011. Homes built 2011 or later get a lower base rate, justified by a cladding standard our own inspectors began enforcing that year.
2. A vendor-supplied "fuel-composition class" that partitions properties into six vegetation categories. The vendor will not disclose how the boundaries were drawn or why there are six classes, but the classes fit our historical loss data better than any scheme we already use.
3. An "exposure-decade" factor: a score counting fire scars within one mile of a property over the most recent ten years, with the scar weights re-derived after every major fire season. In back-testing it explains more loss variance than all our other factors combined.

For each proposal, say how far the procedure gets before stopping or escalating, what the decision would be, and whether that decision is one my team is likely to resist. End with what the procedure cannot do: the cases where a non-projectible category would still slip through, or a projectible one would be killed, and what that residual uncertainty should do to our confidence in the exercise. In your notes to me, separate what is settled about induction and projectibility from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
