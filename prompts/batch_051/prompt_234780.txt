I keep running into a version of this in my reading group, and I want an actual philosopher to sort it out rather than a guy with a blog (no offense). I'm attracted to a deflationary theory of truth — something like Horwich-style minimalism or a disquotational view: the whole content of calling a sentence true is exhausted by the disquotation schema, a sentence like "snow is white" is true just in case snow is white, and the role of "true" is mostly expressive, letting us endorse infinite conjunctions or blind ascriptions like "what Sarah said is true." I also have a strong, persistent intuition I can't shake: when I say general relativity is *closer to the truth* than Newtonian mechanics, I'm not just saying one set of sentences is more useful or better confirmed. I'm attributing a property that comes in degrees — approximate truth, verisimilitude, distance from the way things actually are — and I take that to be involved in explaining why later theories do better. It feels like a brute fact about the world, not just about our language.

But it's not obvious to me that deflationism actually forbids this. Maybe minimalism is only a theory of the word "true", not of truthlikeness, and truthlikeness is just a separate relation between theory and world that deflationists can happily quantify over. Or maybe once you give up truth as a substantive property, you're left with no non-circular way to explain what "closer" means; verisimilitude talk just becomes disguised talk about our standards of evidence, and my intuition is a reification. I can't tell wether which move is right, or whether there's a reading on which they're compatible.

Then give me a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be dropped as a pre-theoretic gloss, should deflationism be amended or modified to accommodate degrees of truth, or is there a reading where they're compatible?
