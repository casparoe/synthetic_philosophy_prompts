Okay, so I just got back from a long argument with a friend about the St. Petersburg game, and it left me with a idea for a concrete mechanism I want someone to actually pick apart. My friend kept saying that once you cap the payout the paradox is defused and you can just price the game at its expected value; I kept saying the skew still does something, and we couldn't settle it. So here's the thing I want tested.

A state is considering replacing its ordinary lottery with what it calls St. Petersburg certificates. Each certificate costs $22. At the end of each month, for every certificate, the state runs an independent, publicly auditable fair draw: it flips a fair coin until the first tails appears. If the first tails is on flip n, the certificate pays 2^n dollars. The payout is capped at 2^20 dollars, that is, $1,048,576. The accounting argument runs like this: each of the flips 1 through 19 contributes one dollar of expected value, and the capped tail contributes another two dollars, so the expected payout per certificate is $21. The state keeps $1 per certificate, which is a much smaller house edge than a normal lottery. There is no limit on how many certificates a person can buy, and payouts above $10,000 are reported as income.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral question. Walk through how it would actually behave. What kind of buyer does it attract, and how is that different from a normal lottery or from simply paying the fair price of $21 for the same capped game? What happens once unlimited purchases are allowed—does that create a whale stragegy, a diversification strategy, or something like arbitrage? What does the state's actual risk exposure look like as the number of certificates grows, and does the $1-per-certificate edge survive contact with reality? Where does it get gamed first: income-reporting thresholds, nominee buyers who keep each payout below $10,000, secondary trading of certificates, people pooling into funds, timing around the monthly draw, or something else? Which of those failure modes can be fixed without turning the whole thing into a different mechanism, and which are baked in?

Please think carefully and start by redoing the expected-value arithmetic yourself. Answer as an economist who works on lottery design and decision theory. At the end, tell me how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the point you are least sure about.
