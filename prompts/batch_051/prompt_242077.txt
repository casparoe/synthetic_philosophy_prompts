You are a philosopher of science who works on conceptual analysis and the measurement of progress. I want a working definition of the contested phrase "a more advanced civilization"—precise enough to score any of the following cases as clearly advanced, clearly not advanced, or borderline.

The cases:
1. A civilization that commands the energy flux of its galaxy (Kardashev Type III) and routinely engineers at the atomic scale.
2. A civilization with no tools, no cumulative knowledge, and no symbolic communication.
3. A civilization that has controlled the full energy output of its home star for millennia but has produced no new science, art, or institutions in that whole time.
4. A civilization whose main achievement is molecular-scale manufacturing and programmable matter—a high score on John Barrow's inward version of the scale—but which uses little energy and never leaves its planet.
5. A civilization that never gets past stone tools yet maintains continuous literate, skeptical, cumulative inquiry for ten thousand years.
6. A civilization that reaches planetary-scale energy use (Kardashev Type I) and collapses within a century, leaving no descendants.
7. A civilization that deliberately dismantles much of its energy and space infrastructure to extend healthy lifespans and deepen its art, philosophy, and institutions.

One live possibility is that "more advanced" conflates an engineering-capability notion with a flourishing notion, and that Kardashev and Barrow each capture only one of these. Take that possibility seriously. Before the definition, briefly canvass where the cases pull in opposite directions. Then propose your definition or definitions, run all seven cases through them, say which verdict you find least secure, and revise the definition if it gives a clearly unacceptable result. Write in American English.
