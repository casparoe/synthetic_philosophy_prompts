A friend pressed the following argument on me in a discussion about how much a society should pay now to prevent climate harms two centuries out, and I cannot find where it goes wrong, though I want to.

(1) Pure time preference — counting a benefit or harm as less important merely because it occurs later — is rationally indefensible. If a benefit is certain and I will be alive to enjoy it, preferring a smaller benefit now to a larger benefit later is not a reason; it is a bias.

(2) What is indefensible for an individual's own future is indefensible for a society's treatment of future people. A person born in 2200 does not matter less than a person alive today merely because of when they are born.

(3) Therefore, in social evaluation we should be temporally neutral: weight future harms and benefits only by their probability and their size, never by their date, and choose the policy with the greatest probability-weighted sum of benefits over harms.

(4) On any plausible outlook, the future contains vastly more people than the present — many generations across thousands or millions of years.

(5) So for almost any major policy, the probability-weighted sum is dominated by far-future consequences; near-term effects are a rounding error.

(6) Therefore, a temporally neutral society is committed to maximizing far-future expected welfare even at severe cost to people alive today — forced saving, sharp restrictions on present consumption, and perhaps the kind of centralized control needed to keep a population investing in the far future rather than enjoying the present.

(7) But no acceptable morality can require the present generation to immiserate itself for an indefinite sequence of future generations.

(8) So one of the premises must go. My friend says the cheapest premise to reject is (1): a positive pure rate of time preference is not merely a political compromise but a genuine normative principle.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2); pure time preference has always struck me as a bias, and I do not want to escape by denying that future people matter or by gesturing at deontology and walking away. But I worry that keeping them commits me to something like (6), which I find monstrous.
