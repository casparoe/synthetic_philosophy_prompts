I have been puzzling over an argument I encounter in discussions of heritability, and I would like you to assess it precisely. Suppose a careful study estimates the heritability of a cognitive trait in a particular population at 0.8. The argument runs:

(1) Heritability is the proportion of the trait's variance, within the population studied, that is accounted for by genetic differences among individuals in that population.

(2) So a heritability estimate of 0.8 means that genetic differences account for roughly 80% of the observed differences among individuals in the trait, while environmental differences among those individuals, together with measurement error, account for at most about 20%.

(3) If the full range of environmental differences actually present in the population accounts for at most about 20% of the variation among individuals, then an environmental intervention—even a large one, and even one that produces environments outside the observed range—could not change the population average by very much.

(4) Therefore, environmental interventions aimed at raising average levels of the trait in that population are unlikely to be effective.

(5) Therefore, resources intended to raise the trait should be shifted from environmental programs to genetic means, such as embryo selection.

I suspect there is a slide somewhere in this argument, but I want it located precisely rather than merely gesturing at the thought that heritability does not imply immutability. Is the argument valid? If not, at which step does it fail, and what exactly is the failure? In particular, address whether (1) and (2) license claims about the causes of a particular individual's trait, and whether a proportion of variance within one range of environments tells us anything about what would happen under a shift to a different range of environments.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
