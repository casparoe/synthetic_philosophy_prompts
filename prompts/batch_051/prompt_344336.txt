I run an undergraduate historiography reading group, and our last session on Thucydides ended in an argument that turned out to be about the epistemology of the distant past rather than about philology. The passage that set it off was Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 1.20 (trans. Richard Crawley):

"Having now given the result of my inquiries into early times, I grant that there will be a difficulty in believing every particular detail. The way that most men deal with traditions, even traditions of their own country, is to receive them all alike as they are delivered, without applying any critical test whatever."

The students disagreed sharply about what that "critical test" amounts to and how far it can take us. Some held that a tradition can transmit real knowledge across centuries if it survives critical inquiry; some held that only convergence among independent sources does the work; some held that non-textual remains such as inscriptions, archaeological layers, and radiocarbon dates are the only reliable court of appeal; some held that arranging the sources into a history always partly constructs the past rather than recovers it; and a few drifted toward the view that the distant past is simply unknowable. I would like an instrument that sorts the members of the group by what they actually accept about cases, not by what label they happen to like.

Please build me a decision tree, or a short "which epistemology of the distant past do you actually hold?" quiz, for this debate. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first derive branch questions from the commitments visible in the passage (that there is a real past to inquire into, that belief in "every particular detail" is separable from belief in the general result, and that tested traditions differ from merely received ones), then lay out the answer combinations, then locate actual positions among them. The audience is advanced undergraduates with no formal historiography background. Aim for eight branch questions and between eight and ten leaves. Every branch question should be a concrete case or claim answerable without knowing the positions—for example, a tradition reported by a single ancient author, an archaeological find that contradicts a text, two independent texts agreeing on odd details, or a war whose motives survive only in the victor's account—and should never ask something like "are you a realist about the past?" The leaves should include at least the following: a Thucydidean critical reconstructionism; an independent-attestation or convergence view; a material-evidence priority view; a narrative constructivism; a testimonial traditionalism that trusts transmitted accounts unless decisively refuted; and a Pyrrhonian refusal to claim knowledge of the distant past. For any answer combination that no live position occupies, say briefly whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and flag any two leaves the tree cannot really separate rather than pretending it can.

Return a single JSON object, with no prose outside it, using these fields: "audience" (a string describing the intended readers); "branch_nodes" (an array of objects with "id", "question", "answers", "rationale", and "routing", where "routing" maps each answer to the next node or leaf id); "leaves" (an array of objects with "id", "position", "path" as the list of question-answer pairs leading to it, and "commitment", a one-sentence statement of its most distinctive or awkward commitment); "empty_combinations" (an array of objects with "path", "diagnosis" being one of "incoherent", "unattractive", or "unexplored", and "note"); "inseparable_pairs" (an array of objects with "positions" and "why_the_tree_cannot_separate_them"); and "omitted_positions" (an array of objects with "position" and "reason_omitted_or_unreachable").
