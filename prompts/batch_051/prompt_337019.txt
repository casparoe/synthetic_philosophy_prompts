You are a professional philosopher whose specialty is value theory and the ethics of future generations, and you are being consulted on a problem that has been bothering me. I want you to work through it slowly, not just give me the first plausible answer. Begin by brainstorming the strongest candidate justifications for the asymmetry I describe, stress-test each against the hard variants I mention or variants of your own, and only then give your considered verdict. I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I want your own defended judgment.

Consider two facts about periods of cosmic time in which no human being exists. The first is in the past: for the first several billion years of the universe, there were no human beings, no human projects, no human memory, and no human concern. This fact does not disturb us. We do not mourn the people who never existed before the species existed, and we do not treat humanity's late arrival as itself a loss. The second fact is in the future: suppose I learn on excellent authority that human beings will come to a permanent end — no catastrophe, no suffering, just a final generation that lives out peaceful, fulfilled lives and dies without fear, after which no further human beings are ever born. I find this prospect devastating, and I suspect you do too, even after setting aside the ordinary badness of those final deaths.

The asymmetry is that human absence after me feels like a tragedy while human absence before me feels like no loss at all. But in both cases there is a stretch of time — one before my life, one after it — during which no human being exists, no human project is advanced, and nothing human is valued from the inside. So what, if anything, justifies treating the two stretches differently? Is there a principled asymmetry between the past and the future of the species — for example, in the forward-looking structure of projects and valuing, in the fixity of the past as against the openness of the future, or in the conditions under which a human life can be meaningful — or is the ordinary reaction an artifact of a more local and possibly irrational asymmetry in our attitude toward our own deaths, projected illegitimately onto the species? The future extinction I stipulated is just as fixed as the already completed past, so fixity alone cannot carry the weight, unless you can show why fixity plus direction of time matters.

Test your answer against a person who cares exactly as much about the missing human past as about the missing human future, against a culture whose practices are oriented backward toward ancestors rather than descendants, and against a version of the case in which the future contains no one I could ever know. Then tell me whether the ordinary asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned, and what your verdict implies about whether the badness of human extinction is a distinct kind of badness or only the shadow of individual death thrown forward.
