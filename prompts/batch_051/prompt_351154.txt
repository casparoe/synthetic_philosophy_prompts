After a career spent in the natural sciences, I have recently taken up philosophy, and I now meet regularly with a small reading group of retired colleagues and younger researchers. For several months two members of the group have been arguing past each other about the ownership of ideas. One holds a labor-based, natural-rights view: when a person creates a new discovery, invention, or expression through their own intellectual labor, they acquire a moral claim over it, and someone who uses or copies it without permission wrongs the creator quite apart from any social consequences. The other holds an instrumentalist view: an idea, once expressed, can be used by many people without anyone's use subtracting from anyone else's, so ideas are not the sort of thing that can be owned; intellectual property is at most a legal privilege a society grants and should calibrate solely by whether it promotes the production and spread of new ideas. They agree on far more than their arguments suggest, but nobody has yet made them write out where the agreement ends and the disagreement begins.

They have now agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and they have asked me to draft the document. I would like you to write it for me. It should be a constructive text both parties could sign without embarrassment, while making the residual disagreement exact rather than papering it over. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: statements about the nature of ideas, intellectual labor, legal rights, copying, attribution, and the public domain that both sides can affirm without qualification, phrased substantively rather than vacuously. Second, a disagreements table whose rows are the precise points of divergence; state each side's position in wording that side would accept as accurate and fair, and mark each row as normative, empirical, or mixed. Third, a section identifying the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would change at least one side's view. For each crux, describe a concrete case, observation, or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts it will show.

I have my own guesses about where the true cruxes lie. They include whether the fact that one person's use of an idea does not diminish another's use undermines the labor argument or merely shows that the object of the labor claim must be described carefully; whether independent creation and roughly simultaneous discovery show that new ideas cannot be fenced off in the way parcels of land or pieces of apparatus can; and whether the current system's long and broad protections are actually justified by their instrumental benefits, or whether both parties could accept something considerably weaker if the factual record pointed that way. You should not treat my guesses as constraints if the document you produce locates the genuine differences elsewhere.
