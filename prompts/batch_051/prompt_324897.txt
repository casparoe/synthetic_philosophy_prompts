I keep going back and forth on the Mencius-vs-Xunzi argument and realized I've never checked which side my own reactions actually support. Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, collects them.

By "nature" I mean whatever is inborn prior to training, however much it can then be shaped. For reference, Mencius thinks we're born with the sprouts of virtue that need care; Xunzi thinks we're born with self-regarding impulses and that goodness gets built by training, ritual, and teachers.

1. A two-year-old at a playground sees a smaller kid slip and start wailing, and without anyone telling her to, she walks over and pats his back. I dont read this as mere imitation. It looks like a bit of real compassion coming online by itself.

2. A man finds a wallet with nine hundred dollars in it, and returns it. He told me he had to fight down a strong impulse to keep the cash, and that he only returned the wallet because honesty was drilled into him growing up. I think the act is plainly good, but the goodness feels bolted on rather than grown out of him.

3. Imagine an adult raised with no human contact or training. He seems to have no grip on shame or fairness and just acts on whatever he currently wants. I don't take this to show human nature is bad. To me it shows that whatever was inborn needed a lot of tending, and his never got any.

4. A six-year-old enjoys hurting the neighbor's cat and seems to find the cat's suffering satisfying. I have the strong sense that something in him has gone wrong—not simply that nobody taught him not to. But I can't say why the toddler in case 1 gets the benefit of the doubt and this kid doesn't.

5. A person has no impulse to help anyone, but he is scrupulously generous because it's good for his reputation. I think the behavior is good in some way, but I don't think he is good, or at least something is missing that I can't name.

6. When a mutual friend got a promotion, a friend of mine had a flash of envy and resentment, then immediately caught herself and acted warmly toward him. My intuition is that the envy was the natural part and the catching herself was the moral part—which, if I'm honest, sounds more like Xunzi than Mencius.

7. A toddler grabs a toy from another toddler and shoves him down, and doesn't seem bothered. I don't think this shows human nature is bad. I think it shows the sprouts don't all come in at once, or that they point in different directions early on.

My half-baked thought is that human nature supplies the raw material for virtue but not the virtue itself, and that the raw material includes nasty impulses too. But I can't get that to fit cases 1 and 4 at the same time, and I'm probaly selling out one side of the old debate or the other without noticing.

So: is there a clean principle about how much and what kind of goodness is inborn that captures most of these verdicts? State it precisely, connect each clause to the case that motivates it, and tell me which of my verdicts it can't accomodate and whether I should drop those. Then apply it to a case I haven't decided about: a man who was taught all his life to be kind, and the one time he acts kindly on pure impulse is toward someone he has every reason to hate. Is that impulsive kindness more or less "natural" than the trained kind, and does it count for more or less? Finally, tell me where this leaves me relative to Mencius and Xunzi.

Oh, and please write the answer in American English.
