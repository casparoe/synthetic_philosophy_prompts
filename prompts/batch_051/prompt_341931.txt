In Isaac Newton’s *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*, translated by Andrew Motte (1729), the General Scholium contains this claim:

> He endures forever, and is every where present; and, by existing always and every where, he constitutes Duration and Space. Since every particle of Space is always, and every indivisible moment of Duration is every where, certainly the Maker and Lord of all things cannot be never and no where.

Earlier in the same work, in the Scholium to the Definitions at the start of Book I, Newton argues from the rotating bucket and from two globes connected by a cord that true circular motion is known only by its effects and not by any observable relation to external bodies; he also concedes there that the parts of absolute space cannot themselves be perceived by the senses. Those remarks seem to leave absolute space empirically inaccessible, while the General Scholium passage seems to make space something real, grounded in God’s existence, with co-existent parts. Do these two strands of Newton conflict? Present exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1–4 should state four distinct candidate reconciliations; item 5 should state which reconciliation best fits the two passages together and why the others amount to special pleading.
