Pretend you're a philosopher who has thought alot about Kant's universalizability test and about the consequentialist versions of it, and who is good at turning theory into usable rules. I work on a big platform's trust & safety team, and every week I make around ten judgement calls about borderline behavior: an account scrapes and mirrors every post from a small private group without consent; a coordinated campaign mass-downvotes a rival using real (not bot) accounts; someone posts hundreds of polite but empty AI-generated replies; users exploit loopholes in our rules. We constantly reach for a loose what-if-everyone-did-that test, but it gives us different answers depending on whos in the room. Turn that test into a decision procedure I can actually run in about twenty minutes per case, as a checklist with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision back to a person. Keep the two Kantian failure modes distinct -- the practice being unthinkable if universalized, versus thinkable but not rationally willable -- and separate both from the plain consequentialist question of whether the world would be worse if everyone did it; say roughly when those tests come apart. Then run the procedure on the three cases, make the reasoning visible, and include at least one case where the procedure would force me to allow something I'd personally want to ban. Flag the exact steps where a value judgement leaks in and what the whole approach is blind to.
