I keep turning over an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I'd like you to tell me where it comes apart. It's about how a superintelligent AI ought to talk to humans if it is extremely persuasive.

Suppose we build a system that is, by design, a near-perfect persuader: for any proposition it wants a person to accept, it can choose an utterance that gets the person to accept it, wether or not the proposition is true or supported by the evidence. The obvious safety rule is that such a system must not manipulate people. But the following argument seems to show that the rule either bans the system from ever speaking or collapses.

(1) For a near-perfect persuader, there is no way of speaking that leaves the hearer's belief up to the hearer, since whatever words it selects, the hearer's response is a function of that selection.
(2) If the speaker's choice of words determines the hearer's belief, then the belief is produced by the speaker's choice rather than by the hearer's own assessment of reasons.
(3) Producing belief in that way is manipulation, and it is wrong, or at least something we must design AI systems not to do.
(4) So the only safe policy is for a near-perfect persuader never to speak to humans at all.
(5) But a blanket ban like that proves too much: it would also forbid the system from telling us true and useful things, even life-saving things, merely because it could have phrased them differently.
(6) Therefore the principle behind (3) must be false: sometimes it is permissible to let a persuasive AI shape our beliefs.

I cannot accept (6), and I would much rather keep something like (3). So which step should I reject instead, and what does rejecting it cost?
