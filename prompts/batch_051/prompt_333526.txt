I've been reporting a piece on whether we can build a machine that does the right thing, and the more people I talk to — roboticists, philosophers, a few safety folks at AI labs — the more I realize they're not actually disagreeing so much as answering different questions. One person tells me the only sane approach is to hard-code rules like "don't deceive." Another says that's hopeless because every rule has exceptions its authors didn't foresee, and what you actually want is a system that can learn from thousands and thousands of human judgments about particular cases. A third says both are dodging the real issue, which is that a machine can't be held responsible, so we shouldn't let it make morally loaded decisions in the first place. I'd like to turn this into something interactive for readers: a "which school of machine ethics do you actually belong to?" sorting device.

Could you build me a decision tree (or a quiz with a scoring key, whichever makes the sorting sharper) for a general-interest science reader with no philosophy background? Each branch node should be a concrete case or claim on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so someone can answer it without already knowing which label it leads to. The leaves should include at least: hard-coded rules, learning from human judgments, a hybrid of the two, something like moral particularism about machines, a "humans must stay in the loop" position, and a "don't build these systems at all" position — plus any other leaf the logical space forces you to add. Use these cases at minimum: a care robot that lies to a dementia patient to get her to take her medication; an autonomous car in a no-win situation; and a customer-service LLM that has picked up a nasty pattern from its training data. Please flag any pair of positions your tree can't realy separate, and for any unoccupied combination of answers, say briefly whether you think it's empty because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or nobody's gotten around to defending it. Aim for no more than, say, nine branch nodes so it stays usable in a magazine.
