I am a university student in a political philosophy course, and we recently spent several sessions on Rawls's treatment of envy and resentment. I find myself strongly drawn to the distinction he draws. On his account, resentment is a properly moral sentiment: it responds to a wrong done to someone and presupposes principles of justice, so a well-ordered society can make room for it without embarrassment. Envy is different in kind. The envious person does not chiefly want the other to stop enjoying an undeserved advantage; he wants the other not to have the advantage at all, and would pay a cost to bring the other down even if doing so benefited no one. On this picture, envy is not a signal of injustice but an antisocial passion that a just society should dissolve rather than honor. That is an elegant, unified view, and it explains why we should distrust envy even when it is felt by the least advantaged.

But I keep running into a case that pushes the other way. A close friend of mine spent months preparing an application for a competitive internship and lost it to a classmate who obtained the position through a family connection, after a string of similar unearned opportunities. What I feel toward that classmate is not clean resentment. I do want him to lose something, and I would take some satisfaction in his being cut down even if no one else were made better off. Yet that ugly feeling also seems to be tracking something real, an unjust distribution of opportunity that my principled resentment is too slow to name. Rawls has a category, excusable envy, for cases in which inequalities are large enough or the social bases of self-respect are under threat, but that still treats envy as a foible to be excused rather than as a signal to be listened to.

I cannot tell which side should give way. Is the sharp envy/resentment distinction doing real normative work here, or is it protecting the theory at the cost of silence about a diagnostically useful emotion? Give me the strongest case for each side, and then say whether the right move is to preserve the distinction and treat my feeling as excusable envy or disguised resentment, to amend the distinction so that some envy deserves to be treated as a signal of injustice, or to abandon the idea that the emotion is tracking anything beyond my own frustrated comparison. What would justify each of these moves, and which would you make?
