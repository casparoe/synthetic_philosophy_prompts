I'm a novelist, and I'm trying to write a scene where a neuroscientist and an AI researcher get into it at a dinner party about whether a large language model is doing what a brain does. Reading around for that scene, I went down a rabbit hole into predictive processing and the free energy principle, and now I can't shake a worry about the grand version of the story. The grand version, as I understand it, says the brain is a prediction engine: it builds a model of the hidden causes of its sensory input, keeps generating top-down guesses about the next moment, and treats the mismatch as prediction error, which either updates the model or gets canceled by acting on the world. Friston's free energy principle goes further and says this isn't just a quirk of brains but a property of any self-organizing system that persists: to resist disorder it must minimize free energy, and for an organism surprising states are expensive and destabilizing, so perception, learning, and action all fall out of the same imperative.

What's bothering me is that this argument was built for creatures with a body, a stable boundary, homeostatic needs, and skin in the game. Enter LLMs, which are trained on exactly one thing: predict the next token. They minimize prediction error over a huge corpus of text, and in doing so they seem to grow an internal model of the world—they can reason about physics, people, plots. But they don't have a metabolism, they don't maintain a homeostatic envelope over time, and they only 'act' when you prompt them. So is the LLM case a vindication of the free energy story (look, minimzing prediction error gives you a world model), a problem for it (the system isn't ensuring its own survival, and there's no active inference in the usual sense), or just orthogonal? I'd like to know which specific premise is under pressure, and whether there's a repaired version of the argument that says something weaker but still true and useful. I suspect the answer would sharpen the neuroscientist's position in my scene.
