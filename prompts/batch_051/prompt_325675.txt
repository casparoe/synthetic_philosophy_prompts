In 1926 W. E. B. Du Bois argued in his *Crisis* essay *Criteria of Negro Art* that art is never politically neutral: every artwork functions as some kind of propaganda, and Black artists should therefore consciously make their work propaganda for racial emancipation rather than pretend to a false universality. Suppose that in January 1927 the editors of *The Crisis* had adopted this view as a binding editorial rule. Henceforth no poem, story, sketch, photograph, or essay would be accepted solely on the strength of its formal excellence; the literary editor would have to certify that a work's probable effect on Black and white readers would advance the struggle for full civil rights and Black self-respect, and a standing committee of three would reject submissions it judged merely decorative, decadent, defeatist, or politically ambiguous. The magazine announces the new rule publicly and invites readers to contribute work that furthers the cause.

Predict, with explicit reasoning, how Black literary and visual culture around *The Crisis* looks after twenty years, in 1947. In particular: which kinds of writers and genres flourish under the rule; which writers stop submitting or are rejected and where they go; what formal and tonal pressures the rule exerts on poems, stories, and visual art; how it blurs or sharpens the line between art and advocacy; how white publishers, critics, and patrons respond; and whether the policy achieves Du Bois's political aims or instead extracts costs that outweigh its gains. Where you are confident, say why; where the outcome depends on controversial assumptions, name them and say what would have to be true for the policy to succeed on Du Bois's own terms.
