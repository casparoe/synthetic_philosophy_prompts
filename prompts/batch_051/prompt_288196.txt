I am a retired research chemist who has come late to philosophy, and I have been working through the foundations of probability. Cox's theorem has become the most compelling piece of philosophy of science I know: start from the thin assumptions that degrees of plausibility are ordered and that any two ways of assessing the plausibility of the same proposition must agree, and it follows that those degrees must be transformable into ordinary probability assignments, with the product and negation rules forced on you as the only consistent option. That kind of derivation, getting the whole calculus from consistency, is exactly what I want in a foundation. And yet I cannot shake a conviction carried out of decades of actual inference: that some of the most important scientific judgments, such as which of two proposed reaction mechanisms better explains a new data set, or how seriously to take a colleague's half-formed hypothesis before the alternative set has even been articulated, are qualitative and only partially ordered. Assigning them a number, even a rough one, seems to falsify the structure of the judgment rather than refine it. On the theorem's reading, this conviction is either an illusion of introspection or an admission that I have not done the modeling work. On the intuition's reading, Cox's theorem does not justify probability as the logic of plausible reasoning; it characterizes a special case and leaves legitimate non-numerical states of evidence outside its scope.

I want a negotiated adjudication rather than a side-taking. Please organize the response as follows. (1) The case for the theory: why the consistency conditions are hard to reject, and the strongest debunking account of my intuition, for example as a confusion between the burden of eliciting degrees and the claim that no degrees exist (about 150 words). (2) The case for the intuition: what the qualitative comparison might be tracking, whether a partial order too coarse for any single real-valued measure, possibilities I cannot yet partition into a space over which numbers are well-defined, or something else, and why such states could be rational endpoints rather than merely incomplete models (about 150 words). (3) The compatibility reading: under what interpretation of degree of plausibility, or of what it is to be rational in holding one, the theorem and the intuition could both be true (about 120 words). (4) My verdict: what should give way or be amended if they cannot be reconciled, and the specific consideration that decides the matter (about 120 words).
