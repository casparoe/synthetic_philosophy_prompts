So I'm a journalist who covers ideas and social policy, mostly UBI stuff, and I've been digging through old pamphlets rather than the usual think-tank reports. Thomas Paine's 1797 pamphlet Agrarian Justice has this passage near the start that I can't stop chewing on:

"it is nevertheless true, that it is the value of the improvement only, and not the earth itself, that is individual property. Every proprietor therefore of cultivated land, owes to the community a ground-rent"

I've started calling this the "improvement-only" principle. My theory is that if Paine is right, then the unimproved earth is still a kind of common asset that every landowner is borrowing, and that debt doesn't get cancelled by buying the land later or by improving it. It just gets passed down the chain of title. So a UBI financed by a land value tax isn't actually a transfer from rich to poor; it's more like paying out the accumulated ground-rent that was never paid to the community. Some people in my orbit call this "Paine's dividend" and some call it a reductio ad absurdum.

I want to build a long feature around it. But before I pitch it, tell me honestly: is this a real argument or just a vivid metaphor? Has the "improvement-only" move already been worked out under another name, and does it collapse somewhere? For example, if I buy land today at a price that already capitalizes that ground rent, haven't I already paid the seller for the future rent? And where does the common inheritance become active—only at the first enclosure, or does it regenerate every time land changes hands? Give me the three strongest objections and say whether any is fatal rather than just awkward.
