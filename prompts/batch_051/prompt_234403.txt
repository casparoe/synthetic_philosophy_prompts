I've been going back through Kant's Groundwork, specifically the First Section where he tries to isolate the concept of a good will. He lays out what seems like a two-part test: an action has moral worth only if it's done from duty rather than from inclination, and its worth lies in the maxim, not in the results. The famous case he uses is the person who is naturally sympathetic and helps others because he loves doing it; Kant says that's nice but has no moral worth. Then he describes the friend of humanity whose own grief has put his sympathy to sleep, but who still helps because duty demands it; that action has moral worth. All that is in the text, but I keep being unsure about a boundary case.

Suppose someone, call him Jamie, used to be exactly the sympathetic type: he volunteered because the people's gratitude made him feel good. After a bad stretch of depression, the warm feelings basically disappear. But Jamie has spent years thinking about his obligations and has formed a personal policy: when someone asks for help, he checks whether the request is one he'd accept as a universal rule and, if so, acts. He still gets no satisfaction from it. Does Jamie's helping have moral worth on Kant's criteria? Now suppose another person, Priya, helps out of a desire to avoid the guilt she knows she'd feel if she didn't. Kant seems to treat guilt as just another inclination, but is that right? Is there anything in the categorical-imperative test itself that would let us classify Priya's action differently from Jamie's? If the criteria give the same verdict for both, does that show the criteria are missing something about acting for the right reasons?
