I'm in a philosophy of cognitive science course and we keep coming back to the question whether the "representations" people find inside neural networks are real or just a useful metaphor. I've been trying to make this precise by using this rough criteria for something in a system to count as a representation of X:

1. It carries information about X—i.e., it correlates with X in a way that would let you discriminate or predict X.
2. The rest of the system actually uses that information downstream, so it isn't just something a researcher reads into the model afterward.
3. The content is recoverable in a fairly simple, predictable way (like a linear probe), not only with a huge trained decoder that could find a dog-detector in pure noise.
4. It's at least partly content-specialized, so the same state isn't standing in for forty unrelated things at once.

Now the borderline case. Interpretability work on trained language models keeps finding features stored in superposition: instead of one neuron per feature ("this neuron is dog"), thousands of features live as directions in a shared high-dimensional activation space, and individual neurons are often polysemantic—a single neuron fires for Eiffel Tower, spaghetti, and a grab bag of other stuff. The feature is a direction, not a tidy unit. There's also the annoying wrinkle that with enough training data, you can often train a linear probe to decode almost any concept you decide to look for, including ones the model probably doesn't actually use.

Act as a philosopher of cognitive science who knows the interpretability literature, and think carefully about this as a borderline case. Start by brainstorming a few different ways one might apply each of my four criteria to a feature-direction in superposition. Then go criterion by criterion: does a direction inside superposition count as a representation of, say, "dog" under my criteria, or does the case show that one or more of the criteria are wrong? What would it mean for a network to genuinely represent something if superposition and polysemanticity are the norm rather than the failure case? I don't need a clean yes/no; I want the structure—where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and whether this case shows the whole idea of a neuron-level representation was the wrong place to look.
