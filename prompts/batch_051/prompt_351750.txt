I've been chewing on sovereign immunity lately — I don't have any academic background in law or philosophy, so I might be using these words a bit loosely. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that the state can't be sued without its own permission, and that in some strong sense the state can't be the kind of thing that legally wrongs you the way another person or a company can. Its always bothered me, because it seems to put the state in a special position where by definition it can't owe you anything for the harm it does. I had an idea for how a country could get around that, and I want someone to take the idea apart.

Suppose a country abolishes sovereign immunity and passes a law that says anyone can sue the state for wrongful harm just like they'd sue a company. But the damages aren't paid from a central fund or a general treasury line. Instead, any judgment or settlement comes out of the budget of the specific agency or department that caused the harm — the police force pays for police wrongs, the child welfare agency pays for child welfare wrongs, the prison service pays for prison wrongs. If the agency can't cover the judgment in a single year, the unpaid amount becomes a standing debt attached to that agency's future budgets, and servicing that debt comes before anything else the agency wants to spend money on, including salaries, maintenance, or its core work. Individual officials are not personally liable; the agency as a body carries the debt.

I'm not asking whether this is just, or whether its a good idea in the abstract. I want to know how it would actually behave. What would it push agencies to do? How would they game it or make themselves hard to collect from? What would it do to who sues, who settles, and who walks away empty? What happens over a few years — to how agencies operate, how courts rule, how the public reacts? And could any of the obvious failure modes be fixed without just turning it back into ordinary sovereign immunity?

One more thing. I know people who study this kind of thing genuinely disagree about the deeper questions, like whether the state can wrong someone the way an individual can, and whether sovereign immunity is defensible or just a relic. Please flag which parts of your answer are pretty settled and which parts are genuinely contested among people who know the area. Don't present one side of a live debate as if it's the consensus.
