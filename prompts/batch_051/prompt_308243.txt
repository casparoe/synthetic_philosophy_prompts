I'm putting together a seminar on mysticism and ineffability, and I keep coming back to William James's treatment in The Varieties of Religious Experience, the 1902 Gifford Lectures. He lays out four marks of mystical states: ineffability, a noetic quality, transiency, and passivity. The first one is the one that interests me. James says, roughly, that the handiest mark by which he classifies a state as mystical is negative: the person who has it says it defies expression, that no adequate report of its contents can be given in words, and that it must be directly experienced to be understood. But then he also says mystical states feel like knowledge; they carry a sense of insight, of seeing into depths of truth the discursive intellect can't reach, and he treats this noetic quality as just as characteristic as the ineffability. I'm trying to figure out how those two features are supposed to fit together, and what role each plays in distinguishing mysticism from other sorts of intense experience, or from mere emotional highs.

Here's the assignment I'd give you. Treat the four marks as competing criteria for a definition or identification of mystical experience. Assign each a weight from 0 to 1, weights summing to 1, according to how much work you think it does for James specifically in sorting mystical from non-mystical experience. Defend the weights, and tell me whether ineffability should carry the most weight or whether that just makes the category unworkable. If you had to rank the four, what would the ranking be? And here's the deeper question I'm after: can what cannot be said be known in any sense that isn't just a subjective feeling of certainty? I'd like your actual evaluation, not just the standard list.
