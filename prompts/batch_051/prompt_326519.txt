In a recent reading-group discussion about the hard problem of consciousness, two different people tried to cut off a debate by saying 'consciousness is an illusion' — and I am fairly sure they meant different things by it. The first used it to dismiss a question about phenomenal character: why there is something it is like to see red. When pressed, they clarified that they only meant that the sense of a continuous, unitary self is an illusion. That may be right, but it seems to leave the original question entirely untouched. The second person appeared to mean that there are no experiences at all, which sounds self-defeating: an illusion is itself something it is like to undergo. Separately, I know there is a philosophical position called illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, but I have seen it described both as the crude claim that consciousness does not exist and as the subtler claim that introspection systematically misrepresents our experiences as having private, ineffable, qualitative properties that nothing purely physical could have. I do not know which of these, if any, is the official view, or whether the slogan can carry any of them without collapsing.

Please lay out, as a list I can compare, the distinct precise claims that 'consciousness is an illusion' could be used to assert, ordered from the most deflationary or trivial to the most radical. For each reading, specify: what exactly is being called an illusion; to whom the illusion is supposed to appear; and whether the claim is something nearly everyone should already accept, a substantive but defensible philosophical thesis, or false or incoherent. Then tell me which of these readings would actually support the move of treating the hard problem as dissolved rather than merely relabeled, and which reading someone is most likely deploying if they use the slogan as a knockdown answer in a debate about the hard problem. I want a menu of precisifications with verdicts, not a single essay defending one interpretation.
