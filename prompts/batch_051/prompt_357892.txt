My university's animal-care committee is stuck, and I'm the one who has to get us unstuck by Friday. A neuroethology lab has asked to inject a small amount of dilute acetic acid into the arms of common octopuses — a sharp but brief sting — and then record what each animal does: whether it curls the injected arm in toward its body, grooms the site with its other arms and beak, and avoids the end of the tank where the injection happened on later trials. The lab argues no analgesia is needed on the grounds that nociception (the raw neural detection of tissue damage) plus associative learning can explain all of these behaviors, and they lean hard on Morgan's canon: don't attribute felt pain when a lower-level mechanism would do.

Two of my committee members keep countering that in any vertebrate, wound-directed grooming and place avoidance would be taken as evidence of pain and would trigger mandatory analgesia, and that Morgan's canon, applied this way, just protects a bias against crediting pain to animals whose inner lives feel alien to us. Our policy says researchers must intervene when a procedure is likely to cause more than brief pain, but it was written with vertebrates in mind and gives no test at all for an invertebrate. The commitee has gone in circles, and the members mostly agree we can't pretend the losing side has no real point; the memo has to state it honestly.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should land on a concrete recommendation — approve as written, approve with conditions, or reject — with a specific condition or change and a time frame where relevant, and it should actually work through Morgan's canon rather than just invoke or dismiss it.
