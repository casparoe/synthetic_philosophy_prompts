Below is a draft of a short essay on the old puzzle of how instrumental music can be sad. I would be grateful for rigorous comments on it. Please write in American English, as a continuous piece of prose in the manner of a thoughtful email or letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; lead with the comment that matters most rather than with a summary.

The essay runs as follows. The question how sounds can be sad is usually forced into a dilemma. On one horn, sadness is something the music itself has, but that seems to require an inner life somewhere in the sound, which an inanimate sequence of frequencies cannot have. On the other horn, calling music sad is loose projection or metaphor, a way of reporting what it does to susceptible listeners. Neither horn fits how we actually talk: the first offends a constraint on emotion ascription, while the second cannot explain why our judgments are shared and disciplined, why one can get a piece's sadness wrong, and why the sadness presents itself as perceived in the music rather than merely read into it.

I defend a third option. Music is sad by possessing the dynamic form of sadness: the falling contour, slackening tempo, narrowed range, soft attack, and ebbing phrase endings through which sadness also shows itself in a human voice, gait, or face. The form can be instantiated by ordered sound without any subject who feels it. I call this an expressive appearance, and I claim it is neither felt emotion nor metaphor. Metaphor, on my account, works by contrast between a literal and a transferred use; but the dynamic form is literally in the sound and perceptually available to a competent listener, roughly as sadness is visible in a face without being inferred from an inner state. The facial analogy is the load-bearing part: seeing sadness in a face is a way of perceiving an expression, not a way of detecting a ghost; hearing sadness in music is the same kind of perception, only without the person behind it.

The final section makes a stronger claim. What music presents, I suggest, is not a set of discrete nameable emotions but a continuous space of expressive dynamics, regions of which we label sad, triumphant, nostalgic, and the like. On this view the special mystery of musical sadness largely dissolves, because the unit of analysis was never the discrete felt state named by the word sad. I expect this to be the most exposed part of the argument, and I have a nagging suspicion that the essay may simply be relabeling a familiar view rather than improving it.

I would like comments on four points. First, whether the distinction between felt emotion and expressive appearance actually does the work I assign it, or whether the third option collapses back into one of the original horns when probed; be specific about where the collapse would occur. Second, whether the metaphor claim is defensible, or whether I have bought a controversial theory of metaphor at too high a price and could reach the same conclusion without it. Third, whether the facial analogy carries weight or is only vivid decoration, and whether disanalogies between faces and music should make me abandon it. Fourth, what class of musical cases would be hardest for the account to describe, and what would have to be true for those cases to refute the account rather than merely complicate it. End by suggesting one revision that would let the essay claim less while saying something more interesting.
