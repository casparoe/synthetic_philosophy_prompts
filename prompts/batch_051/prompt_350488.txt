I am a retired behavioral ecologist. Most of my working life went into questions about how animals generalize from very few encounters — how a predator that has eaten one toxic prey item knows which further items to avoid — and in retirement I have been reading philosophy of induction and concept formation. Bongard problems now strike me as the cleanest laboratory version of my old question: six simple diagrams are placed on the left, six on the right, and a solver must infer the rule that separates the two groups from those examples alone. The philosophical trouble is familiar, but sharper here than in the usual textbook discussions: since endlessly many rules fit the six examples on each side, what, if anything, makes one of them the correct rule?

I would like a sorting instrument for my reading group, which is a mix of retired and working scientists. Please write a "which theory of rule induction from few examples do you actually hold?" quiz of eight to ten questions. Each question should be a concrete Bongard-style case, described in words, with two or three answer options; the point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so no question should ask them to choose a philosophical position. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto the following leaves, though you may add or merge leaves where your cases force it:

- Rule realism: a well-posed Bongard problem has a determinate correct rule, usually the designer's intended rule, and ambiguity is a defect of the puzzle rather than a fact about induction in general.
- Simplicity objectivism: the correct rule is the shortest or least ad hoc rule that separates the groups, and human salience tracks this only imperfectly.
- Cognitive-salience naturalism: the correct rule is whatever a normal human visual system finds overwhelmingly salient, because the puzzles are about human perceptual concepts rather than rules in the abstract.
- Pragmatism: a rule is correct relative to a purpose or an intended distinction; absent such a purpose, asking which rule is the real one has no answer.
- Underdetermination skepticism: almost every Bongard problem admits wildly different fitting rules, so solving one is best understood as guessing a convention rather than discovering a fact.
- Adaptationist or learning-theoretic view: the right generalization is whatever an inductive bias together with a history of reward or selection would converge on in the relevant environment.

Use the following two cases as the first two questions, and compose the rest yourself.

First case. The left six panels are six small circles; the right six panels are six large squares. The rule "circle versus square" and the rule "small versus large" each separate the groups perfectly. Candidate options: (i) circle versus square is the correct rule, because shape outranks size; (ii) size is equally legitimate, so the problem is ambiguous; (iii) there is no fact of the matter unless we know the designer's intent, in which case the intended rule is correct.

Second case. The left six panels each show two identical figures; the right six panels each show two different figures. The relational rule "same versus different" fits perfectly, but so does a baroque disjunctive rule that simply lists the twelve specific panels. Candidate options: (i) the relational rule is the only acceptable one, because the disjunctive rule is plainly gerrymandered; (ii) both fit, but the relational rule is preferred for pragmatic reasons of brevity or teachability, not because it is more true; (iii) strictly speaking neither is more correct, and choosing between them is a convention.

The remaining six to eight cases are yours, but they should probe at least the following: whether real-world background knowledge may enter the rule; whether a rule only a trained observer would notice can be genuinely correct; whether shrinking the examples to two and two rather than six and six changes anything; and whether a non-human animal learning the same discrimination has a different standard of correctness. Build from logical space: first set out the underlying yes-or-no questions or choice points, then place the labeled positions in that space, and note any unoccupied combinations, explaining whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Tell me plainly which leaves your cases cannot separate and where any boundary is being drawn by stipulation.

Do not include a reading list or citations; I want the instrument and its reasoning, not a survey of the literature. Please answer in British English, although I have written this request in American English. Finally, end with a short note saying where you are least confident in the scoring key, which question is doing the least discriminating work, and what empirical finding about human or non-human performance on these cases would lead you to redraw the tree.
