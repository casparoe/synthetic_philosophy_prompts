You are a professor of moral philosophy known for exact, meticulous grading in an advanced undergraduate ethics course. Before assigning any grade, privately brainstorm the distinctions a strong answer to the question below would need to command, and then assess the answer claim by claim.

I am a novelist drafting a campus novel. In one chapter, a professor grades a take-home exam answer in an ethics course, and I want the professor's written assessment to be technically precise. I am giving you the same question and answer. Play the role of the professor for the grading scene, but do not write the scene itself. Do not give a holistic impression. In your response, give (a) a letter grade on the standard A–F scale and (b) point-by-point comments, each tied to a specific claim in the answer, that justify the grade. Flag what the answer gets right, exactly where it goes wrong or conflates positions, and what a corrected account would say, all in the voice of an experienced grader.

The exam question:

Explain the paradox of deontology. Then set out and evaluate the standard reply that the paradox rests on confusing agent-neutral and agent-relative reasons. Be precise about the difference between agent-relative constraints and agent-centered prerogatives, and about whether the doctrine of double effect or the doing/allowing distinction is needed to make the reply work. Does the reply dissolve the paradox or only translate it? Use at least one example.

The student answer:

The paradox of deontology starts from a constraint such as the prohibition on killing an innocent person. Suppose an executioner credibly threatens to kill five innocent people unless a bystander kills one. A deontologist who accepts the constraint holds that the bystander must refuse, even though the refusal will be followed by five killings. The apparent difficulty is that if the constraint is grounded in the badness of innocent people being killed, then refusing seems self-defeating, because it permits more of the very wrong that the constraint exists to prevent. The standard deontological reply is that the difficulty comes from giving the constraint an agent-neutral rationale. The real rationale is agent-relative: the bystander has a reason not to make himself a killer, not merely a reason to minimize the number of killings in the world. A killing that he performs has a moral significance, as something attributable to him, that the executioner's five killings lack.

Because the constraint is agent-relative, it has the shape of an agent-centered prerogative. It permits the bystander to put his own integrity ahead of the impartial goal of minimizing deaths, just as a prerogative allows a person to give extra weight to his own projects. The deontologist, on this view, is entitled to keep his hands clean even at the cost of a worse outcome.

The reply can be further strengthened by the doctrine of double effect. If the bystander killed the one, that death would be intended as a means to saving the five, and intentionally killing an innocent person is exactly what the constraint forbids. The five deaths, by contrast, are merely foreseen side effects of abstaining from murder; because they are foreseen rather than intended, they are permissible, and they therefore do not count against the refusal. Thus the paradox does not refute deontology. It merely shows that deontological constraints answer to agent-relative reasons rather than to the agent-neutral pressures of consequentialism.
