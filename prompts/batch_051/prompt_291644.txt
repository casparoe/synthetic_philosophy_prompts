I've been working through hinge epistemology and Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and I find that I can recognize the difference between a doubt I can take seriously and a doubt I can only mouth, but I can't state the principle behind it. Here are my verdicts on seven cases; I want the principle that captures them, if there is one.

(1) When a friend tells me in ordinary conversation that he isn't sure he has hands because he might be a brain in a vat, I don't treat this as a doubt he actually holds; it strikes me as a philosophical maneuver, and I have no idea what evidence would speak to it. (2) When I can't find my keys, I genuinely doubt that they're in my bag, and I look. (3) When someone claims that George Washington probably never existed, I think he's wrong, but I treat the claim as a normal historical hypothesis that testimony and archives could bear on. (4) When someone claims the earth is only a few thousand years old, I also think he's wrong and I reach for geology; but I notice that my confidence that the earth is old is firmer than any particular radiometric date or stratum, and in that respect it doesn't feel like an ordinary empirical belief. (5) When a skeptic suggests that the whole past — fossils, memories, records — might have come into existence five minutes ago as an apparent past, I can't make that a live possibility either; it seems to dissolve the background against which anything counts as evidence. (6) When a child says that maybe two plus two equals five, I don't hear a rival arithmetical hypothesis that ought to be weighed; I hear someone who hasn't yet mastered a practice. (7) When I wonder whether my friend is angry with me, I can be wrong in a perfectly ordinary way, and tone, context, and later behavior are exactly what would settle it.

Is there one principle — something like a hinge-commitment account — that yields all seven verdicts? I'm especially troubled by the contrast between (4) and (5): I want to argue against the young-earth claim with evidence, yet I also want to say my belief in an old earth is sturdier than any particular piece of evidence for it, while the five-minute-old-world claim seems to close down the very game in which evidence is played. Please also rule on a further case I keep wobbling on: someone who, after reading about a possible astronomical catastrophe, treats whether the sun will rise tomorrow as a genuinely open empirical question in the ordinary, practical way — is that the same sort of doubt as the keys case, or is it the Humean worry about induction in disguise? Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the principle; how it sorts the seven cases; which of my verdicts it forces me to give up; its verdict on the sunrise case; and its sharpest remaining problem.
