I was arguing with a friend last night about whether his phone is literally part of his mind. He keeps his commitments, his to-do list, and directions in a cloud-synced notes and reminder app, and he insists that by Clark and Chalmers's parity principle the app is as much his memory as anything in his skull. I thought that was clearly absurd: the app is also synced to his wife's phone, she edits some of the notes, the app reorganises entries during overnight updates, and he sometimes acts on a note while unsure whether he or his wife wrote it. We went round in circles, and I realised we never actually tested the passage against the details of his case.

Here is the passage, from Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind", Analysis 58 (1998): 10–23.

> If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process.

The authors were thinking of Otto's notebook: a single, stable, personally curated resource. My friend's case differs in the three ways I mentioned. Please give a careful ruling on whether the parity principle, read as Clark and Chalmers meant it, reaches this case. Work through the words first, then the purpose of the principle, then what the authors would most plausibly have said in 1998 if asked about a shared, auto-syncing, algorithmically reordered notebook. If those three approaches come apart, say which should win and why, and say whether the three differences matter individually or only in combination.
