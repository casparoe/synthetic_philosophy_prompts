I'm a journalist who covers ideas at the intersection of language, logic, and the sciences of mind, and I've been asked to teach a single two-hour evening class on Russell's theory of descriptions to an adult-education group, none of whom has formal training in logic. I don't need the theory summarized; I need a plan for the session, built around this passage from Bertrand Russell's "On Denoting," published in Mind in 1905:

"Take as an instance 'the father of Charles II was executed'. This asserts that there was an x who was the father of Charles II and was executed. Now the, when it is strictly used, involves uniqueness; we do, it is true, speak of 'the son of So-and-so' even when So-and-so has several sons, but it would be more correct to say 'a son of So-and-so'."

Give me the plan: the ideas that must be in place before the sentence beginning "This asserts that there was an x..." can make its intended point, and what depends on what; the one carrying example for the evening, whether that should be the passage's own "father of Charles II" example or a better one and on what grounds, and where "the present King of France" does or doesn't belong; the misconceptions this audience will arrive with or form as the sequence unfolds, and the exact point where each should be headed off; what should be deliberately left out at this level and named as omitted; and the one closing question that would tell me they've grasped the analysis rather than memorized a paraphrase.
