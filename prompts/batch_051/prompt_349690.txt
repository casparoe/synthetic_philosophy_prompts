I am a PhD candidate in political science working on the measurement of political violence, and I have spent the past semester in an interdisciplinary seminar on long-run trends in violence that has been circling the same disagreement without any visible convergence. The two camps have by now learned each other's arguments well enough to recite them. One side holds that the long-run decline of violence is robust: homicide rates, deaths in war as a share of the human population, and cognate indicators all trend downward across centuries and recent decades, and the finding survives most reasonable adjustments. The other side holds that the finding is an artifact of contestable decisions in operationalization: counting only direct, physical, and often lethal harm while excluding structural violence, preventable poverty-related mortality, carceral violence, and forms of state repression; normalizing by population, which allows absolute numbers of victims to rise while rates fall and lets a few peaceful decades mask regional reversals; and relying on historical data whose quality degrades the further back one looks. What unsettles me is that the two groups are not disputing the same spreadsheets anymore. We have sat together with the same battle-death series and the same homicide estimates, and everyone agrees about what the numbers say under each coding rule; the disagreement is about which coding rules to adopt and what the resulting claim is a claim about.

My working hypothesis is that this is less a factual dispute than a disagreement about the point of the concept of violence — one side using it as an epidemiological-explanatory category for comparing rates of direct physical harm, the other using it as a morally loaded category that ought to track avoidable suffering broadly construed — and that, on this reading, there is no single fact of the matter about whether the world as such has become less violent, only facts about particular indicators under particular decisions. But I can see at least two competing diagnoses: it may be a genuine empirical disagreement about long-run trajectories dressed up as a conceptual quarrel, or a methodological disagreement about what counts as a reasonable measurement convention. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right. What kind of disagreement is this — verbal, conceptual, factual, methodological, or some mixture — and what, given that diagnosis, would count as progress? If you think the question as usually posed is ill-posed, say what the better-posed question would be and whether the two camps could converge on it.
