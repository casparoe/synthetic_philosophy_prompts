I am a philosopher of science, and for the first chapter of a book on induction I have been collecting my own intuitive verdicts about which generalizations may be projected from their observed instances. I would be glad of a substantive account rather than a bibliography: I want to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts, and where it forces revisions.

Consider the following seven cases. The definitions are in my own words.

1. All emeralds examined so far — in many places, under varied conditions — have been green. I confidently expect the next emerald examined to be green, and I take the evidence to support "all emeralds are green."

2. Let a thing be grue if and only if it is first examined before some fixed future time t and is green, or it is first examined at or after t and is blue. All emeralds examined so far were first examined before t and were green; so the evidence equally supports "all emeralds are grue." Yet I do not expect the next emerald examined after t to be blue, and I regard the grue generalization as a trick rather than as a genuine competitor to the green one.

3. Suppose a geologist stipulates that a thing is emeraud if and only if it is green when located in valley V and blue when located elsewhere. Every sample examined so far, all from valley V, has been green and hence emeraud. I do not expect samples from outside V to be blue, and I treat the emeraud generalization as gerrymandered.

4. Let a thing be bleen if and only if it is first examined before t and is blue, or it is first examined at or after t and is green. All sapphires examined so far were first examined before t and were blue; hence they were bleen. I do not expect newly examined sapphires after t to be green on that basis.

5. A zoologist studying a newly discovered island species has observed many individuals, all from the island's lowlands, and all are crested. I expect the next individual observed on that island to be crested, but I would not extend the expectation to a nearby island. The island boundary feels principled, because the species is known to be confined to the one island.

6. Every patient with infection Q who was treated before the introduction of drug D died; no post-introduction patient has yet been observed. I do not predict that the first post-introduction patient will die. The introduction of D seems a causally relevant boundary; "dies if treated before D exists" does not strike me as a projectible property.

7. Now define smeen so that a thing is smeen if and only if it is green when first examined on a Tuesday and blue when first examined on any other day. All emeralds examined so far were first examined on Tuesdays and were green; hence they were smeen. I do not expect an emerald first examined on a Wednesday to be blue. The day-of-week boundary strikes me as arbitrary, and its arbitrariness is doing all the work in my judgment.

I am drawn to the following two-part principle, which I cannot get to cohere.

P1: A predicate is projectible only if the boundaries of time, place, or disjunction that occur in its definition are relevant to the property in question; an arbitrary boundary defeats projectibility.

P2: Projectibility is a feature of the world rather than of our vocabulary; if two predicates are intertranslatable without loss of empirical content, they should have the same projectibility status.

P1 appears to exclude grue, emeraud, bleen, and smeen while letting green through. P2, however, seems to make the green/grue asymmetry disappear, because green and blue are intertranslatable with grue and bleen in a language that takes time-relative color terms as basic. If P2 is right, a community that always projected grue would be no worse off than one that always projects green, and my case-2 verdict would be mere vocabulary chauvinism. If P1 is right, I need a non-question-begging account of "arbitrary" that survives translation. I cannot see how to have both.

Please structure your response under the following headings, in this order:

1. Candidate principle (roughly 250 words). State the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures the verdicts above, precisely enough to apply mechanically. Motivate each clause by the case it is meant to handle.

2. Fit with my cases (roughly 300 words). Run the proposed principle through cases 1–7 individually. State which of my verdicts it delivers and which, if any, it overturns.

3. New cases and predictions (roughly 250 words). Give at least three further cases not on my list, chosen to stress-test the principle, and state its verdict on each. Also state what the principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: whether a community using grue/bleen vocabulary as basic would be justified in projecting grue, or whether our refusal to project grue is parochial.

4. Forced revisions (roughly 150 words). Tell me plainly which of my verdicts, or which of P1 and P2, must be revised or abandoned, and with what replacement.

5. Hardest remaining problem (roughly 100 words). Identify the deepest unresolved issue for the position you propose, and say what kind of consideration would decide it.
