I'm a science reporter covering ideas about infinity, and I'm trying to predict how one philospher I'm profiling would rule on a new supertask case. I'm working from a copy of James F. Thomson's 1954 Analysis paper Tasks and Super-Tasks. The part I keep coming back to is where he sets up the lamp and then argues, if I follow him, that if the lamp is on at the end, something must have turned it on, but the series of flips has no last member; same if it's off. So the stated procedure doesn't fix a final state. He seems to locate the problem in there being no last change, not just in the on/off sequence failing to converge.

Over the years, this philosopher has issued a bunch of one-line rulings on supertask puzzles. Here are the ones I'm sure about:
- On Thomson's lamp, exactly as Thomson describes it: she says the final state is underdetermined; the instructions don't settle it.
- On a modified Thomson lamp where, after the infinite run of flips, someone gives the switch one ordinary final flick at exactly two minutes: she says the final state is whatever that last flick set it to; the preceding infinite toggling doesn't matter to the final outcome.
- On a runner approaching an infinite sequence of barriers, where each barrier stops her only if she has not already been stopped: she says the setup is impossible; no particular barrier can do the stopping, but she can't pass them all either.

Now here's the new case I want to predict. A display starts at 0. At t=1, the machine adds 1/2. At t=1.5, it adds 1/4. At t=1.75, it adds 1/8, and so on, each time adding half the previous amount. There is no final action and no post-supertask stipulation. After two minutes, is the displayed total determined, and if so what is it?

I can see at least two rules that fit her earlier decisions. One is a limit rule: the final state of a supertask is the limit of the finite states, when that limit exists. That rule gives 1. The other is a final-change rule: a final state is fixed only if there is a last change that sets it; if there is no last change, the state is underdetermined. That rule says the displayed total is not determined, even though the finite sums converge.

Predict which way she'd go if she's tracking the principle from the Thomson passage. Identify the closest analog among her earlier rulings. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, say what principle she'd be adopting either way, and suggest the further case that would best tell the two rules apart.
