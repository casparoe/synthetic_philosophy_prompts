Someone on a forum I read floated a legal reform that's been stuck in my head. Take every strict-liability regulatory offense and split it into two tracks. The default is the old strict-liability charge but with the penalty capped at a civil-style fine and no criminal record. The defendant can instead opt into a 'fault track': the prosecutor has to prove negligence under a reasonable-person standard, with the Hand formula (B < PL) written into the statute, but a conviction there carries triple the old penalty and does give you the record. The election has to be made before discovery, so you can't peek at the prosecutor's evidence first. The pitch was that this smuggles a mens rea requirement back into regulatory law without gutting enforcement. On paper it's a screening mechanism—poeple who actually took care should self-select into the fault track and beat the charge, while everyone else eats the fine and moves on. I suspect the real behavior is weirder. Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral question: who opts in and who doesn't, what does this do to prosecutorial charging and to how corporate compliance departments behave, where does it get gamed first, and does it fix the punishing-without-fault problem or just relocate it?
