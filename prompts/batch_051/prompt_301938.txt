Me and my friend have been going back and forth about the phrase "what if everybody did that?", and I've noticed something weird. When the target is fare-dodging, or grabbing more than your share of free samples, the question feels decisive: the trains couldn't run if nobody paid, the samples would disappear if everybody took double, and the dodger or grabber is plainly counting on everyone else not doing it. But when the target is, say, somebody announcing they'll have exactly one kid, or go into therapy rather than plumbing, or spend their savings on a year of travel instead of starting a business, the same move seems to apply — if everyone had one kid the population would crash, if everyone became a therapist there'd be nobody fixing the pipes, if everyone spent their savings on travel the economy would shrink — and yet we file those under "personal choice," not "moral failure." Somebody who says "yeah, but what if everyone did that?" in response to your career plan sounds a bit unhinged.

So the test clearly tracks something narrower than the slogan says it does. I'm trying to figure out what. Is it just marking out free-riding on a cooperative scheme you still want other people to uphold, with the universalization line acting as a rough diagnostic for that? Or is there a more general principle — something like the universal law formula in Kant, or Hare's idea that an ought-claim commits you to a universal prescription for everyone in relevantly similar circumstances, or the move Singer makes when he pushes from the drowning child to distant poverty — that would generate both judgments and explain why the test lands in one case but not the other? Give me the strongest candidate principle you can find, test it against a couple of nearby cases, and then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.
