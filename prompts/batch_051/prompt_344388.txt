I'm an M.A. student in a seminar on teleology and providence in history, and a dispute came up last week that I think was closed with a slogan rather than an argument. We were debating whether the long sequence running from the abolition of the slave trade through decolonization and the civil rights movement to, say, the legal recognition of same-sex marriage shows a cumulative direction in human affairs. One participant insisted it does: there is an arc visible in the record. Another replied that this is selection — people inside each of those struggles could not have known which way history was tending, and the same two centuries produced industrial warfare, famine, and totalitarian regimes that look nothing like justice bending anything. Someone then invoked the line about the arc of history bending toward justice, and the discussion moved on as if a case had been made. I doubt that any precise claim was actually stated.

What I want is a disambiguation of the slogan *the arc of history bends toward justice*. Treat it as hiding several candidate theses and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be used to express. I can see at least four: a modest empirical claim about the long-run direction of some social indicators; a strong providential or immanent-teleological claim that justice is the end toward which history moves; a normative claim that justice is the standard by which history ought to be judged rather than a property it displays; and a rhetorical or practical stance addressed to the present rather than a prediction about the future. These blur together in everyday use, and I suspect the line gets its authority from that blurring. For each candidate reading, say what kind of claim it is, whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false (or indeterminate), and above all whether that reading would support the work the slogan was made to do in our exchange: licensing confidence that present and future injustices will in the relevant sense be overcome, or at least that historical precedent gives grounds for such confidence. Be explicit about whose long run each reading invokes, and whether on that reading the saying is functioning as a prediction about how things will go or as a demand about how we should act.

Please format your answer as follows. First, a table with one row per reading and these columns: Reading (a short label); Precise thesis (one or two sentences); Kind of claim (e.g., empirical, theological, metaphysical-teleological, normative, rhetorical); Verdict (trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or indeterminate, with a clause saying why); Supports the use (yes, no, or only if amended, specifying the amendment). Second, one short paragraph stating which reading, or which combination, the slogan had to be expressing in that discussion if the invocation was to count as settling the argument — and whether, on that reading, it actually settles it.
