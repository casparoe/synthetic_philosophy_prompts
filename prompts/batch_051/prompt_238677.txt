I'm giving a presentation next month to a law-and-technology working group: mostly evidence-law scholars and media lawyers, plus two First Amendment specialists and a former photojournalist who now consults on digital verification. I will be defending the following claim. The most serious epistemic danger of deepfakes is not that particular fabricated recordings will be believed, but that deepfakes erode the epistemic backstop for recorded evidence — the widely shared default assumption that audio and video of public figures are probably authentic unless there is a specific reason for doubt. My further claim is that this backstop cannot be rebuilt by better detection tools, because the erosion is driven less by any single convincing fake than by the public's knowledge that cheap, convincing fabrication is now available; the more stable response is therefore institutional: require independent corroboration before recordings are treated as decisive, reduce the default evidential weight that courts and journalists assign to them, and stop treating the mere existence of a video as a neutral endpoint of an argument.

I spent last evening arguing about this with a friend, and he pressed exactly where I am softest. He said I was running together two different things: people coming to believe more falsehoods, and people believing recordings less. If the second happens, he argued, that is simply an appropriate recalibration of everyone's credences rather than a crisis, and calling it a lost backstop is vivid language that does no argumentative work. I held the line on the distinction but could not defend why the erosion of a shared backstop is worse than a mere downward shift in trust.
