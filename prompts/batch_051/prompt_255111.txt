I'm in sixth form and we've been doing a unit on critical thinking, and a few weeks ago my teacher showed us a bunch of pictures of paintings and asked us to sort them into forgeries, copies, fakes, originals and so on. The class spent the whole lesson argiung about two of them, and I've been mildly stuck on it since. Part of the problem is that we never settled what a "forgery" actually is: does it need an intention to deceive when the thing is made, or is it about how the object gets passed around later, or is it something about the object itself? I don't want a dictionary defintion, I want a working defintion that does some actual sorting work. The bigger question behind it is why an original matters at all when nobody can tell the difference, but I think I need the concept pinned down first.

Here are the cases I keep going back and forth on. (1) A practice copy of a Constable I paint myself, with my initials on the back — clearly not a forgery. (2) A painting made last year by a skilled copyist to look like a lost Caravaggio and signed with his name, sold to a museum as newly rediscovered — clearly a forgery. (3) An artist paints "in the manner of" a living famous painter and signs that painter's name as a private joke between friends; years later a gallery sells it without correcting the signature. (4) A restorer gets a genuine old painting with a destroyed bottom corner and repaints the missing bit so well that nobody can tell where the original ends; my instinct is to say the painting isn't a forgery, but I can't put my finger on why, since part of it is new and meant to pass. (5) A good-quality print of a Van Gogh from the museum shop, with "reproduction" printed on the back — clearly not. (6) An anonymous 16th-century picture that a dealer honestly attributes to a named master and sells as such; later experts agree it's by some lesser unknown painter, and the buyer feels cheated. I lean not-a-forgery here, but again I'm shaky. (7) Someone types "a lost Rembrandt" into an image generator, then signs the output with Rembrandt's name and tries to sell it as a real one. My gut says forgery, but then I wonder whether that's weird, because the machine didn't intend anything and the person didn't paint it.

So: propose a definition of forgery that gets your own verdicts on these cases right, run all seven through it, and tell me where it gives an answer you didn't expect or no answer at all, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Then, the bit I actually care about: tell me which of your seven verdicts you'd give up first, and what kind of argument or case would make you change the whole defintion — I want to see the reasoning's weak spot, not just the answer.
