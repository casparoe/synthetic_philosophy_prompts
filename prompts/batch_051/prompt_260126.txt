Henri Poincaré, in *Science and Method*, chapter IV, Chance, asks us to imagine a cone balanced exactly on its point. His point, as I understand it, is that we know the cone will fall, but we cannot say to which side, because the side it takes is decided by differences too small for our senses to register. A very small cause produces a very large and visible effect, and Poincaré says we call the result chance when our ignorance of the minute causes prevents prediction. He uses this to distinguish chance that arises from amplified small causes from chance that consists in the coincidence of independent causal series.

Now consider the popular butterfly-effect image: a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil can set off a tornado in Texas, and this is supposed to show that the atmosphere is unpredictable. The move from Poincaré’s cone to the butterfly is usually treated as a harmless update, but I want an audit of how far it really is the same picture.

What corresponds to what between the balanced cone and the butterfly? Where does the mapping break down? Pay particular attention to whether the butterfly image quietly imports assumptions the cone does not have—for example, that the system’s state evolves on a strange attractor, that local divergence is paired with global confinement, that we have a specific causal link from one perturbation to one event, or that the unpredictability is a feature of finite computational beings rather than an intrinsic feature of the system. Which of those additions, if any, are doing real work when people infer from the butterfly to a conclusion about long-range weather prediction? And does the conclusion usually drawn from the butterfly—small causes, big effects, therefore prediction is impossible in principle or in practice—survive once the disanalogies with Poincaré’s cone are on the table? If not, what would a better image be?
