I'm in a democratic theory seminar and every week we circle back to who should get the vote — lowering the age to 16, letting permanent residents vote in municipal elections, whether non-resident citizens keep theirs, felon disenfranchisement, compulsory voting — and every time someone picks one flashpoint case, reverse-engineers a principle from it, and then acts like the other cases are settled. Before I write my final paper I want the spec, not another theory. Write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of the franchise has to meet: the cases it has to handle or at least give a principled answer to (children, non-resident citizens, permanent residents, prisoners, people with serious cognitive disabilities — maybe even future people if a reform would lock in its own electorate), the distinctions it can't collapse (subject to the laws vs. affected by the laws vs. having a stake vs. being competent vs. being a member), and the facts or empirical results it can't casually contradict — with the reason each item is a requirement. It should also take a stand on whether voting is a right, a duty, or neither, since the compulsory-voting debate hinges on that. Flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just one camp's preference dressed up as a constraint. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the popular views, whether any pair of genuine requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and keep straight what's actually settled vs. genuinely contested in the lit — I don't want a contested view presented as the consensus. A table for the requirements is fine, but skip a full scorecard of theories.
