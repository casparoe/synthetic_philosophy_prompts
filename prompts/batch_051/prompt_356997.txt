a friend and i just spent three hours arguing about whether our values are actually many different things or just one thing wearing disguises, and by the end i had defended a whole pile of claims. he says two of them obviously contradict each other, and i think he's wrong about that particular pair, but now i can't shake the feeling that the real problem is somewhere else in the pile. can you tell me whether this whole set is jointly tenable?

(1) I'm a value pluralist: achievement, close relationships, pleasure, not being humiliated, aesthetic experience, and so on don't all reduce to one underlying thing like happiness or preference-satisfaction, and they're not just different sources of the same stuff.

(2) But I don't believe in incommensurable options. For any two outcomes, I think one is better, the other is better, or they're exactly equal. When someone says two options are incomparable, I hear that they just haven't worked out the weights yet.

(3) I also think some values are lexically ordered: no amount of the lower one outweighs even a small amount of the higher one. My go-to example is that no amount of added mild pleasure makes it ok to subject someone to an hour of undeserved agony.

(4) But I'm fine with trading off other pairs, like a little privacy for a lot of safety. I think people who treat privacy as something consequences can never touch are dressing up a preference as a principle.

(5) I lean particularist about reasons: whether a feature counts as a reason, and how much weight it has, depends on context, and I'm suspicious of fixed weights that are supposed to hold everywhere.

(6) But I also think there's at least one exceptionless rule: never knowingly punish an innocent person, not even to stop a riot or prevent some bigger evil.

(7) I think for public policy you can't just point at multiple dimensions and call it a day; you need a single common metric and you have to aggregate somehow, even if the weights are ugly or arbitrary.

(8) But I also think a good human life needs each kind of good, and no amount of achievement or pleasure makes up for a total absence of close relationships. A life with zero relationships is bad in a way that extra success just can't reach.

my friend says (3) and (4) are blatantly inconsistent, since (3) says some values are lexically ordered and (4) says we trade off higher and lower values all the time. i think he's mixing up different pairs of values and they were never meant to be the same pair, but maybe i'm the one fooling myself. seperately i can't get rid of the feeling that something in the back half — maybe (5) and (6), maybe (7) and (8) — is pulling against something harder than it looks. where's the actual inconsistency, and if i have to weaken or drop exactly one claim, which one does the least damage to the rest?
