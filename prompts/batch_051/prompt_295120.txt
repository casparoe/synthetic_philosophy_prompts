In the type foundry where I work, a senior designer keeps settling a standing dispute with the same analogy. We are trying to decide whether a new display face's most abstract glyph is an 'a': some of us read it as an 'a', some as an 'o' with a tail, one person as a 'd' turned the wrong way. The senior designer says this is exactly what letters are like. All the marks we accept as 'a' — the double-story 'a' of a Didot, the single-story 'a' of a grotesque, the italic, the ultra-black weight, our new glyph — form a family, he says: they share no single trait, yet they hang together the way cousins do, through overlapping family resemblances rather than through a definition. From this he concludes that a letter has no essence, only a family; that 'a'-ness is a graded, context-sensitive judgment rather than a specifiable fact; and that the studio should stop trying to write a formal acceptance standard for letterforms and trust the trained eye instead.

I cannot tell how much of that conclusion the analogy is quietly doing. Audit it. Give me an explicit correspondence table — what in the letter-world is supposed to play the role of the relatives, the common ancestor, the family name, the inherited traits — and then say where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Does the no-essence conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the family-resemblance analogy carrying it the whole time? If another analogy would do better — theme and variation, a role and its realizations, biological homology versus analogy — say what it is and what it licenses.
