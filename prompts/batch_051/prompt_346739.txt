Douglas Hofstadter's MU-puzzle gives a small formal system. The alphabet consists of three symbols: M, I, and U. The sole starting string is MI, and there are four rewrite rules: from any string ending in I, one may append U (xI yields xIU); from any string beginning with M, one may double the rest of the string (Mx yields Mxx); any occurrence of III may be replaced with U (xIIIy yields xUy); and any occurrence of UU may be deleted (xUUy yields xy). The target string is MU. Inside the system, the puzzle is simply a search problem: apply the rules and see whether MU turns up. But the puzzle yields to a standard move made from outside the system. The starting string MI contains one I; appending U and deleting UU leave the I-count unchanged; doubling the rest of the string doubles the I-count; and replacing III with U lowers the I-count by three. Since one is not a multiple of three, and doubling a non-multiple of three never produces a multiple of three, while subtracting a multiple of three preserves the residue modulo three, no derivable string can contain a number of I's divisible by three. The target MU contains zero I's, which is divisible by three, so MU is unreachable. Hofstadter calls this kind of move—stepping outside a formal system and discovering a general fact about all of its derivable strings, a fact the system itself neither states nor uses—jumping out of the system, abbreviated JOOTSing.

I want a working definition of JOOTSing, precise enough to classify all of the following cases.

1. A logician solves the MU-puzzle by the modular argument above: she proves that every theorem of the system has a non-multiple-of-three number of I's and concludes that MU is unreachable.
2. A search program starts from MI and enumerates strings by applying the four rules. It never constructs an invariant, and after exhausting its time or memory it has failed to find MU but cannot say whether MU would eventually appear.
3. A proof assistant checks a long MIU derivation line by line and certifies that each line follows from the preceding one by one of the four rules.
4. A chess engine assigns every position a numerical evaluation and searches among legal continuations. Every move it plays is permitted by the rules of chess, but the evaluation function that selects its moves is not part of the rules of chess.
5. A person writes out derivable strings from MI for an afternoon and observes that every string she has produced contains at least one I. She conjectures that MU is unreachable because of this pattern, though she cannot identify any rule-preserved property that would prevent the pattern from breaking.
6. A neural network is trained on many strings labeled reachable or unreachable in the MIU system. It classifies new strings with high accuracy, and probes of its internal behavior suggest it has latched onto something equivalent to the I-count invariant, although no one programmed that invariant in and the network emits no proof.
7. A mathematician proves that a formal system is consistent by working in a stronger metatheory and treating the original system's derivations as objects about which one can reason.

I am confident that 1 and 7 are clear cases of JOOTSing and that 2 and 3 are clear non-cases. I keep changing my mind about 4, 5, and 6. Please propose a definition, run it through all seven cases, and tell me where it surprises you. If a case forces a revision, revise the definition; if you retain a surprising verdict, defend it.

As you answer, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who think about formal systems, metamathematics, and machine learning. The unreachability of MU is settled mathematics, and so is the modular argument that establishes it. But the boundaries of JOOTSing—whether it requires an explicit proof, whether it requires consciousness or self-awareness, and whether a trained artificial system can be said to jump out of its own system—are contested.
