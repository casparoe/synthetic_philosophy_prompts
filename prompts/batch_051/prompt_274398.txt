I run the reliability team at a mid-size software company; after every customer-facing production incident we hold a one-hour postmortem and file a root-cause summary that an engineering manager, not an engineer, signs off on, and the engineers in the room have no philosophy background. Our current format is a timeline plus "five whys," and it keeps collapsing onto a single triggering event — the bad deploy, the retry loop — while skipping the structural and material conditions that made the failure possible. I would like a reusable incident-analysis procedure grounded in Aristotle's four causes (material, formal, efficient, final) that our on-call engineers can execute within that hour. The procedure must include an explicit rule for teleological language: software components have no purposes of their own, yet postmortems constantly say things like "the cache eviction routine deleted the entries in order to stay under its memory cap." Say when such "in order to" talk is legitimate, when it is just misleading personification, and which of the four causes any legitimate teleological claim actually belongs to. Give the procedure as an ordered checklist, work through two or three short example incidents including at least one where the procedure reaches a conclusion the team will not like, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
