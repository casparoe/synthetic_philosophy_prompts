I have been trying to work out what I actually think about multipolar traps — the situations people sometimes call Moloch, races to the bottom, or coordination failures, where each party acts sensibly by its own lights and the result is something none of them wanted. I have been going by my gut on a set of cases, and I want someone to tell me what principle I am implicitly using, or should use.

Here are my verdicts.

Two neighboring states are in an arms race. Each would prefer that neither buy the next generation of missiles; each would prefer to buy them if the other does; and neither can credibly commit to restraint. Both end up poorer and less secure than under mutual restraint. I call that a trap.

Twelve fishers work an open-access lake. Each additional boat is privately worthwhile at first, but the total catch eventually collapses. The fishers would all prefer a quota, but a fisher who stays home while the others go out simply loses the day's catch. I call that a trap.

A group of friends finds that everyone has started spending more and more on grooming and dress to avoid slipping in status. No one ends up better regarded, everyone is poorer and more anxious, and they would all gladly return to the old lower-effort norm — but no one can stop unilaterally without paying a social price. I call that a trap.

Job candidates in a field all inflate their resumes because the honest applicants get selected out. Hiring managers learn to discount the inflation, so the resumes become largely uninformative, and people who would have been honest end up penalized. I call that a trap.

Two coffee shops on the same street keep improving their drinks and trimming their margins to win customers from each other. The owners would each rather not have to work this hard or earn this little, and neither can afford to be the first to slack off. Customers get better coffee at lower prices. I do not call that a trap; I think it is the system working.

Several drug companies race to produce a vaccine during an outbreak. They spend enormous sums, several lose money, and the winner's pricing is constrained by the runners-up. The public gets a vaccine sooner than a single producer would have delivered. I do not call that a trap.

Social media companies converge on infinite scroll, autoplay, and other attention-harvesting features because engagement rankings punish any platform that opts out. Every firm would prefer a world in which none of them did this, and users report feeling worse. I call that a trap — though I notice it resembles the coffee-shop case more than I would like: the firms are all doing what they would rather not because their competitors do it.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this. Several universities compete for applicants by building expensive amenities — recreational facilities, renovated dormitories, elaborate student centers — while tuition rises and instructional spending stays flat. Each university would prefer that none of them had to spend on this, but any one that stops fears losing applications. Students get nicer facilities but leave with more debt and no better education. Is that a trap, or is it more like the coffee shops?

What I want is this: state the best principle, or the two or three best candidate principles, that captures my verdicts; for each clause, say which case forced it; apply the principle to the university-amenities case and to two or three further cases of your own construction that would distinguish the candidates; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate. If the candidates split on the university case, say which one you would bet on and why.
