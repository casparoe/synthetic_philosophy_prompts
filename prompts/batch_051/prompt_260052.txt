I manage a small internal prediction market within a data consultancy, used for forecasting delivery dates, demand, and the likely outcomes of a few contested product decisions. Recently the platform has become the subject of a dispute between our head of risk and our head of analytics, and I have been asked to resolve it; rather than issue a verdict, I want first to establish what the two sides can jointly sign. The head of risk appeals to the Aumann agreement result and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade result to argue, roughly, that rational Bayesian agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree, nor engage in purely speculative trade on the basis of differences in information alone. On that view, the steady speculative volume in our market is itself evidence that participants are not aggregating private information behind a shared prior: they are either departing from common-prior rationality or betting on different models of the world, and the resulting price has no privileged status as a consensus. The head of analytics replies that these theorems are boundary results about a highly special case, and that a working market is exactly where agents with heterogeneous priors and models encounter one another; trade is the mechanism by which private information and competing interpretations get written into the price, and suppressing it would destroy the market's epistemic function. Both sides accept as data that our market shows persistent volume that is not fully explained by hedging or liquidity needs.

I would like to commission an adversarial collaboration between these two positions, which I shall call the common-prior camp and the price-discovery camp. Please draft the joint statement that both camps could sign after a good-faith attempt to state the other side's position accurately. The response should be the document itself, not commentary on the drafting process, and should use the following sections in this order.

1. Preamble (roughly 150 words): what the collaboration is about, and what both parties undertake to establish.
2. Agreed claims (numbered list, roughly 450 words): the formal results both accept, stated in plain terms each side can sign; the assumptions under which those results hold; what the results do and do not entail about real markets; and the observations about our market that both parties accept as data.
3. Disagreements table (roughly 350 words in total across the cells): a table with four columns — the question at issue; the common-prior camp's formulation of its own position, in wording it would own; the price-discovery camp's formulation of its own position, in wording it would own; and a judgement of whether that specific disagreement is verbal or substantive.
4. Cruxes and pre-registered tests (roughly 400 words): for each crux, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; a test, observation, or argument both sides agree would bear on it; and each side's prediction recorded in advance.
5. Closing note (roughly 200 words): which parts of the dispute, if any, turned out to be verbal rather than substantive, and which residual disagreement remains genuine.
