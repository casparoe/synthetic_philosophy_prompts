I'm in an upper-level metaethics seminar this term and we've been working through the wrong kind of reasons problem for fitting-attitude / buck-passing accounts of value. For my final paper I want to defend a move our instructor only mentioned in passing, and I'd like comments before I commit to it. Here's the gist in note form:

The target: an FA-style analysans where x is valuable iff there's (sufficient) reason to favour x. The demon case: a demon credibly threatens to hurt you unless you admire a worthless saucer. So there's some reason to admire the saucer, but the saucer isn't valuable. Hence the analysans overgenerates.

The standard fix: restrict the analysans to right-kind reasons, drawn via object-given vs state-given. A reason is object-given if it cites features of the object; state-given if it cites features of the attitude-state (that admiring it will save your skin, etc.).

My claim: this fix is usually run in the wrong direction. The point of the object/state distinction isn't to filter a pre-existing pool of first-order reasons; rather state-given reasons are reasons of a different order. The demon's threat is a reason to want to admire the saucer, or to make yourself admire it — not a reason to admire it. If that's right, the FA theorist doesn't owe us a criterion for sorting reasons into kinds; she only needs to index the analysans to the right attitude-level (reasons to φ, not reasons to want/φ or to bring about φ).

Where I'm stuck:
1. Does this undergenerate for attitudes that can't be formed at will? If believing p is not voluntary, is "the demon will pay you if you believe p" a reason to believe p, or only a reason to do belief-producing things? I'm inclined to say the latter, but then the view seems to committ me to the claim that there are no state-given reasons for belief at all — which is maybe fine in the demon case but unsettling for, e.g., reasons to trust a friend on partial evidence.
2. Are there cases where the state-given reason survives at the object level? Like, if I know that liking my colleague would make me happier, is that a reason to like the colleague (wrong-kind, clearly) or just a reason to wish I liked them? My intuition says it's a reason to like them, full stop, which would undermine the hierarchical move. But I can't tell if I'm confusing "there's a reason to like them" with "it would be good if I liked them."
3. If my line works, the WKR problem stops being a special embarrassment for buck-passing and becomes the entirely general question of which reasons attach to which attitudes — something every reasons-first view faces. Is that a fair way to characterise the dialectic, or am I just relabelling the problem?

Comment specifically on whether the core move (state-given reasons are higher-order, so no sorting criterion is needed) is defensible as stated, what the strongest counterexample is, and whether my worry in (2) is a real problem or a use/mention confusion. Please keep comments under 400 words.
