I have been covering a sampling lawsuit, and the legal categories keep pushing me toward a philosophical puzzle I cannot get straight. A single piece of recorded music typically carries two distinct copyrights. One covers the musical work: it is usually owned by the songwriter or publisher, fixed in some medium at a definite time, and can be infringed by a later song that copies its melody or lyrics but shares no actual sounds with it. The other covers the sound recording: it is usually owned by the performer or label, and is infringed only by copying the captured sounds themselves. A cover version raises rights in the work but not in the earlier recording; a sample lifted from a record raises rights in both. Courts also speak of one and the same work being performed indefinitely many times without being identical to any particular performance. My question is what this practice presupposes about what a musical work is. On its face, the practice seems to treat a work as a repeatable abstraction—a type that performances instantiate—while also saying the work is fixed and created at a moment, which sounds like the biography of a concrete historical object. Reconstruct the implicit theory of musical works that the copyright system appears committed to. Then tell me whether the practice strictly presupposes that theory or merely fits well with it, and say where it is underdetermined among rival accounts: a work as a set of instructions, a work as a historical individual, a work as nothing over and above its performances and recordings. If the presupposed view were replaced by one of those rivals, what in the legal treatment of covers, samples, and similarity would have to change, and could the rights themselves survive the change? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
