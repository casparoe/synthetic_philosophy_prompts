Take the canonical two-player prisoner's dilemma as the standard model of international climate mitigation. Two large emitting countries, A and B, each choose between cutting emissions (the cooperative move) and continuing business as usual (the defecting move). Use the standard payoff ordering — temptation greater than reward greater than punishment greater than the sucker payoff — so that defecting strictly dominates for each player while mutual cooperation is Pareto-superior to mutual defection.

Now vary the case as follows. Country B, and only B, possesses a mature solar geoengineering capacity (such as stratospheric aerosol injection) that it can deploy unilaterally at moderate direct cost. Deployment produces a cooling benefit that is fully public: it is enjoyed by both countries whether or not they cut their own emissions, but it does not remove greenhouse gases, so B may deploy while continuing to emit. Deployment carries a termination shock risk: if B ever stops deploying before emissions have fallen, temperatures rebound sharply and both countries suffer a large climate loss. In addition, the mere availability of B's technology appears to weaken A's willingness to cut emissions, since A expects cooling regardless of what it does.

To make the variation determinate, use the following payoff scheme. Payoffs are linear in units of cooling. Each unit of cooling, whether produced by abatement or by deployment, gives each country +4. Cutting emissions contributes 1 cooling unit and costs the cutting country 5. Business as usual contributes nothing and costs nothing. Geoengineering, available only to B, contributes 3 cooling units, costs B 6 to deploy, and imposes an expected termination penalty t on each country. A therefore chooses between C and D, while B chooses among C, D, and G.

Then work through the analysis. (a) Write the modified game as a 2-by-3 matrix and find the pure-strategy Nash equilibria as a function of t, including the threshold at which B becomes indifferent between deploying and its best alternative. (b) Say what survives of the original prisoner's dilemma structure: does G strictly dominate for B, does D still strictly dominate C for A, and in what sense is the original dilemma still present? (c) Locate the moral hazard precisely: in this one-shot game, whose abatement falls relative to what, and does the availability of G actually crowd out mitigation in equilibrium? State what would need to be added to the model for a genuine mitigation-deterrence effect to appear. (d) Explain how the one-shot parameter t captures termination shock, and then explain what it fails to capture when the game is repeated: who is hostage to whom, and under what conditions would deployment become a commitment device rather than a public good? (e) Finally, turn to the question of who should decide: given that B's unilateral option changes A's payoff without A's consent, does the equilibrium analysis by itself settle whether B may deploy, or does it show that one must first justify the structure of the game and the solution concept before drawing a policy conclusion?
