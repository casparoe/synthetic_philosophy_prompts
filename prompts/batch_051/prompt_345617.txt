I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and the analogy I keep reaching for is the difference between a raster buffer — a grid of pixels that is inspected rather than interpreted — and a vector description or scene graph, where nothing spatial is stored except in the instructions for rendering it. I do not know whether that mapping is fair, and I would like the dialogue to test it rather than assume it.

Set the scene wherever seems natural — after a talk, at a workshop — and give them one concrete piece of evidence to fight over: the finding that people take longer to scan between two points on an imagined map when the points are farther apart. Have the propositionalist reply that this only shows someone knows how long the scanning would take and simulates that expectation, and have the depictivist reply in turn that this 'tacit knowledge' manoeuvre can be made to fit any data. Then push the exchange past the standard summary: let the propositionalist press the objection that a depictive buffer needs an interpreter reading it, and let the depictivist press the objection that a propositional account explains behaviour only by smuggling spatial facts back in as stored knowledge. Let them work through whether the disagreement concerns the format of a representation, the architecture that operates on it, or something else, and what would actually settle it.

Make the two characters equally competent and equally generous. Neither should be a strawman, and each should answer the point the other has just made rather than moving to the next prepared statement. Aim for something genuinely in the weeds — the interest should lie in distinctions a standard summary would skip.
