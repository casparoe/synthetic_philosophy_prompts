I am a philosophy professor working mainly on criminal law, and I have been drafting a paper on the rule-of-law requirement that criminal statutes be determinate enough to give notice and constrain enforcement discretion. In connection with it I have assembled my own intuitive verdicts on a set of hypothetical statutes, and I find I can no longer state the principle behind them. The cases, simplified but familiar: an ordinance punishing anyone who "behaves in a public place in a way that annoys others" — impermissibly vague; a statute punishing "breach of the peace," defined to include fighting, making an unreasonable racket, or using words intended to provoke a fight — permissible; a traffic law requiring that a driver never exceed "a speed that is reasonable in the circumstances" — permissible; a sentence enhancement for an offense committed "in an especially cruel or callous manner" — I lean toward impermissible, but with hesitation; a statute punishing "obstructing an officer who is carrying out a lawful duty" — permissible, though I could not draw the boundary of the offense in advance; a statute punishing "recklessly endangering others," with "recklessly" defined as "aware of and disregarding a serious and unjustified risk of harm" — permissible; and a statute punishing "acts that tend to corrupt public morals" — impermissible.

My working principle had been that a term is acceptable when it invokes a standard an ordinary person can apply to their own prospective conduct, and unacceptable when it delegates the normative question to the arresting officer. But I now doubt the distinction: "reasonable speed" and "annoying" both require an ordinary person to make an evaluative judgment, yet I place them on opposite sides.

I want a principled account of my verdicts, not a survey of vagueness doctrine: state the principle precisely, identify which elements of it are doing the work for which cases, and then apply it to the case I keep vacillating on — a harassment statute defined as "a course of conduct that would seriously alarm or distress a reasonable person and that has no legitimate purpose." Test my tentative principle against the cases before adopting or revising it. Be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and say whether the principle or the verdict should give way. Please begin your response with the bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, the candidate principles, or the conclusion that no single principle is available — then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
