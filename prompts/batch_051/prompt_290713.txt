I've been working through Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments — the old-engineer habit of always looking for a mechanism, I guess — and this bit from Part V, Chapter I keeps bugging me:

"When two objects have frequently been seen together, the imagination acquires a habit of passing easily from the one to the other. If the first appear, we lay our account that the second is to follow. Of their own accord they put us in mind of one another, and the attention glides easily along them. Though, independent of custom, there should be no real beauty in their union, yet when custom has thus connected them together, we feel an impropriety in their separation."

He's talking about aesthetics there, but he says later that custom and fashion also govern moral sentiments. I want to press him on the moral-revolutions debate—the Williams/Appiah thing about whether norms like dueling or slavery end through arguments, interests, or honor.

Here's the puzzle. Suppose Smith somehow saw the end of foot-binding in China, which mostly collapsed in the early twentieth century, after his death. Would his passage predict that it ended mainly through a change in custom/fashion, through deliberate argument, through material interests, or through something about honor? And how would he explain the unevenness—some families dropping it fast, others clinging to it?

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, and I dont want an introduction or conclusion.
