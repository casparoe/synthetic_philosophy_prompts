I'm in a seminar on rational choice and self-defeating behavior, and we've been working through the dollar auction: an auctioneer sells a one-dollar bill to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder must also pay the amount of their final bid and receives nothing. Once two people are locked in, the second-highest bidder finds each next raise locally rational — quitting would mean accepting a certain loss of everything already pledged, while raising keeps open at least the chance of winning the dollar and cutting that loss — and yet each raise raises the stakes for the other bidder, who then faces the same choice. The two lines of reasoning feed on each other, and the bids ratchet past the value of the dollar with no natural stopping point. I want a poem that dramatizes why this machine runs, not just a description of its rules.

Please write a villanelle. I want the form to carry the argument. Let the two refrain lines be the two bidders' respective justifications for staying in, one refrain for each bidder. The first refrain should read, in its early appearances, as a harmless piece of auction arithmetic about the small cost of one more bid; by its later appearances the identical words should have become the voice of sunk-cost commitment. The second refrain should do the complementary work: at first it should sound like the clearly correct principle that a second-highest bidder who quits loses everything pledged and so may rationally raise instead, and by the end it should read as the engine of runaway escalation. Use the standard nineteen-line pattern, with the refrains alternating as the last line of the following tercets and then closing the final quatrain together, so that the reader sees the two justifications feeding on each other. The lines between the refrains should do the descriptive work — the opening bid, the crossing of the dollar value, the growing loss on both sides — so the refrains stand out as the bidders' reasons. A game theorist reading it should recognize the specific features of the game: both bidders pay, only one gets the dollar, and cutting losses becomes a reason to keep raising. A newcomer should come away understanding why two rational-sounding stepwise choices can leave both people paying far more than the dollar is worth.
