I spent most of my career in the physical sciences, where we kept a working distinction between a measurement, a calculation, and a conclusion the evidence actually warranted, and I have been surprised, since retiring and turning to the philosophy of mathematics, by how unsettled the corresponding distinction for proof appears to be. I keep reading arguments about computer-assisted proof that move straight from a single example—the Appel–Haken proof of the four-colour theorem, the heavy computation in the proof of the Kepler conjecture, or the later fully formal, machine-checked derivation of that result—to a sweeping claim about whether mathematics has become experimental, or whether proof is just whatever the community accepts. Before I read any further, I would like the specification first: what, precisely, must a theory of mathematical proof be able to do if it is to be adequate to a practice that now includes verifications no single person can survey?

Write the desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each. Cover at least: the cases such a theory must get right (a short classical proof, a long proof distributed across many authors so that no one person has checked every step, a computer-assisted finite case-check, and a machine-checked formal derivation); the distinctions it must respect and not collapse (validity versus surveyability, correctness versus acceptance, proof versus numerical evidence, and a priori warrant versus empirical confidence in the hardware and software); and the settled facts of practice it must not flatly contradict (recognizably mathematical results whose proofs are nevertheless unsurveyable by any individual mathematician are treated as theorems). For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—for example, a demand that every proof be humanly surveyable together with a demand that finite-exhaustion results count as proofs. Then, keeping this part brief, score the main contenders—a classical surveyability view, a formalist view, and a social-process view of proof—against the list. I am less interested in the final score than in whether the criteria are the right ones in the first place.
