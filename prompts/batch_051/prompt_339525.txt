I recently took over the programming for a small, member-run cinema society in my city. Around the same time, a friend pressed on me an account of Rousseau's critique of the theater, and I have found it difficult to set aside. The argument, as I understand it, is that theatrical spectacle cultivates a transient and self-flattering pity that exhausts itself in the experience and substitutes the pleasure of watching for the work of acting; that the actor's trade corrupts because it demands the simulation of emotions the performer does not genuinely feel; and that a healthy community would do better to organize public festivals in which the line between performer and spectator disappears. While preparing a season of screenings about spectacle and spectatorship, I rewatched The Truman Show and was struck by how thoroughly the film seems to inhabit these themes: a manufactured town that is one continuous stage, a protagonist whose whole life is performed for cameras, hired actors playing his wife and oldest friend, and an audience within the film that follows his suffering as entertainment without ever intervening. Yet the film also seems to resist the critique—some of its audience members care about Truman in ways that look morally real, and the film is itself a commercial spectacle that asks us to enjoy watching a man struggle against being watched.

I would like you to write the critical reading I can circulate to the society before our screening and discussion. Please offer a reading of The Truman Show through Rousseau's critique of the theater that is faithful in both directions. Identify the scenes and choices where Rousseau's account of spectatorship genuinely illuminates what the film is doing, and be equally candid about where the film complicates, resists, or answers that account—whether by showing that spectatorship can be more than passive self-congratulation or by implicating its own audience in the practice it criticizes. Do not summarize the plot for its own sake, and do not reduce the film to a case study. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that Rousseau's argument alone cannot.
