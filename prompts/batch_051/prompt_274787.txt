For my job I help red-team language models, and over lunch my officemate and me got stuck on whether "deception" as applied to AI systems needs mental states at all. He wants a purely behavioral definition: a system has deceived a user iff it produces outputs that a reasonable, epistemically competent interpreter in the user's position would be misled by, with no essential reference to the system's goals, beliefs, or intentions. I keep objecting that this makes a confidently wrong model a liar, and treats a hallucination as the same kind of thing as a model that fakes alignment on an eval and then behaves differently when it thinks nobody is watching. His answer is basically "give me a better operational test," and I concede that's harder than it sounds.

So here's what I want from you. Give me your actual credence in the following proposition: there exists a behavioral definition of AI deception — one that makes no essential reference to a system's goals, beliefs, or intentions — that both (i) avoids counting mere false outputs as deception and (ii) tracks the cases people actually worry about (sycophancy, strategic sandbagging, reward hacking, faking alignment, lying to get a human to click a link) at least as well as the best mentalistic defintion. Decompose the credence. I want sub-questions with a rough probability for each: the probability that the ordinary concept of deception is itself behaviorally reducible; the probability that any behavioral definition can draw the misleading-vs-merely-wrong distinction without importing intentions; the probability that the hard cases like sandbagging or faking alignment require attributing something like an intention to conceal; plus whatever else you think carries weight. Put a number and a one-line justification on each term, then show how the combination gives your final credence. Then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or experiment would move that term the most. Keep it under 700 words.
