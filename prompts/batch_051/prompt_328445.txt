I hold a secular, naturalised version of the familiar view that the arc of history bends toward justice. By this I mean three distinguishable empirical claims, not a single vague one. First, on the scale dimension: over the long run, measured across centuries rather than decades, the frequency and social acceptability of serious interpersonal and state violence have declined. Second, on the scope dimension: the set of beings whose interests are taken to matter has tended to expand, from kin and tribe to nation, and then beyond lines of race, sex, and species. Third, on the depth dimension: the content of justice has thickened — once it was enough that the powerful refrain from cruelty; increasingly it is held that persons are owed positive provision, recognition, and participation. I do not claim that progress is inevitable, monotonic, or providence-backed, only that the long-run pattern is real and directionally stable, and that it is explained by identifiable mechanisms — such as expanding trade, rising literacy, institutional competition, norm cascades, and the ratchet effects of rights — rather than by chance or by the selection of a flattering sample.

I want you to pressure-test this position by telling me what would count against it. I am not looking for a rebuttal or for the usual counterexamples; I want a map of the position's exposure. What would the world have to look like, or have looked like, for the thesis to be false rather than merely dented? Specifically: which historical developments, present trends, or near-future trajectories would most seriously undermine each of the three dimensions, and which would undermine only one dimension while leaving the others intact? Which of the mechanisms the view leans on could in principle fail or run in reverse, and what would their failure imply for the overall claim? Which philosophical objections cut deepest — for example, that the apparent direction is an artefact of which indicators are measured, of survivorship bias among societies, of a standard of justice chosen so that the conclusion follows, or that a direction produced by mechanisms without any agent or goal is no more than chronology dressed up as teleology?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing between considerations that a defender of the view would themselves concede count against it and considerations they could reasonably dismiss as consistent with the thesis. Also assess honestly whether the position, as I have stated it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind or a tolerable feature of claims about very long historical processes.

Your answer should be in British English.
