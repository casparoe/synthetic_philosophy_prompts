I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about the axiom of choice by checking my reactions case by case, and the pattern is not coming out clean.

For countably many pairs of shoes, I can specify a set containing one shoe from each pair by a rule: take the left shoe. I am completely comfortable saying that set exists. For countably many pairs of identical socks, where no such rule is available, I cannot exhibit a choice set; I still believe one exists in the standard mathematical sense, but it nags at me, and I don't know why the shoe case feels legitimate while the sock case feels slightly illicit.

I accept the theorem that every vector space has a basis and use it without discomfort, even though the standard proof goes through Zorn's lemma (a maximality principle equivalent to choice) and gives no recipe for the basis.

I accept that the Banach–Tarski decomposition of a ball into finitely many pieces that can be rearranged into two balls of the same size is a correct theorem given choice. I also think it says nothing about what could be done with a physical ball; it is a fact about nonmeasurable sets, not a paradox about matter. I hold both views, and I worry they amount to enjoying choice's power while refusing its strangeness.

I accept that the real numbers can be well-ordered, and I also think no one will ever specify such a well-ordering. That combination doesn't trouble me. But I can't say why this abstract existence is untroubling while the sock case is troubling.

Consider the hat puzzle: countably many prisoners each wear a black or white hat, and each can see all the hats of prisoners ahead of them. Beforehand, using choice, they fix one representative sequence from each equivalence class of binary sequences that differ in only finitely many places, and each prisoner guesses their own hat according to the representative. I accept the mathematics: this strategy guarantees all but finitely many correct guesses. But my gut says the required choice function over equivalence classes of binary sequences is too strong to be legitimate, even though I accepted choice for vector space bases. I suspect a principle should overrule one of those reactions, but I can't tell which.

I am fine saying that Vitali sets—the nonmeasurable sets obtained by picking one representative from each coset of the rationals in the reals—exist, and fine saying they have no Lebesgue measure. They don't feel more objectionable than the socks. So why does the hat puzzle feel so much worse?

Is there a single principle about when nonconstructive choice is legitimate, or about what existence should mean in these cases, that captures all of these verdicts? If no clean principle captures all of them, which of my verdicts should I abandon, and why those? What does the best principle you can find say about a case I haven't listed: the theorem that a countable union of countable sets is countable, which standard mathematics takes to require at least countable choice?
