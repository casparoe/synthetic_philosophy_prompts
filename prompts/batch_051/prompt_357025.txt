For a few years I have been writing a historical novel whose hinge is a small, almost accidental occurrence — a letter delayed half a day — and I have found that my working beliefs about how history moves have quietly become load-bearing for the plot. Before I hand the manuscript to my editor, I would like an audit of whether these commitments actually hang together. I have been too close to them to see where the strain is, and I am not precommitted to revising any particular one.

Here they are.

(1) Small, accidental occurrences genuinely redirect the course of large historical developments. The old proverb about the missing nail and the lost battle captures something true about the world, not merely something convenient for a novelist.

(2) The tininess of a precipitating cause does not make it explanatory noise. My novel asserts, and I am prepared to maintain, that if the letter had arrived on time the treaty would not have collapsed; I take this counterfactual to be capable of being true, not just aesthetically right.

(3) At the same time, I believe the very large outcomes — wars, revolutions, the fall of a regime — are almost always overdetermined at the macro level: remove any single precipitating occurrence and the outcome would still have arrived, perhaps by another path.

(4) I believe narrative, including historical narrative and my own plotting, imposes a retrospective necessity on events. Once the outcome is known, every earlier occurrence comes to seem as though it had been leading there. I regard this as a standing distortion.

(5) I accept the minimal-rewrite discipline for counterfactuals: a counterfactual about history is legitimate only if it holds as much of the actual world fixed as possible and alters one small occurrence.

(6) Yet I also believe consequences ramify chaotically, so that any counterfactual carried more than a short distance beyond the altered occurrence is in practice invention rather than inference. The longer the horizon, the more a counterfactual history simply is a novel — which, as it happens, is what I write.

(7) I believe individual character and choice do real causal work — the delay in my plot comes from a particular person's particular failing — and I also believe structural forces such as prices, demographic pressure, geography, and institutions do the slower and larger causal work, setting the menu of choices that can matter at all.

I would like you to go through these and tell me which pairs or larger groupings are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Then identify the single revision that would do the most to make the whole set cohere, and state what it would cost me as a novelist — which plot, theme, or conviction I would have to surrender. Throughout, please distinguish what is settled among people who work seriously on historical causation and explanation from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
