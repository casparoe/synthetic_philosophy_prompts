In a discussion about whether large language model hallucinations count as lying, someone offered me the following argument:

(1) Lying requires an intention to deceive.
(2) A large language model has no intentions: it is a system trained to predict the next token.
(3) So when a model confidently asserts something false — a hallucination — it is not lying.
(4) On Harry Frankfurt's account, what distinguishes bullshit from lying is that the liar deliberately misrepresents the facts, while the bullshitter simply does not care whether what he says is true.
(5) A model's false statements are produced without any concern for truth: its training objective is predictive, not truth-tracking.
(6) Therefore, hallucination is bullshit.

I half-suspect something goes wrong in the move from (4) and (5) to (6), but I cannot locate the failure. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, are the premises true? If not, which step fails? In particular, say whether (5) establishes the same thing that (4) requires, or whether Frankfurt's category requires an agent with aims of a kind a language model lacks — and whether the argument at most shows that someone else is bullshitting through the model. If the argument fails, does hallucination fall better under the category of plain error, or outside these categories altogether?
