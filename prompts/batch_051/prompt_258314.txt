I am a philosophy professor working on a paper about dispositional predicates, and I have constructed an argument that I suspect proves too much. I would like you to act as a professional metaphysician, think through the argument carefully, and tell me where it goes wrong. Begin by brainstorming several possible diagnoses before settling on the one you think is best, and write your answer in American English.

Here is the argument I have been toying with.

(1) A standard disposition ascription, such as "this glass is fragile" or "this substance is poisonous," is true in virtue of the object's having some categorical basis B—some actual physical state or property—that would, in appropriate circumstances, bring about the relevant manifestation when the disposition's stimulus occurs.

(2) For any disposition D with stimulus S and manifestation M, and for any candidate categorical basis B, it is possible to describe a fink or a mask: a fink would remove or alter B when S occurs, so that M does not occur, while a mask would leave B intact but block B's normal contribution to M. In either case, the object still intuitively possesses D, yet the counterfactual "if S were to occur, M would occur" is false.

(3) Therefore, no ordinary subjunctive conditional of the form "if S, then M" can supply the truth conditions for a disposition ascription: whenever such a conditional is proposed, there is a possible fink or mask that makes the conditional false without making the disposition ascription false.

(4) If the manifestation condition cannot be stated as a subjunctive conditional, then the manifestation itself is not part of what makes the disposition ascription true.

(5) So the only thing left to make a disposition ascription true is the categorical basis B. The dispositional predicate is merely a way of pointing toward whatever categorical state happens to play that functional role.

(6) But if that is right, then all disposition talk should in principle be eliminable: any true statement about a disposition could be replaced, without loss of information, by a statement about categorical properties and laws of nature.

(7) Yet that conclusion seems clearly false. We often know that something is fragile or poisonous without knowing its categorical basis. Moreover, the same disposition can be realized by many different categorical bases—glass and bone are both fragile, though their underlying structures differ. Dispositions seem to be useful, irreducible, and multiply realizable.

So one of the earlier steps must be faulty, or the conclusion does not follow from them. Where does the argument first go wrong? Is it in the move from the possibility of finks and masks to the rejection of all ordinary counterfactual analyses? Is it in step (4), where manifestation conditions are dropped entirely? Or is step (7) not as forced as I take it to be? In your answer, please distinguish genuine fink and mask cases from merely possible but irrelevant ones, and explain what the cheapest way to block the argument costs for the traditional simple conditional analysis of dispositions.
