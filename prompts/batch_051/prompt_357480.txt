Heres a question thats been bugging me. The standard line on racist dog whistles treats speaker intent as the load-bearing thing: a phrase counts as a racist dog whistle when a speaker picks it because they know it will signal something racist to an in-group while sounding innocent to everyone else. Intent is what stops us from saying every word with a racist history is a dog whistle whenever someone uses it literally — and thats also what lets a speaker deny the charge. On this view, whether the statement is racist is about what the speaker meant, not just what the words happen to do.

Now put a modern twist on it. A language model trained on human political text gets used to generate speeches or op-eds, and it reliably produces the kinds of phrases a human would be accused of using as racist dog whistles — only there is no individual speaker choosing them as code. The phrases are just statistically fluent continuations. Suppose readers primed to hear the signal do hear it, and the text does the same in-group signalling work a human dog whistle does. The intent is smeared across the training data and whoever prompted or deployed the model, and theres no point where someone thought 'I'll dog whistle here.'

Does the standard intent-based account of racist dog whistles survive this? Is 'racist dog whistle' still the right category when everyone agrees the phrase is functioning as one but no individual intended it? Or does the case show the account was always really leaning on something else — the phrases history of racist use, or its foreseeable uptake — and the LLM case just forces us to say so? I'm asking about the concept, not about any particular platform or its policies.
