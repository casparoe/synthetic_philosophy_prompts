I'm a retired chemist who has lately been working through formal social choice theory, and I keep reconstructing an argument that appears to undermine the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. Since I take the theorem to be sound, my reconstruction must contain a fault, and I would like you to locate it precisely. Please act as a philosopher with a solid working knowledge of social choice and decision theory. Before giving your verdict, briefly brainstorm the candidate trouble spots in the argument; then go through the steps and say exactly which one fails and why.

Fix three alternatives, a, b, and c, and consider strict rankings only. A rule is strategy-proof if, for every voter and every pattern of the other voters' reports, submitting her true ranking gives her an outcome at least as good as any false ranking she could submit. A rule is dictatorial if some voter's top-ranked alternative is always the chosen outcome. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that, with at least three alternatives, every strategy-proof rule that is defined on all strict preference profiles and is onto the set of alternatives is dictatorial.

Now the argument:

(1) With two alternatives, majority rule with ties broken by a fixed status quo is strategy-proof: a voter's truthful report can only move the outcome toward the alternative she actually likes better, and an insincere report can only move it toward the one she likes less, so insincerity can never benefit her.

(2) With three alternatives, generalize majority rule in the obvious way: hold a majority vote on each pair. If some alternative beats both others pairwise, select it. If the three pairwise votes form a cycle, select the alternative that comes highest in a fixed external priority order over a, b, and c.

(3) In this generalized rule, the only facts determining the winner are the three pairwise majority outcomes. A voter can affect these only through the pairwise comparisons encoded in her own ranking. To flip the majority on any pair, she must rank the alternative she truly likes less above the alternative she truly likes more in that pair; and that cannot make the eventual winner more congenial to her, because it only weakens a candidate she strictly prefers.

(4) Therefore no voter can ever gain by misreporting, whatever the other voters report; the generalized rule is strategy-proof.

(5) The generalized rule is plainly non-dictatorial: the winner is fixed by majority pairwise structure except when a cycle forces the external priority, and no single voter's top alternative always wins.

(6) So we have a non-dictatorial, strategy-proof rule for three alternatives, contradicting the theorem.

My suspicion is that step 3 is where a scientist's local-to-global instinct goes wrong, but I cannot make the failure sharp. Which step is false? If step 3 fails, describe the exact mechanism, ideally with a concrete three-voter, three-alternative profile showing how a voter could benefit from misreporting. If some other step is at fault, diagnose that instead.
