I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I somehow wound up as the student rep on the grants committee of a small family foundation that funds theoretical physics. It's about two million dollars a year, two cycles a year, and the committee is mostly people without physics backgrounds: a retired civil engineer, a former science journalist, the family trustee, and me. Each proposal gets maybe fifteen minutes of discussion before a majority vote, so anything I hand them has to be something non-experts can actually run in real time, and the deliverable only has to produce a recommendation plus a list of questions that must be answered before the vote. The recurring fight is over proposals in string theory, quantum gravity, and multiverse-adjacent work where nobody can honestly point to an experiment this century. One faction wants to fund whichever theory looks deepest or most beautiful; the other wants to kill anything that can't name a plausible experiment in the next twenty years.

I've been reading the literature on non-empirical theory confirmation, the stuff about how considerations like the no-alternatives argument (the fact that no serious rival research program is being developed might itself be evidence) or unexpected explanatory coherence (a theory unexpectedly turning out to explain something it wasn't designed to explain) could count for something even when direct tests are out of reach. I'd like you to turn that into an actual reusable procedure for the committee: a checklist or decision tree in words, with questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision back to a person because it's a values call rather than a settled point. In particular I want it to force a distinction between untestable in practice and untestable in principle, between being merely consistent with the data and making a novel prediction that would be hard but not impossible to test, and it should be honest about wich steps are judgment dressed up as procedure and when the no-alternatives or explanatory-coherence moves are really just ratifying our aesthetic preferances. Then run the procedure on three sample proposals: (1) a highly developed string cosmology proposal about the landscape and the multiverse, with no concrete predictions expected in the next thirty years; (2) a relatively cheap tabletop experiment to test a specific prediction from a currently unfashionable theory; and (3) a proposal developing an alternative to inflation that has mostly been ignored but does make one definite observational prediction. Full disclosure: I find proposal (1) the most intellectually exciting, so I want the procedure to be honest enough that it's capable of overruling me.
