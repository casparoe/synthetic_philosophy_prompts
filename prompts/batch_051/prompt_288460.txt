After forty years in the natural sciences, I have turned in retirement to political philosophy, and I have been arguing with a neighbor who holds strong libertarian convictions. In a recent exchange, he dismissed the idea that we might deliberately shape the distribution of wealth by invoking the line that "liberty upsets patterns." He then ran the familiar Wilt Chamberlain argument: begin from any distribution you consider just — strict equality, distribution according to need, some other pattern — and let people freely use their justly acquired holdings as they choose. If a million people voluntarily pay twenty-five cents apiece to watch Wilt Chamberlain play basketball, then Chamberlain winds up considerably richer than the pattern permits, through no theft or fraud. Either you prohibit the transfers, or you take the resulting holdings away, or you accept that the pattern has been upset. From this my neighbor concluded that any patterned or end-state principle of distributive justice can be realized only through continuous interference with people's lives, so redistributive taxation, whatever good it might aim at, is always liberty-infringing in exactly that way.

I find that the slogan does a great deal of silent work in this argument, because "liberty upsets patterns" sounds like one crisp claim but seems, on inspection, to compress several quite different theses. I would like you to lay them out as a menu. What distinct precise claims could the sentence be making — distinguishing, for example, which liberties are in view (to transfer, to give, to bequeath, to contract), which patterns matter (a momentary distribution, a long-run statistical regularity, a distribution of opportunities), and what "upsets" amounts to (causally tends to disturb, renders impossible to maintain, or merely makes maintenance coercive and costly)? For each candidate reading, give a short verdict: is it trivially true, substantively true but too weak for his conclusion, or false? Then say which reading, if any, actually supports the conclusion that patterned justice requires continuous interference, and which readings a defender of redistribution could concede without giving up anything important.
