I've just moved from a product ML team onto an AI safety team, and I keep finding that the word "aligned" is doing a lot of silent work. In a meeting last week someone brushed off a worry about sycophantic models by saying "this is all overblown — alignment just means the system does what the user wants." Nobody objected then, but a colleague told me afterwards that this is exactly the confusion: doing what the user wants is only *intent* alignment, the narrow definition, and the real problem is *value* alignment. The two of us spent the rest of coffee going in circles about whether those are two readings of the same slogan or two seperate projects.

I'd like you to take that slogan, "alignment means the system does what the user wants," and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, as a menu rather than picking one. The readings should differ in what the system is actually required to do: follow the literal instruction, pursue what the operator intended to achieve, act in the user's actual interests or on their actual values, merely respond to a particular training signal like human feedback, or whatever else you think is hiding in the phrase. For each reading, tell me whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible engineering/safety target, or just false, and say explicitly when a reading changes depending on whether "wants" means current expressed preferences versus some more idealised or aggregated notion of human values. Finally, say which of these readings my colleague would need to be true for his dismissal of the sycophancy worry to actually go through, and whether the slogan as people tend to use it supports that reading.
