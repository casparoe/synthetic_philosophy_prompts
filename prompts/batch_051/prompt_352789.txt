I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and the vocabulary I give you here is the one I use fluently every day. I have been trying, informally, to follow a debate about what fixes the meaning of a text, and I want it restated in those terms.

The debate. One position: a text means whatever its author intended it to mean. A second: once a text is made public, its meaning is fixed by what the words themselves, together with the relevant public conventions, yield in context; the author's private mental states no longer settle what it says, and the author counts as just one more reader of something that has left their hands. A third, mixed position: the meaning that matters is neither the author's actual private intention nor whatever effect the text happens to have on readers; it is what an adequately informed reader, knowing the genre, the context, and the operative conventions, would reasonably infer the author intended the text to do.

What I want, in this order and clearly separated:

1. A translation. Restate each of the three positions as faithfully as you can in software-engineering vocabulary. Treat the text as code, as a released API that downstream projects link against, or as a long-lived library whose behavior changes across versions; pick the most precise frame and stay consistent. Authorial intention should map to the original developer's intention as expressed in design notes, commit messages, README prose, inline comments, and the mental model behind the code. Public meaning should map to the observable behavior of the code: language semantics, the test suite, type signatures, and the contract a downstream consumer can actually rely on. The mixed position should map to what a competent reviewer or downstream developer, reading the code together with its documentation and deployment context, would infer the API was meant to promise, whatever the original developer privately had in mind.

Use the concepts I actually reason with: comments versus behavior, spec drift, regression tests as public checks, API contracts, semantic versioning, backward-compatibility promises, undefined behavior, deprecation notices, code review, tech debt, and the works-as-coded versus works-as-intended distinction. But each mapping must be justified; don't decorate, and mark where you're giving an analogy rather than an exact counterpart.

2. A residue report. For each of the three positions, say what translates cleanly into engineering concepts, what fits only with distortion and where the distortion is, and what has no counterpart at all in how I reason about software. Then give a verdict on the failures: when the translation fails, is it because my engineering vocabulary is missing something the philosophical debate genuinely needs, or because the philosophical position was less determinate than it looked, and only seems substantive until it is forced to say what the meaning of a text operationally consists in?

Be honest rather than flattering to my framework. Do not silently import software assumptions — such as the idea that a system's meaning is just its passing tests, or that behavior beats spec when they conflict — and flag any place where an engineer's habits would bias the translation toward one of the three positions.

To stress-test the mapping, run it through at least three of these trouble cases and, for each, say which philosophical position handles it best and what the corresponding engineering situation is: the author's stated intention diverges from what the text conveys; the author misspeaks, mistypes, or has a false belief about a word's meaning; the text has no identifiable single author or has been revised by many hands; the author uses a word in a new or idiosyncratic way; the text is read long afterward in a context whose conventions have changed.

No bibliography or citations: describe the positions by content, not by scholarship.
