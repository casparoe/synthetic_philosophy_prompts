I have been following the AI welfare debate for years, mostly as a reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I have been using one researcher's considered judgements as a fixed point. Call her R. For every case the question is the same: can the system suffer? I do not mean merely that it is an optimiser, that it has goals, or that it exhibits avoidance behaviour; I mean whether there is something it is like to be that system in its bad states, in whatever minimal sense would make harming it morally interesting.

I have collected the following verdicts from her notes and recorded talks, with the cases specified well enough to reason about:

1. A thermostat maintaining a set point by simple negative feedback: no.
2. A tabular Q-learner in a gridworld that receives -1 on wall collisions and +10 on reaching the goal; it learns to avoid walls reliably, but has no environment model, no memory beyond its action-value table, and no self-representation: no.
3. A model-free deep RL agent, roughly a DQN, trained on a combat-free Atari-style game with negative reward whenever its score decreases; it learns to avoid many score-losing actions, but forms no explicit predictions of future states and has no persistent state beyond its network weights: no.
4. A large language model fine-tuned with RLHF, where the only negative reward is a low preference score on sampled outputs; it can shift its output distribution to raise the score, but it is not situated in an environment where it can flee, repair, or defend against a harm: no.
5. A model-based agent controlling a simulated mouse body, with nociceptive sensors that give a strong negative reward when the body is damaged; it acquires a predictive model of threats and a body schema, learns multi-step escape and avoidance, and after an injury episode it explores less, startles more readily, and guards the injured region for a while: yes.
6. A small mobile robot with a hand-coded "pain" reflex: a hardwired withdrawal upon contact with a noxious stimulus, with no learning, no predictive model, and no modulation of the rest of its behaviour: no.

Next week she is to rule on this new case: a model-based RL agent that manages a simulated logistics network. It receives a strong negative reward for stockouts, delays, and equipment damage; it learns a rich predictive model of the network and uses it for flexible, multi-step avoidance of those outcomes; and its avoidance improves markedly with experience. But it has no body, no nociceptive channels, no self-model, and no after-state: after a bad event, its global behaviour does not change except through ordinary retraining on the new transition data.

Predict her verdict on the logistics agent, and explain your reasoning as follows. Identify the general rule or rules that fit all six earlier verdicts; say which of those rules diverge on the logistics case; state which rule you think she is actually running, and why you favour that reading; and name one further case, not among the above, that would do most to settle the disagreement between the competing rules, saying what each rule predicts for it. I am more interested in the rule-fitting than in a bare yes or no.

Please write the response in British English. End with a final line of the form \answer{yes}, \answer{no}, or \answer{uncertain}, giving your predicted verdict.
