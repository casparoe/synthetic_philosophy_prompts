I have spent the past few years working in palliative and chronic-pain support, and that experience has pushed me toward a view I did not expect to hold. The view is a form of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. It says there is a level of suffering — call it the threshold — such that no amount of positive well-being, however large and however widely distributed, can compensate for a single instance of suffering at or above that level. Below the threshold, pain and pleasure trade off in the ordinary way: a bad headache can be outweighed by enough joy, including joy in other lives. Above the threshold, the exchange rate shuts down; one episode of genuinely unendurable agony is not made worth having by any finite amount of happiness elsewhere. What attracts me to this is that it offers a principled answer to cases that wreck simple aggregative views. It refuses to say that a vast population of lives barely worth living could justify one life of torment; it captures the conviction that some harms cannot be made up for by benefits; and it yields a clean priority rule: eliminate above-threshold suffering first, and only then weigh lesser pains against pleasures. I grant that the threshold is vague, but I am inclined to think that is a feature, not a bug, if it marks a real difference in kind rather than a merely arbitrary place on a continuous scale.

And yet I have a firm intuition that contradicts all of this: for any finite pain, however dreadful, there is some finite amount of good that could compensate for it. I do not mean I would accept the trade in practice. I mean that pleasure and pain are, at bottom, quantities of the same kind, and no gap between them should be absolute. If I could be offered an arbitrarily large but finite amount of knowledge, beauty, love, or relief, it seems there must be some point at which the scales tip. I find this hardest to give up when I think about individual lives: I cannot locate a point on the pain scale beyond which I would say that no possible remainder of a life, and no possible good for others, could ever be worth it. The lexical theorist must say there is such a point, and that it lies somewhere in the finite range of actual experience, not merely at the limit of infinite pain or infinite pleasure. I do not see how a finite cut-off could fail to be arbitrary, and I am not sure I can believe in a genuine difference in kind along what feels like a continuous dimension.

I am not asking for a verdict. I want to understand what each concession would cost. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — a debunking explanation of it as a continuity illusion, or a demonstration that I am failing to imagine what above-threshold suffering is like while still imagining myself as the one who would be compensated? What would it take for the theory to be amended instead — for example, by making the lexical priority defeasible rather than absolute, by restricting it to whole lives or populations rather than single episodes, or by replacing the sharp threshold with an exchange rate that rises without bound but never reaches a true lexical cut-off? And is there a reading of compensation on which both sides could survive — perhaps one that distinguishes within-a-life judgments from cross-life aggregation, or one that treats the intuition as about decision-making under uncertainty rather than about what is finally good? Please present both the theory and the intuition at their strongest before you adjudicate.
