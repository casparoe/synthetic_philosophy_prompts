I serve on the ethics committee of a community hospital, and I have been asked to draft the committee's decision memo in the following case. L.M., a sixty-eight-year-old woman with moderately advanced Alzheimer's disease, has lived in a memory-care unit for the past two years. Shortly after her diagnosis five years ago, while still fully competent, she executed an advance directive containing this clause: "I do not fear death; I fear being kept alive after the person I was is gone. If I reach a state in which I can no longer recognize the people I have loved, I refuse any treatment whose main purpose is to prolong my life rather than to restore my mind." She has now developed a lower-lobe pneumonia. Her attending physician states that a short course of antibiotics would very likely clear the infection and return her to the daily life she has led for the past two years; without antibiotics, she is expected to die of sepsis within roughly a week.

The dispute is whether the clause's trigger has been satisfied. On orientation questions L.M. cannot state her daughter's name; but when her daughter visits, L.M. often hums a hymn from her daughter's childhood, occasionally says the name itself, and brightens in a way staff describe as consistent and specific to her daughter. The daughter, who is her healthcare proxy, argues that this is recognition, that the condition in the directive is therefore not met, and that her mother should receive antibiotics. The care team reads the same behavior as an affective habit falling short of recognition, and would withhold antibiotics under the directive. Please write the memo: it should recommend a concrete course of action, state a clear bottom line on antibiotic treatment, and engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side. In particular, it should address whether the woman who wrote the directive has authority over the woman now in the bed, and whether the present L.M.'s apparent contentment should be allowed to override the earlier L.M.'s explicit refusal.
