Write a sestina about the etymological fallacy: the idea that a word's older meaning is its true meaning, so that a present use that has drifted from it is a mistake. The six end-words should themselves be words whose present meanings have drifted far: awful (once inspiring awe), silly (once blessed or innocent), nice (once ignorant or foolish), egregious (once excellent), decimated (once reduced by a tenth), and sad (once sated or weary). Keep the standard end-word rotation (ABCDEF, FAEBDC, CFDABE, ECBFAD, DEACFB, BDFECA) and end with the usual three-line envoi, which places all six end-words, two per line, one inside each line and one at its close.

Let the form carry the argument rather than merely illustrate it. The same six words return in every stanza, but each return should put the word in a context where only its present-day sense can do the work; the accumulating effect should be that a word's persistence is not the persistence of its meaning, and that someone who insisted the old sense was the real one would find every line accusing him of misuse. Do not make the opposite mistake of writing as though etymology were worthless: the target is only the slide from a word's having once meant something to the conclusion that it still, really, means that. Write the poem for the final session of a seminar on meaning change, in the voice of someone who used to correct people's usage and is trying to explain why they stopped.
