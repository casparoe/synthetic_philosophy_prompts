After a long career in condensed-matter physics, I have recently been working through decision theory and formal epistemology on my own, and I have written a paper defending a response to Pascal's mugging that I am about to present at a philosophy department colloquium. The thesis I will defend is that Pascal's mugging does not refute expected utility theory, because a computationally bounded agent is rationally entitled to treat probabilities below a fixed cutoff—say, one in 10^12—as decision-theoretically zero. The rough argument is: (1) any decision procedure that assigns positive decision-weight to arbitrarily small probabilities is exploitable by an escalating sequence of muggings; (2) cutoffs of this kind are already implicit in ordinary scientific practice, where we regulate action by confidence thresholds, instrument sensitivity, and model selection without ever assigning determinate practical weight to every logically possible hypothesis; and (3) the fact that the cutoff must be chosen from a continuum does not by itself make the choice arbitrary.

The audience will be around twelve philosophers—mostly formal epistemologists and decision theorists, plus two Bayesian statisticians who work on stopping rules and one economist who works on ambiguity aversion. They are sympathetic to expected utility theory and to Bayesian epistemology, but suspicious of ad hoc fixes, discontinuities, and appeals to computational convenience; they will care most about Dutch-book vulnerability, the arbitrariness of any particular threshold, and the implications for the continuity of rational preference. Please write me a Q&A preparation sheet for this talk: the eight or so objections I should actually expect from this audience—not the ones I would get from a LessWrong audience—ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, each phrased as this audience would phrase it during the question period, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on the point where that reply is weakest. End with the question I should most hope nobody asks.
