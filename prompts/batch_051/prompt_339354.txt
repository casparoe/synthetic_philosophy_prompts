Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether the fact that I exist tells me anything about how many observers the universe contains. He was pushing the self-indication assumption (SIA): all else equal, a theory that says there are many observers is more likely than one that says there are few, because I'm more likely to find myself existing in a world with lots of observers. I was inclined toward the self-sampling idea instead — my existence isn't evidence one way or another about how many observers there are. I kept hitting him with the presumptuous philosopher: take two theories identical in every other respect, except one predicts a trillion observers and the other a trillion trillion. SIA seems to say you should be a trillion times more confident in the second, purely from the fact that you exist — which is a wild thing to conclude from an armchair.

Then the argument turned a corner. We realized that the whole debate, and the presumptuous philosopher in particular, quietly assumes observers form a finite, well-defined pile you can count and multiply. But if eternal inflation is right, the universe may be spatially infinite, with every finite arrangement of matter repeating indefinately — so any theory that permits at least one observer permits infinitely many. At that point the tidy finite ratio the original argument multiplies by is gone, because both theories may have infinitely many observers. So here's what we couldn't settle: does the presumptuous philosopher objection to SIA actually survive this development, or does its counting premise give out before SIA does? If it does survive, whats the repaired version — is there a principled way to compare infinities of observers that still does the philosophical work, without just choosing an ad hoc density measure?
