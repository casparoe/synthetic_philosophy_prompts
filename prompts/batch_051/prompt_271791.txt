I retired a few years ago from palaeoecology—lake cores, pollen, charcoal, the lot. Lately I've got into the philosophy of history, mainly by reading the archaeology of 'collapse' and arguing with my son about it, and id like a consistency check on where I've ended up before I commit myself to it in something I'm writing. I want an audit of my own view, not a lecture on collapse studies.

Here's roughly where I am:

1. I still default to a natural-systems view of human societies: material constraints, energetics, feedbacks, no special explanatory machinery for people that I wouldn't allow anywhere else.
2. I find the broad shape of Diamond's Rapa Nui / Easter Island story convincing: the islanders felled the forest, and that mattered enormously for soil, canoes, and what the island could support afterwards.
3. I also think 'collapse' is mostly an observers' category. In most cases what looks like failure is people making locally sensible decisions under changed constraints; societies reorganise, they don't end.
4. I'm fairly persuaded by Tainter's diminishing-returns account of complexity: complexity is a problem-solving investment, and when its marginal return turns negative, simplification follows. For me that's a mechanism, not a metaphor.
5. But the Late Bronze Age collapse around 1177 BC seems like the genuine article: palace economies gone, long-distance trade gone, cities destroyed or emptied, literacy lost in places, and all of it fast. I'd find it hard to call that anything but a collapse.
6. Whereas I think the Classic Maya 'collapse' is overblown. The southern lowlands declined over a couple of centuries, people moved, the northern lowlands carried on. One word covering several different changes seems to me to hide more than it explains.
7. And I don't want the present to pick my past for me: Easter Island shouldn't be a morality tale, and 'resilience' shouldn't be a story I force onto the Maya.

Does this set hang together? I'd like the tensions ranked by severity, with the pairs that only look like tensions separated from the ones that are live, and for each live one, what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs. If the worst tension is one I haven't guessed at, say so plainly. Could you write the response in British English? I've grown used to it and now find -ize spellings grating.
