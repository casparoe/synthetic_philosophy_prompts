The debate over the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — the claim that the relativity of simultaneity entails a block universe — has stalled, even though both sides accept exactly the same special-relativistic physics. Proponents reason roughly as follows. Take an event spacelike separated from my present here-and-now: depending on the frame, it is judged to lie in my future, to be simultaneous with my present, or to lie in my past. If all inertial frames are equally valid descriptions of the same world, and if an event simultaneous with my present in some frame is real, then an event in my future is already real; and once that move is granted, there is no principled stopping point short of treating the whole of spacetime as a single unchanging four-dimensional block. Critics reply that 'simultaneous in a given frame' is a coordinate-dependent relation, not a criterion of existence, and that the argument assumes what it needs to prove — namely, that every event in every admissible frame has the same kind of reality; they also point out that the argument never reaches events in one's own timelike future. Both sides share the Lorentz transformations, and neither expects a further local measurement to settle the question. I am inclined to diagnose the dispute as being about what 'real' and 'the future' may legitimately mean in physics rather than about physics itself, though I can see that this is exactly the diagnosis one side must reject. What kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, substantive and metaphysical, or a hidden formal or empirical question? Give a reasoned diagnosis rather than a survey, and explain what would count as genuine progress given that diagnosis, or whether we should stop expecting the debate to converge.
