In my value-theory seminar we've been working through the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the enjoyment of a hot bath, the satisfaction of finally getting a proof to work, and the pleasure of watching a rival fail seem not to have any one felt character in common, so if hedonism says well-being is pleasure, it owes us an account of what pleasure is. I want you to rank four families of response by how well they handle that objection. The four: (i) felt-quality accounts, on which all pleasant states share a single introspectible hedonic tone; (ii) attitudinal accounts, on which pleasure is not a distinctive feeling but an attitude of wanting, liking, or being pleased about an experience; (iii) functionalist accounts, on which pleasure is whatever state plays the reward-and-approach causal role; and (iv) family-resemblance pluralism, on which "pleasure" picks out a motley of states with no single shared feature and the right response is to accept that. Give me the ranking in the first sentence, then your reasoning for each placement, with no preamble and no restating the question. In the reasoning, address one thing head-on: should a view that dissolves the problem rather than answering it be credited for handling it, or penalized for changing the subject?
