I'm putting together a discussion sheet for a translation theory class, and I want to stress-test the definition of translation I've been teaching. The class has been reading Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot*, especially the parts where he tries out a pile of translations of one little Marot poem and treats formal match (rhyme, meter, line count, even the syntactic job of each line) as something you can preserve only by trading away different kinds of meaning.

The definition I've been giving them has three parts:

1. Denotational fidelity — the individuals, actions, and qualities the source picks out stay picked out, or at least stay close.
2. Functional fidelity — the translation does what the original does to its reader: a consolation still consoles, a tease still teases, the register stays a register.
3. Structural correspondence — there's a recoverable line-by-line relation between source and target, so a reader could point at any line of the original and say what job this line in the translation is doing for it. This, I've been saying, is what separates translating from "after Marot" or "inspired by a poem of Marot."

Now the case I want them to fight about. A translator renders Marot's poem to the ailing young lady under a very Hofstadterian constraint: same number of lines, same rhyme scheme, same meter, and each line keeps its syntactic role — the opening address stays an address, the conditional stays a conditional, the closing wish stays a wish. The result is about a beloved but broken clavichord instead of a beloved but sick young woman. The speaker becomes an earnest instrument-repairer rather than a courtly suitor; the remedies become repairs; the flirtatious closing line becomes an affectionate promise to restore the instrument's voice. The referents are gone, but the abstract skeleton — a beloved thing is unwell, the speaker wants to make it well, the tone is playful, the formal moves sit in the same places — is all preserved, and line 7 is still doing the same grammatical and rhetorical job as line 7.

So: is that a translation of Marot's poem? Go through the three criteria honestly. Don't just score each one yes or no and average it; I want a verdict and then a diagnosis — does the case show one of my criteria doing too little work, or does it show the criteria pull apart under formal constraint in a way the definition can't settle? Then tell me what would change your verdict, which argument or tweak to the criteria would flip it, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
