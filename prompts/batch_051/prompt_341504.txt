I've spent the last few years reviewing protocols for an ethics board, and I've basically accepted clinical equipoise as the right standard for when a randomized trial is ethical: you can randomize people when there's genuine uncertainty or disagreement in the expert community about which arm is better, so enrolling a patient doesn't violate the duty of care even if the individual clinician has a private hunch. I know the usual complaints — community uncertainty is vague, it lets trials keep running after the evidence has tilted, its gameable by finding clinicians who still disagree. What I want instead is a map of what would actually count against the position, because I'm starting to suspect it's insulated itself with qualifications.

Play it straight. What would have to be true for clinical equipoise to be the wrong framework, not just imprecise around the edges? What does it predict about which trial designs are permissible that rival standards don't predict (or get wrong)? Which cases or results would be decisive against it, and which would just be awkward? In particular I'd like to know whether it can handle how trials are actually run now — platform trials, adaptive designs, pragmatic trials embedded in routine care — or whether those strain the concept past coherence. I'd personally find it most damning if someone showed the uncertainty condition is doing no normative work: it just restates "we don't know which arm is better" in fancier vocabulary, and the real justificatory work is being done by informed consent and a bare risk threshold. And tell me whether the view is unfalsifiable in a bad way: it seems like it can be stretched to cover a trial where the primary endpoint is already settled and everyone agrees on it, but there's "community uncertainty" about some secondary outcome or about cost-effectiveness. If it can stretch that far, is that a feature or a fatal insulation? Say what defenders would themselves concede counts against it, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reason through it. No preamble, no restating the question.
