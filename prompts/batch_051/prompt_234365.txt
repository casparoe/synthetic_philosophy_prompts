A friend of mine has been defending the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and in the course of trying to state the objection as sharply as possible, he produced an argument that seems to show that ordinary prudence is a mistake. Neither of us can locate the misstep, and I am no longer sure whether to blame the objection itself or the way he has formulated it.

Here is his argument.

(1) That a larger benefit would accrue to one person cannot by itself justify imposing a smaller cost on a different person; only a subject who bore the cost and enjoyed the benefit could be said to be compensated, and in the interpersonal case there is no such subject.

(2) If the absence of a single compensated subject is what blocks interpersonal trade-offs, then it blocks cross-temporal trade-offs as well: a present cost and a future benefit are no more co-experienced than a cost to one person and a benefit to another.

(3) The earlier self that pays the cost and the later self that receives the benefit are distinct subjects in the relevant sense. The earlier self is never compensated for its sacrifice, and no single moment of consciousness contains both the cost and the reward.

(4) Therefore, if the separateness objection is sound, it is not justified for me to accept a painful medical procedure now for the sake of future health, to study now for the sake of a later career, or to save now for the sake of retirement. A future benefit cannot pay a present cost.

(5) But that conclusion is plainly wrong: these intrapersonal trade-offs across time are among the clearest cases in which a present cost is justified by a future benefit, and prudence is not a confusion.

(6) So either the objection has been formulated too strongly, or it was never a good objection in the first place — unless one is prepared to say that saving for retirement is the same kind of error a utilitarian makes.

Tell me which premise or step should be rejected, and explain what rejecting it costs. If the right response is that the objection has been misstated, show how it should be reformulated so that it still condemns the interpersonal trade while permitting the intrapersonal one. I would most like to keep something in the neighborhood of (1) and also keep (5), but I want an honest account of what an adequate formulation must say about the difference between distinct persons and distinct person-stages.
