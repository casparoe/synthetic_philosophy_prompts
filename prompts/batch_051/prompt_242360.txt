OK so I'm a PhD student working on early modern philosophy, and the chapter I'm drafting on Descartes's beast-machine doctrine keeps running into a wrinkle I can't quite settle. In Part Five of the Discourse on Method, Descartes gives what sure looks like a confident argument: he lays out two tests that would reveal a mind — using language in a novel, open-ended way, and acting from a universal reason rather than from the mere arrangement of one's organs — says animals flunk both, and concludes we can take them to be machines without thought. He even treats the idea that animal souls are of the same nature as ours as one of the most dangerous errors for leading weak minds away from virtue. That's the Descartes everyone remembers.

But then in his late correspondence with Henry More, he seems to back off. He says we cannot prove that there is any thought in animals, and — more strikingly — that we also cannot prove there is none, since the human mind can't see into their hearts. He also says he doesn't deny animals life, or even sensation, as long as sensation is understood as something bodily. So I'm left with what looks like two Descarteses: the published one who treats the beast-machine doctrine as all but established, and the letter-writer who admits it's at best probable and lets animals keep a kind of sensation.

I want to know wether these are actually inconsistent, or whether the letter is just making explicit a distinction the Discourse already left room for — and if so, which distinction is doing the heavy lifting. What exactly is he claiming in each place, what are the candidate reconciliations (different senses of a term, a change of mind, a distinction he never draws explicitly), and which one is honest rather than special pleading? I'm less interested in being told he simply wrote differently for different audiences — I want to know what the underlying position actually is.
