I've been kicking around an idea about AI alignment that I can't decide if its either completely standard or quietly insane. I call it the convergence criterion. The claim is: we don't actually need a system to be aligned at every point along its development; what we need is for alignment error to shrink as capability grows, roughly monotonically, tending toward zero in the limit. A model that's 40% aligned at GPT-2 scale and 70% aligned at GPT-4 scale is doing fine on my account, even if 70% would be catastrophic at GPT-6 scale, because by the time it reaches GPT-6 scale the same trend should have pushed it past the point where its competence and its harmfulness stop overlapping. I think of corrigibility as a sort of derivative: not the level of alignment but the sign and size of the change in alignment with respect to capability. If that derivative is positive and large enough, the level takes care of itself before the system can do irreversible damage.

I know the obvious reply is that this is just "we'll align it later" with extra steps, but I don't think that's fair. The criterion makes a falsifiable prediction about each new generation of models, and it gives you a reason to prefer scaling a system whose errors are shrinking over a system that's more aligned right now but flat or trending the wrong way. It also explains why I'm not much moved by jailbreaks of current models: a 20% jailbreak rate is bad as a product but fine as a leading indicator, if the rate is falling. My friend says I've just reinvented something someone wrote a decade ago, or else made optimism into a definition.

I want an honest assessment, not reassurance. Is the convergence criterion a real addition, or does it already exist under a name I haven't seen? What does it get right, and where does it break? Give me the three strongest objections and say which of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. And I specifically want to know whether the criterion is even well-defined before you have a way of measuring alignment error that doesn't smuggle in the answer, and whether "roughly monotonic" is doing more work than the examples make it look like.
