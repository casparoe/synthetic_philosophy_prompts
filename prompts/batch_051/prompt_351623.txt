Last night I spent a long evening arguing with a friend who is reading for the Bar, and I have not been able to stop turning the argument over since. She had been walking me through the way common-law judges actually reason about new cases: they are bound by the ratio of earlier decisions, and they extend or "distinguish" those decisions by asking whether the present facts are sufficiently analogous; the categories of the law are, on her account, built up case by case through resemblance rather than fixed by definitions. I pushed back that this cannot count as a method at all, because unless one already knows which similarities are legally relevant, "analogy" is just a story told after the fact; she replied that knowing which similarities count is precisely what the accumulated cases teach, and that no one needs to state the relevant rule in advance. In fact she defended the stronger claim, which I found rather alarming, that analogy is not one legal technique among others but the fundamental operation of the mind, and that the common law is simply an unusually honest institutional expression of that fact. We then noticed something strange: when the same judges interpret a statute, they behave as though concepts have tolerably determinate definitions and that canons of interpretation settle boundaries; yet when they develop the common law, they speak and act as though categories are fluid, graded, and extended by resemblance. The same institution appears to presuppose two different pictures of what a category is.

Could you reconstruct as precisely as possible what the practice of common-law reasoning by precedent and analogy actually commits its practitioners to about the nature of concepts and categories? I would like to know which rival views of concepts the practice quietly rules out and which it merely fits comfortably; whether it is genuinely committed to the claim that analogy-making is the core of cognition, or whether it could equally be defended by someone who believes that legal categories are really hidden definitions or rules; where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of concepts could license it; and what would have to change in the way courts write and reason their judgements if one replaced that implicit picture with a strictly definitional, rule-based picture of legal categories.
