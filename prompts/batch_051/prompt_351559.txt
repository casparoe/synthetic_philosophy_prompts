In the course of reporting on the history of psychiatric classification, I keep running into a tension that seems to sit at the foundation of the field, and I would like a considered verdict rather than a summary of positions. On one side is a picture with real systematic virtues, associated especially with Ian Hacking's work on what he called the looping effect. The idea is that classifying a kind of person is not like classifying a kind of rock: people know how they are classified, react to the classification, and in reacting change the very kind to which the classification refers. A category such as hysteria or multiple personality appears, gives people a new vocabulary for suffering, attracts certain patients and repels others, shapes how psychiatrists perceive and treat them, and eventually burns out or is replaced. This looping picture explains, with one mechanism, why psychiatric categories are historically volatile, why some diagnoses become self-fulfilling, why a label can alter the course of the condition it names, and why human kinds should not be expected to behave like fixed natural kinds. It has the elegance of a single, almost methodological rule: for any classification of people, ask first how the classified may be moving under the classification.

On the other side, I have a clinical intuition I find difficult to abandon, and so do the psychiatrists I interview: that at least some of the underlying phenomena are there before and apart from the label, with a stability that looping alone seems too weak to explain. The most striking cases are syndromes that have been described, under different names and in very different cultures, with a recognizably constant core — severe mania, melancholia, a first psychotic break with persecutory voices — and that persist even when the surrounding culture stigmatizes them and gives a person every incentive not to be identified. If the looping effect were doing most of the work, one would expect the strongest pressure on the most fashionable categories; yet some of the most durable patterns are among the least socially useful identities a patient could adopt. I am left with a firm sense that classification is tracking something robust that would be there with or without the label, and that a theory on which the label does most of the constituting cannot be the whole truth.

I would like you to arbitrate between the theory and the intuition, taking each at its strongest. Should the intuition of a stable underlying pathology be debunked and dropped — for instance, by showing that apparent stability across centuries is itself an artifact of retrospective reading, institutional inertia, or the looping of the researchers rather than the patients? Should the looping theory be amended, perhaps by saying it applies to some categories and not others — and if so, by what non-circular principle do we draw that line? Or is there a reading on which the two are compatible: a solid causal core can coexist with a label that loops around it, so that stability and classification effects are not competitors but two aspects of the same phenomenon? I want not just a side, but an account of what would justify each of these moves, and which one the evidence and the argument best support.
