I’ve been defending the self-sampling assumption in a reading group on anthropic reasoning. My line has been that the self-indication assumption is too costly, because it turns the fact of one’s own existence into a kind of evidence and ends up licensing the presumptuous philosopher: it seems to let a cosmological theory win just by multiplying the number of observers it predicts, even when all the independent evidence is otherwise equal. But I’ve realized I’ve mostly been attacking SIA rather than stress-testing the position I actually hold. I want a serious map of SSA’s exposure. What would have to be true for SSA to be false or untenable? I don’t just mean which rival assumptions it conflicts with, or what its critics say. I want the kind of thing an honest defender of SSA should already regard as counting against it: specific formal results, cases where SSA yields a verdict its own advocates cannot accept, or failures in how it handles observation selection effects. Which possible developments would be decisive, and which would merely be awkward? For instance, would a principled solution to the reference class problem that forced SSA into SIA be decisive? Would an unresolved reference class problem count as a standing vulnerability, or is it tolerable for the assumption to say nothing until a reference class is supplied? And how much weight should a single strong intuition—like the doomsday argument’s pressure, or the presumptuous philosopher’s absurdity—carry against a rule that otherwise works? I’d personally be most moved by a demonstration that SSA, once its reference class is fixed non-arbitrarily, collapses into SIA, or by a case where SSA’s own adherents disown its output. But I want an honest accounting of how close SSA is to that, not a debater’s brief.
