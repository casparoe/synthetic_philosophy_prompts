There's a pattern in alphabetic writing systems I keep tripping over, and I'm less interested in a fancy name for it then in getting the boundary right. Take two languages that both devoice final consonants. German spells the word for wheel with a final d, even though the sound you actually make is closer to t; it does that because in the genetive the d-sound comes back, so the spelling keeps the stem looking stable even when the letter doesn't match the pronunciation of the bare form. Turkish does the opposite with the word for book: it writes a p when the word stands alone, and a b when a vowel suffix follows, tracking the surface sound in each form. So far this is just the familiar split between scripts that record sound and scripts that record something more like a meaning-unit.

The part I can't pin down is that a single script often does both at once, and it doesn't feel like a global setting — it seems to decide alternation by alternation which ones are worth preserving. English keeps the silent g in sign because it comes back in signature, but in leaf versus leaves it lets the f turn into a v in the spelling as well as the sound, tracking the surface instead. Is there an established term for that per-alternation selectivity, as opposed to the usual sound-tracking versus meaning-tracking contrast applied to a whole script? If there isn't, how would you define it so it doesn't just collapse into the ordinary idea that some spellings preserve stems? Give me the name if there is one, and if there isn't, coin one and mark off the edge cases.
