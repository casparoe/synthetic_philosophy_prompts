I am a novelist, and in my work the line between a seduction and a violation often turns on details that the law's blunt categories leave untouched: a false claim, a withheld fact, an unequal hope. Years of deciding whether scenes of intimacy are fair or foul have left me with firm verdicts about particular cases and no general account that yields them. I would like to know whether such an account exists.

Let A be the person seeking another's consent and B the person deciding whether to give it. The cases below are the ones I keep returning to, with my verdicts.

D1. A tells B that A has been sterilized and cannot cause pregnancy, knowing that B is deciding partly on that ground and would not consent without it. The statement is false. My verdict: not valid.

D2. A removes their wedding ring and lets B infer that A is single, knowing B would refuse a married person. A is married and says nothing. My verdict: not valid.

D3. A falsely tells B that A has published two novels and lived for years in Berlin. B is drawn to A partly for these invented facts, though B has not said that they are conditions of consent. My verdict: valid, though A's dishonesty is real and I would think less of A for it.

D4. A tells B that A is falling in love with B in order to bring B to bed, when A feels nothing of the kind and intends nothing beyond the night. My verdict: valid, but blameworthy on grounds other than consent.

C1. A, who is B's employer, tells B that B's contract will not be renewed unless B has sex with A. B agrees. My verdict: not valid.

C2. A tells their partner B that if the two of them do not have sex, A does not think A can remain in the relationship. B, who loves A, agrees. My verdict: valid, though I find the move cruel.

C3. A tells B that A will disclose an old and damaging secret of B's to their shared circle unless B has sex with A. B agrees. My verdict: not valid.

K1. B is so drunk that B slurs and cannot walk steadily. A, who is sober, has sex with B. My verdict: not valid.

K2. B has had two glasses of wine over a long dinner, remains clear-headed, and is plainly eager. A and B have sex. My verdict: valid.

K3. Three days after burying a long-term partner, B is numb, barely sleeping, and barely eating. A, who has long desired B and knows the circumstances, initiates sex and B listlessly agrees. My verdict: valid, though I judge A harshly for accepting it.

I can see the difficulty myself. D1 and D4 are alike in that A obtains consent by a deliberate falsehood about something A knows B cares about, yet I call one invalidating and the other merely wrong. C1 and C2 are alike in that A makes sex the price of keeping something B wants, yet I distinguish them sharply. My own half-formed rule is that consent fails when a lie or a threat distorts what the act actually is between these two people — its physical conditions or its relational meaning — and merely suffers a defect when the lie only burnishes the picture of the other person. But I cannot say which side of that line C2 falls on, and I suspect the line is only my hunches wearing a definition.

I have one further case on which I keep going back and forth. U1. A, a well-known writer in their forties, sleeps with B, a nineteen-year-old aspiring writer who sought A out as a mentor a week earlier. No lie, no threat, sobriety on both sides. B is flattered, eager, and a little awed; A is aware of the age gap and the adoration, and later realizes A could not tell whether B would have refused anything A proposed. Is B's consent valid?

I am not asking for a survey of positions or for authorities; I am asking what structure the cases themselves force. Please set out the best principle you can find, test it against my cases, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot keep.
