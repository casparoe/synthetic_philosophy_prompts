so i keep coming back to this: every casino i've ever heard of posts a maximum bet and a maximum payout, and even state lotteries cap the advertised jackpot or split it into annual instalments. this is the same world where econs textbooks still teach that a rational player should accept any bet with positive expected value. the old st petersburg coin-toss game is the extreme case: you pay a fee, they flip until the first tails, and you get 2^n dollars, so what should you pay? in principle the expected value is unbounded, and the industry's answer is that no one would ever actually offer that game for real, because the cap is what makes anything like it offerable at all. so here's my question: what view of the value of money, and of rational choice, is built into the practice of capping a payout rather than letting it grow without limit? does a cap presuppose that money has diminishing marginal value, or that there is some point beyond which more money stops mattering, or just that the house has a finite bankroll and wants to survive a bad streak? those are very different commitments and they're rarely told apart. and is the practice coherent with the same state institution selling lottery tickets whose expected value is negative while people buy them by the millions? reconstruct the implicit theory, say which rival theories it quietly rules out, and tell me what a casino or lottery run on a strict expected-money-value rule would actually have to change.
