I work in political theory, on market-based institutions and their democratic critics, and I have been compiling the standard objections to futarchy — the proposal to have citizens vote on a welfare metric and then let prediction markets select the policies that maximize it. So far my list is as follows, and I take each item to be familiar from the existing discussion.

1. The metric problem. Futarchy relocates rather than resolves the question of ends: the choice of welfare metric — GDP, consumption, subjective well-being, a capabilities index — does enormous normative work, and the mechanism itself supplies no defensible procedure for choosing among metrics. The disputes about values reappear one step upstream, untouched by the machinery.

2. The conditional-outcome problem. A market needs a contractible payoff, but the object of interest is the counterfactual difference between the metric under policy A and under policy B. At least one branch is never realized, so it cannot be settled; and the realized branch is contaminated by everything else that happened, so the market's apparent success is hard to audit even after the fact. Either the market must trade on proxy outcomes that are themselves contestable, or it cannot run at all.

3. The thin-market problem. Many concrete policy questions attract too little informed money to produce reliable prices. Subsidizing trade or mandating participation would be needed to sustain liquidity, and each of those fixes introduces its own distortions.

4. Manipulation. A well-resourced trader can move prices to swing a policy decision. The standard reply, that informed arbitrage will correct manipulation, presumes precisely the depth and informed capital that many policy markets would lack.

5. Correlated error. Markets aggregate dispersed private information well, but they cannot correct a mistake that most traders share. When participants reason from the same flawed model, the market can be confidently and persistently wrong.

6. Goodhart drift. Once policies are selected because markets expect them to raise the metric, agents begin to optimize the metric itself rather than the underlying value it tracks. A policy can then pass the market test while degrading what the metric was supposed to stand in for.

7. Reflexivity. If a market prices a recession, a run, or a collapse as likely, that very belief can bring the outcome about. A selection mechanism that acts on market prices can therefore lock in expectations rather than track policy quality.

8. Outcome-causing incentives. A market that pays off on a bad event — an assassination, a terrorist attack, a war — gives traders a financial reason to cause the event. This is the familiar objection to any market on political violence or disaster, and it extends in principle to policy markets whose outcomes include such events.

9. The democratic objection. Wealth-weighted bets aggregate policy judgment in proportion to capital and abandon equal voting. And because voting also serves expressive and dignitary functions, replacing it with a price mechanism may erode the legitimacy and stability of the adopted policies even if prices were perfectly accurate.

I am fairly confident that this list already contains the objections that appear in the standard literature on the proposal. What I want is an objection to futarchy that is not on this list and not a variant of anything on it — not a rewording, not a special case, not a conjunction of items already listed. Then say candidly whether the new objection is any good: whether it marks a genuine pressure point in the design, or whether there is already a satisfying reply available from inside the futarchy framework.
