I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, preparing a long feature on a researcher in embodied cognition whose work draws explicitly on Merleau-Ponty's distinction between motor intentionality and representational planning. She sorts filmed movements into two categories. A movement is labelled 'M', motor-intentional, when the body, without step-by-step execution, already inhabits a situation and is drawn forward by its invitations. It is labelled 'P', planned or representational, when it is steered by an explicit representation — a verbal rule, a diagram, a score, an imagined sequence, or a third-person image of the body itself.

Over several interviews she has classified the following filmed examples for me. Where a single example yielded two verdicts, treat each verdict separately.

1. M — an experienced driver who brakes for a child who darts into the road before she can form the thought 'brake'.
2. M — a tennis player returning a fast serve, whose racquet is already in motion before she consciously sees the ball's direction.
3. M — a wheelchair user threading through a crowded market, adjusting to shifting gaps without having planned a path.
4. M — a goalkeeper diving for a penalty, moving before the words 'dive left' are formulated.
5. P — a novice driver narrating each step of a three-point turn and sequencing her limbs to the narration.
6. A patient in rehabilitation brushes her teeth effortlessly every morning, yet cannot mime the action, without a toothbrush, when a clinician asks; she labelled the habitual brushing M and the mimed performance P.
7. P — a climber on an unfamiliar route pausing to visualise a sequence of holds before committing to it.
8. P — a person assembling flat-pack furniture by consulting the diagram between each step.

Next week she has agreed to classify a new film. It shows a jazz saxophonist improvising over chord changes she has never encountered, in front of an audience. She has no sequence of fingerings planned, and afterwards she can report almost nothing of what her fingers did; while playing, however, she is acutely aware of each fingering as her fingers find it.

Predict the label she is most likely to give. Work through it as follows: identify the two or three principles about motor intentionality that fit her existing verdicts equally well; state what each principle predicts for the saxophonist; give your best guess, with the reasons for preferring one principle; and describe the further piece of film that would most cleanly reveal which principle she is actually running.

Return your answer as a single JSON object with this schema:

- "candidate_principles": an array of objects, each with "name" (a brief label), "statement" (one or two sentences), and "fit_with_existing_verdicts" (one or two sentences on how it accommodates examples 1 to 8);
- "predictions_for_new_case": an array of objects, each with "principle" (the name used above), "predicted_label" (either "M" or "P"), and "reason" (a single sentence);
- "best_guess": an object with "predicted_label" ("M" or "P"), "preferred_principle" (the name), "reasoning" (a short paragraph), and "confidence" (one of "low", "medium" or "high");
- "settling_case": an object with "description" (the further film you would show), "expected_labels" (what each candidate principle predicts for it), and "what_it_would_settle" (one sentence).

Please write in British English.
