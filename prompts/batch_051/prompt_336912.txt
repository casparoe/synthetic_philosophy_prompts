At a reading group I sat in on, two participants ended up in an unresolved argument about whether moral judgment works by applying general principles or by responding to the salient particulars of a case. I took notes; the exchange went, as nearly as I can reconstruct it:

1. One of them opened: whenever he judges an action wrong, he has to be able to say under which covering principle it falls, since otherwise he would merely be reporting a private response. If a friend promises to return a borrowed book on Friday and leaves it at home, the friend has done wrong, but only because the judgment is backed by something like *promises ought to be kept unless overridden by stronger considerations*. The principle, he added, is what makes the judgment criticizable, defensible, and teachable.

2. The other replied: the principle is either fixed or shapeless. On the fixed reading, promise-breaking always counts against an act with roughly constant weight. But with trivial social promises, promises extracted by coercion, or promises to do what one should never have undertaken, the fact that a promise was made seems to carry *no* weight at all, not merely outweighed weight. On the shapeless reading, promise-breaking counts against an act only when and to the degree the circumstances already say it does, and then the principle merely records the verdict rather than anticipating it.

3. The first answered: she was conflating contributory with decisive considerations. A pro tanto principle gives promise-breaking a default negative charge while allowing that the charge may be defeated by competing considerations, themselves recognized under further principles. In the problem cases, he said, the charge is present but defeated — which is exactly why we reach for special explanations, such as coercion, absurd undertakings, or changed background, instead of dismissing the cases as irrelevant. They would need no such explanation if there were no charge to begin with.

4. The other replied: then specify the defeating conditions in advance, without saying "when the salient facts call for it." If the list of defeaters is open-ended and can be recognized only after the verdict, the principle-plus-defeaters package makes no predictions; it is a running commentary on the very judgments it was supposed to ground. A standard that can be revised whenever cases go against it is not a standard but a record.

5. The first said: she was demanding a decision procedure that even scientific theories do not provide. A defeasible principle earns its keep by telling us which features of a novel case to attend to and what burden a justification must meet. Her alternative, he claimed, is worse: without default weights, on what basis can she say a new case resembles an old one? Either she can articulate the basis of resemblance, in which case she has a principle in disguise, or she cannot, in which case her judgments are unrevisable by argument and incommunicable to others.

6. The other said: the basis of comparison need not be codified in advance. She can explain after the fact why this new case resembles a settled one — by pointing to a feature that makes the same consideration count in the same way here — without having to claim that any such feature carried a default weight all along. She added a sharper point: he runs together a reason's being outweighed with its being switched off. An outweighed reason leaves a trace, such as residual regret; in several of the cases above, no regret is warranted at all, which suggests the reason was absent rather than merely defeated.

This is recognizably a particularism/generalism dispute, but I would like you to adjudicate the moves as they actually appear here rather than settle the large debate by general argument. For each of the six moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say which of the two has the stronger position *on this exchange*, and identify anything both of them are missing that would improve the dispute.
