A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about luck egalitarianism, and I want help locating where his argument went wrong—or, annoyingly, learning that it didn't. His argument ran as follows:

(1) Justice requires us to compensate people for bad brute luck—the unchosen circumstances that leave them worse off through no fault of their own—but not for bad option luck, the unfortunate results of genuine choices.

(2) An outcome is bad option luck only if the person whose choice produced it was responsible for making that choice.

(3) A person is responsible for making a choice only if she is responsible for the preferences, traits, and capacities that the choice flowed from.

(4) No one is responsible for those underlying preferences, traits, and capacities: they are set by genetic and social lottery. Nor does it help to say that a person chose to cultivate some of them, because the capacity, inclination, and opportunity to engage in that self-cultivation were also unchosen.

(5) Therefore, no one is responsible for any choice.

(6) Therefore, nothing genuinely falls on the option-luck side of the line; all supposed option luck is brute luck in disguise.

(7) Therefore, luck egalitarianism collapses: if even the results of our choices are unchosen misfortune, the view must regard every unfortunate outcome as fit for compensation.

He then added that (7) is not just counterintuitive; it contradicts the distinction asserted in (1), so the view that remains is no longer luck egalitarianism but something that cannot hold anyone responsible for the outcomes of their choices.

I accepted (1) coming in, and I deeply want to reject (7), but each time I try to specify the mistake I find myself drifting to a different step. Is the argument valid? If it is, I should either accept (7) or give up (1). If it is not, tell me exactly which step fails. In doing so, say what "responsible" must mean at each stage for the argument to go through, what the strongest reading of the disputed step is, and whether a luck egalitarian can block the collapse by weakening or replacing one of the premises without quietly abandoning the brute-luck/option-luck distinction itself.
