I have been working through the epistemic program in game theory, and I find its explanatory ambitions compelling. The view I am drawn to runs roughly as follows: when the game and the players' rationality are common knowledge, players maximize expected utility relative to Bayesian posteriors, and iterated conditioning on what is known should filter out exactly the rationalizable strategies, with backward induction as the limiting prediction in finite games of perfect information. The systematic virtues are real: the view offers a uniform account of where equilibrium comes from, explains why shared-prior agents should not agree to disagree, and cleanly separates public structure from private information. Against this I have a firm intuition that something goes wrong from the interim perspective of a player who observes a move that the theory said would not be made. That player is no longer rationally compelled to reason about the deviator as though her rationality were still common knowledge: the correct response seems to depend on whether the deviation was a rare mistake, a sign of irrationality, or evidence that the deviator models the game differently, and common knowledge of rationality appears silent on that question. I do not want a verdict so much as a negotiated settlement. What would it take to debunk this intuition as a confusion between the analyst's ex ante standpoint and the player's interim standpoint? What would it take to amend or abandon the global common-knowledge assumption, moving perhaps to hierarchies of belief or to rationality that is known only along the actual path? And is there a reading of common knowledge of rationality on which the theory and the intuition are both preserved?
