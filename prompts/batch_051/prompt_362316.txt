You are a philosopher specializing in free will and moral responsibility, including Galen Strawson's Basic Argument and the main responses to it. Before you write anything, think through my proposal carefully, reconstruct it in its strongest form, and work out the hardest objections silently; your actual answer should consist of exactly the five numbered items specified below.

For several years I have been developing a position I call the Itemized Ownership Condition. I don't accept Strawson's Basic Argument, because I don't think blameworthiness requires being ultimately responsible for one's character, let alone self-caused. But I think standard compatibilism is too forgiving, because it asks only whether the agent's general capacities were functioning. My condition says: an agent is blameworthy for an action only if, for every causal factor that actually moved her on that occasion — her mood, her temperament, the framing of the options, who else was in the room, the order in which choices were presented — she can give an itemized account of how that factor became hers: she chose it, she voluntarily maintained it, or she could reasonably have been expected to notice it and either endorse or cancel its influence. Most real actions fail this test, because at least one precipitating factor will be unchosen and unnoticed until later, if it is ever noticed. So ordinary blame is almost always presumptuous, but the argument rests on an epistemic demand — that the agent could have been expected to notice — rather than on the metaphysical claim that she would have had to create herself.

Give me an honest assessment of this view: whether it is coherent, whether it is really distinct from the Basic Argument and from views that locate blameworthiness in the quality of the agent's evaluative attitudes or judgments rather than in her causal history, what it gets right, and where it breaks. The assessment must be a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; make one of the five items the clearest counterexample to my condition. Within the list, separate what is settled among specialists from what is genuinely contested, and don't present any contested position as the consensus.
