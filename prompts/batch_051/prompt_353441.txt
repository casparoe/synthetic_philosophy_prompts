I am in a course on language, gender, and social categories, and we recently read Hofstadter’s Person Paper along with some critical responses. I have been trying to work out what my own judgments about linguistic reform actually commit me to, because they feel consistent from the inside but I cannot yet state the principle that ties them together. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases.

I approve of style-guide changes like freshman to first-year student, chairman to chair, and policeman to police officer; these seem to remove an invisible masculine default. I think a campaign to make ordinary speakers say personhole instead of manhole is silly, because in my usage manhole does not mark gender the way freshman does. I found Hofstadter’s satirical parallel, in which a society treats white as a generic term for person and then purges it from compounds, genuinely funny and useful for exposing something about the kind of default freshman encodes. But I also resisted the piece’s implication that the cases are on all fours: the felt history of white and black in language is not interchangeable with male and female in exactly the way the satire assumes. When someone avoids blacklist and whitelist purely because of the words black and white, I think that is overcorrection, but I do support avoiding black as a term for evil in institutional contexts, because that trope seems to me to carry real weight. I support retaining generic she in a thought-experimental passage to make the markedness of generic he visible, and I would not view it as a proposed rule for literal usage; but I would object if a sincere reform movement actually tried to make generic she mandatory, because that seems to replace one default with another rather than remove the default. Finally, I find the proposal to replace seminal with germinal or foundational overetymological and overcautious, even though I agree the metaphor has a masculine origin.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures this pattern? Is there a coherent account of when a piece of language carries a default or a valence that ought to be disrupted, and when it is inert or being read too etymologically? Which of my verdicts, if any, must I overturn to make the set consistent? And what would your principle say about a further case I have not settled: replacing the term master bedroom with primary bedroom? I am not asking for a list of considerations; I want the principle stated precisely enough that it yields a definite verdict on the new case.
