I spent most of my life in a wealthy city where eating a strictly plant-based diet was straightforward, and I accepted the standard arguments for veganism without much friction. I now live and work in a small town in a lower-middle-income country, and those arguments feel less decisive than I expected. Legumes and grains are cheap, but nutritionally adequate plant-based combinations are not reliably available at a price and level of convenience that fits the lives of manual laborers, fortified foods are scarce or expensive, animal products carry real weight in hospitality and communal meals, and many of the people I buy from depend on those purchases in a way that feels unlike anonymous supermarket involvement. A friend at home insists that none of this matters for the underlying obligation: if animals have serious moral weight, eating them is wrong, and someone who cannot meet the standard has done something wrong that is excused, not something genuinely permitted. His credence in the claim I am about to state is around 0.25; mine is around 0.7, though neither of us has worked out the structure of the disagreement.

Let P be the claim that, in a context of the kind just described, a person of ordinary means who eats modest quantities of locally produced animal products because adequate plant-based alternatives are not reliably available at reasonable cost acts permissibly rather than wrongly-but-excusably. I am not asking whether veganism is required in wealthy settings with abundant alternatives; my friend and I agree it is. Please give me your own credence in P, but more importantly, decompose it. At minimum separate: (i) the probability that obligations toward animals are conditional on the realistic alternatives open to the agent rather than absolute, with scarcity going only to excuse; (ii) the probability that nutritionally adequate plant-based diets are in fact reliably available at reasonable cost in the relevant settings; (iii) the probability that an individual's modest purchases in a local market have enough expected effect on animal production and suffering to ground a strict duty; (iv) the probability that the distinctive social and economic costs of abstaining in such settings count as genuine constraints rather than discomforts an agent is obligated to absorb; and any further terms you think belong. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, and show how you combine them into your credence in P.

Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, what evidence or argument would move that term most, which term your final credence in P is most sensitive to, and where you think a person at 0.7 and a person at 0.25 are most likely disagreeing.
