ok so I’m giving a talk in three weeks to my philosophy department’s language and logic workshop, and I’ve spent too long in rationalist forums, so my default move is to reach for computational metaphors before I’ve earned them with this crowd. The paper is a defense of what I’m calling procedural truth. The two-sentence version: Alfred Tarski’s 1944 paper *The Semantic Conception of Truth and the Foundations of Semantics* has this paragraph where he says, roughly, that in colloquial language you can form a sentence that says of itself that it is not true, and from that plus ordinary logic you get a contradiction, so the everyday concept of a true sentence is inconsistent, or at least cannot be used coherently without restrictions. I think the derivation is right but the conclusion is too strong. My view is that ‘true’ is a procedure for checking whether a sentence matches the world; on normal input it returns true or false, but on the liar input the procedure doesn’t halt, so we should model truth as a partial function with a no-verdict output that is a fact about the procedure, not a third truth value. I then argue this gives you almost everything ordinary truth gives you, and the liar becomes a runtime error, not a paradox.

The audience is not my people. It’s a philosophy workshop. There are three deflationists who think the moral of the Liar is that disquotation just needs to be restricted; one of them works mostly on Horwich-style minimalism. There are two classical logicians who are convinced Tarski’s hierarchy was the right answer and will see my third status as smuggling in a semantics for ungroundedness without doing the fixed-point construction. There’s one dialetheist who will say I’m just refusing to accept the true contradiction. And there’s a cluster of epistemologists who don’t much care about truth but will care if my view means you can’t apply ‘true’ to sentences we normally rely on. I’m worried that my hole card is Kripke’s fixed-point construction and that if I bring it up they’ll say I’ve reinvented it badly, and if I don’t bring it up they’ll say I have no semantics.

Give me a prep sheet for Q&A, not a defense of the view. I want the objections in roughly the order they’ll come, phrased the way this specific crowd will phrase them, from the likely players, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my replies are soft. Include the question I’m hoping nobody asks. Also flag the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. And if the computational metaphor is doing more work than the arguments, tell me that too.
