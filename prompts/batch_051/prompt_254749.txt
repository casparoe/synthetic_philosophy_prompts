I follow a policy in political argument that I would like you to assess. When someone supports a proposal for the United States by citing a Nordic country—say, Danish flexicurity, a Swedish education reform, or Norway's sovereign wealth fund—I discount the example almost to zero unless the person can state the causal mechanism behind the success and the background conditions that would have to travel with it. I apply the rule most strictly when the proposal appears to depend on high taxation or high social trust, because those strike me as conditions a country cannot simply legislate into existence. This means I often reject the inference before examining the details. Is this rule a defensible epistemic shortcut, or a bias dressed up as a methodological scruple? Under what conditions should one successful foreign case—or a handful of similar ones—move my credence about a domestic policy, and is there a version of the rule that keeps its benefits without its costs? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study comparative political economy and institutional transfer, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
