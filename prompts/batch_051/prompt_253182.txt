I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, writing a chapter on the distribution of phenomenal consciousness across nonhuman animals, and I have been struck by how little the debate has moved despite decades of increasingly sophisticated empirical work. The parties largely share the behavioral data: fish perform flexible avoidance learning and show trade-offs between nociceptive responses and other motivational drives, cephalopods solve novel problems, and some insects display forms of learning once thought to require much larger nervous systems. What they disagree about is whether any of this licenses the attribution of felt experience. On one side, inclusivists hold that consciousness is phylogenetically widespread, extending across the vertebrates and into at least some invertebrates, and they treat a broad range of behavioral and cognitive markers as sufficient, at least cumulatively, to justify attribution. On the other side, restrictivists hold that phenomenal states are limited to a narrow set of taxa, paradigmatically mammals and birds, and they regard complex behavior in fish, cephalopods, and insects as achievable without subjective experience; on their view, attribution requires a neural or computational substrate of a kind not found in those animals. The result is that the two sides look at the same experiments and draw opposite conclusions, and neither has a clear procedure for being shown wrong.

My working hypothesis is that this is not best understood as a straightforward empirical dispute that more data will settle. It looks instead like a disagreement about the epistemology of consciousness attribution: about what sort of evidence could in principle warrant an inference from behavior and anatomy to felt experience, about whether the concept of phenomenal consciousness is held fixed across the two camps, and about where the burden of proof should fall given the asymmetric moral stakes of false positives and false negatives. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict for one side. Is it substantive or partly verbal, with the two camps tracking different properties under the same term? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, such as whether convergent evolution can produce the same kind of experience through radically different neural architectures? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, one about the functional profile of cognition and one about the presence of subjective experience? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a validated marker, a formal result, a sharper conceptual distinction, a change of question, or an honest argument that no convergence should be expected?
