I'm not sure this is a philosophy question exactly, but people keep telling me I'm 'doing philosophy' when I raise it, so here goes. I run a decent-sized forum for developers, and I've started noticing the same pattern in every language-war or politics thread. Someone will post a single example of a teardown or leaked post from the other side — one bad commit, one awful take — and the thread immediately treats it as evidence about what that whole community is really like. Then within the same day, often by the same people, someone posts an equivalent example coming from our side, and suddenly the rule is 'you can't generalize from one data point,' 'that person doesn't speak for us,' 'why are we platforming an outlier.' The weird thing is that nobody seems to notice they're applying two different evidential standards; if you lay the two cases side by side, they'll insist the cases are different, but they can't articulate the difference beyond who's side it was.

There's also a second move I'm fairly sure is related: the out-group bad actor gets classified as 'the mask slipping' — this is what those people would all do if they could — while the in-group bad actor gets unlisted from the group entirely, like a No-True-Scotsman exception. My hunch is that both moves come from one underlying switch: an asymmetric rule for deciding when a sample of size one counts as evidence about a whole population. Does this have a name? I've bumped into terms like out-group homogeneity bias and fundamental attribution error, and obviously No True Scotsman, but none of them seems to pick out the exact thing — the standard for generalizing flipping depending on whose side produced the example. I'm less after a label than the carving: is this really one phenomenon, how does it differ from those near neighbors, and where are its edges?
