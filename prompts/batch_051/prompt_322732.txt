I am a novelist, not a scientist, and the center of my current book is a creature I cannot decide how to describe on the page. I need a single argued ruling, committed even if provisional, because the sentences of the book will depend on it. I am not looking for a literature review or sources; I am looking for a decision drawn from the criteria and the case.

The principle is the old comparative-psychology one, often called Morgan's canon. Stated loosely: when an action can be explained by a lower psychological faculty, we are not entitled to explain it by a higher one. For my purposes the relevant higher faculty is genuine memory and anticipation—an internal state that represents how the environment was or will be, and is used flexibly to guide building. I have been using five criteria. A system counts as having such a state only if:

1. There is a trace that outlasts the triggering episode and can be shown to be reused later.
2. The trace has content: it is about something—say, the coming dry season—rather than a bare silt reading.
3. The trace drives behavior flexibly across contexts rather than through one fixed releasing condition.
4. The behavior cannot be retrodicted as well by a lower-order mechanism—associative learning, homeostasis, a fixed action pattern, or stigmergy, in which the environment rather than any animal carries the trace.
5. Attributing the state does new predictive work: it predicts a behavior not among the ones used to infer it, and that prediction survives.

Now the creature. It is a colonial marine polychaete—a bristle worm—that I am calling the silt-lace worm. Each individual is about a centimeter long, nearly blind, lives roughly two days, and senses only local water pressure, oxygen tension, and suspended-silt load. The reef as a whole, over months, builds and maintains a porous lattice of cemented sand channels; the lattice's shape controls how oxygenated water reaches the brood chambers between tides.

Four observations matter. First, the lattice is seasonally remodeled: before the dry season the colony reworks its openings for lower, slower flow, though many generations of worms have lived and died since the previous dry season, and no builder has experienced both. Second, after experimental destruction the colony does not restore the old shape. It builds something novel—a curved deflector, a raised vent—that is appropriate for the flow field that will exist only once the rest of the structure has regrown. Third, the natural lower-order explanation is stigmergic. Each worm follows a local rule: deposit grains where the local shear-stress gradient is steep, dissolve cement where ammonia accumulates. The lattice stores the results of past choices and changes the gradients that later worms meet, so the apparent intelligence would lie in the changing structure, not in any individual. Consistent with this, when researchers reverse the channel flow for a day and then restore it, colonies whose local gradients are matched to controls nonetheless build a different final lattice—taller and narrower—apparently because the cement retained micro-stresses from the reversal: a memory laid down in cement, read by later cement work, and never held by a worm. Fourth, one fact seems to resist that deflation. If the researchers block the pressure sense, the colony still performs its seasonal remodel, using oxygen gradients alone; if they block oxygen, it uses silt-load gradients alone; only when both are blocked does remodeling fail. The same environmental variable appears to remain available to the building process through either channel, although no individual worm ever compares channels.

The question is this. At the colony level, does the silt-lace worm meet the five criteria well enough that the novel may honestly say the colony remembers or anticipates? Or should the book say only that the river, the lattice, and the local rules co-remember for it, while no worm and no colony ever does? Go through the five criteria one by one and place the case against each honestly. Where a criterion underdetermines the answer, say so, and say whether the difficulty lies in the organism or in the criterion itself. If the criteria themselves are at fault, say which one and what should replace it. Then commit: give me a definite ruling—memory, or no memory—and identify the single feature of the case, if removed, that would most likely flip your verdict.
