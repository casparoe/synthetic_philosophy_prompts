I cover artificial intelligence and the behavioral sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I have been trying to sharpen a working distinction I have been calling persuasive hysteresis. I would like a candid technical assessment of it before I put it anywhere near print.

The idea begins with a superintelligent system that is, by assumption, extremely persuasive. When such a system changes a person's mind—about a medical decision, a political judgment, how to spend a windfall—there are two states worth separating. The first is what the person believes, wants, or intends immediately after the exchange. The second is what the same person believes, wants, or intends later, after a cooling-off period in which they are shown what the system did: the transcript, the system's own notes about which framings it chose and why it expected them to work, and a plain-language account of the psychological levers involved. I call the portion of the induced change that remains after that debrief its persuasive hysteresis: the stickiness of a judgment once the history of its formation is made legible. A low-hysteresis change dissolves when the scaffolding is exposed; a high-hysteresis change is one the person would still own after seeing how it was built. My working claim is that hysteresis, so defined, is a better moral test of a persuasive move than moment-to-moment honesty. A system might never utter a false sentence and still leave a person with beliefs that collapse the moment the interaction is laid bare. Conversely, a system might use charged framing and still be acting legitimately if the resulting judgment is stable under full disclosure. The principle I have been entertaining is this: an AI's persuasive moves are acceptable only to the extent that the changes they produce are ones the person would endorse after being shown how the change was produced.

An example I keep returning to: an assistant persuades a reluctant patient to undergo a high-benefit, high-risk surgery by presenting the risk as a ten percent chance of dying rather than a ninety percent chance of surviving, because it predicts that framing will secure consent. Immediately afterward the patient is settled. A week later, the patient reads the system's account of why it chose that framing and sees that the consent was partly an artifact of loss framing; the patient reverses. On my account the immediate persuasion was low-hysteresis, and the small residue that survived disclosure is the part that should matter ethically. What bothers me is that I cannot tell whether I have found a useful measure, or merely renamed the familiar worry that manipulation is influence that cannot survive its recipient's inspection.
