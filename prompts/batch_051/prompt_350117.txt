I came across this passage in an explainer on the legal principle that ignorance of the law is no excuse, and I want to know how much of it I can trust before I repeat it to my students:

"In most legal systems there is a presumption that everyone knows the law, which is why ignorance of the law is no defence. The rule is harsh but basically just. It is practical, because if ignorance were an excuse every defendant would simply claim not to have known, and courts cannot look into people's minds. It is also fair in substance, because the criminal law overwhelmingly tracks ordinary morality: the acts it punishes are wrong independently of the law, so anyone with a normal conscience is already on notice, and the law merely supplies the precise penalty. The only serious problem arises from obscure administrative regulations, but that problem is handled decently by treating those regulations as civil matters rather than true crimes."

Something about this feels too neat, but I cannot tell where the distortion is. Please give me an accuracy audit. For each element of the passage, separate what is an accurate statement of doctrine, what is a defensible rationale, what is a common simplification, and what a careful defender of the rule would qualify or reject. Then answer two further questions. First, do the stated rationales support the broad rule — ignorance is never an excuse for any criminal charge — or only a narrower principle, and which narrower principle would that be? Second, does the "merely civil" move actually resolve the worry about obscure regulations, or does it rest on a misdescription of how such offenses are classified and punished? Finally, say whether the author's overall conclusion — that the rule is basically just — survives once the corrections are made.
