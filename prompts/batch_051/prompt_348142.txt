I have been trying to get clear on a family of views that all get called nihilism, and I keep sliding from one to another in a way that worries me. In a reading group we looked at mereological nihilism, the claim that there are no composite objects: strictly speaking, there is no table in front of me, only simples arranged table-wise. We contrasted it with moral error theory, the claim that there are no moral properties or facts, so that sentences like stealing is wrong are not literally true. What struck me is that both views seem to have the same shape. Each denies the reality of something our ordinary discourse is committed to, and each therefore seems to make a huge swath of ordinary talk false. For the mereological nihilist, there is a table in this room comes out false; for the moral error theorist, stealing is wrong comes out false. But if that is right, I do not see why the question whether nihilism is livable should be treated as a deep open problem. If you really believed the view, you would have to think that almost everything you say in daily life is false — not inconvenient or impolite, but false. How could anyone sincerely inhabit a life like that, rather than just acting as if tables and obligations exist while privately denying that they do? Does the shared structure of these views settle the livability question from the start?

I suspect I am running together a metaphysical claim about what exists and a semantic claim about which sentences are true, or perhaps running together different senses of nihilism, but I cannot locate the slide. You are a professional philosopher working on metaphysics and metaethics. Please think slowly and carefully here: start by disentangling the different claims lumped together under nihilism, then say whether my inference from no composite objects or no moral facts to most ordinary talk is false is sound in each case, and finally clarify what the livability question is really asking when it is asked about each view.
