I keep noticing a move in arguments about what ultimately matters, and I would like to know whether it has an established name and how it is best analyzed. The move goes like this. Someone claims that beneath the apparent variety of human goods there is really only one kind of thing that matters — call it well-being, or happiness, or the satisfaction of desire. When someone objects that friendship matters in a way that does not reduce to that one thing, the reply is that friendship is really just a component of it, or a reliable means to it. When someone objects that understanding matters for its own sake, the reply is that understanding is just a particularly valuable kind of mental state, which is part of the one thing. When someone objects that justice matters even when it leaves everyone feeling worse, the reply is that caring about justice is itself part of the one thing, or that justice tends toward it in the long run. Each individual reply sounds reasonable, but over the course of the conversation the one favored thing gets quietly redefined to include whatever the other person was pointing to, so that no concrete case could ever count against the original claim. And yet the person making the claim insists that it is a substantive discovery about the structure of value, not just a decision to use one word broadly.

What I want to know is this. Does this pattern — defending a single-value view by successively reinterpreting each apparently distinct good as a disguised form of the one, until the view is compatible with any pattern of concrete judgments — have a name of its own? How exactly does it differ from ordinary reductionism about values, from a merely verbal dispute, and from the general strategy of making a claim immune to counterexamples? And what would actually show that the values really are many rather than one thing in disguise? I am less interested in a label than in the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are. Please answer in American English.
