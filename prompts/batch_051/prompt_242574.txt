I hold a conciliationist view about peer disagreement, specifically the equal weight view: when I learn that someone I have good reason to regard as my epistemic peer — approximately as likely as I am to evaluate the relevant evidence correctly, and in possession of the same evidence — believes otherwise about a proposition I have carefully considered, I should move substantially toward their credence, typically splitting the difference. The view is motivated in part by Adam Elga in Reflection and Disagreement (2007) and David Christensen in Epistemology of Disagreement: The Good News (2007). Elga gives the core reasoning in a passage early in his statement of the equal weight view, which I would paraphrase this way: once I have discovered that a peer disagrees with me, my prior confidence in my own appraisal of the shared evidence is no longer a neutral starting point I am entitled to trust more than theirs, because the disagreement itself bears on whether my appraisal was correct. Christensen presses a similar line through a case in which a friend, equally good at mental arithmetic, reaches a different figure for a restaurant bill.

I want a detailed map of what would genuinely count against this view. Please give me the defeaters, sorted by how decisive they would be, and separated into three groups: fatal defeaters — things that, if shown true or if they occurred, should make me abandon or severely restrict conciliationism; awkward but nonfatal defeaters — things that force revision of detail or expose real costs without toppling the view; and apparent defeaters that a sophisticated conciliationist may reasonably set aside as targeting a strawman or a different question. For each candidate, specify the evidence, intuition, formal result, or case as it would actually arise; explain why it threatens the view; and then say how a committed conciliationist should respond — accommodate, neutralize, or admit the cost.

Pay particular attention to defeaters that do not simply deny the peer assumption: introspection and self-trust as asymmetrical evidence; disagreement with one's own past or future self; cases where the other party is superior in one relevant respect but inferior in many others; hard cases of cognitive penetration or bias affecting both parties; and the worry that equal weight leads to skepticism about controversial beliefs. For each, say whether it is a live empirical or dialectical possibility rather than a merely logical one. Finally, assess the overall exposure of the position: has it, with the qualifications you would grant it, been insulated from refutation, and if so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of a claim about rational response to evidence?
