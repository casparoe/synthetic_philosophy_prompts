I have recently become preoccupied with how a conversation can end up settling propositions that nobody ever actually put into words. A recurring disagreement with my partner follows this pattern: by the end, we are both somehow answerable for claims neither of us remembers stating, and I cannot tell whether that is a defect in how we talk or a normal feature of language. A friend in a linguistics course sent me a handout on presupposition, accommodation, and conversational scorekeeping that explains the mechanism this way. A sentence presupposes p just when p must already be in the common ground for the sentence to be either true or false; for instance, 'The present king of France is bald' presupposes that there is a present king of France, and is otherwise neither true nor false. When a presupposition is missing, hearers accommodate by quietly adding it to the shared stock of beliefs, even if they privately doubt it, just to keep the conversation going. The handout's conclusion is that conversation is governed by a public, objective scoreboard, and that the only rule that ultimately matters is the prohibition on asserting falsehoods. Something about the word 'already' nags at me, since the handout also says missing presuppositions get added after the fact, but I am not sure whether that is a real tension or just my inexperience. I find the scoreboard picture appealing because it fits my experience, which makes me worry I am accepting it too uncritically. Is this a fair summary of the view, and which parts would a philosopher of language want me to qualify or give up?
