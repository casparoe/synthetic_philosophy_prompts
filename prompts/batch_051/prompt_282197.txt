I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've recently gone down a rabbit hole implementing elementary cellular automata — one-dimensional systems in which each cell's next state depends only on its own state and those of its two immediate neighbors. I was genuinely surprised by how much complicated-looking behavior falls out of such a tiny rule space, and by the fact that Rule 110, which looks unremarkable, turns out to be Turing-complete. Reading Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science, I keep hitting the same question: is the principle of computational equivalence a scientific claim about the world, or is it a philosophical gloss on some mathematical results?

Write a dialogue between two equally competent characters who disagree about this. One defends Wolfram's project as science: simple programs can be computationally universal, the principle of computational equivalence is best read as an empirical law about systems in nature, and computational irreducibility explains why we often cannot shortcut a prediction. The other grants the mathematical results but argues the project fails as science: the principle is too vague or nearly tautological to test, "more complex than it looks" is not a measurable criterion, and the framework has mostly re-described known phenomena rather than producing novel predictions that could have turned out wrong.

The two characters should respond directly to each other's actual points, with real back-and-forth, and neither should be a strawman. Have them work through, at least, these questions: what observation would count as a violation of the principle of computational equivalence; whether a proof that a rule is universal is a different kind of claim from an empirical statement that some physical process is computationally equivalent to that rule; whether computational irreducibility is a fact about the world or about our current inability to find a shortcut; and whether the disagreement is ultimately substantive or mostly a dispute about what should count as science.

The dialogue should end without a tidy winner, so I can see where each side has to concede and where the disagreement bottoms out. As you write, please keep what is settled clearly separated from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as if everyone accepted it.
