I'm a software engineer who has been building a morphological engine for English and a couple of constructed languages, and the design work has forced me into a set of commitments about what a grammatical rule is that I'd like checked for internal consistency. I have no formal background in philosophy, so I'd ask you to keep the diagnosis tied closely to the claims as stated rather than surveying the literature.

By suppletion I mean forms like go/went and good/better, where the inflected item is not derived from the base by any general mapping. By the wug test I mean the usual procedure of giving a speaker a novel item such as wug or blick and seeing whether they can supply the inflected form, such as wugs or blicked; I treat extension to novel items as the standard evidence that a productive rule is in play.

The commitments are as follows.

1. A grammatical rule is a function from a base item and a set of features to an inflected output, and what makes it a rule rather than a description of a finite list is that it applies by default to novel items. A rule can have exceptions and still be a rule.

2. Exceptions are not cases where a rule runs and fails. When go surfaces as went, the past-tense rule is blocked from applying at all, and a stored form is retrieved instead. Exceptions are overrides, not partial applications.

3. Suppletion is not generated by a rule. It is simply a lookup table keyed by the base item and the feature set, returning a stored form. In my engine I implement it as an override layer that short-circuits the regular transformation.

4. Sound change is exceptionless in the historical sense: when a sound changes, it changes in every word in which the conditions for the change are met at the time. Apparent exceptions are the result of later analogy, borrowing, or a second, more specific sound change.

5. The wug test is the right criterion for deciding whether a pattern is a rule in a speaker's grammar or merely a convenient summary of a list. If a pattern does not extend to novel items, it is not a rule.

6. Suppletive forms nevertheless occupy regular cells of the paradigm: speakers know that went is the past tense of go and that better is the comparative of good, in exactly the slots where regular outputs appear. The slots are there even when the regular function does not fill them.

7. A sound change, once it has run its course, is not a synchronic rule. Present-day speakers do not reapply a historical sound change when they produce new forms. The regularity of sound change is regularity in a historical process, not in a live computation.

I cannot tell whether this set is jointly consistent, and if it is not, which commitments are causing the trouble. Please audit the set: identify which pairs are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is, distinguish any apparent tensions that dissolve once the terms are made precise, say what the cheapest repair is for each genuine tension and what it costs, and tell me which single revision of one of these commitments would remove the most strain from the whole set.
