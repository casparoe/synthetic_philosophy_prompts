I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people—including people otherwise alert to the sunk cost fallacy—judge two situations that seem structurally alike, and I want to know whether anything actually justifies it.

Case one. A person buys a non-refundable theatre ticket; that evening she feels feverish and would stay home but for the thought that staying home would waste the ticket, so she goes. Most people are happy to call this a mistake: the money is gone either way, and going adds only a miserable evening.

Case two. A student has finished five years of a six-year professional qualification and has come, on good evidence, to think the profession will make her unhappy. She nonetheless completes the final year, saying she has come too far to stop. Many of the very people who would correct the theatregoer regard the student's decision as sensible, even admirable.

Both cases involve letting an irrecoverable past investment settle a present choice; the past is unreachable in exactly the same way in both. So the difference in our verdicts stands in need of justification. Is the relevant difference that the student's sunk time has altered what completing the qualification would now mean or yield for her, while the theatregoer's sunk money alters nothing? Is it something about commitment or identity that time can build and money cannot? Or is the asymmetry only a bias we indulge when the sunk cost flatters us as persistence rather than as waste?

Give me the strongest principle you can construct that delivers both verdicts, test it against at least one case in which past time, like the ticket's price, changes none of the person's future payoffs—a queue one could leave without cost after having already waited, or a book one has read two-thirds of without enjoyment or insight—and then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, extended, or abandoned. In doing so, take a position on the underlying question of whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational, or sometimes rational, and what makes the difference in each case.
