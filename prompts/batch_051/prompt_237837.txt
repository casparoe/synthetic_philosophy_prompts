I'm an AI governance researcher, not a lawyer, and I keep running into an argument that feel off but I can't quite diagnose. Whenever the question of giving advanced AI systems legal personality comes up—ability to own property, enter contracts, be sued, maybe even hold rights—someone says there's nothing new here, because 'corporations are people,' and we've been doing exactly this for centuries. I know the line mostly from a politician getting mocked for saying it, but it gets used as a serious argument in this crowd.

I'd like you to pull apart what 'corporations are people' could actually be claiming: a point about legal technique (the law needs some bundled entity to bear rights and duties), a point about constitutional rights, a point about moral personhood or group agency, a point about limited liability and asset partitioning, a point about the whole thing being a convenient legal fiction, or whatever other readings seem live. For each reading, I want to know whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or misleading. And the crucial part: which of those readings would actually support the inference that AI legal personhood is nothing new, and which are true—or at least plausible—but don't license that inference at all. I want a menu, not a single chosen reading, because I keep watching people talk past each other with this line and I'd like to be able to say exactly which reading someone is leaning on. Don't care about the campaign-history of the phrase.
