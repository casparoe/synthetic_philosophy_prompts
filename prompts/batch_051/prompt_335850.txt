I'm a retired seismologist who's gotten into philosophy over the last few years, and next month I'm giving a lunch seminar to our university's climate policy group. My thesis is that the pure rate of time preference in social discounting should be zero — temporal position shouldn't matter morally any more than spatial position — but we should still discount future consumption in money terms, for exactly two reasons: expected growth in consumption (diminishing marginal utility) and the chance there will be no one around to receive the benefit. My controvercial move is that this second factor isn't a constant add-on to the discount rate, as the standard Ramsey-style setup treats it, but a survival-weight on the benefit itself; because it compounds, you get an effective discount rate that declines over very long horizons and a near-term rate lower than what most integrated assessment models use.

The audience is mostly economists working on climate damages and cost-benefit analysis, a few philosophers (two straightforward utilitarians, one contractualist who suspects intergenerational ethics rests on a category mistake), and a handful of climate scientists. The economists think a positive pure time preference is just descriptively realistic; the philosophers think discounting wellbeing is either confused or monstrous.
