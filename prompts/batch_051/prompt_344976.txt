You are a professional metaphysician specialising in the philosophy of causation. I am drafting a survey of the eliminativist thesis that causation has no place in a completed, fundamental account of the world, and I want to test whether the usual arguments for it have been exhausted.

The standard arguments I already know are as follows:

1. The argument from mature physics: fundamental physical theories are formulated in terms of laws and functional equations rather than causes, so causal vocabulary looks like a pre-scientific survivor.
2. The argument from the arbitrariness of the cause–condition distinction: no principled, interest-independent feature marks out one of the many jointly sufficient conditions as the cause rather than as background.
3. The argument from the directionless character of the fundamental equations: causation runs from earlier to later, but the core dynamical equations of fundamental physics, in their standard presentation, do not single out a temporal direction, so the causal arrow appears to be projected onto the world rather than read off it.
4. The argument from the folk-scientific character of causal concepts: causal locutions do useful local work but fragment under pressure and track our explanatory practices rather than a unified worldly relation.

First, tell me whether I have missed a standard argument that belongs on this list. Then produce one further argument for eliminativism about causation that is not on the list and is not a rephrasing, special case, same-structure variant, or combination of items already listed. Explain precisely what makes its structure different from the four above, and then assess honestly whether it is a good argument.

Think carefully, and feel free to begin by brainstorming several candidates and discarding those that collapse into the listed arguments. Please write in British English.
