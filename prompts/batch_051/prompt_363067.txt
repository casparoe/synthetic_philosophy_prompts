Write a sonnet about Cox's theorem, the result that a coherent scheme for comparing the plausibility of propositions, built from a small number of modest conditions, is constrained to obey the ordinary rules of probability up to a continuous monotone rescaling of the scale. Use the octave to set out the desiderata as they appear before the theorem is in force: plausibilities are represented by real numbers in a way that tracks qualitative judgments of more and less plausible, and reasoning is required to be consistent, so that the same state of information combined in different orders or by different routes cannot yield different conclusions. Let the volta fall at the point where these apparently weak demands do their real work, closing off the alternatives and forcing the product rule and the sum rule, with uniqueness only up to the choice of scale. Use the closing couplet to state exactly what has and has not been established: the structure of coherent degrees of belief is fixed, but no particular prior assignment is dictated, and the form of the rules is preserved by any continuous strictly increasing regraduation. The poem should be precise enough that a probabilist who knows Cox's argument would recognize the technical turn without wincing, and clear enough that a reader who has never encountered the theorem still sees how thin a set of commitments can force an entire calculus.
