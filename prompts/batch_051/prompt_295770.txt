Ok so I'm in this course on interpretability and the philosophy of AI, and for my final paper I keep coming back to a version of the unfaithful chain-of-thought position. The way I'd put it: chain-of-thought text isn't a transcript of the model's actual causal reasoning; it's a plausible-sounding reconstruction the model generates while producing the answer, and even though the chain is causally involved (it changes what gets output) that doesn't make it a faithful report of how the model really got there. I'm not denying CoT has an effect on the answer—obviously it does—I'm saying effect and faithful self-report are two different things.

I want to stress-test this before I commit. What would count against it? Not the lazy objection "but CoT changes the answer," since I think the view can absorb that. I want the version of reality where the unfaithfulness claim is wrong: what evidence, experiments, or arguments should actually move me? Could be probing results, causal interventions on intermediate activations, fine-tuning studies, cases where a model catches and corrects its own reasoning mid-chain, or even a conceptual argument that the whole distinction between reporting and reconstructing is confused. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Also tell me which things someone already committed to my view would concede as genuine counterevidence, as opposed to the stuff they'd dismiss as compatible. And be honest about whether I've set the view up so that nothing could count against it—and if I have, whether that's a worse problem for this kind of empirical/interpretive claim than it would be for a normal philosophical position. I'm personally guessing the scariest evidence for me would be something like a model having access to its own intermediate computation seperately from the verbalization, with the CoT systematically constrained by that, but I'm not sure that's actually the right thing to be afraid of.
