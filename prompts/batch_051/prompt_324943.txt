I staff a legislative review unit in a federal state. Every few weeks we receive a proposal that would create, relocate, or restructure a public power, and our job is to recommend whether the power should sit with the central government, with the constituent states or provinces, with some combination, or with neither of those options because the proposal itself needs to be redesigned. A single analyst reviews each proposal at a desk in under two days; the analyst is a capable policy generalist with no formal training in federalism or political theory; and the final recommendation is delivered to a committee of elected legislators who must defend it in public. We need a reusable procedure for applying subsidiarity — the principle that public action should be carried out at the level closest to those affected unless a defensible reason places it higher. The procedure must not simply restate that slogan.

Please write that procedure as a sequence of questions to be asked in order. For each question, specify: (a) what counts as a sufficient answer and what kind of evidence would satisfy it; (b) the direction in which a given answer pushes the allocation; and (c) whether the procedure can stop at that step and, if so, with what recommendation. Where a question is irreducibly evaluative rather than empirical, mark it as a judgment call, say who should make it and on what basis, and do not disguise it as a factual test. The procedure should remain honest about which parts of the principle do not survive translation into a desk-level test.

In particular, make the procedure respect the following distinctions instead of allowing them to collapse:
- subsidiarity as a restraint on the higher level versus subsidiarity as an obligation of the higher level to enable or assist the lower level;
- spillovers that require coordination versus mere interconnection among units;
- heterogeneity of preferences and local information versus the value of a shared floor or a common standard;
- economies of scale and administrative capacity, including whether the higher level should act directly or only set a framework that lower levels adapt;
- uncertainty about the best rule as a reason for temporary local experimentation rather than uniform centralization;
- and the separate questions of who sets the standard, who administers it, who finances it, and who resolves disputes about it, rather than treating the whole policy as an indivisible block.

Then apply the procedure you have written to three items currently on our docket:
1. A central ministry proposes a detailed national minimum curriculum in history, civics, and language, arguing that divergent local textbooks produce unequal preparation for national university entrance examinations.
2. A coalition of states proposes a central authority to license and price groundwater extraction across a shared aquifer, after two successive inter-state agreements failed to halt the aquifer's decline.
3. A consumer-affairs agency proposes a single national standard for the collection and recycling of small household batteries, replacing three inconsistent state-level systems that have created confusion among retailers and waste operators.

For each item, show the path through the questions and the resulting recommendation. Make sure at least one case ends in a recommendation the committee's leadership — which tends to favor centralization — will not welcome, and at least one case reaches the end of the procedure without a determinate allocation, so that the residual decision is handed to elected legislators. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot decide — for example, the substantive merits of a policy, the proper amount of compensation for a unit that bears a disproportionate burden, and whether a preference expressed by a unit is legitimate.
