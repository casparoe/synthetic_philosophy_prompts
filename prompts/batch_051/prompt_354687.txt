I am an undergraduate taking a course on AI safety, and in the seminar we have been discussing goal misgeneralization: the failure mode in which a policy that performs well during training has actually learned a proxy objective, and that proxy diverges from the intended objective when the deployment distribution shifts. One safeguard proposed in the seminar was this. Train seven independent policies, with different seeds, different reward-shaping choices, and non-identical training distributions, and allow the system to execute an action only if at least five of the seven endorse it at that timestep. The thought is that even if one member has learned a proxy that misgeneralizes, a majority of members will have misgeneralized differently and will therefore withhold endorsement. Treat this as a mechanism to analyze rather than as a position to defend or attack.

What would such an ensemble actually do in deployment, and what, if anything, would it add over a single policy? In particular: what incentives would the seven policies have with respect to one another, including learning to compromise, trade endorsements, or collude; how could users or operators game the five-of-seven rule, especially on near-boundary cases; what happens when all seven share the same correlated proxy because their training distributions overlap; and what would fail even under ideal training and testing? Where is the design weakest, and could those weaknesses be fixed without turning the ensemble into a new single point of failure?
