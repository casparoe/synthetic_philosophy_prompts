Mathematicians and game theorists routinely prove that the first player in certain games cannot lose, or in some cases has a winning strategy, by means of a strategy-stealing argument. Consider tic-tac-toe. Since the game is symmetric between the two marks, one argues as follows. Suppose, for contradiction, that the second player has a winning strategy. The first player makes an arbitrary opening mark and thereafter plays as if they were the second player, following that winning strategy but placing their own marks; whenever the stolen strategy calls for a square that the first player's extra mark already occupies, they play any other empty square. An extra mark of one's own can never make a tic-tac-toe position worse for its owner, so the borrowed strategy is at least as good for the first player as it was for the second. Therefore, if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could win with it as well — which is impossible. So the second player has no winning strategy, and the first player cannot lose. The same style of argument yields stronger conclusions in Chomp and Hex, where it proves that the first player has a winning strategy.

I want to understand what this accepted proof technique presupposes. When a mathematician accepts a strategy-stealing argument as legitimate, what view of games, strategies, and mathematical proof are they quietly committing themselves to? Reconstruct the implicit commitments as precisely as you can, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. In particular, scrutinize two features of the argument. First, the assumption that an extra move or mark can never hurt its owner: is that a harmless technical condition on the game, or does it import a substantive picture of agency, symmetry, and advantage? Second, the way the argument treats a strategy as something that can be detached from one player and adopted by the other: what does that presuppose about what a strategy is? Is there any tension in the fact that the extra move is treated as both harmless and as the very thing that makes the theft possible? Notice also that the argument proves the existence of a non-losing or winning strategy without exhibiting it; does the practice presuppose that such a non-constructive existence claim is a perfectly good result? Finally, say what would have to change — in the proofs, in the conclusions, or in the underlying picture of games — if one of these commitments were denied: if the two players' positions were not symmetric in the relevant way, if an extra move could be a liability, or if strategies were understood as something a player constructs rather than as complete conditional plans that exist independently of any player.
