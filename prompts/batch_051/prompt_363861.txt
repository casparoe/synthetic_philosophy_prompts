I cover cognitive science and philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I have been reading Paul Churchland’s 1981 paper *Eliminative Materialism and the Propositional Attitudes*. The passage that keeps bothering me is this:

> We shall want to know how the brain represents the world, and how it computes, and how it produces behavior; and we shall want to know how it learns, and how it stores information. But we shall not, in that completed account, find any mention of beliefs, desires, fears, hopes, or any of the other states that figure in our common-sense psychology. They will have gone the way of phlogiston, caloric fluid, and the luminiferous aether.

Churchland wrote that when the dominant picture of minds was the rule-governed manipulation of sentences and symbols, and his target was the idea that folk psychology is a stagnant empirical theory that neuroscience might simply overturn. I now spend a lot of time talking to researchers who build large language models. They tend not to talk about stored propositions or explicitly represented plans; they talk about distributed vectors, attention patterns, gradients, and next-token prediction. Yet when they describe what a model “knows,” “wants,” or is “trying” to do in producing some output, they reach constantly for belief–desire vocabulary, often adding that it is just loose shorthand.

My question is not whether Churchland was right on the neuroscience of his day. Rather, I want to know what his eliminative argument would look like if run against this newer situation. On one hand, connectionist and vector-based systems might seem friendlier to eliminativism than classical symbol systems were: the internal states are even less like sentences, and the “rule” of processing is a statistical function, not a stored proposition. On the other hand, models trained on human text reproduce the patterns of folk-psychological ascription so fluently that researchers cannot seem to describe their behavior without reinstating the full idiom of beliefs, desires, and intentions. Does the argument survive the shift from symbolic AI to trained neural networks, or does it now cut the other way? Please answer as if for a sophisticated lay reader: locate the premise of Churchland’s argument that would bear the weight, then say whether the current situation weakens it, strengthens it, or changes what eliminativism would require.
