I have spent a few years working out an idea on my own about how pain and pleasure relate to one another, and I want an honest assessment of it. I have no academic background in philosophy, so I ask you to judge the idea itself and to repair it where it needs repair rather than dismissing it for being said clumsily.

The idea begins with a distinction I have come to trust. There are two kinds of badness in a life, and they do not sit on the same scale. The first kind is ordinary misery: a hangover, a tedious job, a broken arm, the grief of a friendship ending. These things can be traded against ordinary goods, and we accept such trades constantly, rightly so. The second kind I call breaking-pain: torture, the terror of being awake during surgery, psychotic fear, watching one's child die slowly, being trapped as a fire approaches. Breaking-pain does not merely hurt; it unmakes the self that would have to absorb and integrate the hurt. The difference is in kind, not just in degree.

From this I derived the principle I call the split ledger. Ordinary goods and ordinary bads may be weighed against one another, with real exchange rates between them. But breaking-pain sits on a second ledger, and that ledger has no exchange rate with the first. No amount of ordinary good can justify inflicting breaking-pain on someone, no matter how many people receive the ordinary good and no matter how large each share is. Nor can breaking-pain be made up for afterwards: a lifetime of pleasant afternoons does not partly pay off an hour of torture, not even slightly. The debt is not enormous; it is of another kind. I do not mean that extreme suffering merely counts for a very great deal of negative weight. I mean that no quantity of below-the-line good sits on the other side of the scale at all. And I mean the principle to be asymmetrical: I claim nothing comparable about any positive good above the line, such as transcendent joy outweighing any amount of ordinary harm. The only unpayable entry on the books is breaking-pain.

I can already see some of the trouble. The boundary between ordinary misery and breaking-pain must fall somewhere, and wherever it falls it will look arbitrary to someone walking through a graded series of cases from one end to the other. I also see that the principle, acted on, might demand enormous sacrifices from everyone in order to prevent a single person's breaking-pain, and I am not sure I can live with the demands. But I do not want to abandon the idea before it has been tested as strongly as possible.

Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. Is the split ledger coherent and defensible once stated carefully, or does it collapse when pushed, or can it be repaired by an amendment you would specify? Give me the three strongest objections you can raise and say which of them, if any, is fatal and which merely makes the view costly or strange. I also want to know what the idea gets right, if anything, because I am not asking for it to be merely defended or buried. I suspect I have reinvented something that already exists under another name, so tell me what the nearest existing idea is and whether mine is a version of it or something different. Write this as continuous prose, like a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
