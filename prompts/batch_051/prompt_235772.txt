So I run a philosophy club at a high school—14 kids, mostly 16-17, one hour—and this week I want to do something on why people are so reluctant to treat bare statistics as proof, even when the probabilities are high. Like the blue bus thing or the rodeo gatecrasher thing. I don't want to lecture about it up front, and I don't want them to hear phrases like "naked statistical evidence" or "proof paradox" until after the activity. Could you design a runnable session, maybe a jury game with candy or points at stake, where the students themselves run into the collision between the numbers and what feels like proof? I'm imagining three short cases: a civil case where a cyclist was run off the road by a bus that didn't stop, and the only identifying evidence is that the Blue Bus Company operates 80 percent of the buses on that route at that hour; a gatecrasher case where 499 out of 1000 people at a rodeo actually paid and one person is picked out basically at random and sued; and maybe one control case with direct eyewitness evidence of middling quality.
