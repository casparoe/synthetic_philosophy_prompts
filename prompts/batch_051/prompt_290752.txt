I'm taking a course called Mind and Machine and we've been covering Dennett's tower of generate-and-test, with Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. The way I understand it, each rung moves the trial-and-error further inside: Darwinian creatures only have natural selection testing whole phenotypes, Skinnerian creatures generate behaviors and let actual reinforcement prune them, Popperian creatures can run simulations internally so bad options die in the head rather than in the world, and Gregorian creatures inherit other people's pre-tested ideas through language and tools, so they don't have to do all the testing themselves.

I keep seeing the same four levels in ML and I'm not sure whether its a real structural parallel or just a loose analogy. Evolutionary strategies are basically Darwinian. A vanilla RL agent that only gets reward from actually acting in an environment is Skinnerian. Model-based RL and planning look Popperian because the agent tries out rollouts in a learned world-model instead of reality. And LLMs trained on a huge store of human-produced text and then prompted look Gregorian: the model is mostly importing a culture's already-thought thoughts rather than doing its own generated-and-test. My term paper idea is to claim this analogy is deep and use it to say something about the limits of each level, but I'm worried I'm just pattern-matching on words like "environment" and "selection."

Can you help me stress-test this? In particular: where does the mapping hold and where does it break? In robotics there's the sim-to-real gap, where a policy that works in an internal model fails when it touches the real world. Does the Popperian rung have the same built-in blind spot, and is that a fair way to think about things like rumination or rehearsal? And does the Gregorian rung make any sharp prediction about LLMs that we know is false? Mostly I want comments on whether the analogy is deep or superficial and which parts of the essay argument I'd have to hedge.
