I spent my career in a biology department — mostly ecology and animal behavior — and retired a few years ago, which is when I started reading philosophy and behavioral economics properly. The thing that keeps bothering me is that I seem to have two completely different reactions to the same phenomenon depending on whether the display is made by a bird or by my neighbor. Take the peacocks tail, or a gazelle that stots in front of a lion. The story I used to teach is that these displays are honest precisely because they are costly: an individual in poor condition cannot afford them, so the cost buys credibility. Nobody thinks the peacock is irrational or a show-off. Now take the human cases. A friend spends a years salary on a wedding so everyone will see she is serious and solvent. Another buys a watch that keeps worse time than his phone, where the entire point is that it was expensive and hard to obtain. A third goes through an initiation that hurts and leaves marks because completing it signals commitment to the group. My first reaction, and I suspect most peoples, is that these are wasteful status games — irrational, vain, maybe a bit contemptible. But by the handicap logic I taught for thirty years, the cost is exactly what makes the signal informative: expensive, hard to fake, and therefore credible. I can go from one sentence to the next thinking the peacock is a marvel and the wedding is a mistake.

I have tried to find a difference. Perhaps the animals cost is tied to its physical condition while the humans is just money. Perhaps animals do not choose their displays while humans do. Perhaps the peacock is an evolutionarily stable signal while the wedding is a runaway arms race. None of these has survived five minutes of scrutiny, since a wedding that truly strained the couple to afford would be differentially costly in just the animal sense, and the fact of choice cannot do the work by itself. So I want the strongest principle you can find that either generates both of my reactions or shows that at least one of them has to go. Be honest about how much weight the principle can really bear, and say whether you would keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or drop it. Please end with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase such as "asymmetry defensible" or "asymmetry should be dropped".
