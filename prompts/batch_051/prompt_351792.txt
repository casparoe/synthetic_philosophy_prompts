Something I've never seen anyone nail down: the GiveWell-ish charity evaluator world spends enormous energy debiasing its analysts and donors—scope insensitivity, the identifiable victim effect, optimism bias, you name it—but then treats 'moral weights' (how bad is a year of blindness compared to a year of chronic pain, how much do chickens count compared to people) as a separate kind of input, and treats disagreement about those weights as legitimate moral uncertainty rather than bias to be corrected. Meanwhile, if a donor says they just care more about the person in front of them than about a stranger, that gets classed as a bias to debias, not a legitimate preference. What theory of the preference/bias distinction does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as possible: does it presuppose that biases are deviations from some bias-free baseline of preferences, that impartiality or reflection is the dividing line, or something else? Say what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits with, where its parts pull in different directions, and what would have to change in how evaluators work if the line were drawn differently. Also tell me where your reconstruction is shakiest and what kind of argument or evidence would make you change your verdict.
