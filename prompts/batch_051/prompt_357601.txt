I work as a systems programmer, mostly on compilers, profiling tools, and garbage collectors, and I have no formal background in philosophy. Some time ago I read a popular-science piece about higher-order theories of consciousness, and the idea has been nagging at me ever since. As best I understood it, the view says that a mental state becomes conscious only when some further mental state is directed at it — representing it or thinking about it — so that a bare perception or desire can occur without being conscious until a higher-order state takes it as its object. I gather there are at least two variants, one in which the higher-order state is a kind of thought and one in which it is a kind of inner perception, and I want to know whether those two really come apart.

I would like you to restate this position, or these positions, in the vocabulary I actually think in: objects and pointers, processes inspecting the state of other processes, metadata and monitoring, introspection APIs, recursive or tiered system designs, and the difference between reading a value and changing what it is. Please make this a systematic translation rather than a loose analogy. Then give me a residue report: which claims translate cleanly into something like a higher-order state being a pointer to, or a monitor attached to, a lower-order state; which claims only translate with some distortion; and which claims have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — I suspect the felt character of experience may be one such leftover. I would also like to see how the stock worry about an infinite regress (does the higher-order state need yet another state to make it conscious?) looks from a systems standpoint, where an introspecting process does not need a second introspector. Finally, give me your honest judgment: does the residue show that my computational vocabulary is simply too impoverished for the phenomenon, or that some of the philosophical claims were less precise than they looked on the page?
