Write a dialogue between two decision theorists who are examining the choices of an agent who shows the classical Ellsberg pattern. The agent faces two urns, each containing one hundred balls. The first is known to hold fifty red and fifty black balls; the second contains an unknown mixture of red and black balls. She prefers a bet on red from the first urn to a bet on red from the second, prefers a bet on black from the first urn to a bet on black from the second, and would pay a small amount now to avoid having her payoff depend on a draw from the second urn.

One decision theorist defends these choices as rational: when the agent's information does not pick out a unique probability distribution, it is sensible for her to evaluate acts by their worst-case expected utility over all distributions compatible with what she knows, so her aversion to the unknown urn is caution about her own ignorance rather than ordinary risk aversion about outcomes. The other decision theorist maintains that the pattern is a symptom of incoherence: rational preferences over acts should settle a unique probability, and ambiguity aversion either violates the sure-thing principle or, under mild consistency requirements about plans, permits someone to exploit the agent through a sequence of trades she accepts one at a time.

Write the dialogue as a real exchange, not a pair of set speeches. Have each character respond to the other's strongest argument. The defender of ambiguity aversion should press the defender of sharp probabilities on what exactly is wrong with insuring against one's own uncertainty, and on whether the demand for a unique prior describes agents as they are or only as a normative idealization. The defender of sharp probabilities should press the defender of ambiguity aversion on how a worst-case rule can distinguish cautious reasoning from the application of arbitrary probability mass to uninformative events, and on whether the rule is consistent when the agent must commit to a plan before information arrives and then act on it. Let the dialogue arrive at, but not resolve, the point where each character must accept a consequence of their view that they find uncomfortable.
