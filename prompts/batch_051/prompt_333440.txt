I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I need a reusable procedure for revision. I am working on a novel whose protagonist is a woman recovering from a violent bereavement. The same decision arises dozens of times a week while I revise: when I render one of her strong emotions—fear, anger, grief, joy—should I treat the emotion as part of what makes her life meaningful at that moment, or as a distortion that obscures or corrupts her judgment and must be worked through? I know there is an old argument under this, roughly the Stoics on one side and the Romantics on the other, but I do not trust the simplified version, I have no philosophical training, and I cannot do research before a session.

Please write me a decision procedure I can run at my desk in a single evening, without quoting anyone and without a classicist or editor to consult. The stakes are the novel's emotional logic: if I get this wrong, the protagonist will read as either sentimental or cold. The procedure should tell me which questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which habitual writing moves each question is designed to catch, where the procedure stops with a clear verdict, where it stops because the question is really an aesthetic or ethical judgment only I can make, and where the Stoic-Romantic opposition itself is too crude and the procedure is likely to misfire. It should also say explicitly which conceptual distinctions it is keeping and which it is dropping for the sake of usability.

Then apply the procedure to three passages from my draft: one in which the protagonist's anger at a bureaucratic injustice leads her to write an unwise public letter; one in which her grief during a friend's inept comfort comes out as sharp, half-comic fury; and one in which a quiet moment of delight with her daughter is rendered as fragile and probably delusional given what she has lost. At least one application should return a verdict I will not like, and the procedure should flag the passages it cannot settle.
