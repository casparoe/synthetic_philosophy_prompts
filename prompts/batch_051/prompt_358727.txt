Last weekend my uncle Ben and my cousin Sofia, who's a sophomore in college, got into it at dinner over why some countries are rich and others aren't, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, so I want you to referee it point by point. They're not asking for a source list or anything, just say who's right about what.

Ben started by saying he'd just read a book about this and now thinks its basically all institutions. He pulled the Korea example: same people, same land, same culture before the split, but the South got rich while the North didn't, and pretty much the only thing that changed was the rules — property rights, markets, being able to start a business. He also brought up Nogales, where you can stand at the border fence and living standards jump in a couple blocks even though the geography and people are basically the same on both sides.

Sofia said he was cherry-picking. If you hold geography and culture fixed, of course institutions show up. But across the whole world, things like being tropical, landlocked, or having bad disease burdens predict poverty, and culture — like how much people trust strangers — seems to matter too. Her bigger point was reverse causation: rich countries can afford good courts and low corruption, so the fact that wealth and good institutions go together doesn't tell you which way the arrow points.

Ben said the colonial pattern kills the reverse-causation story. The parts of the world that were rich before Europeans arrived mostly ended up poor, and the parts that were poor back then, like the future US, Canada, and Australia, ended up rich. He said it's because colonizers built inclusive institutions in places where they settled and extractive ones in places where they just wanted to pull stuff out. If wealth caused good institutions, the precolonial-rich places should have stayed on top, but they didn't.

Sofia said that's still not a clean experiment, because colonizers didn't pick where to settle at random. They settled where they weren't going to die of malaria and where the climate fit the crops they wanted, so geography is doing the choosing and the institutions are downstream of it. She also said "institutions" is doing too much work — property rights, courts, corruption, democracy, state capacity are all different things, and people just pick whichever one fits whatever story they're telling. And China grew fast without what Ben would call inclusive institutions, so even the main pattern has exceptions.

Ben pushed back that the pattern of settlement is exactly what gives you the test: places that looked dangerous to settle got extractive institutions, places that looked safe got inclusive ones, and after you check for the direct effect of geography, it's the institutions that predict income today. He said Sofia's culture point is to vague to compete unless she can point to a specific culture channel that isn't just institutions in disguise.

Sofia's last move was that history just doesn't give you enough data to separate these three explanations. There are only around 200 countries and a million mutually compatible stories, so you can point to a Korea for institutions and a landlocked Switzerland against geography, and somebody always has a followup for the followup. She said the honest position might be that we can't know, or at least not with the confidence Ben wants.

They both turned to me to settle it and I just sat there. Can you go through the exchange and say who's right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing? Please do it in these sections, in this order, with rough lengths:

- Round-by-round breakdown (about 150 words): go through each claim in the order it came up and say whether it's strong, weak, or mixed.
- What Ben gets right (about 100 words).
- What Sofia gets right (about 100 words).
- What neither of them considered (about 120 words).
- Who you'd side with overall (about 80 words): say who's more right overall and why.
