I have no formal background in philosophy or psychiatry, but I have been thinking about the way psychiatric categories seem to act on the people they sort, and the case I keep coming back to is autism. Within a couple of decades it went from a narrow label—mostly applied to severely impaired children—to a broad "spectrum" diagnosis, and in the current edition of the main diagnostic manual several previously distinct labels such as Asperger's syndrome and PDD-NOS were folded into a single autism spectrum disorder. Running alongside this, a neurodiversity movement, organized and led to a striking degree by people who are themselves diagnosed autistic, has argued that autism is not a disorder but a natural form of neurological variation, and that the goal should be accommodation and acceptance rather than cure or normalization. These developments do not look independent to me. The wider the category grew, the more people could recognize themselves in it; the more people identified with it, the more they pushed back on what the label was taken to mean; and those changed meanings then seem to have fed back into the diagnostic category itself, into how clinicians talk to families, how schools distribute support, and how the broader public understands the condition.

I recently came across the idea of looping effects, usually attributed to Ian Hacking: the idea that classifying people can change the people classified, and that those people's responses can in turn change the classification. I want to use that idea rigorously on the autism case, and I do not want a vague story about how labels shape identity. I want a disciplined mapping: what exactly the classification is, who is being classified, which behaviors and self-understandings change because people know the classification applies to them, and through which channels those changes loop back into diagnostic criteria, clinical practice, institutions, and public culture. Then I want an honest account of where the looping-effects framework is silent or misleading—for example, where the expansion of the category is better explained by school funding rules, insurance requirements, diagnostic substitution, or shifts in research, rather than by classified people responding to their classification. Finally, what should this case do to the looping-effects idea itself: is autism a clean illustration of it, a case that stretches it past its limits, or something that forces a revision of how the loop is supposed to work? I do not need a history of the diagnostic changes or a summary of the neurodiversity debate; I want the analysis.
