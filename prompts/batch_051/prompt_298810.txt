I'm a retired geologist — spent most of my career mapping outcrops where the same rock face looks completely different depending on which side of the fault you're standing on — and lately I've gone down a rabbit hole reading Jain epistemology. The thing I keep chewing on is anekāntavāda and the sevenfold predication, the saptabhaṅgī: the idea that any claim about a thing is true only syāt, "in some respect," and that careful thought means tracking which respect. The seven modes are built from "it is" (asti), "it is not" (nāsti), and "it is inexpressible" (avaktavya), singly and in combination. I can see why the doctrine gets accused of being a license to contradict yourself, but I suspect that's too quick.

Could you write a dialogue between a Jain philosopher defending this doctrine and a logician trained in classical two-valued semantics who thinks it's either a trivial point about context-dependence dressed up as seven truth-values or outright incoherent? I'd like the logician to genuinely press the standard problems — doesn't a mode like syād asti ca nāsti ca violate non-contradiction? If every claim is true only in some respect, is the claim that every claim is true only in some respect also true only in some respect? Why exactly seven modes, and isn't calling something inexpressible itself a way of expressing something about it? — and I'd like the Jain philosopher to give substantive reponses, not just wave the blind men and the elephant around. Each should have to rethink something because of what the other says. In particular, I'd like to see whether they end up at a genuine disagreement about what a proposition is, seperate from terminological drift. Keep it tight: no speeches, no talking past each other, and don't let either side walk away the clean winner.
