I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me. I'm worldbuilding a city for my next novel and I'd rather it hold together than just have a vibe, so I want to pressure-test the premise. I keep the three modes seperate in my head, but I want you to reason from them, not just recite them at me.

The setup: in 1891, after a brutal civil war, a small Adriatic city-state—call it Revinica, a trading port about the size of Florence—writes a "Law on the Past" into its constitution. The law makes critical history the only permitted public relation to the past. Monumental history is banned: no statues or portraits of individual founders or heroes, no founding myths taught as fact, no school biographies of great men, no holidays that glorify a single person. Antiquarian history is banned too: no museums of everyday life, no heritage-preservation laws, no genealogical societies, no restoring old neighborhoods just because they're old. What is allowed is a permanent critical tribunal: each generation must audit inherited customs, laws, buildings, and institutions, expose what they owe to past wrongs, and formally condemn and discard whatever fails the audit. Archives still exist, but they're run like evidence lockers, not reliquaries.

My novel opens around 1975. I want you to act as a professional intellectual historian who knows Nietzsche on history—the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes—inside out. Predict, with your reasoning visible, how Revinica in 1975 differs from a comparable European city of our own 1975 in each of these: political legitimacy and ritual; family and local identity; what ambitious young people actually aspire to; art, architecture, and the feel of public space; religious life; and the city's capacity to reform itself when things go wrong.

Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the main mechanisms before you commit to any prediction. For each cluster, tell me which predictions you're most confident in, which are guesses, and what hidden assumption would have to fail for the prediction to flip. Don't just paraphrase Nietzsche's essay at me—use it as the spine, but give me an argued forecast. I care more about the chain of reasoning than the final yes/no.
