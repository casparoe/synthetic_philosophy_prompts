I'm putting together a short course on philosophy of religion for a church-basement reading group, and the last session is on petitionary prayer — specifically the puzzle that praying to a God who's omniscient and immutable can look either pointless (He already knows what you need) or impertinent (your asking can't move a will that doesn't change). Most of the group lands somewhere between "well, it's a mystery" and "we're told to pray anyway," and I'd like to end with a poem that doesn't flaten that. Could you write a villanelle in the voice of someone who has fully absorbed the philosophical objection and prays anyway? The two refrains should do the structural work: let one be the voice of the objection (something like "what could my asking change in You?" — your wording) and the other the voice of the practice (something like "still I ask" — again, your wording). They should keep returning and finally meet in the closing couplet, and I want that meeting to let a specialist see the standard resolution — that petition is the appointed means or condition through which God's fixed will gives, not a lever that moves Him — while also showing that the resolution doesn't quite dissolve the strangeness of the repeated asking. A lay reader should feel the repetition of prayer itself, not a theology lecture with line breaks. No citations, no technical terms, and exactly 19 lines with no line longer than ten syllables.
