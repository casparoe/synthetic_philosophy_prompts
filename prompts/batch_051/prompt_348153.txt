I direct a small private foundation's program in conceptual epistemology, and I am drafting the next three-year strategy for our portfolio on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. We can support four postdoctoral-level projects, and the trustees want a clear account of where the field's pressure points currently are rather than a survey of settled positions. My working assumption, picked up from several referees, is that the familiar oppositions — conciliationism versus steadfastness, and the bare question whether epistemic akrasia is ever rational — are now downstream of other questions, and I want the strategy built around that thought rather than restating it.

In recent conversations I have heard a number of candidate lines of inquiry, which I list only to make the task concrete; you should not treat the list as exhaustive. One is whether first-order and higher-order evidence are commensurable at all: can a single rational response aggregate them into one posterior, or do the two kinds of evidence bear on different levels of an agent's cognitive economy, with no well-defined combined output? Another is whether the genuinely unresolved problems are sequential. Most canonical cases involve higher-order evidence arriving only after a first-order credence has already been formed, and it remains unclear whether sequential rationality just is batch rationality updated over time or imposes requirements of its own. A third concerns deference: how should higher-order evidence about my current or future unreliability affect my deference to my own later reasoning, to an aggregate judgment, or to a rule I endorsed before I knew I might be compromised? A fourth holds that progress is blocked until we settle what epistemic akrasia actually consists in — a direct conflict between two occurred beliefs, an incoherence among dispositions, or a mismatch between one's credence and one's assessment of what the evidence supports.

For each question you take seriously, I want to know how upstream it is — which other debates depend on its answer and how much those debates would change if it were settled one way rather than another — as well as how tractable it is for philosophers working with current conceptual and formal tools, and what would count as recognizable progress within a three-year project. Please give me a defended ranking of the two to four questions you would prioritize for funding, state your criteria and the trade-offs you are making, and say which plausible-sounding questions you would deprioritize and why.
