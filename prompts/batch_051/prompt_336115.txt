Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether "embedded agency" names a real problem or just an elaborate restatement of the banal observation that agents are physical systems. His line was that once you concede that the agent is inside the world, all the interesting questions dissolve into ordinary engineering constraints such as bounded computation and noisy sensors. I think he is wrong, but I found that I could not state cleanly what a satisfactory theory of embedded agency would actually have to deliver. Before we pick the argument back up, I want the full specification.

You are a philosopher working at the intersection of decision theory, formal epistemology, and the theory of agency, and you know the embedded agency literature well. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming candidate requirements before you filter and organize them.

By embedded agency I mean the following. The agent is not a Cartesian controller standing outside a world that it models and acts on. Its reasoning is implemented in the world, takes time and other resources, and is itself among the world's processes; its model of the world is a part of the world and must in some way include the fact that it is doing the modeling; the actions it takes alter the very world that contains its future self; and the alternatives among which it chooses are not supplied by an external modeler but are themselves part of its situation. Write the desiderata that a theory of embedded agency must meet to count as adequate for this purpose: evaluating whether a proposed formalism — a decision theory, an account of self-modeling, a criterion for agential boundaries — is a genuine advance on the problem or only a relabeled Cartesian or unbounded picture.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; if the list is partly self-undermining, that is the part I most want to see. Then, in a brief closing section, take the two default pictures — the Cartesian split between agent and environment, and a subjective expected utility agent choosing among a fixed, externally specified set of actions — and show where they fail against your list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring candidate theories.

I already half-suspect a pair of tensions, which you should either confirm or dissolve rather than take as settled. First, the demand that the theory not presuppose a fixed boundary between agent and environment may sit badly with the demand that it still deliver determinate predictions about what some particular agent will do. Second, the demand that the agent represent itself without falling into an infinite regress may conflict with a realistic treatment of its own resource limits. I am also unsure whether the theory has to scale in both directions — applying to an agent's agent-like subparts and to larger systems that contain the agent — without changing its core commitments, or whether that is something it can legitimately set aside. I want the complete list, including the requirements I have not thought of.
