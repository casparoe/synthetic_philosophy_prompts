I sit on the academic priorities committee of a public university, and we must decide whether to recommend the closure of a small program whose two remaining scholars are preparing a critical edition of unpublished medieval Latin commentaries on Aristotle's natural philosophy. The administration does not dispute the quality of the work; it argues that the program enrolls almost no students, that its graduates rarely remain in academic employment, and that its publications are seldom cited, so the cost cannot be justified under the current budget. The scholars accept those figures but reply that if such programs disappear, a whole stratum of intellectual history will become effectively unreadable, and that a university that funds only work with a demonstrable near-term payoff has stopped being a university in any recognizable sense. Asked to state the concrete benefits the edition can be expected to produce, they refuse; they say the demand itself misconstrues what scholarship is for. I have no prior stake in the outcome, but I must submit a written recommendation to the provost. Should I recommend keeping the program? Is the scholars' refusal to give a payoff defensible, or is it a way of evading a legitimate question about scarce public resources? What considerations should actually decide whether a university should host fields whose value cannot be shown in advance?
