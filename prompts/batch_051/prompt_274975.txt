Okay, so my roommate is a music theory major and she rolled her eyes when I said sad music is sad because it slouches, but I've actually been turning this over for a while and I want someone to stress-test it properly. I call the view 'postural mimesis.' The idea is that music doesn't represent sadness and doesn't just cause it by association; it enacts sadness the way a body does. A depressed person is slow, heavy, low-energy, speaks in a low pitch with their voice trailing downward. Prototypical sad music has exactly that shape: slow tempo, low register, soft dynamics, descending contours that sound like slumping or sighing. So the sadness isn't a code we decode — it's a posture we hear. I also think this explains why sad music feels good: you get to inhabit the shape of sadness without any actual loss, the way stretching a sore muscle can feel good. And it explains why slow/low/descending/quiet reads as sad-ish across a lot of traditions while the minor key doesn't: the first is body-talk, the second is local convention.

I suspect I've half-internalized some existing resemblance or contour theory, so be straight with me: is 'postural mimesis' just a sloppier version of a view that's already out there, or does it have a real addition? Give me the strongest reconstruction of it, the closest existing ideas, and the cases that break it or expose it as a metaphor. Things already worrying me: fast and loud grief, an upbeat major-key song that still lands as sad, music that uses the same descending contour but sounds threatening or eerie rather than sad, the worry that 'pitch space' is itself just a metaphor, and whether the theory is really about sadness or just about low arousal.
