I cover science and ideas, and for a piece on how AI systems handle conflicting human values I want to test a familiar argument against a development in how those systems are actually built. The argument runs: a single system meant to serve a whole population must rank possible outputs or policies; people's values conflict; there is no neutral, non-dictatorial way to aggregate conflicting values into one ranking (a lesson often drawn from social choice results such as Arrow's theorem); so any single ranking will favor some people's conception of the good over others—an AI version of the liberal-neutrality challenge. The development is that the field has largely moved away from the one central system, toward personalized assistants each fine-tuned to one user's revealed preferences, with the only shared component being a base model that was itself shaped by population-wide human-feedback data. Does the original argument survive this shift? Could it have depended on the premise of one policy for everyone, so personalization simply dissolves the aggregation and neutrality worries; or do those worries reappear in the base model, in conflicts within one user's own values over time, and in the externalities that one user's personalized choices impose on others? Identify the premise the development attacks, say whether the argument can be restored and in what form, and give a verdict of intact, repaired, or dead. Please also say how confident you are in that verdict and single out the point you are least sure of.
