I recently served on two university committees that each had to redraw a definition in policy, and the difference in how the two tasks were handled has stayed with me. The first committee had to decide which relationships should count as dependent care for caregiver leave; everyone treated the question as a design problem, and we judged proposed definitions by their fairness, clarity, and administrative burden. The second committee had to decide whether the conduct code's definition of discrimination should be broadened to cover conduct that was clearly harmful but did not fit the existing wording. Here the atmosphere changed entirely. People who had been comfortable treating the first definition as something we were free to remake spoke of the second as something we were only free to discover. Supporters argued that the troubling conduct already was discrimination; opponents insisted that no policy revision could make it discrimination, whatever consequences followed.

The same asymmetry seems to recur whenever the concept being revised carries moral or political weight: we treat instrumental or administrative classifications as ours to improve, but we treat thick normative concepts as though they have an independent content that our decisions must track. Is there a principled justification for this asymmetry, or is it a flattering way of refusing to acknowledge that some of our most important categories were made rather than found? If there is a justification, state it precisely, test it against the two committee cases, and say where it gives out.
