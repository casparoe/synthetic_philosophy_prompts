Lately I keep running into a move made by one school of classical Indian philosophy, and I cannot tell whether it has been given a name of its own. The school holds that the Vedic texts have no author at all—not a human one and not a divine one. Instead of treating this as a reason for caution, the school treats it as the very ground of the texts' authority: because no one composed them, there is no fallible or interested mind behind them whose reliability would need to be checked, so their injunctions are to be accepted as valid in themselves unless a specific defect can be shown. This strikes me as the exact reverse of how I would normally treat an anonymous report: I usually trust a text less when I cannot identify its source. I would like to know whether this inversion—absence of an author functioning as a positive epistemic qualification of testimony rather than a gap in certification—is a recognized position with a standard name. If it is, what are the concepts it is usually contrasted with, and how does it differ from ordinary appeals to authority, from assumptions of reliability, and from claims of self-evidence? If it has no standard name, I would like a term for it and a definition with clear boundary cases. I am not looking for a bibliography; I care about exactly what the view is committed to and where its edges are.
