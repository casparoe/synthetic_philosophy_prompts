Like a lot of the younger people I know, I got into veganism partly through freeganism: eating food that was going to be wasted felt different from paying for it to be produced. A year ago I would have said eating any animal product is wrong, full stop. Now I co-run a small sanctuary, the rescue hens lay more eggs than they'll eat themselves, one of the sheep has to be sheared for her own health, and a pig died of old age this spring — and I've come around to the view that using those byproducts is fine, and that the freegan logic actually extends further than I used to think.

I'm giving a short talk at a regional conference of sanctuary operators and animal-rights people this fall, and I want to defend this thesis: a sanctuary's blanket "no animal products, ever" rule is the wrong way to express an abolitionist commitment, and a committed abolitionist vegan can use certain byproducts of rescued animals — eggs from resident hens, fiber from a sheep who had to be sheared, and in narrow cases the body of an animal who died naturally — without becoming any less abolitionist. The audience is mostly longtime sanctuary operators and animal-rights activists, heavily abolitionist (animals as not-property, anti-welfarist, very concerned with message discipline and with the movement sliding into "happy exploitation"), plus a handful of younger welfarists and one or two people running "rehomed animal" micro-dairies. My argument in the talk, roughly: the wrong of animal exploitation is in the relationship — breeding, owning, killing, turning a life into a production unit — not in the substance itself. An unfertilized egg isn't something a hen has an interest in; a sheep needs the wool off regardless; a pig who died of old age has no interests left. Using these things creates no demand, no new animals, no industry profit, so the usual abolitionist objections don't obviously reach the case — and the real cost is the message one: blurring the line makes it harder to tell the public that animals are not here for us.
