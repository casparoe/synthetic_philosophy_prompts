I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about how to price risky gambles, going by my gut on cases. Heres my verdicts.

The classic St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads; if the first heads is on flip n, you get 2^n dollars. I woudn't pay more than about $25 to play it once, even though I know the expected payoff is infinite.

A truncated version: same game, except if the first hundred flips are all tails you get nothing. I'd pay more for this one, maybe $40, even though it can only pay less than the classic. I know that sounds backwards, but the classic feels unbounded in a way I can't get my head around, and this one at least has a clean finite expected value — exactly $100.

A modest version where the payoff is n dollars rather than 2^n: the expected value is $2, and I'd pay about $1 to play. That feels right.

A version paying n^2 dollars: the expected value is $6, and I'd pay maybe $4.

A version I'd refuse at almost any price: if the first heads is on flip n, I pay 2^n dollars. No realistic fee would get me to take that on, even though it's just the mirror image of the game I'd happily buy for $25.

Is there a coherent principle about pricing risky gambles that captures these, or nearly all of them? If there is, state it precisely, with the case each clause is doing work for.
