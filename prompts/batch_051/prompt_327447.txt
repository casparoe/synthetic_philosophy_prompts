ok so i'm prepping for a reading group on forgiveness and i've been asked to put my actual views in one place so the group can poke at them before i present. act as a moral philosopher who works on the reactive attitudes and the ethics of forgiveness. think carefully, and start by brainstorming what each commitment actually commits me to before you compare them. i don't want a literature review, i want a consistency audit.

here's roughly where i've landed:

(1) forgiveness is never strictly obligatory. it's always elective in the sense that the victim has a right to withold it, even when the wrongdoer has done everything wrongdoers are supposed to do. at most it can be supererogatory or a matter of grace.

(2) but if someone wrongs you in a fairly minor way, and they sincerely apologize, make amends, and show they get what they did, then refusing to forgive them from then on is a genuine moral failing. you'd be holding their past against them in a way that fails to treat them as capable of change.

(3) forgiveness shouldn't be conditional on the wrongdoer's response. the victim's ability to lay down their resentment ought to be open to them regardless of whether the other person ever repents. being able to forgive even an unrepentant wrongdoer seems important for the victims own freedom, not a reward the wrongdoer earns.

(4) self-respect requires holding onto resentment toward someone who seriously wronged you unless and until they acknowledge it. forgiving a serious wrong without that acknowledgment is servile — it's letting someone treat you badly and then doing the work of releasing them from it.

(5) forgiving someone is fully compatible with still wanting them punished, or still wanting the truth to come out. forgiveness doesn't require letting the wrongdoer off the hook socially or legally.
