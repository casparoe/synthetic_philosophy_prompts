I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on Marx’s account of machinery and automation, and I keep returning to a passage from Karl Marx, *Capital*, Volume One, Chapter Fifteen (“Machinery and Modern Industry”), Section 1:

> John Stuart Mill says in his “Principles of Political Economy”: “It is questionable if all the mechanical inventions yet made have lightened the day’s toil of any human being.” That is, however, by no means the aim of the capitalistic application of machinery. Like every other increase in the productiveness of labour, machinery is intended to cheapen commodities, and, by shortening that portion of the working-day, in which the labourer works for himself, to lengthen the other portion that he gives, without an equivalent, to the capitalist. In short, it is a means for producing surplus-value.

In our reading group, this passage has produced a sharp split about what Marx is committed to in debates over the politics of technology. One side reads it as evidence that Marx is an early substantivist: capitalist machinery is not a neutral instrument that happens to be misused, because its design, deployment, and technical rationality are themselves shaped by the drive to produce surplus-value. The other side insists that Marx’s point is narrower: he is criticizing the *capitalistic application* of machinery, and the same machinery could, under different social relations, serve precisely the reduction of toil that Mill and others hoped for.

Please act as an expert on Marx’s philosophy of technology and on the instrumentalist/substantivist debate. Before writing, think through the strongest formulation of each interpretation, paying close attention to how each side would handle the phrases “capitalistic application” and “means for producing surplus-value.” Then write a dialogue between two equally competent researchers defending these two positions. Let the first speaker open by using the passage as evidence for the substantivist reading; the second should reply by distinguishing machinery as such from its capitalist employment. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other’s actual claims, making at least two substantive rebuttals per side, and conceding real tensions in their own view where appropriate. Do not resolve the disagreement artificially.
