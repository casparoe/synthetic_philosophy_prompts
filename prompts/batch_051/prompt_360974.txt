For a graduate seminar I teach on formal theories of collective decision-making, I have been trying to separate the questions in judgment aggregation that are merely elegant from the ones that would actually reorganize how we think about democratic institutions. The field's point of departure is familiar: each member of a group may hold perfectly consistent judgments on a set of logically connected propositions, and yet majority voting over those propositions can yield an inconsistent collective judgment — the discursive dilemma. The subsequent literature has produced impossibility results, escape routes such as premise-based and conclusion-based voting, distance-based aggregation procedures, and rich connections to social choice theory and formal epistemology. But I do not find the field finished, and I am unsure which gap most deserves the scarce years of a trained researcher's time.

Suppose I have three years of funding for one technically strong postdoc to work on a self-contained project, where the aim is work that is both formally serious and potentially informative for the design of courts, legislatures, or expert committees. Give me exactly five open problems, research questions, or lines of work in or closely adjacent to judgment aggregation, ordered from most to least worth pursuing. For each item, state the problem clearly, defend why it deserves its place by saying what other questions hang on it or what institutional practice it could change, and note the consideration that would most plausibly make you demote or promote it. I want a defended agenda, not a survey of the field and not a reading list; assume the basic framework and tools.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
