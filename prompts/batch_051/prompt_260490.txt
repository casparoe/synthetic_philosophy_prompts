I've just come back from a long argument with a mate about Max Black's 1952 paper The Identity of Indiscernibles. He asked me to imagine, as Black does, a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres—same size, same shape, same colour, same temperature, same composition, nothing else around them—and then argued that this gives us two distinct things with all the same properties. I think there's a way out, but my friend kept saying I was cheating. I want to know whether my argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails.

Here is the argument I defended:

1. If two things have all the same properties, then they are the same thing (the identity of indiscernibles).
2. In Black's two-sphere universe, the spheres are two distinct things.
3. The spheres are exactly alike in their intrinsic properties: both are iron, same size, same shape, same colour, same temperature, and so on.
4. But one sphere has the property of being at position P1, and the other has the property of being at position P2, and P1 is not P2.
5. So the two spheres do not have all the same properties.
6. Therefore Black's universe is not a counterexample to the identity of indiscernibles.

My friend said the problem is step 4. He said being at P1 and being at P2 are not genuine qualitative properties; they are just labels for the fact that there are two distinct spheres, so using them to show the spheres are discernible is circular. I replied that if positions count as properties at all, then the principle is not violated, and if they don't count as properties then the nontrivial version of the principle starts to look impossible to state. But I couldn't make that stick. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the right distinction between a qualitative property and a merely numerical or identity-involving property in this context?
