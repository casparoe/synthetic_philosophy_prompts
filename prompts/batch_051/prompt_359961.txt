I'm a novelist, not an academic, and the book I'm writing right now has a character who keeps a private ledger of other people's pains and pleasures, trading them off against each other. Writing her has made me notice a weird asymmetry in my own moral bookkeeping, and I want someone who actually does ethics to tell me whether it can be defended.

Here it is. Below a certain level of badness I convert pain into pleasure without blinking, and I suspect you do too. A splitting headache is something I'd happily trade for a genuinely excellent dinner after, maybe two. If a stranger on a train has a breifly lousy afternoon — a jammed finger or a bad fright — but this lets a hundred other people laugh really hard, I'd probably call that a fine trade, or at least not obviously monstrous. Down in this register, pain and pleasure seem to sit on a single scale, and we move tokens around on it.

But once the pain gets severe enough — not exotic torture-chamber stuff, just hours of unrelenting, white-hot pain — I stop doing the arithmetic entirely. Offer me as many people as I like getting small to medium pleasures — ice cream, good jokes, excellent novels — to balance one person's few hours of genuinely severe pain, and I won't just say the quantities aren't enough. I'll say the comparison itself is a category mistake, like asking how many pleasant afternoons make up for an amputation without anesthetic. No number does it. The suffering suddenly gets treated as though it lives on a different scale, one that pleasure can't even reach.

So the asymmetry is this: for minor pains we treat pain and pleasure as commensurable — one currency, its exchange rate negotiable. For major pains we treat pain as lexically prior: no pile of mild pleasures is allowed into the comparison at all. And notice that this isn't just weighting pain very heavily. A heavy weight can always be outvoted by enough opposing weight. We're doing something structurally different at the sharp end.

I suspect a good utilitarian could tell me my intuition is just innumeracy or a failure of nerve: if a headache is worth two dinners, then ten thousand headaches are worth twenty thousand dinners, and at some point severe pain has to be buyable too, however distasteful it feels. But I keep wanting there to be a real difference between the two registers, not just a difference in how queasy the arithmetic makes me.

So please act as a professional philosopher here, and think carefully rather than reaching for the first plausible-sounding principle. Start by brainstorming the best candidate justifications for this asymmetry — something like the lexical threshold some negative utilitarians reach for, something about the unity of a person over time, something about what kinds of goods can compensate what kinds of harms. Then pick the strongest candidate, stress-test it against awkward nearby cases (where exactly is the line, and why there? is it absolute or does it shift? what about severe pain that is freely consented to, or severe pain that is itself desired — masochism makes a good test case), and give me an honest verdict: should we keep this asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up and go back to doing the arithmetic as if pain and pleasure were one currency all the way up?
