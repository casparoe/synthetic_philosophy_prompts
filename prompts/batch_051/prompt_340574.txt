I work as a software engineer on a trust and safety team at a large platform, and I recently read through some of Pascal's Pensées for the first time — mostly the passages where he discusses justice, force, custom, and why people obey authority. I don't have any formal background in philosophy, so I'd welcome correction if I've misread him, as part of the answer.

Here is what I took from those passages, in my own words. Pascal seems to think that justice without force is ineffective, and that force without justice is tyranny. He also seems suspicious that the particular rules a society treats as just are grounded in some independent moral standard; he keeps returning to the idea that custom, opinion, and imagination are what actually hold authority up and make people obey. But he doesn't look like an anarchist to me — as I read him, he thinks established law has to be obeyed because the alternative is civil war, and his real concern is how force and justice get joined rather than opposed.

I want to apply this framework to a problem I deal with at work. A handful of private companies now write and enforce the rules under which a very large share of public speech happens. They can remove accounts, throttle distribution, or change their rules overnight, and users have little of the due process we attach to a legal system. At the same time, some speech is genuinely harmful, and without enforcement the rules would be empty.

So here is my question: if Pascal had been brought up to speed on modern content moderation — how the systems work, how the rules are made, how enforcement is automated and applied unevenly — what would his verdict be? Please give me the most faithful reconstruction you can of his likely position, reasoning from his commitments rather than just restating the passages. In particular: where would he see legitimate force joined to something like justice, and where would he diagnose force wearing the costume of justice? Would he treat a platform deplatforming a user as closer to a magistrate enforcing law or to a faction exercising bare power? And where does his framework genuinely underdetermine the answer, because two defensible readings of Pascal pull in opposite directions? I'm not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the reasoning.
