You are a philosopher of mathematics who works on the continuum problem and on the justification of new axioms for set theory. Think through this slowly, and before you answer, steelman the position I describe rather than brushing it aside, because I suspect the standard reply is too quick.

I'm auditing a graduate seminar on set theory, and we have just been through the proof that the continuum hypothesis is independent of ZFC: neither the claim that there is a set of reals whose size lies strictly between the natural numbers and the continuum, nor its negation, can be proved or refuted from the standard axioms. The thought I can't shake is this: in most other areas, when a sentence is independent of your axioms, you conclude that the axioms do not settle the matter, and you either refine the question or concede that the original question was underdetermined by your starting point. Why shouldn't I say exactly that about the continuum hypothesis? If our best theory of sets leaves the size of the continuum undecided, then the natural conclusion seems to be that the continuum problem has been dissolved rather than left open. The activity that remains — searching for new axioms that might decide it, and arguing over whether large cardinal axioms or forcing axioms are 'good' axioms — then starts to look like trying to determine by philosophical argument alone which of several alternative geometries the universe 'really' has after the parallel postulate was shown to be independent.

I suspect I am sliding between several distinct claims, and I would like you to separate them with more care than the usual sources do: (a) independence as a theorem about what follows from ZFC, (b) indeterminacy as the claim that there is no fact of the matter in the intended universe of sets, and (c) the further claim that the intended universe of sets is itself a myth, so that talk of 'the' set-theoretic universe should be abandoned. Where exactly is the slide, and which picture actually requires which step? If you think the multiverse picture is right and there really is no determinate answer to the continuum question, then I want to see why that conclusion requires substantially more argument than the bare independence result, rather than being a direct consequence of it.
