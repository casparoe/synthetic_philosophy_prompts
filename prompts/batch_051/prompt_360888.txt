I've been trying to get a clear statement of a view I've run across called tranquilism. As I understand it, the view is that the only thing that matters, morally or prudentially, is the absence of craving and suffering. Positive states like pleasure, satisfaction, achievement, even meaning, are at best neutral—nice when they happen, but they add no value; what is bad is simply being in a state of craving or suffering. I keep wondering whether the view is even coherent, because everything depends on what gets counted as a craving. I'll grant you a rough handle on "suffering" for now; it's "craving" I can't pin down.

So here's what I want: a working definition of "craving" in the ethically relevant sense—one that lets tranquilism say what it wants to say without collapsing into triviality or into its opposite. Test your definition against the following cases, and tell me which verdicts it forces on you and which ones you'd revise the definition to avoid.

The cases: hunger after skipping breakfast; the pain of a broken leg; an itch you can't scratch; a mild preference for tea over coffee; wanting to know how a novel ends; boredom during a dull lecture, with the pull to check your phone; grief for someone who has died; a painful nicotine withdrawal; dread of a root canal scheduled for tomorrow; and an intense ambition to get a job you don't need but really want.

For calibration: I'd call the hunger, the itch, and the nicotine withdrawal clear cases of craving; the tea preference and wanting to know how the novel ends clear non-cases; and I have no settled view about the boredom, grief, dread, and ambition—maybe you'll tell me they're all one thing, or maybe some fall outside craving into "suffering" or into ordinary preference.

I suspect "craving" is doing at least two jobs—something like an insistent, aversive felt desire versus the bare fact of having an unfulfilled preference. If you agree, say so and give me the definition that does the better job; either way, run it throught the whole list, and flag any place where the most natural definition gives a verdict you wouldn't actually defend. If the upshot is that tranquilism can't be stated as a stable view, say that plainly.
