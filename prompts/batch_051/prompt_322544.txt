I've been chewing on a passage from Frederick Douglass's 1860 speech "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" He says:

"the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States."

That line has started to look like a general claim about written constitutions: their moral and legal character is fixed by the public text, not by what the framers secretly intended, what ratifiers expected, or what officials later did under them. But I realize I keep looking for confirmation when I should be asking what would count against it. Suppose I treat Douglass's claim as a live thesis. What would have to be true for it to be false? Which possible facts, cases, or arguments would be decisive, and which merely awkward? What would a defender of the text-only view have to concede as a real cost, and which objections would they reasonably reject as missing the point? I want an honest map of the thesis's exposure, not just someone else's list of objections.
