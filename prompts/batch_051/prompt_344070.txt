My working thesis is that the question "how old are nations?" actually bundles at least three questions that the usual debate runs together: (a) continuity, whether some present-day nation is continuous in membership, territory, or name with an earlier collectivity; (b) salience, how much national membership shaped ordinary life and politics relative to religion, locality, or dynasty at a given time; and (c) packaging, how old the traditions, symbols, and stories are through which a nation presents itself. My rough argument is that the strongest parts of the Hobsbawm-and-Anderson line concern (b) and the packaging side of (c), not (a). The invented-tradition evidence shows that many supposedly ancient practices were standardized, formalized, and given false antiquity during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and Anderson's print-capitalism story explains how vernacular print and mass literacy could produce a new kind of horizontal solidarity among strangers. But neither move settles continuity: a practice can be repackaged in 1880 and still be a genuine descendant of an older practice, and a community can acquire a new self-understanding without becoming a different community. So I want to defend the claim that the nation-form is modern even when its ingredients are older, much as a building can be new though its bricks are old.
