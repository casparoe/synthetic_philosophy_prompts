I'm trying to work out what I actually think about intervening in wild animal suffering, going by my gut on cases, and I'm not sure my verdicts add up. I'd clean and release an oiled seabird without hesitation. I'd support painlessly euthanizing a deer with a broken leg lying beside a trail. I'd approve vaccinating a wild chimpanzee population against a lethal outbreak. For a predator-free deer population that overpopulates and starves each winter, I'd accept contraception, and I'd reluctantly accept culling the healthy surplus if the alternative is slow starvation. But I would not intervene in a lion killing a zebra, and I don't think the fact that predation is natural does the real work there. And I'd refuse a plan to use a gene drive to eliminate a parasitic wasp species whose larvae eat caterpillars alive, even though it would prevent far more suffering than most of the things I just approved. My loose guess is something like: intervene against disease, starvation, and injury, but don't eliminate sentient species or suppress predator-prey relations. But that doesn't explain why I'd kill one injured sentient deer while refusing to eliminate one sentient parasitic wasp, and it struggles with the vaccination/gene-drive asymmetry, since both are large-scale technical interventions against a biological cause of suffering. Is there a principle that gets these verdicts, or close? What does it say about cases I keep returning to: a lion-proof fence around a reserve that would stop most predation there; a generation-long plan to replace wild carnivores with painlessly sterilized ones so predation disappears without killing anyone; and a proposal to eliminate a parasitic worm from a lake? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and tell me which of my verdicts I should drop if I want the cleaner version.
