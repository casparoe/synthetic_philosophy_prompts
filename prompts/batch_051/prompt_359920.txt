I am a philosophy professor preparing a fifty-minute seminar for first-year undergraduates in a general philosophy programme, on the topic of physicalism. The students will have read a short introductory piece that characterises physicalism as the thesis that everything is physical, and I have found in previous years that they are far too willing to accept that slogan without noticing that the word 'physical' is doing a great deal of unexamined work. I want to devote the session to Hempel's dilemma: the difficulty that if 'physical' means whatever current physics says there is, then physicalism is hostage to the history of physics and is probably false, since past physics has been repeatedly overturned; whereas if it means whatever an ideal, completed future physics would say there is, then the thesis seems to lose its empirical content, becoming either trivially true or impossible to state with enough precision to assess. My aim is not to refute or defend physicalism, but to teach the dilemma itself as a transferable piece of conceptual machinery.

I would like a teaching plan rather than a worked-up explanation. In particular, I want the sequence of components and what depends on what; the single running example or case that should carry the session, and what it must and must not have if it is not to mislead; the misconceptions first-years predictably bring or form, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a check for understanding that would show the dilemma has actually landed; and what I should openly leave out at this level, and how to signal that to the students.
