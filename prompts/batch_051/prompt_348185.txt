I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I want a reusable procedure for deciding how much weight to give an institutional metric before we run a story on it. The problem recurs weekly: a hospital publishes a median emergency-room wait time, a school district touts teacher ratings built from test-score growth, an AI lab reports a benchmark — and I know that a number people are rewarded for hitting can stop meaning what it seems to mean. I've heard the idea called Goodhart's law and specification gaming, but I need it turned from a slogan into a test a working reporter can actually run. The procedure should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer, where the process should stop and say "don't treat this number as evidence" or "report only with heavy caveats," and where it should hand the decision to a person because the checklist can't settle it. My colleagues aren't trained in measurement or economics and we are usually on deadline, so it has to work in about fifteen minutes, without formal models and without data we'd need a records request to obtain.

Ground it in what is actually known about Goodhart's law and specification gaming, but in operational terms rather than theory, and make it distinguish between a metric that is being gamed and one that is merely an imperfect proxy, since those are different cautions for our readers. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a hospital that begins publicly reporting median emergency-department wait times after tying part of administrators' compensation to that number; (2) a school district that rates teachers partly by year-over-year student test-score growth; and (3) our own publication, which is considering rewarding writers by the number of engaged minutes readers spend on their stories. For each, say roughly what a reporter should conclude about whether the metric can be taken at face value. Do not let the third case off the hook: if the procedure says our own engagement metric is suspect under the same test, say so plainly. Finally, mark the steps that are genuinely judgment calls rather than mechanical checks — I suspect deciding what an institution's real objective is, and how much gaming actually matters, are the hard ones — and say what the procedure cannot settle, including how a reporter should proceed when the incentives behind a number are not observable.
