Alright, here's a thought I've been chewing on. In Hume's essay Of Money he has this bit about what happens when new money enters a country. As I remember it, his claim is that the increase doesn't raise prices right away; the new coins pass through lots of hands first, and during that stretch people feel busier, merchants take on more work, and industry quickens. Only later do prices catch up, and when they do the extra money is just... more money, no real gain. The good stuff all happens in the lag before prices adjust.

So here's the counterfactual. Suppose a central bank—say the Bank of England—discovers in 2026 that it can create new base money in a way that is genuinely unobservable from the outside: no published balance sheet entries, no detectable change in reserve quantities banks see, no leak into market interest rates because it's all routed through a conduit that looks like ordinary foreign safe-asset inflows. It distributes the new money by buying goods and services from the real economy, so the money enters exactly the way Hume's inflow enters, through spending. The only difference is that no one can tell the money supply has grown, and this invisibility can be maintained indefinitely.

Predict, with reasoning, what happens to real wages, output, asset prices, the distribution of wealth, and the pound over the next twenty years. Is the country actually wealthier than before, or does the usual long-run neutrality still reassert itself somehow? Which of your predictions are you most confident of, and which depend on assumptions about how people form expectations (e.g., do they ever notice their own nominal incomes rising and start spending accordingly)?

Keep it under 800 words. I care way more about the mechanism chain than about the final yes/no.
