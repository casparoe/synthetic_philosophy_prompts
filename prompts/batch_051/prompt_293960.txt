I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been thinking about something I keep seeing at work. I maintain the automated claims-screening rules at a midsize insurance company. The rule set has accumulated over more than twenty years from many sources: employees who have since left, experimental runs that were never documented, and patches added after individual fraud losses. The current rule set seems, to me, to have a coherent risk orientation of its own: it treats certain combinations of timing, region, and policy type as suspicious even though no living employee can say how that combination became salient, and no single person ever chose the whole orientation. Different colleagues explain the same behavior in mutually incompatible ways, and others say there is nothing really there, that we are just telling a story after the fact to make the system feel manageable.

I have started to wonder whether the stronger statement could be literally true: that a non-person process, such as a rule system, a market price, an evolved trait, or a committee's written decision, can have a reason that no person has ever formed, entertained, or endorsed. I want to know whether that claim is the kind of thing evidence could ever bear on, or whether it is only a metaphor. If it can be tested, I would like the strongest concrete test you can design. Specify what you would compare, what you would vary or hold fixed, and what observations would count in favor of the claim and against it. Then be precise about what a positive result would and would not establish, and about which part of the claim no test can reach.
