You teach applied epistemology and the psychology of expertise. A student describes the following policy for forming and revising beliefs. Before engaging any live dispute about an empirical or policy question, they run a fixed drill: first reconstruct the strongest version of the opposing view until the other side agrees with the reconstruction; next list the three most damaging objections to their own view; then write down what evidence would change their mind and assign credences before and after the exchange. They drill this sequence daily on low-stakes practice disputes—chess openings, sports trades, historical counterfactuals—the way a martial artist repeats katas, on the theory that the moves will then fire under pressure in real arguments. They ask you: is this policy genuinely training rationality, or is it producing a fluent performance of open-mindedness that mostly inflates confidence? Think carefully about transfer: whether competence in a canned sequence survives contact with hostile interlocutors, time pressure, and identity-threatening topics, and how the student could tell from the inside whether the drill is improving them or just feeling virtuous. Begin by brainstorming the strongest case for and against the habit as a training regimen, then give a verdict and describe the better-trained version of this policy.
