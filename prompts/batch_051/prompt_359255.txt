I want to start from two reversals of the usual order of explanation. The first is Darwin's: the appearance of design, of organisms exquisitely fitted to their circumstances, can be produced by a mindless process of variation and selection, with no designer who understands the result. The second is Turing's: thought might be produced by a mechanism whose parts do not themselves think or understand anything, and one can in principle build such a mechanism without first having a complete theory of what thought is. I will take both reversals as fixed points rather than as positions to be defended. Given them, give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of understanding must satisfy: a theory of what it is for a system—human, animal, or machine—to understand what it is doing. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it; I want the criteria to carry the argument, not just the labels. Among the distinctions a theory should not collapse: genuine understanding versus merely competent performance, original understanding versus merely borrowed or derived understanding, and doing something correctly versus grasping why it is correct. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really only preferred by one camp—substrate-neutrality, representationalism, and introspectability are the ones I have in mind, but add any others you think are parochial. Then say whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable; I suspect a theory must make comprehension both something more than competent performance and something that can itself emerge from comprehension-free processes, and those two demands may pull against each other. End with a brief scorecard for three stock positions—behaviorism, the intentional stance, and a representational or computational view—against the list, enough to show which requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Keep the emphasis on the desiderata themselves.
