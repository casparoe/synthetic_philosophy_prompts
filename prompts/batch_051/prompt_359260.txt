I have been reading LessWrong and the adjacent consequentialist blog ecosystem long enough to hold the standard view that expected value maximization is more or less forced on us as a decision theory, while simultaneously being unable to shake the feeling that the fanatical cases are doing real damage to that view. I want your actual credence, not a survey.

Here is the proposition I want a number for: expected value maximization, applied in the ordinary way to real-world practical decision-making, is fatally compromised by fanaticism. That is, the correct theoretical response to the standard fanatical cases — Pascal's mugging, the St. Petersburg gamble, and astronomically valuable far-future interventions with tiny success probabilities — is to abandon or substantially revise expected value maximization, rather than to accept the fanatical verdicts as bullet-biting conclusions we should live with.

Decompose the credence. I see at least four sub-questions:

1. How likely is it that the fanatical verdicts genuinely follow from expected value maximization, with the tiny probabilities and enormous payoffs taken literally rather than dismissed as ill-defined or decision-irrelevant?
2. Conditional on their following, how likely is it that those verdicts are actually unacceptable — real marks against the framework — rather than merely intuitively shocking conclusions we should learn to accept?
3. How likely is it that some principled alternative — bounded utility, risk-weighted expected utility, discounting of very small probabilities, constraints from moral uncertainty, or something in that family — blocks the fanatical verdicts without introducing distortions at least as bad?
4. How likely is it that such an alternative, if it exists, is a view we would endorse on reflection and not merely a patch adopted to avoid embarrassment?

Give me a number for each with a line or two of justification, state how you combine them into an overall credence, and give the overall number. Then tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to and what evidence — formal, empirical, or conceptual — would move it most. My own credence is around 0.35 that fanaticism fatally compromises expected value maximization; a friend whose judgment I respect is at 0.7. Tell me where the disagreement between a 0.35 and a 0.7 is most likely actually located. If you think my decomposition misses a term, add it and weight it.
