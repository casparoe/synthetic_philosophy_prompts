I've been going down a writing-systems rabbit hole recently because my cousin and I are trying to put together a practical orthography for a language our grandparents speak, and it turns out you can't even decide whether to use one letter or two for a sound untill you settle what a script is supposed to encode in the first place. I listened to a podcast about the invention of the alphabet, and the host gave a confident little summary that went something like this: "Every writing system has to pick a level of language to represent. Logographies like Chinese are basically ideographic: a character stands for an idea or a word, not really for a sound, which is why a Cantonese speaker and a Mandarin speaker can read the same newspaper even though they can't understand each other's speech. Syllabaries write whole syllables. Alphabets go one level finer and write phonemes — the smallest physical units of sound that distinguish one word from another. So an alphabet is, in principle, a phonemic transcription: it records the sounds of the language, which is the great advantage alphabets have over earlier scripts. A properly alphabetic system would have one symbol per phoneme and one phoneme per symbol, each with a stable pronunciation. English is sort of a mess because its spelling preserves morphemes and older pronunciations, so it's not really living up to that ideal. But the core of the alphabet idea is still a one-to-one mapping between letters and sounds."

Parts of that felt fishy to me, because I've picked up somehwere that Chinese characters actually have a lot of phonetic components and aren't just idea-pictures, and a friend who studied linguistics once told me English spelling is morphophonemic — which makes it sound like a deliberate trade-off rather than a failed alphabet. But I might just be repeating half-remembered Reddit threads. Is the host's summary fair? I'd like an accuracy audit — which parts are right, which are normal simplifications for a general audience, and which are actually wrong — and I want to know whether the host's bottom line (alphabets are phonemic transcriptions, and English is a defective one) survives once the details are straightened out. And could you write in American English?
