I am a practicing software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or law. My working model of ownership and abandonment is built from the systems I deal with daily: a software artifact has a name, a version, a license, a maintainer, a repository, and a position in a dependency graph. When a maintainer disappears, I call the artifact unmaintained, and I decide what to do with it on the basis of its license, provenance, forkability, and the maintenance burden of adopting it. I would like to test how far that vocabulary can be pushed to make sense of the long-running discussion of orphan works and abandoned software. I am not looking for a reading list or case citations; I need the conceptual map.

Please do two things.

First, restate the main positions in the debate over orphan works — copyrighted material such as books, films, and photographs whose rightsholder cannot be identified or located — and, where they diverge, the debate over abandoned software (sometimes called abandonware). Translate the positions rather than merely analogize: the view that a work remains fully protected even when the owner cannot be found; the view that a good-faith use after a diligent search should be excused; and the view that a sufficiently long disappearance amounts to a kind of abandonment. Where possible, express each in terms of default-deny versus default-allow dependency policy, license-as-policy, provenance and title search, fork rights, upstream disappearance, bus factor, deprecation, and escrow.

Second, give me the residue report. For each position, identify what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to fit, and what has no expression in the software vocabulary at all. Pay particular attention to the gap between unmaintained and orphaned: an artifact can be unmaintained while its owner is fully reachable, whereas legal orphanhood is about the inability to locate the rightsholder regardless of the owner's intent or neglect. Conclude with a judgment on the residue: which failures of translation show that my software vocabulary is missing something real about ownership of creative and cultural works, and which failures show that the legal-philosophical debate itself is less precise than it appears. Keep the failures visible rather than papering them over.
