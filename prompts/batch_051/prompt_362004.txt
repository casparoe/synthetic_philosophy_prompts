I have been trying to get clear about what it takes for a self—an "I"—to persist across time. The picture I was taught goes something like this: a self persists only if the same conscious subject continues, with an unbroken stream of experience, memories and intentions carried forward by that stream, and the whole thing realized in one living brain. I have sharpened that picture into four criteria: (1) continuity of a single first-person point of view; (2) psychological connectedness over time, through memory, intention, and disposition; (3) realization in a sufficiently fine-grained physical system, in practice a living brain; and (4) a determinate boundary between one self and another.

Now consider a case, inspired by a view I associate with Douglas Hofstadter but to be judged against the four criteria on their own terms. A woman is married for forty years and works closely with her husband throughout. She absorbs his habits of mind to an unusual degree. After he dies, she finds she can call up, with high fidelity, how he would have reacted to new situations: she anticipates judgments of his that surprise her, conducts internal disagreements with him that alter her view, and sometimes produces remarks in his voice that she feels she would not have produced on her own. Friends come to rely on her for his likely opinion, and they report that the simulacrum is disturbingly accurate even in domains they never heard him discuss with her. Yet nothing in her feels like a second stream of consciousness. The episodes occur only when she invokes him, they are experienced from her own point of view, she can usually separate her own thought from his, and the pattern is, she suspects, implemented entirely in her brain and nowhere else.

Does anything in that case amount to the husband's self persisting, even partially, under the four criteria? Work through the criteria one at a time, and say plainly where a criterion yields a definite answer and where it merely underdetermines the verdict. Then say what the case shows: whether the criteria should be revised to admit degrees of a self persisting across another brain, or whether they should be kept and the apparent survival dismissed as a vivid simulation with no "I" behind it. Either way, I want a precise account of whether, and in what sense, an "I" can be distributed across brains.
