A few years ago a relative of mine began having vivid visual hallucinations while otherwise thinking clearly, and it started me wondering whether the experience of seeing something that is actually there and the experience of seeing something that is not are the same kind of inner event, just with different causes, or two different kinds of events altogether. I have read around the subject as an interested outsider, and the position I have ended up with is disjunctivism: a genuine perception and a matching hallucination are not one common mental state plus a difference in what produced it, but different kinds of states, with nothing essential in common except that the person cannot tell them apart from the inside. I do not mean the trivial point that one has a real object and the other does not; I mean the stronger claim that there is no single underlying state present in both cases. Part of what pushed me here is that the argument from illusion, which is often used to establish that hallucination and perception must be the same kind of state because they can be indistinguishable, strikes me as moving too quickly from sameness of appearance to sameness of underlying state.

I would like you to play it straight with me and map out what would count against disjunctivism. What would have to be true — in neuroscience, in the character of perceptual experience, or in the logic of the view itself — for this position to be false or in need of serious revision? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed disjunctivist would accept as counting against the view rather than dismiss as question-begging or beside the point. I would personally be most moved by a demonstration that the very same underlying process is doing the perceptual work whether the object is present or absent, since that seems to cut against the heart of the view; but I do not know how decisive that would actually be. I also want an honest assessment of whether disjunctivism is insulated from evidence: if a subject cannot tell a veridical perception from a hallucination from the inside, and the main difference the view allows is the external object itself, does the view risk anything at all? And if it is insulated, is that a serious problem for a view of this kind, or is that simply what a view about the nature of perceptual experience should look like?

Finally, please keep the epistemic status of things clear: separate what is settled among people who work on perception from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus. I am less interested in who holds which label or in a historical survey than in the structure of the view's exposure — what it stakes and what could make it lose.
