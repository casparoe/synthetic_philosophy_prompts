I am a graduate tutor preparing a two-hour seminar for final-year philosophy undergraduates who have met Bayes' theorem and conditionalisation but not confirmation theory itself. The subject is the problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory. I am not after an exposition of the problem; I am after a plan for teaching it.

I intend to build the seminar around the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion: a known, quantitatively precise anomaly well before General Relativity explained it, and one that seemed to tell strongly in General Relativity's favour once it did. If you judge that case unsuitable as the running example, say so and say what should replace it; otherwise, say what the case must supply and where it might mislead. I want the choice justified against the common alternatives rather than simply asserted.

The arc should make its dependencies explicit. I would like the students to leave with a distinction between three things the phrase 'old evidence' runs together: the evidence's being temporally prior to the theory, its already sitting in the background corpus with probability one, and its having been used in the theory's construction. The problem, strictly stated, arises only from the second. Tell me how to order the material so the students are forced into this distinction rather than handed it, and justify each step by what it makes possible later.

Give me a misconception map for the resistances I should expect: the student who thinks old evidence obviously cannot confirm a new theory, the student who thinks the whole difficulty is an artefact of assigning probability one to a fallible measurement, and any others you anticipate. For each, say where in the sequence it is likely to surface and how it should be handled at that point rather than deferred.

The seminar should end with a check for understanding that discriminates between having grasped the puzzle and having grasped the structure of its solutions; I do not want a recall question. Something in the neighbourhood of this: describe a case in which chronological newness and Bayesian newness come apart, and say which one is doing the confirmatory work. Improve on that if you can.

Finally, state what should be openly set aside at this level. I take it the formal representation results around logical learning are off the table; tell me what else should be left out, or explicitly flagged as beyond the seminar, and why each omission serves the session.
