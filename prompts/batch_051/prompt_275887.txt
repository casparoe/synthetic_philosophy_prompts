For the past few years I have been reading moral philosophy as a retirement project; my working life was spent in experimental physiology, and I am aware that my instincts run toward causal descriptions of things. I have written down the commitments I now hold about the moral distinction between doing harm and allowing harm — killing and letting die, in the usual shorthand — and I would like a careful audit of whether they hang together. I am not asking for a restatement of the literature, and I do not need corrections sourced to particular authors; I want an internal audit of the set itself.

(1) There is a genuine moral distinction between doing harm and merely allowing harm. Other things equal, the prohibition against doing harm is stricter than the prohibition against allowing the same harm: doing harm requires a substantially stronger justification.

(2) The distinction is to be drawn by examining the causal structure of a situation, not by whether the agent's body moves and not by what the agent intends. An agent does harm when her conduct initiates or actively sustains the harmful process. An agent allows harm when she does not interfere with a harmful process already in train that she did not initiate.

(3) From (2) it follows that switching off a ventilator for a patient who cannot recover, and whose ventilation is merely delaying death, is not in the relevant sense killing. Switching off the machine does not initiate the underlying disease process; it removes a support that was holding that process in check. I therefore classify withdrawing life support as allowing the patient to die, and I regard it as morally on a par with never having started the ventilator in the first place.

(4) At the same time, I hold that a nurse who deliberately omits a scheduled and clearly beneficial dose of antibiotics, so that the patient's infection proves fatal, has killed the patient rather than merely allowed the patient to die. This is so even though the nurse's body did not move and even though the infection is an independent biological process, because the nurse has undertaken the patient's care.

(5) I accept the standard verdict about the two cousins and the bath: the cousin who holds the other's head under the water does something worse than the cousin who watches him slip and drown, but both are gravely wrong. The distinction marks a difference in seriousness, not in whether the conduct is wrong at all.

(6) When I try to construct pairs of cases that differ only in whether the harm is done or allowed, with everything else held fixed, I find that the doing/allowing difference by itself does not shift my moral verdict, neither the judgment of wrongness nor the degree of it. I do not take this to show that the distinction is unreal. My working view is that the distinction matters only against a background of other factors: motive, relationship, prior undertaking, and the like.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which of them pull against one another, ordering any tensions from most to least severe. For each, say whether it is a genuine tension or only an apparent one, what would have to give to resolve it and at what cost, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent.
