Some friends of mine share a lake house for a week each summer, six of us, and we split groceries and chores. Our friend Dana always opts out of the food pool and the chore wheel, saying she's trying to keep costs down, but then all week she eats the group dinners, drinks the beer, and uses the kayaks. People mostly let it go, but after this year's trip Marcus started a whole argument in the group chat, with Eli pushing back. I'll try to reproduce it from memory.

Marcus: Dana's a free rider, and it's wrong even if it's small. She makes an exception of herself: everyone else cooks and cleans and pays, and she takes the benefit of all that without doing her part. The problem is the unfairness. If everyone reasoned like her there'd be no house, no dinners, nothing. She's relying on the rest of us to be cooperative in exactly the way she's refusing to be, which is a kind of exploitation.

Eli: That's too fast. 'What if everyone did it' doesnt get you anywhere when the actual situation is that everyone won't. What actually follows from Dana's choices? Nothing bad. The house is rented either way, the food is bought either way, and the kayaks sit there either way. Her marginal effect is basically zero, so you can't say she's making anyone worse off. Calling it exploitation is overblown.

Marcus: You keep translating 'wrong' as 'worse off.' Wronging someone and harming them aren't the same thing. Dana acts on a rule she could never accept as a general practice — guests can opt out of costs but still take the benefits — while depending on the rest of us not acting that way. It's a stance toward us, not an outcome. And by your marginal-effect logic, sneaking into a movie or skipping your turn in a rotation would be fine as long as no particular showing or turn was ruined.

Eli: The difference is Dana isn't sneaking. Everybody has known for years that she does this, and we still invite her back. A parasite takes without the host's acceptance, but here the host keeps issuing the invitation. Maybe she's contributing in a different currency — she's the one who makes the playlists, smooths over arguments, keeps the week fun. If we value her presence enough to keep inviting her, then it isn't taking without contributing; the ledger just has more columns than groceries and chores.

Marcus: The ledger point is the best thing you've said, but it doesn't fit the facts. She didn't offer her company as her contribution; she explicitly opted out of shared costs. And 'we keep inviting her' just shows we're conflict-averse, not that we accept the arrangement. Forbearance isn't consent. A pleasant free rider is still a free rider; she's free riding on our willingness not to make a scene.

Eli: If the group's willingness not to make a scene is doing the work, then that's on the group. Nobody has to invite her. And once you do, year after year, telling yourself 'she'll chip in this time,' you've accepted the deal in practice. It's like when a friend says 'don't worry about gas money' — you can't call them a mooch later for not paying. The invitation changes what she owes.

They went around like this for ages and I honestly couldn't settle it. Can you go through the exchange and say who's right about which specific point, where each of them overstates or misses something, and who's more right overall about Dana? Also, please write the answer in American English.
