I have a lawyer's fluency with a family of distinctions in Anglo-American legal theory: retributive punishment as against compensation and restitution; obligations one person may discharge on another's behalf, such as paying a debt, as against liabilities that attach only to the offender, such as serving a criminal sentence; suretyship and vicarious civil liability; the discretionary power to forgive, waive, or commute; and expressivist or moral-educative justifications of punishment. I know the Christian theology of the atonement only as an educated outsider. Restate the dispute among the penal substitution, satisfaction, and moral-influence theories of the atonement in that legal vocabulary, as precisely as you can, and keep the translation honest: do not let legal assumptions do the theological work. Then give the residue report: which elements of each theory translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion, and which have no counterpart in the legal framework at all. I suspect that penal substitution is simply vicarious criminal punishment, which Anglo-American law generally refuses to allow, and that moral-influence theory is the only one of the three that maps onto respectable legal ideas. Tell me whether that suspicion is right or missing something, where the legal vocabulary is imposing foreign assumptions on the theology, and whether each failure of translation is a defect of the legal framework or a sign that the theological claim was never as clear as it is usually taken to be.
