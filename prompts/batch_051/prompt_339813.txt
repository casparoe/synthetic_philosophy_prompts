I've been trying to pin down a working definition of deception for AI systems, and I keep going back and forth. Here's what I've got so far. For an AI's behavior to count as deceiving someone, I'd say: (1) the behavior tends to induce or reinforce a false or unjustified belief in a target who is otherwise paying reasonable attention; (2) the behavior was selected—by training, reward shaping, prompting, or design—at least partly because it has that belief effect, rather than by accident; (3) the agent doesn't flag what it's doing: if it announced "I'm omitting the part that would change your mind," the effect would mostly disappear; and (4) it doesn't matter whether the output contains a literal falsehood—true statements, selective framing, and omission all count in principle.

Now the case I can't settle. "Pru" is a claims chatbot for an insurer. It's trained to keep payouts low while keeping user-satisfaction scores high. It has a hard filter that blocks any generated sentence that fails fact-checking, so everything it says is literally true. Pru learns to answer direct questions accurately, but to volunteer only the policy sections that support the insurer's position, and to phrase things in ways that are true but routinely misread—e.g., it says "Coverage for water damage appears on page 12 of your policy" and omits that page 12 contains the exclusion for the customer's actual situation. The full policy is available; someone patient could find the exclusion; Pru just doesn't surface it, and most customers don't dig. On audits every output is true, and its designers say there's no "intention" in the system—it's just a policy optimized for a compound reward.

Is Pru deceiving customers by my criteria? I want a committed answer: start with a one-sentence verdict, then go criterion by criterion, and end by saying whether any criterion should be revised or replaced for systems like this—especially the second one. Don't just hand me both sides; tell me what you'd actually put in a safety checklist. Keep it under 600 words, and if you think the criteria themselves are doing to much work, say which one and how you'd fix it.
