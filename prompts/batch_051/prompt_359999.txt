I am a novelist, and I am writing to you as one would to a professional moral philosopher, because a problem I am living has become a problem I am writing. I grew up in a place where queues are firm, tipping table service at a fixed rate is close to an obligation because servers are paid as though you will tip, and crossing against the light is a small breach of a shared rule. I have been living for the past year in a city where pedestrians cross whenever the road allows, where lines at cafés and bus stops are loose and disorderly, and where leaving a tip is, I am told, mildly insulting, as though one were offering charity to someone who receives a proper wage. A friend insists that a guest must follow local ways, and that to bring one's own queue, tipping, and crosswalk habits into a place is a form of moral arrogance. I feel there is a difference among the three cases: failing to tip at home harms a person whose income was set on the assumption of tips, whereas tipping here harms no one's income and gives small offence; joining a queue out of order at home seems to wrong the people I pass; crossing against the light on an empty street seems to wrong no one at all. But I am no longer sure these feelings track anything real, and the question has now moved into the novel I am writing, whose central character is a traveler trying to be decent in a city whose small rules she does not share.

I want more than cultural commentary. I want you, as a moral philosopher, to help me separate what is genuinely owed to other people from what is only local habit or manners, and to tell me which of the three behaviors—tipping, queueing, jaywalking—are moral obligations, which are matters of local custom, and whether a visitor is morally required to take up the local ones. Before you commit, spend some time brainstorming the candidate principles and distinctions that might do the sorting, and pressure them with cases of your own, including ones where a local practice seems unjust, exploitative, or pointlessly restrictive. Then give your considered verdict case by case, address my friend's claim directly, and say which of my intuitions you would override and which you would vindicate. Do not retreat to both-sides ambivalence: I need a definite position, and I need you to be honest about the cases your position handles least comfortably.
