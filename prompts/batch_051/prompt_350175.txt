Before I read any more proposals about how a computationally bounded reasoner should assign probabilities to mathematical statements it has not yet proved or disproved, I want a specification of what a theory of logical uncertainty must deliver. Write the desiderata for such a theory. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say which requirements are genuine constraints on any adequate account and which are merely parochial demands of one research program. The list should address at least the following pressure points: how the assignment respects implications the reasoner has actually derived; how credences move when a proof or disproof is found; why the reasoner should not be forced to be certain of every consequence of the axioms it accepts; how logical uncertainty differs from ordinary empirical uncertainty; and what it means for the assignment to be computable, or approximately computable, by a bounded agent. Include one or two concrete pressure cases, such as credences about the digits of an uncomputed constant, about an open problem like the Riemann hypothesis, or about the likely payoff of the reasoner's own proof search. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify which requirement does the most work to exclude candidate theories. If it helps, briefly score a logically omniscient Bayesian, a proof-search heuristic, and a logical inductor against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
