I have come to accept the view, associated with Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, that no non-trivial condition is luminous: being in a condition does not in general put one in a position to know that one is in it, and in particular knowledge does not iterate in the way the KK principle requires (Kp does not entail KKp). I would like a disciplined account of what would count against this position. Which premises or background assumptions of the anti-luminosity argument could in principle fail — the sorites structure, the margin-for-error principle, the link to safety or reliability, the way the relevant condition is individuated — and what would a world, or a body of formal or experimental results, have to look like for each to be a live route of resistance rather than a merely logical possibility? Which intuitions and cases, for example about self-knowledge, introspection, deliberation under uncertainty, assertion, or planning, would defenders of anti-luminosity themselves concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as misdescribed? What does the view predict about introspective access that a luminosity-friendly rival does not? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated by qualifications, and whether that insulation is itself a problem for a view of this kind.
