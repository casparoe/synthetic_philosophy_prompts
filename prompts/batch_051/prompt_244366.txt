In my American constitutional thought seminar we read Frederick Douglass’s speech “The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?” Two people in my breakout room got stuck on this bit:

“In all matters where laws are taught to be made the means of oppression, cruelty, and wickedness, I am for strict construction. I will concede nothing. It must be shown that it is so nominated in the bond. The pound of flesh, but not one drop of blood.”

Marcus said Douglass is doing a fairly lawyerly thing here: the Constitution never actually says “slave” or “slavery,” so if you’re going to read it as a slaveholding instrument, you have to show that the slaveholder’s power is explicitly granted. His line was that Douglass is making an interpretive burden-shifting argument, not just a wishful moral one. Priya pushed back and said that’s way too clean. For her, Douglass is being selectively textualist: he wants strict construction when it helps abolition, but then he reads the due process clause and “persons” broadly and morally. She also said the absence of the word “slave” doesn’t settle anything, since the fugitive slave clause and the three-fifths clause function as pro-slavery compromises without using the word. Marcus replied that those clauses still don’t amount to a guarantee of a right to property in man, and that Douglass’s “pound of flesh” principle applies exactly to a claimed right to treat a person as property: the grant must be shown with irresistible clarity. Priya countered that “irresistible clarity” is doing all the work, and that the rule only helps once you’ve already decided which background purposes count as legitimate.

Neither would budge, and I honestly couldn’t tell which parts each of them were getting right. Can you adjudicate the actual points they made, point by point? I want to know who is right about the role of the missing words “slave” and “slavery,” whether Douglass’s strict-construction principle is being applied consistently in that passage, and what a fair reconstruction of Douglass’s argument really needs to say about legal construction versus background moral aims.
