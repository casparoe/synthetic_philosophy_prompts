I am a retired biologist who has, rather late in life, begun reading the philosophical literature on mathematics in science, and I recently encountered a book written for scientifically literate nonspecialists that presents the indispensability argument together with some recent work on mathematical explanation in nature. I would like to know how far to trust its summary, which runs, in my paraphrase, as follows.

The Quine–Putnam indispensability argument begins from the fact that our best confirmed scientific theories quantify over numbers, functions, and other mathematical objects, and that their laws cannot be stated without them. Because these theories are confirmed by observation as integrated wholes, the mathematical components are confirmed along with the physical ones. By parity of reasoning, we therefore have the same kind of empirical warrant for the existence of numbers that we have for electrons and genes. In recent work, the book continues, philosophers have strengthened this by showing that mathematics is not merely indispensable but genuinely explanatory. Periodical cicadas emerge on 13- and 17-year cycles; these numbers are prime, and that primeness explains why cicada broods avoid synchronizing with predators and parasites whose shorter life cycles are composite. Replace the number-theoretic description with a purely physical one and the explanation collapses. Similarly, the fact that regular hexagonal tiling is the most efficient partition of the plane for enclosing a given area with the least perimeter is what explains the hexagonal structure of the honeycomb, and it does so without needing to attribute geometrical knowledge to the bees. The book concludes that genuinely explanatory science is impossible without mathematical facts, and that accepting the science therefore commits one to accepting the existence of those facts.

Something about this summary strikes me as too fast, though I cannot fully articulate where the slide occurs. I am inclined to accept that mathematical language is doing more work in biology than mere bookkeeping, and I would like the realist conclusion to come out true; but the move from the theory being confirmed as a whole to numbers being confirmed just like genes, and the move from the hexagon theorem figuring in an explanation of honeycomb structure to the hexagon theorem explaining why bees build hexagons, feel like exactly the places where an honest summary must be most careful. Is this a fair account of the indispensability argument and of the explanatory-indispensability literature built on cases like these? Please separate what is accurate from what is a common simplification, identify any overstatements or conflations a defender of these arguments would disown, and say which of the book's conclusions survive after the corrections. In particular, I want to know precisely what the cicada and honeycomb cases establish, if anything, about the reality of mathematical objects, and what a thoughtful nominalist would concede and deny about each step.
