In my Philosophy of Play seminar, a student pressed me on something I couldn't answer cleanly, and its been nagging at me since. Here's the asymmetry. An adult who spends her evenings learning violin—not for auditions, not to teach, not even to perform, just because the piece has its hooks in her—tends to get called cultured, serious, maybe even engaged in the kind of leisure Pieper describes as an end in itself. An adult who spends the same hours in an equally absorbing, rule-governed, deliberatly pointless activity—speedrunning an old game, running a chess club, painting miniatures for a tabletop campaign—tends to get called childish, escapist, or a time-waster. Both are voluntary and autotelic; both have the temporary order and separateness from ordinary life that Huizinga treats as a mark of genuine play; both are done for their own sake rather than for money or health. Yet the evaluative language forks hard, and the fork seems to track something like fine art versus games rather than anything internal to the activity.

I want to know whether some principle can generate both judgments, or whether the asymmetry is just inherited status distinction we should stop taking seriously. Give me exactly five items, numbered, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion: the five strongest candidate justifications for this asymmetry, each with your verdict on whether it survives or collapses.
