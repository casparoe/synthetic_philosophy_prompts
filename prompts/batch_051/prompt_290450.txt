I've been going back and forth on this bank-type case and I want a full worked-out verdict on it. Friday afternoon Maya checks her account before buying a $100 textbook, sees the balance is healthy, and tells a friend she knows she can afford it; not much rides on it, and her evidence is just a recent ATM reciept and a quick look at the banking app. Saturday evening an unexpected $2000 medical bill lands, and her rent and several other autopay charges are about to hit the same account. Now, staring at the exact same balances and receipts, she refuses to say she knows she can afford the textbook — in fact she says she doesn't know. Contextualists and pragmatic encroachers (subject-sensitive invariantists included) can both explain why Saturday feels different, but I care about exactly what kind of difference it is.

Here's the essay I'd like. Work through what contextualism, subject-sensitive invariantism/pragmatic encroachment, and plain classical invariantism each say about: (a) whether Maya's Friday claim "I know I can afford it" was true, (b) whether she actually knew on Friday, full stop, and (c) whether her knowledge gets destroyed when the stakes rise, or whether it's only the conversational standards for using the word that move. Then consider the bounce-back case: the bill gets canceled on Monday and the stakes fall again, with her evidence unchanged the whole time — does the knowledge come back, and does your preferred view handle this better than the alternatives? Weigh the main arguments and defend a definite conclusion about whether knowledge is interest-relative, whether the word "knows" is context-sensitive, or whether the bank cases just track how much risk of error is worth taking when you assert.
