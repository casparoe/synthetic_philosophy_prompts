I run people operations at a 200-person company and have spent years reading rationalist blogs, so I know the wrong kind of reasons problem in its familiar forms: a demon who will punish you unless you admire him gives you a reason to admire him, but not a consideration that makes him admirable; a demon who will hurt you unless you desire a saucer of mud gives you a reason to want the mud, but not a consideration that makes the mud desirable. My team ships several culture interventions a quarter — awards, bonuses, mandatory trainings, promotion criteria — that are supposed to get people to actually hold attitudes like valuing safety, honesty, and customer outcomes, not merely to display them. I need a reusable audit procedure for these interventions, to be run before rollout and again a quarter later, that distinguishes the cases where our incentives leave the attitude being held for reasons that bear on its object from the cases where we have built a small demon-and-mud situation into the org chart.

Write this as a decision procedure a non-philosopher can run in half a day, whose output I can defend to a CEO with no patience for abstraction. It should specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a satisfactory answer to each; where the procedure returns red, yellow, or green; where it must stop and hand off to human judgment because the philosophical distinction is genuinely unsettled; and what the procedure cannot detect. Then apply it to three of our programs: (a) a weekly cash award for employees who report near-misses, framed as honoring people who value safety; (b) a mandatory annual training module plus a signed statement that employees understand and personally endorse the company values, with completion tied to promotion eligibility; (c) a compensation formula that ties part of customer-support pay to a customer-satisfaction score, alongside internal messaging that this is meant to make staff care about customers. Make sure at least one application returns a verdict my team will not like.
