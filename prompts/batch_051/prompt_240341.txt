I'm a PhD student in theology with a side of paleontology (long story), and there's a verse I keep coming back to that I can't get straight.

Genesis 1:31, NRSVUE: "God saw everything that he had made, and indeed, it was very good. And there was evening and there was morning, the sixth day."

The thing the authors of Genesis were obviously not picturing when they wrote that: the fossil record shows a world for hundreds of millions of years before any human beings existed, and before any sin that the Fall story is meant to explain, in which animals are killing and eating each other, dying of parasites and infection, starving, and going extinct. There's tooth marks on bones and tumors in dinosaur fossils, all of it far earlier than anything like a moral agent. The theology I was raised in says death and suffering enter creation through human sin. But the suffering creatures predate humans by eons, so how does "very good" apply to them, if it does?

I'm less interested in whether the verse "contradicts" evolution than in the interpretive problem. What kind of judgement is "very good" here — is it aesthetic or functional ("it's fit, it works"), moral ("nothing bad was happening"), or something else the authors could have meant? If the authors of Genesis 1 had been shown a Cretaceous scene of a tyrannosaur opening up a hadrosaur and asked whether that scene was "very good," what would they have said, and how much should that matter? Does the verse apply to the non-human creation at all, or is it really about the order God made for human life? If we read it as a claim about the whole created order, is there a coherent reading on which it can be true at the same time as what paleontology shows, or does it just lose its force? I'd like you to run it through the different approaches — the plain words, the purpose the verse serves in Genesis 1, what the authors would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader today should make of it — and tell me which approach should carry the most weight for this kind of case and why.
