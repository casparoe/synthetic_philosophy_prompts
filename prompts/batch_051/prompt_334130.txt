When we are not actually making a decision, we have no trouble assigning probabilities to our own choices. Before I start thinking seriously about whether to move to another city, I might say, "I'll probably stay, maybe 70%." Looking back afterward, I might say, "Given how I was then, there was maybe a 40% chance I would go." And I can assign probabilities to other people's choices all the time: "I think my brother will take the job, maybe 65%." But when I am in the middle of deliberating — genuinely trying to decide whether to go — the request "Assign a probability to the proposition that you will go" seems to misfire. If I give a high number, it feels as though I have already decided; if I give a middling number, I feel as though I am treating my own future action as an external event rather than as something I am choosing. The very act of deliberating seems to crowd out the act of predicting.

Decision theorists have noticed that these two stances are in tension. Some claim that a person who is truly deliberating cannot coherently assign probabilities to their own possible choices, because assigning a probability would amount to taking the predictive stance toward oneself, and the predictive stance conflicts with the practical stance of choosing. Others think the conflict is superficial — that we simply dislike the feeling of self-prediction but that nothing formal rules it out. I suspect the asymmetry may track the difference between choosing the world and predicting the world, but I am not sure.

What I want to know is whether this asymmetry is justified. Is there a principle that explains why it is fine to assign probabilities to another person's choices and to our own past or not-yet-under-deliberation choices, but not to the choice we are currently making? Does the principle survive hard cases — for instance, a case where someone else is in exactly the same epistemic situation and can predict my choice better than I can, or a case where I have a strong habit that predicts what I will do even while I deliberate? And if the asymmetry is not justified, why does it feel so natural?
