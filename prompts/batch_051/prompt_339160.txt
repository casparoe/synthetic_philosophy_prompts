A friend of mine, whom I'll call R, has a running interest in anthropic reasoning, and for the past year I've been recording her verdicts on puzzles about what you may infer from your own existence. Three of those recorded cases now strike me as unable to tell the candidate principles apart. First, the blue-and-green universe: a fair coin is tossed before any observers exist; heads yields exactly one billion observers, all blue-skinned, and tails yields exactly one billion observers, all green-skinned. You wake, inspect your hands, and find they are blue. R's verdict: heads, with near-total confidence, because the coin's fairness becomes idle once you have observed blue — green observers exist only on tails. Second, a case in which two cosmological theories predict exactly the same total number of observers over cosmic history but differ in their predictions about a measurable constant, and new data favor the second theory. R's verdict: shift to the second theory by the ordinary likelihood ratio, with no anthropic correction. She added that she could not see how the number of observers that might have existed could matter, though in that case the counts were equal, so I don't know how much to read into the remark. Third, the guaranteed crew: a machine will create exactly one hundred observers, one per numbered room, whichever way a coin lands; the coin determines only whether the rooms run east-to-west or west-to-east. You wake in room 37 remembering the setup. R's verdict: even odds on the coin — your room number is silent about its orientation.

The new case I want to predict is the one people in this debate call the presumptuous philosopher. Two otherwise equally good cosmological theories are observationally indistinguishable for now, but theory A predicts roughly a trillion observer-lives over the history of the universe while theory B predicts a quadrillion. R has not yet commented on it. The philosopher in the case announces that, purely from the fact that she exists, she can be virtually certain that B is true, because a universe containing a quadrillion observers is vastly more likely to contain her than one containing a mere trillion.

How should I predict R will rule on that? I can see at least two candidate principles that fit her three earlier verdicts: the self-sampling assumption, on which you reason as if you were randomly selected from the observers that actually exist, and the self-indication assumption, on which your own existence is stronger evidence for hypotheses that contain more observers. Both seem to give the same answers to my three recorded cases, because in none of them do the rival hypotheses disagree about how many observers exist. Given that, lay out what each principle would commit R to in the presumptuous philosopher case, say where the slack is in her record if it underdetermines her, give your best-guess prediction of her verdict with the reasons for preferring it, and describe the further thought experiment — not already on my list — that would settle which principle she is actually running.
