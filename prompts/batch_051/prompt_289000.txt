My aunt was raised with a very Ubuntu-tinged, relational picture of what a person is — the gist is that you don't just start out a person by being born human, you become one through other people, over time. She's never written any of this down, but for years I've been writing down her verdicts whenever a case came up, and they're weirdly consistent:

- A newborn baby: not a person yet, she says. Definitely a human, but a person-in-waiting.
- A twelve-year-old who's started getting real responsibilities and is included in adult conversations: partly a person now, like someone still under construction.
- Her mother while she was alive and arbitrating disputes and knowing everyones genealogy: a full person. Maybe more of a person than most.
- That same grandmother after death, because the family still sets a place for her and speaks to her: still a person, she'll tell you, just not one you can see.
- An uncle who emigrated, stopped calling, and never visits: his personhood is drying up, she says, like a cut-off river.
- A foreigner passing through who speaks a different language and sleeps one night in the village: she won't call him a person. Not yet, anyway.
- The family dog, who has a name and gets sung to and fed from the table: obviously not a person, and she treats that one as barely worth discussing.

So here's what I want. Tell me what she'd most likely say about an old woman in the family with advanced dementia, who's cared for at home, called by name, told stories, and sat at the table for meals, but no longer recognizes anyone or talks much. Is she still a person, and how much of one? I don't want just the verdict — lay out two or three rules that fit all of the past verdicts about equally well, run each of them on the dementia case, and give your best guess about which rule is really hers, with the reasons. Then give me one further case that would most cleanly separate your top two rules, and say what each rule would predict for it.
