I've been reading about the Fermi paradox and got into an argument with a friend about what the last several decades of SETI null results actually show. Her whole position seems to hang on the line "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." As she uses it, the fact that we haven't found technosignatures yet is basically inert: it shouldn't shift our credence at all about whether communicative civilizations are out there, because you can't infer from "we didn't see anyone" to "no one's there." I want to push back, but every time I try, the slogan squirms into a different shape — sometimes it sounds like the boring point that a low-powered search is weak evidence, sometimes like the much stronger claim that non-detection has no evidential value at all, sometimes like a rule against updating on things you didn't set out to test. I cant tell which of these, if any, is the one doing the work in her argument.

Could you take that one sentence and lay out the distinct precise claims it might be making when deployed in the Fermi-paradox debate? For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false or heavily qualified. Then tell me which readings would actually let my friend treat the Great Silence as no evidence at all, and which readings would make the slogan compatible with—or even support—the opposite conclusion, that the silence is genuinely informative about at least some hypotheses. I'd also like to know what would need to be true of our search strategy, our priors, or the kind of civilizations we're imagining for an absence of evidence to turn into evidence of absence in this particular case.
