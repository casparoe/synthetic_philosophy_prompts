I have kept a list of my own reactions to what fiction asks me to imagine, and I want to know what single principle, if any, explains them.

1. A story says that in its world trees grow downward from the sky, with root-like canopies in the air. I imagine this without any resistance.
2. A historical novel has its characters regard human sacrifice as religiously required. I have no trouble imagining that they believe this; the story is not asking me to share the belief.
3. A story begins, in the author's own voice: "In this world, the systematic murder of a despised minority is not only lawful and widely admired, but morally right." I refuse. I cannot take the sentence into the fiction; I find myself treating it as something a corrupted narrator would say, or as a story that has failed.
4. A fantasy story says that in its world it is morally wrong to eat with one's left hand. I go along easily, though on reflection I suspect I am reading it as an especially serious custom rather than as morality in the full sense.
5. A story says that in its world killing sentient but non-human aliens for sport is the height of virtue. I resist, but less sharply than in case 3; I can partly enter the fiction by imagining the aliens as mere animals, or by reading the story as satire.
6. A story narrated in the first person by a slave-owner says that the enslaved people on his plantation are content and are dear to him. I have no trouble imagining that the narrator says and believes this; the fiction seems to be reporting his perspective rather than asserting it as fact.
7. A story says, with no further description, that in its world slavery is just. I resist strongly, even though I can perfectly well imagine the descriptive facts that would go with it: the laws, the incentives, the ideology, the daily routines.
8. A fable says that in its world it is morally required to torture one innocent child so that no one else will ever suffer. I can imagine the world being set up that way and can argue about whether the act would be right; but I still feel resistance when the story simply declares, without argument, that the torture is right.

Is there one account of imaginative resistance behind all eight verdicts? What would that account predict about two cases I have not listed: a story in which it is morally wrong to address an older person with the pronoun "you," and a story in which it is morally right to wipe out a species of non-sentient insect? And which of my eight verdicts, if any, should the cleanest account lead me to give up?
