I spent thirty years in an analytical chemistry lab and only recently started reading moral philosophy, so I have been pestering a philosopher friend about loyalty and whistleblowing: when is exposing a secret a justified disclosure, and when is it a betrayal? She humours me with verdicts on little invented cases, and I have kept a list.

- A scrub nurse reports a surgeon to the hospital board for operating while under the influence, without talking to the surgeon first. Friend: did the right thing, no betrayal.
- A software engineer, after her internal complaints were ignored, leaks to a journalist that her company sells users' location data even though its privacy policy promises it won't. Friend: justified.
- A mid-level employee tells the manager that a coworker has been padding her expense reports by about two hundred dollars a year, without saying anything to the coworker first. Friend: that's ratting; she should have spoken to the coworker directly and only escalated if it didn't stop.
- A man gives his brother-in-law a chance to come clean about having gambled away the family's emergency savings and taken out a loan in his wife's name; when the brother-in-law refuses, he tells his sister. Friend: justified; the sister had to know.
- A woman tells her best friend's wife about a one-night stand the friend had years before the engagement, with no ongoing contact. Friend: disloyal meddling, stay out of it.
- A doctoral student, after the authors brushed off her requests for raw data, writes to a journal alleging that the figures in their published paper are fabricated. Friend: justified; she does not owe them another warning.

This weekend I gave her a new one, and she said she'd tell me tommorow. A man discovers that his best friend, who runs a small youth sports charity, has been quietly taking money out of it over two years. It is enough that the charity will likely collapse and the local kids will lose their teams, though nobody is in physical danger and it is not quite theft from any single identifiable person. He goes straight to the police without first telling his friend or the charity's board.

I can see at least two rules that fit her earlier verdicts, and they point different ways on this new case. Lay out the candidate rules, say what each one predicts about the police case, identify which of her earlier cases is the closest precedent, give your best guess about what she will say and why, and say what further case would cleanly settle which rule she is actually running.
