I've been mulling over Simpson's paradox for a while and I've worked myself into a view I want pressure-tested. I've taken to calling it "level-agnosticism": the direction of an association — whether X helps or hurts — is never a fact about the world, only a fact about the grain at which you've choosen to look. A drug can help in every hospital but hurt in the pooled data; a policy can hurt in every district but help overall. So saying "the effect of X on Y is positive" is just reporting where you've set the zoom, not stating a truth. From this I've drawn a rule: I won't accept any single coefficient or headline rate as "the" effect unless I'm told what it's for and what would count as the wrong level of aggregation.

A statistician friend says I'm overreacting, that there are right and wrong ways to aggregate depending on the causal question you're actually asking, and that my view collapses into "there is no fact of the matter" when in fact there is. So: reconstruct my level-agnosticism in its strongest form, tell me whether it already exists under a better name, and show me exactly where it breaks — ideally with one example where the pooled effect is the one to believe and one where the subgroup effect is. Also, please separate what's mathematically settled here from what's genuinely contested among statisticians and people working on causal inference, and don't present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
