I've been going down a rabbit hole on supererogation—acts that are morally good but not strictly required—and it's got me stuck on something concrete. My church was founded in the 1890s, and our covenant (the old membership promise, never updated) includes this clause: 'We covenant with one another to bear each other's burdens, to minister to one another in sickness, and to relieve each other's necessities, as each is able.' Last month one of our members, A., went into kidney failure and needs a transplant. Another member, B., turns out to be a near-perfect match; donating would be relatively low-risk for B. but of course not zero-risk. B. is now asking whether the covenant actually requires him to donate, since he is, in the words of the clause, 'able.' The deacons who wrote this obviously had in view soup, firewood, nursing, maybe money. They could not have had living organ donation in mind.

I don't need a pastoral ruling exactly. I'm trying to understand it analyticaly: what would a plain-language reading of the clause say about B.'s situation? What about a purpose-of-the-rule reading, or asking what the 1890s drafters would have said if someone had described a kidney transplant to them? And what would a reasonable member today take the clause to mean? If those readings diverge, which should govern here, and does the fact that they diverge mean the covenant was quietly turning some supererogatory acts into obligations all along?
