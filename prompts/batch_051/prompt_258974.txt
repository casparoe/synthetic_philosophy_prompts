I run a monthly discussion night for a mostly technical audience — software engineers and data scientists, several of them longtime readers of LessWrong and adjacent forums — and in a few weeks I'm leading a session on the dollar auction and escalation of commitment. The problem I expect is that they think they already know this: 'sunk cost fallacy' is a cached label for them, and they assume that if they were ever in such an auction they would recognize it and stop. I want to break that assumption and teach one specific distinction: the retrospective error of letting costs already paid weigh on a decision, versus the forward-looking trap in which continuing to escalate can be individually rational at each step given your beliefs about whether the other player will stop, even though entering was a mistake and the game as a whole is rigged against both of you. They need to feel the trap in a live auction before they reason about it. Plan the session (roughly forty minutes) as a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself: the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what — in particular where the ex ante/ex interim distinction has to be established before any claim about 'rational escalation' can land without being mistaken for an endorsement of the sunk-cost fallacy, and what role precommitment should play in the story; the single carrying example (the live auction itself, presumably) and what its rules must specify and must rule out for it to reliably produce the right experience rather than a cheap lesson; the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface, and where in the sequence to defuse each one — I am especially worried about 'this is just the sunk-cost fallacy,' 'a rational person simply never bids,' and 'the other player will fold after one more bid'; a check at the end that would tell me whether someone has understood the forward-looking trap rather than just repeating the vocabulary; and what I should explicitly decline to cover at this level, with a sentence on why each omission is safe rather than evasive.
