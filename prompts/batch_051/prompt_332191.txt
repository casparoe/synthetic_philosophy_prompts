I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the opening ten-minute talk at a small museum conference on war and games. The audience is a genuine mix: game developers, historians, and a contingent of veterans and military families, and I've been warned the veterans are tired of talks that either scold everyone or wave the whole problem away with "it's only a game." Write the speech so the philosophical worry isn't a garnish — it should decide what the speech can and can't say. Organize it around an idea I can't quite work out myself: that a war game can go wrong less by making death fun than by making death fungible — turning suffering into a spendable resource, a score multiplier, or a respawn economy. For the developers in the room, draw the line between gravity and realism: a game can be gory and still frivolous, stylized and still serious. And aim for the last beat to feel like an invitation into the problem, not a verdict on the audience.
