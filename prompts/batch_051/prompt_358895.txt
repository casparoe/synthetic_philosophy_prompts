I am a compiler engineer with no formal training in epistemology, but I keep running into Hans Albert's Münchhausen trilemma. As I understand it, the claim is that there are only three ways a chain of justification can go, and all of them are unacceptable: it can go on forever, it can loop back on itself, or it can stop at a point that is simply asserted. Give me a systematic translation of this trilemma into the vocabulary I actually live in — recursion without a base case, circular dependencies, dependency graphs, bootstrapping, and termination proofs. Then tell me what survived the translation and what did not: which parts of the philosophical problem map cleanly onto how software handles chains of dependency and control flow, which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I have a standing intuition that the trilemma is just a missing base case, and that an arbitrary base case is not a scandal, because in software every system bottoms out in something stipulated rather than proved — the operating system, the processor, the compiler that built the compiler. I want to know whether that intuition is correct, or whether the trilemma is pointing at something in the very idea of justification — a requirement that truth be transmitted along the chain, say — that my vocabulary cannot even state.
