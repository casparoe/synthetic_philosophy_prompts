I have been trying to articulate a distinction that I keep reaching for when I read work on respect and blame, and I cannot tell whether it is a real contribution or a renaming of something obvious. I distinguish between episodic blame and settled ascription. Episodic blame is occurrent and dated: I am angry with you for a particular thing you did; the anger rises, and it either resolves or hardens. Settled ascription is different. It is a standing interpretation of a person that runs in the background before any new occasion to judge them: it determines which of their remarks get taken seriously, which failures are predicted, which actions get put down to character rather than circumstance. My hunch is that respect is largely a set of constraints on settled ascription rather than on episodic blame. It is easy to be good at the episode—to respond to the latest mistake proportionately—while quietly maintaining a standing picture of the person that no single episode could ever disconfirm.

The principle I want assessed is what I call the revision condition on settled ascriptions. It says that a settled negative view of someone—careless, self-serving, unreliable, ill-willed—is justifiably held only if it is coupled to a revision path: some describable set of observations or some exchange with the person that would dissolve or weaken it. An ascription for which nothing could count as revision is not, on this view, an assessment of that person anymore; it has become a fixed rule for experiencing them, and we owe one another not to be treated as fixed rules. Accuracy is not the issue, or at least not the whole issue: a settled view can be true and still fail the condition, just as a guilty person can be convicted by an unfair process. The wrongness here is distinct from the wrongness of any hostile act, which is why someone can act with perfect courtesy and still fail at respect: the colleague whose ideas are always patiently received and then quietly filed as noise has been wronged, even if no decision is ever based on it. The same point applies to the sibling read as unreliable at forty because of what he did at twenty, and to the friend whose motives are reinterpreted after one bad year.

That distinction and principle brush against several familiar discussions—Strawsonian reactive attitudes, doxastic voluntarism, the ethics of belief, perhaps epistemic partiality—and I cannot tell whether the revision condition is a genuine addition or just one of those recombined. I also see where it may overreach: perhaps nothing is owed in the domain of private, unchosen, standing attitudes; perhaps the condition collapses for people who are dangerous or impossible to address, for the dead, or for anyone I will never meet again; perhaps there are accurate settled views that should not be held revisably because that would be naïve. I would like an honest assessment rather than a defense: is the distinction real or forced, is the revision condition defensible or does it demand something attitudes cannot give, and where exactly does the view break even if something in the neighborhood survives? Throughout your assessment, please say how confident you are in each major claim you make, and single out the point in your verdict that you are least sure about.
