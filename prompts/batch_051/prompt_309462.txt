A close relative of mine recently regained sight in adulthood after decades of blindness, and I have been rereading Locke in that context. In Book II, Chapter IX, section 8 of *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, he writes:

“When we set before our eyes a round globe of any uniform colour, v.g. gold, alabaster, or jet, it is certain that the idea thereby imprinted on our mind is of a flat circle, variously shadowed, with several degrees of light and brightness coming to our eyes. But we having, by use, been accustomed to perceive what kind of appearance convex bodies are wont to make in us; what alterations are made in the reflections of light by the difference of the sensible figures of bodies; – the judgment presently, by an habitual custom, alters the appearances into their causes. So that from that which is truly variety of shadow or colour, collecting the figure, it makes it pass for a mark of figure, and frames to itself the perception of a convex figure and an uniform colour; when the idea we receive from thence is only a plane variously coloured, as is evident in painting.”

This is not the original Molyneux passage, but it seems to me to bear directly on the standard Molyneux verdict. I want to vary the classic case. Suppose a person born blind has, before gaining sight, learned to identify cube and sphere shapes reliably by active echolocation with mouth clicks, and has also studied perspective and shading through raised-line diagrams and verbal descriptions of geometry. After surgery, she is shown a cube and a sphere at the same distance, in uniform light, without moving or touching them. Can she now say which is which by sight alone? Molyneux’s original answer is no. But if Locke is right that judgment turns flat, shadowed appearances into perceived convex shapes through learned custom, should the answer for this person become yes? Or does the variation fail because the relevant habit cannot be acquired except by actual visual experience? Give me your considered verdict. At the end, briefly state what would change your mind: which empirical result about newly sighted adults, or which interpretation of Locke’s “judgment,” would reverse your answer?
