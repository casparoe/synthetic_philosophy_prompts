Take the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), the revolt in the French sugar colony of Saint-Domingue that ended with Haiti becoming independent, and analyze it through the lens of the debate over Eurocentrism and global history — specifically the idea that the past should be told from everywhere at once, not from a European center. A Eurocentric historiography long treated Saint-Domingue as a sideshow to the French Revolution, just a violent ripple of 1789 or an awkward exception that got written out. A global/Atlantic-history take treats it as central: you can't explain it without Parisian revolutionary language, the sugar economy, the agency of the enslaved, and links to West Africa and the rest of the Caribbean. But the everywhere-at-once ideal is a high bar for a single narrative. Can one account really hold Paris, the northern plain of Saint-Domingue, London, West Africa, and Kingston in the frame at once, or does something get smeared or lost? I want a careful two-way analysis: what the everywhere-at-once framing genuinely explains about the revolution, where it gets vague or starts hand-waving, and what this case shows about whether global history's promise is coherent or basically a slogan.
