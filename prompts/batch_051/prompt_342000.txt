My partner and I adopted a rescue dog a few months back, and he's started doing something that's become a running arguement in our house, so I'd like an outside take. I've been using four criteria for saying an animal is acting with a goal in the sense that matters — i.e. that it represents some end-state (like 'the chew is hidden') and organises its behaviour around bringing that end-state about, rather than just running off a motor routine:

1. Goal-directedness: the behaviour tends to bring about the end-state, and it stops once the end-state is achieved.
2. Flexibility: the animal adjusts the action to the materials and situation at hand (a substrate, an audience, a new room) rather than performing an identical sequence regardless of context.
3. Outcome sensitivity: the animal's later behaviour tracks whether the end-state actually obtained — e.g. it searches after a real cache and doesn't after a failed one.
4. Minimality: no lower-faculty account (fixed action pattern, simple operant or Pavlovian conditioning) fits the animal's whole behavioural record as well.

After a bare-floor 'burial' he never seems to look for it later — he'll just wander past it and sometimes settle down and chew it. The nose-pushing sequence itself looks identical every time, whether it's carpet, tile or blanket. My partner says this is exactly what Morgan's canon is meant to catch (the principle, roughly, that you shouldn't put an action down to a higher mental faculty if a lower one will do): it's just a caching instinct firing off and I'm being anthropomorphic.
