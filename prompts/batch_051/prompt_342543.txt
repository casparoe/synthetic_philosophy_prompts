ok so I write a column about science and ideas, and I keep noticing a pattern around the World Happiness Report that I don't have sharp language for. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of social science who works on measurement, classification, and policy indicators. Think carefully and actually carve out the concept: start by brainstorming a few candidate terms, then tell me which is closest, where it breaks, and where the boundary cases are. I won't be satisfied with just a name.

The rankings are built from one Gallup question, the Cantril ladder, which asks people to rate their life right now from 0 (worst possible) to 10 (best possible). Not how happy they felt yesterday, not how often they laugh: a reflective life evaluation. The report then explains each country's average using six variables: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption.

Here's the pattern I keep seeing. A country slips a few places and the headline is, say, "Portugal got sadder this year." A minister sets up a unit to get the country into the top ten. The unit doesn't mainly try to make life feel better; it works on the things that would plausibly nudge the six variables or the survey. They retranslate the ladder question because the old wording read a bit to harsh. They tout a GDP tweak that will show up in the average. They launch a "we're already generous" campaign so the generosity item moves. The index was meant to be a rough stand-in for an inner state; halfway through, the table becomes the thing being managed, and the original felt quality shows up as the residual. The ordinal layer makes it odder: a country can rise in the rankings while it's absolute score fell, just because its neighbours fell faster, and still take a victory lap.

It's not exactly Goodhart's law, because the people doing this are usually sincere, not gaming the numbers. It's not quite reification, because any policy person involved will say "obviously the score isn't happiness." And it isn't simply a construct-validity problem, because the report authors are fairly open that they're ranking life evaluations, not joy. So: is there an established concept for that specific loop — a soft measure of a private felt quality hardens into a league table, and then the league table gets governed as if it were the quality? If not, define it and distinguish it from those near neighbours, and tell me where the edge is: when is chasing a ranking healthy, and at what point does it tip into the thing I'm describing?
