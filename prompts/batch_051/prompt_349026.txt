I am drafting a short essay on Dōgen's notion of uji, customarily translated as being-time, and I would welcome comments on the skeleton of the argument before I write it up. My working thesis is that uji is best understood neither as a straightforward claim in the metaphysics of time—that existence and time are ultimately one thing—nor as a merely poetic expression of impermanence, but as an attempt to correct a particular way of locating oneself in time. On my reading, Dōgen is targeting the habit of treating the present as a thin, evanescent slice sandwiched between a no-longer-existent past and a not-yet-existent future; he wants instead to get his reader to experience each moment as the whole of existence taking place, such that past and future are present not as external relata but as dimensions of the moment's own activity.

In the essay I plan to develop this through three moves. First, I want to distinguish my reading from the view of time as an external container within which beings happen, a view the text appears to reject. Second, I want to argue that the apparently paradoxical idea of all times being present in each time should be read as a description of a mode of attention to one's own activity, rather than as a theory about temporal parts, eternalism, or the unreality of change. Third, I want to suggest that this is why Dōgen's discussions of uji keep circling back to practice—to birth, death, dwelling, and daily activity—rather than to formal argument, and that reading the text primarily as a solution to puzzles about the reality of time may flatten what it is trying to do.

The comments I would most value are these. Is the distinction between a metaphysical thesis and a corrective to a habit of mind philosophically sustainable here, or does it smuggle in a separation between theory and practice that Dōgen's own way of thinking would resist? Does my formulation of the second move, with its talk of all times being present in each time, inadvertently turn uji into a static eternity, and is there a better formulation that preserves its dynamic, processual character? Finally, does the third move overstate the anti-theoretical flavor of the text—could someone accept the first two claims while still holding that Dōgen is making a substantive metaphysical proposal about the structure of time? I am less concerned with whether the essay is charitable to the existing scholarship than with whether the position I attribute to Dōgen is internally coherent and worth defending on its own terms.
