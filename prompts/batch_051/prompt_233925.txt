(1) Accuracy-first epistemology holds that a rational epistemic state is the one that maximizes expected accuracy under a scoring rule. (2) Suppose I have a fallible credence of 0.7 in p, and I am choosing between believing p, disbelieving p, and suspending judgment about p. Use the simple scoring rule that a true belief scores 1, a false belief scores 0, and suspension always scores 1/2. (3) Relative to suspending, believing p gains 1/2 if p is true and loses 1/2 if p is false. (4) Since my evidence leaves both p and not-p open as possibilities, the gain and the loss count equally in expectation, so believing p nets exactly zero expected advantage over suspending. (5) Suspending is therefore at least as good as believing p in expected accuracy, and it is strictly less risky. (6) The same reasoning applies to any proposition I am less than fully certain about. (7) So the accuracy-first norm seems to require me to suspend judgment about nearly everything—whether it will rain tomorrow, whether my keys are on the table, whether the seminar room is where I remember it being. That conclusion strikes me as wrong, but I cannot tell which step is doing the damage.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the broader project of explaining epistemic norms in terms of accuracy?
