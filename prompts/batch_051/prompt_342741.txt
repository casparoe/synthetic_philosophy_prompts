Here is the definition of a local theory I was taught. A theory is local if, first, no causal influence travels faster than light, and second, the probability of any event is fixed once one specifies a complete description of the relevant state in that event's past light cone; information from outside that light cone contributes nothing further. Now consider a deterministic hidden-variable theory of the Bell experiment, in which the two measurements are made in spacelike separated regions. Variables set at the source determine how each particle will respond to whichever measurement is performed on it, and the theory never posits a signal between the two measurement events. To reproduce the correlations that violate the Bell inequalities, however, the theory also makes the measurement settings themselves correlated with the hidden variables: on this account, the settings are effects of causes in the shared past that also produced the hidden variables. Does this theory satisfy the two criteria as stated? If it does, then Bell-inequality-violating correlations are compatible with a theory that is local in exactly this sense, which would suggest that the criteria omit an implicit assumption — perhaps that the measurement settings are suitably independent of the hidden variables — and the question becomes where such an assumption belongs in the definition. If it does not satisfy the criteria, trace precisely where it fails. Work through the case criterion by criterion, and say whether the case shows that the original definition of locality needs to be amended.
