This stopped being abstract for me a few months ago. After a falling-out with my sister, I began seeing a therapist, and in one session I gave a fluent, confident account of why I had gone silent at a family dinner: I said I had been angry because she interrupted me. A message I had written before the dinner later showed the order of events was less flattering — the silence came first, the anger built afterward, and the interruption story appeared only afterward to make sense of a reaction I did not understand. I was wrong about my own motive, or at least about its cause. A friend who heard about this gave me an argument that seems to turn one local error into a general catastrophe:

(1) A first-person report of an attitude or motive is authoritative — better than an observer's account — only if it is produced by a capacity that accesses something the observer lacks.
(2) Introspection, as far as I can tell, reaches only present-tense sensory and affective material: felt urges, inner speech, images, bodily tension. It does not reach the standing attitudes, motives, or causal histories that such reports claim to report.
(3) So the content of a report like "I was angry because she interrupted me" is not read off from something detected; it is assembled after the fact from behavior, context, and what would make the action intelligible.
(4) An account assembled after the fact from behavior, context, and intelligibility is just an observer-style inference — and observer-style inference about motives is precisely what first-person authority was supposed to beat.
(5) Manipulated-context cases — split-brain confabulation, hypnotic suggestion, people explaining choices they demonstrably did not make — show that observer-style inference about motives can be false while feeling as authoritative as a true self-report. My own case now looks like one more instance.
(6) If my self-ascriptions are observer-style inferences that can be false while carrying full felt certainty, then they have no special reliability; I should treat my own account of why I acted as one fallible hypothesis among others.
(7) But that conclusion is unacceptable. When I apologized to my sister, I was not venturing a guess about a stranger; and if there is no special reliability, then promising, confessing, and holding people responsible for their own stated reasons all rest on a mistake.
(8) So at least one step must fail. I am least willing to give up (7). I would prefer to keep the thought that a person can be wrong in detail about their motive — as I was — without concluding that first-person reports are generally no better than outside guesses.
