A working group has proposed the following regime for cross-border liability over user speech, intended to split the difference between origin-state and audience-state approaches. Participating states agree that a platform with at least 500,000 monthly active users in at least ten participating states is answerable for any item of user-generated content under exactly one participating state's speech law, and that platforms complying with the scheme are immune from civil and criminal liability for that item in every other participating state. The governing state is fixed by the first 72 hours of engagement: the platform must record each view or playback by the declared country of residence of the viewing account, weighted by time engaged, excluding accounts with no declared country and accounts in non-participating states. If the largest single participating state accounts for at least 40 percent of that weighted audience, its law governs; otherwise, the law of the state where the uploading account is registered governs. If the governing state's law makes the item unlawful, the platform must, within 24 hours of receiving a valid notice, disable access to it for users located in the governing state. It need not remove the item elsewhere, unless the item is also unlawful under the law of the state where the platform has its principal place of business, in which case it must remove it globally. An independent treaty body audits the measurement records and may reassign the governing state if it finds the audience was manipulated, for example by purchased engagement or bot traffic.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a policy to endorse. Walk through what it would do to where platforms choose to incorporate, how they rank and distribute content, what uploaders post and to whom, and which speech laws participating states will find it in their interest to enact. Where does the system get gamed first, and how? What happens to content genuinely aimed at one country but viewed widely in others; to platforms just below the thresholds; to deliberately fragmented or non-participating audiences; and to cases where the alleged harm is felt mainly outside the governing state? Is the home-country floor a protection for free speech or a subsidy for permissive incorporation—and can those roles be separated? Identify the failure modes that could be fixed by tightening the rules, and the ones that would persist no matter how the rules are revised.
