In a fortnight I am presenting a paper to a mixed university seminar on media, technology, and public knowledge. My thesis is that the principal threat posed by deepfakes is not that viewers will be taken in by any particular fabricated recording, but that the 'epistemic backstop' supplied by surreptitious recordings of public figures has been quietly dismantled; and, further, that this backstop cannot be rebuilt by technical means such as detection algorithms, watermarking, or cryptographic provenance. My argument runs as follows. (1) Opportunistically or secretly captured audio and video have functioned as a distinctive last-resort check in democratic politics: when a public figure denied their own words, a recording could settle the matter in a way that testimony by journalists or political opponents could not. (2) The authority of that check rested on its being undesigned: it did not depend on trusting any particular institution, publisher, or platform. (3) Once realistic fabrication is widely believed to be possible, any genuine recording can be dismissed as a deepfake — the familiar 'liar's dividend' — so the backstop no longer settles disputes; where it still does, it does so only among those already willing to trust its provenance. (4) Proposed technical fixes do not restore what was lost, because they convert a warrant that was institution-free into one that depends on trusting certifiers, key-holders, and platform implementations; the vulnerability migrates rather than disappears. I conclude that the deepfake crisis is not best framed as an engineering problem of authentication, and that democratic publics must learn to manage without a single-channel audiovisual arbiter that nobody owns.

The audience includes: two computer scientists from the media-forensics group, one working on detection and one on content-provenance standards, who will read my fourth premise as the weakest and who see the problem as hard but solvable; a media historian of photography and early film, who tends to say every documentary medium has passed through a credibility panic and emerged with new informal rules; a social epistemologist who holds that the real problem is the decay of trusted intermediaries rather than the loss of recordings as such; a philosopher of language working on assertion and testimony, likely to press on what I mean by a recording 'settling' a question and on whether the backstop belongs to artefacts or to practices; and a dozen others from history, law, and journalism.
