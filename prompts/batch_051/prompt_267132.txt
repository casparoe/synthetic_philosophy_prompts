I've been going back and forth with a friend about this idea I can't shake, the unilateralist's curse. The setup as I understand it: there's a bunch of people who care about the same outcome and have roughly the same information, and any one of them can, on their own, take some irreversible action that affects everyone—publish the dangerous result, leak the database, release the engineered organism, whatever. Each person has their own threshold for how sure they'd need to be that the action is net good before they'd do it. Some are cautious, some are bold. If everyone acts independently and the action happens as soon as one person crosses their threshold, then the group's effective decision is being made by its least cautious member. Make the group large enough and you get an action taken that 90 percent of the group, maybe 99 percent, would have voted against—not because any new information showed up, but just because there were lots of independent chances for someone to be wrong in the optimistic direction. That strikes me as a genuinely deep argument for coordination norms and for requiring something close to consensus before irreversible things get done. It's clean, it explains a lot of disasters, and it gives me a reason to distrust systems where one hothead can set policy for everyone.

But here's where I get stuck, and where my friend and I ended up argueing. I also have this stubborn intuition that some of the most necessary things people do are unilateral in exactly that sense: blowing the whistle, refusing to go along with the group, publishing the result everyone in the lab wants buried, acting on your own conscience when the relevant community has talked itself into a mistake. The curse seems to say those acts should carry a heavy default presumption against them, because the actor is exactly the kind of person whose judgment is selected for being extreme. My friend says that's survivorship bias: we remember the whistleblower who was right and forget the thousands of self-righteous people who leaked or defied or published and made things worse, and the curse predicts the overall pattern, so I should bite the bullet. I don't buy that, but I can't say exactly why the intuition isn't just special pleading for my favorite heroes.

I want you to act as a philosopher who works on collective decision-making and individual responsibility. Think carefully and brainstorm a little before committing. Give me the strongest version of both sides: why the curse should make me suspicious of unilateral acts even in the whistleblower-type cases, and why my intuition that some unilateral acts are justified might be tracking something the curse leaves out. Then give me a negotiated verdict. Is this a case where the intuition should be debunked, or where the curse needs to be amended or narrowed (and if so, what exactly has to be added: private information, value disagreement, reversibility, something about how we define the group?), or are the two compatible once the conditions are spelled out? What would justify each of those moves, rather than just picking one? Please write the answer in American English.
