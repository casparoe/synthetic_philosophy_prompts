I keep noticing a distinction I don't have a word for, and it seems to sit at the intersection of typography, the theory of concepts, and the metaphysics of categories. When I see the letter a in many forms — a double-story 'a' in a serif face, a single-story 'a' in a geometric sans, the single-story 'a' of an italic, a blackletter 'a', a child's handwritten 'a' — I immediately recognize all of them as the same letter, even though no particular shape, stroke order, or even number of enclosed regions is common to every instance. Two of those instances can share fewer visual features with each other than one of them shares with certain instances of the letters o or d, yet I never confuse the letters. So there seems to be a stable something — the 'a'-ness — that is neither a single visual template nor a bare convention, and a separate something — the stylistic system — that can vary almost without limit while still counting as an instance of the letter a. Is there an established concept for each of these two things and for the relation between them? I know the type/token and character/glyph distinctions, but this seems different: identity survives across glyphs, and style is not merely a property of individual tokens, since a style propagates across the whole alphabet. I am after the carving, not just a label. What should I call these two things, and how exactly is the boundary drawn — at what point does a stylized 'a' cease to be an 'a' and become a malformed 'o' or a new symbol?
