I’ve been thinking about the old Meno puzzle—why knowledge is better than mere true belief—and I want help figuring out what principle actually captures my reactions to specific cases, not just the standard answer. In the Meno, Plato has Socrates say that a true opinion about the road to Larissa will get you there just as well as knowledge, but that true opinions aren’t worth much until someone ties them down by working out the reason why. I’m not sure I believe that, or at least I don’t know what tying them down really amounts to. Here are my gut verdicts. A chess novice who memorizes a move in one endgame and plays it correctly: his true belief seems as good as a grandmaster’s knowledge for that game, but not as good as the grandmaster’s if the position changes slightly. A nurse who spots that a patient is having a heart attack from an unclear gut feeling and calls the code: as good as the doctor’s knowledge for the patient, and I don’t really care that she can’t explain it. A juror who votes guilty because the defendant’s face looks shifty and happens to be right: not as good as a juror who guilt from the evidence, even though both reach the same verdict. A student who memorizes that the square root of two is irrational and can reproduce the conclusion but not the proof: I’d say his belief is much less valuable than a mathematician’s knowledge. And here’s the pair that bothers me: I think the nurse’s inarticulate true belief is fine, but the juror’s inarticulate true belief is not, and I can’t see a principle that separates them without making it ad hoc. Is there a coherent account of why knowledge is better than true belief that gets all these, or close, and which verdict should I revise?
