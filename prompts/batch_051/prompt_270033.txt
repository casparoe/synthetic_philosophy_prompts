Two people in my reading group went at it last week about a friend of ours, Marcus, who lost his small bookshop during lockdown, and I've been turning it over ever since without landing anywhere.

Aisha said Marcus's whole "only your own choices are up to you" line has been genuinely good for him: he says the lockdowns, the rent hikes, and his customers vanishing weren't in his control, so he refuses to treat them as a personal failure, and he's been eerily calm about starting over. She said that's the dichotomy of control doing real work—locate your self-worth only in what you can actually choose, and everything else is weather.

Ben said that's a butchered version. The Stoics never said "if it's not up to you, don't care about it." They said externals don't determine whether you flourish, but you still pursue them, with the inner rider that the outcome isn't your good. If you turn "not up to me" into "not my problem," you get a quietist, not a Stoic—somebody who could watch a neighbor get robbed and shrug because it's outside his will. He also said Aisha's version would make you unlatch your effort from anything whose outcome isn't fully in your hands, which would rule out nearly everything worth doing.

Aisha came back that Ben is smuggling in a third category through the back door. Either a thing is up to you or it isn't; if you pursue the survival of a shop "with a rider," the only thing you actually control is still your intention, so the outcome stays not yours. She said Ben hasn't refuted the dichotomy, he's just relabeled her version of it. She also said that privately mourning the shop is fine, but treating the closure as a reason for self-blame would be a category mistake—nobody can be on the hook for a global lockdown.

Ben said she keeps running together "the shop doesn't determine whether I flourish" with "the shop doesn't matter." The Stoics even had a slot for things worth pursuing that aren't the good—a functioning business, avoiding pain, that sort of thing. A well-run shop failing is a real setback for one of those, and it's coherent to grieve it while denying it touches your character. He finished with: the control test answers "what's mine to take credit or blame for," and you can answer that narrowly while still treating the shop as a loss rather than weather.
