What should an adequate account of 'the physical' have to deliver? I want the specification before I work through candidate answers, because the candidates keep getting graded against different standards. The fixed problem is Hempel's dilemma. If 'physical' means 'whatever present-day physics says exists,' physicalism comes out almost certainly false, since physics is a going concern and has revised its ontology before. If 'physical' means 'whatever a completed, ideal physics would say exists,' physicalism threatens to come out empty, since we cannot say in advance what an ideal physics might not include, and primitive mental properties could in principle be admitted without contradiction. So write the desiderata that an account of the physical must satisfy if physicalism is to be a substantive, contingent, and possibly true claim about our world. For each desideratum, give the consideration or kind of case that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. I am especially interested in three things. First, where does your list leave the familiar families of view — anchoring to present physics, deference to a future completed physics, a negative characterization of the physical, a structural characterization — and which single requirement does most to exclude candidates? Second, is any subset of your desiderata jointly unsatisfiable, and if so, what should we conclude from that? Third, how confident should I be in the list itself: for each desideratum you would hesitate to defend, tell me what argument or counterexample would lead you to demote it from a requirement to a preference or drop it, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
