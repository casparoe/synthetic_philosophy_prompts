In my philosophy of biology class we've been going around in circles about "survival of the fittest," and I only realized halfway through the seminar that everone in the room was using the same four words to mean something different. The professor uses it as a neutral shorthand for natural selection. One grad student wheeled out the old objection that the phrase is empty: if "fittest" just means "whatever survives," then the slogan only tells you that survivors survive, and natural selection ends up being an untestable tautology. Someone else shot back that fitness should be read as a propensity—an organism's expected reproductive success, a probabilistic disposition to survive and leave offspring that you can estimate independently of who actually made it. By the end, I genuinely couldn't tell which of those readings "survival of the fittest" is even supposed to be making.

So: take the slogan "survival of the fittest" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, ordered roughly from most trivial to most substantive. For each reading, tell me whether it's a definitional truth (and so scientifically empty), an empirical claim (and what would count as evidence for or against it), or just false. Then say which readings actually give the tautology objection its bite, which readings the propensity interpretation is best understood as defending, and which reading was probably doing the work for each person in the argument I just described. I want a menu I could bring to office hours, not one right answer defended to the death.

Also please push back if I've set this up badly—if, e.g., the tautology charge and the propensity reply are answers to different questions and don't really clash, or if the slogan is doing rhetorical work rather than scientific work so the whole debate is a category error. If something like that is going on, tell me plainly instead of humoring the question.
