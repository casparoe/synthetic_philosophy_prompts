I teach philosophy of science and have been trying to decide whether to assign a popular article on computational irreducibility in my graduate seminar. The article's core explanation—which I keep coming back to because it sounds almost right—goes like this: 'For a large class of systems, including cellular automata like Rule 30 and, by extension, much of nature, Wolfram showed that there is no shortcut: the only way to learn what the system will do later is to run it forward step by step for as many steps as you care about. No equation and no faster computation can predict the system's later states. This, the article says, is why weather forecasts and the long-run behavior of economies, brains, and ecosystems cannot be made accurate beyond a certain horizon in principle: the universe is effectively performing the fastest possible computation of its own next state, and any predictor would have to run just as long as the system itself. Wolfram's principle of computational equivalence then says that all nontrivial computations are roughly equal in computational sophistication, and the practical upshot is that traditional science, which searches for simple formulas and compressions, hits a wall—for many systems we will never get a satisfying explanation, only step-by-step simulation, and simulation is not really an explanation at all.'

There's something in there that strikes me as too strong, and something in the last step that feels like a non sequitur, but I cant quite say where the breaks are located. Since I'd rather not hand my students an overstatement dressed up as a theorem, could you go through the paragraph and tell me what a careful person working on foundations of computation or philosophy of science would accept, what they'd wince at, and what they'd flatly reject? I'm especially interested in whether the final claim—that simulation isn't really an explanation—follows from anything true earlier in the summary. And because I'll likely teach from your breakdown: tell me where your own audit is least sure-footed, and what consideration or argument would most move your overall verdict about whether the summary is fair.
