I am giving a seminar paper next month to a mixed faculty and graduate student audience in a divinity school, and I need to prepare for a question period I expect to be bruising. The paper defends the claim that the goodness of creation affirmed in the opening chapter of Genesis is compatible with the evolutionary history of predation, parasitism, and animal pain, because the goodness in question attaches to the direction and fruitfulness of creation rather than to the felt state of every creature within it. My argument distinguishes pain, understood as nociception, from suffering, understood as a phenomenally distressing state that requires self-awareness and appraisal; holds that much animal pain falls on the pain side of that line and does not amount to a moral horror; and treats predation and death as integral to the kind of world that could produce creatures capable of faith, covenant, and moral life at all. I give the Fall a historical and vocational rather than a cosmic-retroactive reading: it wounds the human calling and introduces alienation, disorder, and death experienced as enmity, but it does not secretly install carnivory, parasitism, and pain into a previously vegetarian natural order, a view I argue is both exegetically strained and scientifically incoherent. The audience is mostly systematic theologians working in broadly Augustinian and Reformed traditions, with a smaller number of philosophers of religion who work on natural evil; they are firmly committed to the original goodness of creation, take scripture with great seriousness, and accept an old earth and common descent, but they are suspicious of two things in particular: any theodicy that seems to blunt the reality of suffering, and any account that makes God the direct author of a creation shot through with pain.

Write me a Q&A preparation sheet for this specific audience. I want the objections I will actually get from these people, not the ones a general philosophy audience or a young-earth audience would raise: ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; phrased the way a Reformed systematic theologian or a philosopher of natural evil would actually phrase them; each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections have real force and what I should concede up front, and which one reply has to land for the paper to survive. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Please give me a committed response: do not lay out the options and leave me to choose; tell me which objections I should treat as the serious ones, what I should concede, and what I should hold, with reasons. And write it in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
