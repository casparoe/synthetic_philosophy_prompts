We have been discussing the paradox of hedonism in my ethics seminar, and I keep coming back to a distinction that our professor made but did not develop. In The Methods of Ethics, Henry Sidgwick remarks that the conscious pursuit of pleasure seems to be self-defeating: the more deliberately one tries to make pleasure one's direct aim, the less likely one is to secure it, because pleasure arises most reliably as a byproduct of engaging with objects and activities for their own sake. The standard response is that hedonism should therefore be an indirect strategy—one should aim at other goods and let pleasure supervene. But I wonder whether this response blurs two quite different claims. One claim is about attention: concentrating on the quality of one's own experience interferes with the absorption that actually produces pleasure. Another claim is about motivation: holding pleasure as one's overriding end makes one less attached to the particular people, projects, and norms that normally generate pleasure. These might come apart. Someone could be an indirect hedonist about attention—practically, don't monitor your feelings—while remaining an unapologetic direct hedonist about justification—pleasure is still the only thing that ultimately matters. Or the motivation claim might fail: perhaps one can care wholeheartedly about a project and still be motivated by the expectation of pleasure, just not by constantly checking on that expectation.

Please make an argument for the following position: the paradox of hedonism is often presented as a single empirical obstacle to hedonism, but it is better understood as a composite of at least three distinct mechanisms—attentional, motivational, and evaluative—and only the consistency of the evaluative version (that hedonism is self-effacing as a criterion of rightness, not just as a life strategy) poses a serious challenge to hedonism as a normative theory. In making the argument, distinguish these mechanisms clearly, explain how the standard indirect response addresses only one or two of them, and defend the claim that the evaluative reading, if it succeeds, is the only one that would count against hedonism rather than merely against a naive way of pursuing it. Where you think the evaluative reading fails, say so, but present the strongest version you can before assessing it.
