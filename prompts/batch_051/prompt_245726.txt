I'm a software engineer on a keyboard app team; we support the top 80 or so languages and every quarter we argue about which smaller ones to add. There's a senior engineer who keeps pushing the line that languages are basically species: a language with its last few hundred speakers is a critically endangered species, and letting it die of digital neglect is like bulldozing the last patch of habitat. So she wants us to triage our language queue the way a conservation organization would — skip the mid-sized languages with decent tooling, and put real resources into tiny ones first. Our product lead proposed a rule like 'nothing under 500k speakers,' and she called that the linguistic version of writing off anything that isnt charismatic enough for a conservation campaign. I keep feeling like the analogy is doing alot of work there, but I can't say exactly where it gives out. I mean, languages aren't really born or evolved the way species are; people can speak several at once; a language can be revived from recordings; and it's not obvious there's an 'ecosystem' that collapses when one disappears. But she has a point that a lot of what's lost is irreplaceable.

Can you actually audit this language-as-species analogy for our argument? I want the correspondence table first — what's supposed to map to a species, a population, a habitat, an extinction, maybe an invasive species. Then walk through the disanalogies and tell me which ones matter for her priority claim and which are just cosmetic. And then give me a verdict: does the conclusion survive — that we should bump a tiny endangered language above a healthy mid-sized one, the way a conservation NGO would triage — or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? If it was, is there a better analogy for what a language is and what's actually lost when one dies?
