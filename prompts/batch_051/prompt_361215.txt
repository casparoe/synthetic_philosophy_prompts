I am giving a one-off, fifty-minute seminar to second-year biology students on free-floating rationales: the idea that there can be reasons for traits, behaviours, and arrangements without any agent who reasoned them out. The students already use adaptationist language cheerfully — they will say a giraffe's long neck is "for" reaching high foliage — but under pressure they retreat either to the view that teleological talk is just metaphor or to the worry that it secretly assumes a designer. I want the session to make the "for" literal without any mind. Do not explain the idea to me; produce a teaching plan. What distinction has to be in place before "reasons without reasoners" is even intelligible to them, and which of those moves must I refuse in the opening ten minutes? Choose the single example that will carry the whole session and justify it: what must it have and lack, and why not the giraffe's neck or the vertebrate eye? Then give me the order of the remaining pieces, with what depends on what, and for each step the misconception students are most likely to form — I expect at least "reason is just a metaphor for cause", "if nobody reasoned, then there are only causes", and "the function of a trait is whatever it does now" — and say at exactly which point in the sequence each should be headed off and how. Tell me which distinctions to leave for a later course and how to say so without making the idea look evasive. End with one question for the last five minutes that would tell me whether they have grasped the idea rather than learned to repeat the slogan.
