I am a novelist doing preliminary reading for a book set during the Wars of Religion, and I keep returning to Montaigne’s essay on cannibals. I want to use his thinking about the reported practices of the Brazilian people he describes as part of a character’s slow loss of confidence in the superiority of French custom, but I realize I do not actually know what commitment I would be giving that character. The line usually translated as saying that people apply the word barbarous to whatever lies outside their own usage has become, in my notes, a kind of slogan: I have written it in the margin next to passages on funeral customs, cooking, child-rearing, even pronunciation. But I am no longer sure what precise claim it is making. Could you separate out the different precise theses that sort of remark might express? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false; and say which of these readings would actually do the work of making a character doubt that her own customs are the standard. I would like the answer to pay special attention to the difference between a factual claim about how people in fact use evaluative language, a methodological caution about how much our judgments should be moved by novelty, and a normative claim that all customs are equally good. And please write it all as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; just the kind of plain paragraphing one would put in a private letter or an email.
