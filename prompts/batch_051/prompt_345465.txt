I spent my working life in the natural sciences, where the appropriate response to an idea you dislike is argument, replication, or indifference, never violence. Since retiring, I have been reading Émile Durkheim's The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life, and I keep returning to a passage in Book III, in the discussion of negative rites, where Durkheim treats sacrilege as something more than the breach of an ordinary prohibition: the sacred is set apart by interdicts, and the person who violates those interdicts becomes profane and polluting in a way that demands a collective reaction, not merely private indignation. The passage is not the famous definition at the opening of the book; it is the place where he develops the dynamic of contact, contamination, and expiation, and I would like that account to do real work in an analysis of a concrete event.

Use that Durkheimian account as a lens on the Charlie Hebdo murders of January 2015. Lay out the mapping carefully: what in the episode occupies the role of the sacred, what counts as profanation, who is the polluted party, what the collective reaction is, and where killing someone for a drawing fits Durkheim's mechanism and where it does not. Then turn the lens around and ask what the episode exposes about the theory. Durkheim was analyzing a moral community whose members share the sacred, while the Charlie Hebdo attack involved a secular magazine that did not accept the relevant classification, a minority whose felt offense was understood as universal rather than local, and a state whose legal framework treats blasphemy as protected expression. Tell me which of these features the Durkheimian account can absorb, which it misdescribes, and whether the episode ultimately supports the conclusion that the category of sacrilege deserves an irreducible place in the analysis of religiously motivated violence, or whether sacrilege is better reduced to something else, such as humiliation, identification with a wounded group, or defense of one's standing. End by stating what would change your mind: which further evidence or which counter-argument would be most likely to overturn the verdict you give.
