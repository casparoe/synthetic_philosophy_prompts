I recently reread Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day after catching myself giving fluent, confident explanations of my own motives that later struck me as partly confabulated, and I want to use the novel to think about the reliability of introspection. Read Stevens's first-person narration against the disagreement between those who treat introspection as a privileged, direct channel to one's own states and those who treat self-knowledge as a fallible act of interpretation built from much the same evidence others have. I would like a reading that separates two things the novel might be showing: that Stevens lacks reliable access to his own feelings — about Miss Kenton, about his father, about Lord Darlington — or that he in some sense knows them and is motivated not to own them. Where does the novel illuminate that distinction, which scenes would you put in front of a reading group, and where does it resist the easy conclusion that introspection is simply unreliable? Does the novel add anything to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone miss? Begin with the reading itself, with no preface and no restatement of the question.
