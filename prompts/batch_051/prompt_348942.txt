I'm teaching a small intro class in comparative ethics, and last week I got into a late-night argument with a friend about whether it's wrong to save your own mother from a fire before a stranger who happens to be a doctor. She thought the Confucians where obviously right and Mozi was an inhuman rationalist; I couldn't shake the feeling that Mozi had a point about families hoarding care while strangers burn. I want to turn this into an exercise for my students, but a sorting quiz, not a lecture.

Here's what I need. The two positions that have to show up are Mozi's impartial care and the Confucian graded love view he attacked. As I understand them, Mozi thought we should care for everyone as much as we care for our own parents, and that partiality to our own family is the source of a lot of war and waste; the Confucians thought care has a proper structure, with the thickest obligations to parents and siblings and care thinning as you move outward, and that ritual and social roles do real moral work. I'd like the quiz to be a decision tree built from the logical space of answers to concrete cases, not from the names of positions. Start with questions a first-year can answer without knowing any terminology, then locate actual positions in the space of answer combinations, and flag empty cells: if nobody holds a combination of answers, say whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. The cases I want used (add one or two if you like): rescuing a parent vs a stranger in a fire; a mayor giving a city contract to a nephew; donating to a local food bank vs an international anti-malaria charity; and covering for a coworker who keeps missing shifts because their kid is sick. Leaves should include at least Mozi-style impartial care, Confucian graded love, a Western utilitarian-leaning impartiality, and a view that treats family obligations as strong but not unlimited. Keep it to around seven branch questions and six leaves, and flag any two positions the tree genuinely can't separate. Oh, and word every question so a student isn't just chosing whichever label sounds nice. And I don't want the quiz to smuggle in the verdict that impartiality is obviously right; a student who lands on graded love should feel like that's a real position, not a failure state.
