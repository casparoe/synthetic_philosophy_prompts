I've been arguing with some people in a philosophy reading group about free will, and we keep going in circles because everyone sort of agrees that "the free will worth wanting" probably isnt one single capacity, but nobody can say which capacities actually do the work. One person keeps insisting on the old ability-to-do-otherwise picture — that you're only free if you genuinely could have chosen something else at the moment of choice. Another keeps saying that's a red herring, and what matters is whether the act comes from a self you'd endorse on reflection, even if the menu was fixed all along.

To settle it, we invented two labels. Call menu-freedom the capacity to have had real, psychologically available alternatives at the moment of choice — not just random twitches, but courses of action you could actually have seen yourself taking. Call signature-freedom the degree to which an action is authored by a self you can own: its reasons, its history, its characteristic patterns — even in cases where, given who you were then, you were never going to pick the other door. I want a concrete, ordinary, non-sci-fi example where these two really pull apart: a case with lots of one and almost none of the other. Make the person and the choice vivid enough that the example does the theoretical work for you.

Now the part I actually need a verdict on. Add a third capacity into the mix: revision-freedom — the power to change, over time and in response to reasons or criticism, the standing weights you give your own desires, rather than just choosing within them. In your example, how much revision-freedom does the person have, and which of the three capacities is doing the real work when we blame or praise them? Push past "they all matter"; if a friendship or a criminal court had to pick exactly one of these as the precondition for holding someone responsible, which would you pick, why, and what would be the first thing to break with that choice?
