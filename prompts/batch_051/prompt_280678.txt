I'm a journalist who covers the science and philosophy of mind. A long reporting project on consciousness has left me defending a higher-order theory, and an editor recently asked me what evidence would change my mind—a question I answered less well than I would like. The position, as I defend it, is this: a mental state's being conscious consists not in any intrinsic property of that state and not in its causal role in guiding behavior, but in its being the object of a suitable higher-order representation—a state that represents the mind as being in the first state. Subjective character is conferred by that representational relation.

I can already feel how easily the view can be adjusted to absorb counterexamples by changing what counts as suitable, and I do not trust my own ability to spot when that adjustment has become question-begging. I want you to stress-test the position honestly. Walk through the strongest empirical challenges from blindsight, masked perception, anesthesia, prefrontal cortex function, and metacognitive research, and the strongest conceptual challenges from infant and animal consciousness, from introspection that seems to reveal no higher-order state, and from cases where a higher-order state seems present without any corresponding conscious experience. For each challenge, separate what would be fatal from what would merely force a revision, and say which challenges a sophisticated defender would themselves concede are genuine liabilities rather than confusions. Finally, tell me whether a view with this much built-in flexibility can still be said to make testable predictions at all—and, if it cannot, whether that is a reason to abandon it or simply a feature of philosophical theories of consciousness.
