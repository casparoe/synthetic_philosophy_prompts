I've been rowing with my flatmate about free riders — people who take the benefit of something without putting anything in — and I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases on the night bus home. They feel like they should hang together but they dont. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. My flatmate says the principle is just "if you benefit from other people's work you owe them something in return," but that can't be right given some of the cases below.

Here are the cases with my honest verdicts:

(a) Anjali gets on the bus through the middle doors every morning and doesn't tap her card, even though she can easily afford the fare. Wrong.

(b) Sam has never voted in his life and says it doesn't matter because he benefits from living in a democracy either way. I don't think he's doing anything morally wrong, just being a bit useless.

(c) Ben does literally none of the work in a three-person group assignment but receives the same mark as the other two. Wrong.

(d) Chloe downloads a PDF of a very overpriced textbook that she could never have bought. I can't make myself think that's wrong, even though the author and publisher are worse off than if she'd bought it.

(e) Dan has been claiming a disability benefit he doesn't qualify for, for years. Clearly wrong.

(f) A small company takes open-source code, builds a profitable paid app, and gives nothing back to the maintainers, even though the licence permits it. I think it's fine, but I get a twinge about the maintainers.

(g) Rosa enjoys her neighbour's front garden every day but never helps with the weeding. Fine.

(h) A parent refuses to vaccinate a healthy child for no medical reason, but the child is still protected because everyone else's children are vaccinated. Wrong.

The pair that really bothers me is (a) and (f). Anjali takes a service that would run anyway and pays nothing, and I call it wrong; the company takes code that was written anyway and gives nothing back, and I call it fine. Is there a principle that separates them without being gerrymandered? And what should I say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a worker who never joins the union and never pays dues, but happily accepts the pay rise the union negotiated for everyone in the workplace. Is he doing the same kind of wrong as Anjali, or something closer to Sam?

Please lay out the best candidate principle or principles, run each through all eight cases, tell me honestly which of my verdicts can't be kept and which ones the principle says I should revise. I'd rather have a clean principle that overturns one of my verdicts than a gerrymandered one that saves all of them. Please write the answer in British English, and end with a one-sentence summary as the last line, beginning with "Summary:".
