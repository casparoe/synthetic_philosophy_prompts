I'm on my town's heritage board, and we've built up a weird little body of precedent about what to do when a community's memory of an event and the actual record conflict. I'd like a recommendation on a new case, but mostly I want to figure out what rule we've actually been running.

Our past calls:
- We left alone a plaque inside the old church saying the church was spared by a flood in 1893, even though county records show no flood that year. We said it reads as a providential legend and no one is accused of anything.
- We made the local museum correct an exhibit that said a factory fire was set by striking miners; court records show it started accidentally, and the false version had been used for decades to discredit the union.
- For a 1911 mill collapse where families remember 31 dead but the official count says 17, we didn't pick one; we put up two panels side by side and let visitors see both.
- We refused to change a memorial bench in the park that attributes a line to our town poet, though the line is almost certainly not his; the family that paid for the bench wanted the words kept, not the attribution.

New case: the town wants to put a bronze marker at the fork in the road retelling the old story that the town split in two because the founding families feuded over a stolen horse in 1840, and the school feild-trip guide already tells the kids this. Survey records show no feud: the split is just where a railroad drew a boundary, and the two parishes adopted the feud story afterward. No one is blamed in the story, but it is false, and a history teacher has asked us to at least add 'records tell a different story' to the marker.

What should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our past calls is the closest analog? And if our precedents underdetermine it, what principle would we be adopting with each option — and what further case would best settle which rule we're actually running?
