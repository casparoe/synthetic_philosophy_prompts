I host a small political philosophy podcast, and next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends a participatory-intention account of complicity. The core idea is that you can be complicit in a group's wrongdoing without causing it—even if your own contribution made no causal difference to the outcome—as long as you intentionally participate in a collective project that you know, or reasonably should know, carries a risk of wrongdoing. She's got a pat answer for the bare "I didn't cause it" objection, so I don't want to waste a question on that except as a stepping stone.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions? I'd like at least one question on each of the following, and for each, a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. (1) The boundary problem: if individual causal difference-making doesn't matter, what stops everyone who ever participated in a large collective—a corporation, a military, a political party—from being complicit in everything that collective later does? After she distinguishes accountability from causal responsibility, press her on whether that distinction actually solves the problem or just relabels it. (2) The epistemic threshold: how much does a low-level participant have to know about the larger plan for their contribution to count as complicity, and what happens when leaders deliberately keep workers in the dark? (3) Diffuse or structural harms, like climate change or exploitative supply chains, that don't seem to have a unified joint project at all: does the view undergenerate by requiring intentional participation, or overreach if it doesn't?

I'd also like at least one question on whether she thinks moral complicity and legal complicity should come apart this sharply, given that the law usually wants some kind of causal facilitation for aiding and abetting. And maybe one that forces her to say what the normative payoff really is: once we've called someone complicit, did we learn anything beyond "they contributed to a group that did wrong"? Keep it so it feells like a sharp interlocutor pressing the view, not a fan, and no "what drew you to philosophy" filler.
