Plutarch’s essay On the Eating of Flesh builds its case on the claim that the earth supplies abundant fruits, grains, and vegetables, so killing animals for food is not necessary. That claim carries a hidden geography: it takes for granted a landscape where plant foods can meet human nutritional needs. I have been wondering how the argument behaves when that assumption fails.

Consider two cases. A person in London continues to eat factory-farmed pork even though lentils, soy, and rice are cheap and widely available; most philosophical vegetarians and vegans would describe this as a moral failing. A herding family in the Mongolian steppe eats mutton and dairy because the land cannot reliably support crops; even many committed animal advocates describe this as a tragic necessity or simply a different context. The two cases seem to trigger very different judgments, though the underlying act is the same: an animal is killed for food when plant alternatives exist in one case but not in the other.

I want to know whether this asymmetry is justified by a defensible principle or is merely a psychological accommodation to distance and culture. Formulate the strongest principle you can that condemns the Londoner but not the herding family. Then apply it to nearby cases: a wealthy person who could easily eat plants but occasionally eats wild-caught fish; a poor farmer who keeps backyard chickens because eggs are the cheapest reliable protein; a pastoralist who could move to a city and adopt a vegan diet but would lose her culture and livelihood. In the end, state clearly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
