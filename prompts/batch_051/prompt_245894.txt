I have been trying to decide whether the familiar universalization question — What if everyone did that? — tracks my actual moral judgments, or whether it is a rough heuristic I quietly abandon when it delivers answers I do not want. I have collected a set of verdicts, and I want either the principle behind them or a clear account of which verdicts I must revise. Treat this as reflective equilibrium: propose the principle or small set of candidate principles, state each clause precisely and identify which case motivates it, apply it to cases I have not settled, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Tax underreporting. A self-employed person underreports a modest percentage of her income in a society where most people comply. If every self-employed person underreported at the same rate, the tax base would shrink enough to degrade the public services she relies on. Her own underreporting makes no detectable difference. I judge this wrong, and not merely because it is illegal.

2. Crossing a fragile lawn. A park has a fragile lawn and a sign asking visitors to keep to the path. If everyone cut across the same corner, the grass would become a dirt track. If one person does it once, there is no visible damage. I judge this wrong, though only mildly.

3. Failing to vote. A citizen in a large, functioning democracy stays home on election day. If everyone stayed home, democratic authorization would break down. The single abstention is causally negligible in a large electorate. I am not willing to call this wrong in the same way as the first two; I lean toward saying it is not wrong, though I feel some civic pressure.

4. Choosing not to have children. A person reflectively decides never to have children. If everyone made the same choice, the society would age and shrink and eventually have no future generations. I judge this permissible; the universalized consequence does not make the choice wrong.

5. Breaking a promise for a better offer. A person promises to help a friend move, then backs out when a more attractive invitation arrives. If everyone broke promises whenever a superior option appeared, promising as a practice would collapse. I judge this wrong.

6. Purchasing from an exploitative firm. A consumer buys an inexpensive item from a firm known to use exploitative labor practices, although a fair alternative exists at a modestly higher price. If everyone bought from the exploitative firm, the practice would persist and spread; if everyone bought the fair alternative, the pressure to reform would be decisive. The single purchase has no detectable effect on the firm's behavior. I judge this wrong, though I am less confident than in 1, 2, and 5.

7. Spending on luxury while lives are at stake. A person spends a windfall on luxuries while a donation to a proven aid organization would be very likely to prevent a death. If everyone in a position to help spent only on themselves, many preventable deaths would occur; if everyone gave their fair share, the deaths would largely be prevented. I lean toward judging this wrong, or at least morally deficient, even though no particular death can be attributed to the individual omission.

The pattern I cannot crack is this. Cases 1, 2, 3, and 6 all resemble one another: the agent's own contribution is causally negligible, but if everyone acted the same way, something valuable would degrade or collapse. Yet I judge 1, 2, and 6 wrong and 3 not clearly wrong. Case 4 sharpens the difficulty: the universalized outcome is, if anything, worse than in the other cases, and still the choice seems permissible. Case 5 is the one my intuitions fit easily, but it is also exactly the sort of case that any of several rival principles can explain, so it does not discriminate.

My tentative principle has been roughly Kantian. I have been testing: an act is wrong when its maxim, if made universal law, would either (a) defeat the act's own point because the practice or expectation the act relies on would no longer exist, or (b) be a universal law the agent could not rationally will, given needs no rational agent can disown. This handles 1 and 5 straightforwardly, and 2 with some strain, because crossing the grass remains perfectly possible in a world where everyone crosses it. It gives me no clear reason to distinguish 1 from 3 or to condemn 1 while permitting 4. So I doubt it.

A rival thought I keep returning to is that the real issue is free-riding: an act is wrong when, and because, the agent takes the benefit of a cooperative scheme while exempting herself from a cost on which the benefit depends. That would explain 1, 2, and 5, and arguably 6. But it seems to condemn 3, since a non-voter benefits from the democratic authorization produced by other people's participation; I would then have to say either that failing to vote is wrong after all, or that abstention is not the relevant kind of cost-evasion. It also threatens 4, since a childless person arguably benefits from the existence of a next generation without contributing to it. I do not yet know whether that threat should make me abandon the free-riding principle or abandon my verdict in 4.

What I want from you is this. Is there a principle — or a small set of candidates — that preserves more of my verdicts than either the Kantian test or the free-riding test, without becoming gerrymandered? Does the difference lie in whether the bad universalized outcome would be produced through the same causal mechanism as the individual act? In whether the wrongness is tied to the collapse of a practice the agent uses, rather than to the mere undesirability of the state of affairs? In a threshold or saturation effect? Or in something about the agent's control over whether others follow her lead? State the principle precisely, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop: for example, whether I should stop condemning 1, 2, or 6, or start condemning 3 or 4, or subdivide one of the cases.

Finally, tell me what your principle says about cases I have not settled: a person who harvests one fish from a fishery governed by a quota that others observe; a person who declines jury duty in a system where nearly everyone serves; a person who refuses vaccination during an outbreak; a person who uses an ad blocker on a site that asks users not to; and a person who never develops a demanding skill she is capable of developing. If your principle has multiple clauses, identify what each clause earns and what it costs.
