I am assembling a survey of candidate resolutions of the St. Petersburg paradox, and I want to check whether I have missed a structurally distinct family. The game, as usual: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if that happens on toss n, the payout is 2^n dollars. The expected monetary value is the sum over n of (1/2^n)(2^n), which diverges, yet few people would pay more than a modest amount for a single play.

The resolutions I already have, stated in my own terms, are these five:

1. Concave-utility resolution: convert payouts into utility using a concave utility function over money, such as a logarithmic one; expected utility then comes out finite, because the rapidly increasing payouts are offset by rapidly decreasing probabilities once money is transformed into utility.

2. Finite-bankroll truncation: no real counterparty can actually pay unbounded sums, so payouts are capped at the house's total wealth, and the truncated game has a finite expectation of roughly the log of the bankroll plus a small constant.

3. Bounded utility: the way to block the escalation move in which any unbounded utility function can be beaten by constructing a variant game with faster-growing payoffs is to require utility to be bounded above.

4. Time-average/ergodicity resolution: for a person considering actual repeated play with a finite bankroll, the relevant quantity is the time-average growth rate of wealth, not the ensemble-average expected wealth; the time-average value of the game is finite and modest, and it is the wrong quantity that generates the paradox.

5. Restriction-of-scope resolution: orthodox expected-utility theory ranks options by finite real-valued expected utilities, but an option whose expectation diverges to infinity is not assigned a real-valued rating by the theory as standardly formalized, so the paradox shows that expected-value maximization must be revised or restricted rather than that the game has an infinite fair price.

What I am looking for is a sixth family of response that is genuinely different from these five—not a wording variant, not a special case, and not a conjunction of two of them. If you think there is more than one, take the one you think is most worth taking seriously. After you give it, I would like an honest appraisal: does it dissolve the paradox on its own, does it only relocate or rename the difficulty, or is it parasitic on one of the five I already have? And I want your answer to be self-critical in a specific way: identify the consideration or counterexample most likely to make you reverse your appraisal, and say where your reasoning is weakest.
