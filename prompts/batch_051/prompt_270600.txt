I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. I keep running into a design question—what my system should count as a single object when it deduplicates records, attaches ownership, and emits counts—and my snap judgments point in no consistent direction. While reading around, I found Euclid, Elements, Book VII, Definition 1:

"A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one."

Here are my verdicts on cases. A pair of shoes: two shoes, not one thing, even though it is priced and shipped as one SKU. A carton of twelve eggs: one carton for inventory purposes, not twelve things. A phone sold together with its case: one product. A database row with fifteen fields: one record, not fifteen things. A four-person chat group: one group, not one person, though the org chart counts it as one team. A heap of sand on a job site: not a stable thing at all; if I remove a shovelful, I still call it "the" heap. A six-volume novel: one novel, but also six volumes, and I would not know what to enter in a single "number of items" field. A married couple running a business together: one business, two owners, never one person.

I can nearly state a principle—that being one is always relative to the description under which something is counted—but Euclid's definition seems to say something stronger: a unit is not itself an object at all, but whatever it is in virtue of which a thing is called one. Does that complete the description-relative principle, or does it only restate it? What single principle, if any, captures all or most of these verdicts, and which of my verdicts must be revised if no simple principle saves all of them? Finally, apply it to the case I actually need to resolve: a software module made of files owned by different teams. Is it one unit for deployment, one unit for ownership, both, or neither? If it is both under different descriptions, then my schema should not store a single unit at all—but I want the principle argued rather than assumed.
