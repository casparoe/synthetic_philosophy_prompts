A friend who does history keeps insisting that counterfactual tests are what seperates real causes from mere background conditions, and I'm starting to think the view can't even make sense of overdetermination. Here's the argument as I've pieced it together.

(1) To explain an event historically is to cite the factors without which it would not have happened.
(2) If something is a cause of an event, then citing it explains that event.
(3) In a genuine case of overdetermination, two distinct factors each suffice on their own to bring the event about. Imagine a regime falls: the harvest fails, and the treasury is empty, and either alone, given the rest of the circumstances, would have toppled it.
(4) So neither factor is a without-which condition: without the harvest failure the empty treasury would still have brought the fall, and without the empty treasury the harvest failure would still have done so.
(5) By (1), then, neither factor explains the fall.
(6) But by (2) and (3), each factor does explain the fall.
(7) That's a contradiction. So, given (1) and (2), overdetermination cannot occur: anything that looks like overdetermination was either misdescribed or has a unique explanatory cause after all.

Is this argument valid? If it is, which premise should be rejected, and why? If it isn't, which step fails?
