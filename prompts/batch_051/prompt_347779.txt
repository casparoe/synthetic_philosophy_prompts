A friend of mine is finishing a math PhD, and when I complained that topology felt like being handed a list of baffling axioms, he summed up the motivation like this:

"Historically, people defined continuity with epsilons and deltas, which only makes sense when you have a distance function. The key realization was that the distances are doing less work than you'd think: you can rebuild the whole epsilon-delta story from the open balls, and the open balls just tell you which points count as nearby without telling you how near. So mathematicians kept exactly the structure they needed and threw away the rest. A topology is a family of subsets closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections, and the reason those are the axioms is that they're the weakest conditions under which you can prove that a function is continuous iff the preimage of every open set is open. That's all a topology is: the minimal structure that makes the inverse-image definition of continuity work. Which is why topology is really the study of continuity, and why the axioms look so bare."

I don't need the definition explained from scratch — I've taken the course. What I want to know is whether his story about why that definition is the right one is fair. The "minimal structure... and nothing more" part and the claim that topology is really the study of continuity both set off my alarm bells a little, but I can't quite argue it. Which parts would a working mathematician accept, which are subtly off, and does his overall story about why the open-set axioms are chosen hold up?
