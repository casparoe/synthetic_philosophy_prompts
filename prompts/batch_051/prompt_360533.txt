I'm on a small-town budget committee, and we've been going in circles about a one-time surplus. Four proposals survive: rebuilding the footbridge to the elementary school, digitizing the historical society's archive, funding a part-time social worker for a year, and putting in an accessible community garden. Half the committee keeps saying the goods involved are incommensurable and that any scoring rubric is just lying with numbers; the other half says we still have to fund exactly one project. A friend who does deliberative democracy stuff proposed a mechanism to force a hard choice without a cost-benefit analysis. It works like this.

Everybody on the committee gets two protection tokens for the whole process. In each round, any member can move to eliminate one proposal, but they need a second. Before the vote, the mover has to state out loud the main distinct value that proposal serves and why the committee can live without it. If anyone wants to defend that value, they can spend one token to table the elimination for the rest of the round; the defender then has to write a sentence in the public record saying which value they protected and why it should not be weighed against the others. Once all tokens are spent, subsequent eliminations go by plain majority vote with no statements. Last proposal standing gets the money.

What would this actually do to which proposals die first, who spends tokens when, and how the statement ritual changes the debate? I can already see people hoarding tokens for their favorite proposal and attacking ones they see as weak in order to drain other people's protection, and someone is bound to introduce a decoy proposal just to soak up votes. Are those failure modes fixable - say by giving eliminated proposers a fresh token, or by making the statements count formally somehow - without turning the whole thing into exactly the cost-benefit analysis we were trying to avoid?
