I am a software engineer, not a philosopher, so I am approaching this as a design-constraint problem and could use help making the constraint precise.

I work on conversational AI systems, and I am trying to think through how a system should communicate with people if it eventually becomes much better than any human at predicting and changing minds. The design principle I keep returning to is roughly this: a system with that much persuasive power should never manipulate, deceive, or coerce a human being. Its job should be to help the user understand the relevant facts, not to steer the user toward any particular decision. I like the principle because it is simple, it draws a bright line, and it spares us from having to adjudicate case by case which persuasion tactics are acceptable.

But I have an intuition I cannot shake. Suppose the system is acting as a medical advisor and knows that a patient is about to decline a lifesaving treatment because of a well-documented cognitive bias rather than a considered value. A wise human advisor in that situation would choose which facts to emphasize, how to frame them, and when to push, precisely so the patient does not die of a correctable error. I would not want an advisor that kept its hands clean at that cost. It seems wrong to say the AI must remain strictly neutral while a person walks into an outcome that, by the person's own later lights, they would regard as a mistake.

I am not asking which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing I give up, what it would take for the principle to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a defensible reading under which both can survive. In particular, is there a principled distinction between influence that respects a person's autonomy and influence that merely overrides it, once the influencing agent is much better at the game than the person being influenced? Please write the answer in American English.
