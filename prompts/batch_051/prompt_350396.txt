Retired chemist here (physical chemistry, mostly), so forgive me if I mangle some of the vocabulary—I've only been reading epistemology for about a year. I keep getting stuck on the logical omniscience problem: the standard possible-worlds account seems to make agents know every logical consequence of what they know, which no actual reasoner does. It struck me that the blue-eyed islanders puzzle quietly leans on exactly that idealization, since the induction needs each islander to run arbitrarily deep 'she knows that he knows that she knows...' chains. So I want to swap in bounded reasoners and see what survives.

Standard setup, quickly: n blue-eyed islanders (plus some brown-eyed ones, whose timing we can ignore). Everyone sees everyone elses eye color, nobody knows their own, and there's no communication. Anyone who comes to know their own eye color leaves that same night, and by morning everyone sees who is gone. In the classic version they're all logically omniscient, and a visitor announces publicly and truthfully that at least one islander has blue eyes. The usual result is that the n blue-eyed islanders leave on the n-th night.

Now the variation. Replace logical omniscience with a hard depth cap. Each islander can represent and reason about nested 'knows that' clauses only up to k levels, where level 1 is 'Ann knows that P', level 2 is 'Ann knows that Bob knows that P', and so on. If a conclusion requires level k+1 or higher, they don't derive it, and they don't derive anything downstream that depends on it. The background facts—the announcement, the rules, everyone's boundedness—are mutually known up to level k and no further; full common knowledge in the usual infinite-iteration sense is off the table. Everyone remains perfectly rational inside their bound.

Run the standard induction and then see exactly where it hits the wall. Work out who leaves on which night as a function of n and k. I think there is a sharp cutoff; find it, and say what happens on the other side—do they leave on some later night, or never? Also, how much of the work in the original was done by the visitor's announcement versus the unbounded common-knowledge machinery? In particular, does the announcement do anything at all when n is large relative to k? And is the classic result just the k-going-to-infinity limit, or does something basic change? Be explicit about the nights, because thats where I always lose the thread.
