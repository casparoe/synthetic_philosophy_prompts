Since I took over the management of my parents' retirement accounts, I have been struck by a tension in how careful people handle extremely small probabilities. The same person who pays several hundred dollars a year to insure a house against a one-in-five-hundred flood, and who buys a lottery ticket for a one-in-several-million jackpot, will refuse to give a comparable sum to an intervention with a one-in-a-trillion chance of preventing an enormous amount of future suffering, treating the last probability as if it were zero. A version of the same refusal appears even when the threatened harm is personal: many people would not pay a stranger who offered to remove a one-in-a-trillion chance of a catastrophe to their own family, calling the risk negligible. Yet in each case the relevant calculation appears to have the same structure: a small probability is multiplied by a large value, and the resulting expectation is set against alternatives. I am not asking whether the lottery ticket is a good financial decision in any ordinary sense. I am asking whether ordinary practice is entitled to make some tiny probabilities decision-relevant and others not. Give me the strongest principle you can find that would underwrite this asymmetry, test it against adjacent cases—a one-in-ten-thousand chance of saving a thousand lives, a one-in-a-billion risk that is as well-calibrated as an insurer's tables, the same trillion-to-one claim made by a source we trust completely—and then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or replaced by consistent expected-value reasoning, with whatever fanatical conclusions that consistency would carry. If you conclude that only a psychological explanation remains, say so explicitly, but only after showing that the candidate principles fail.
