Lately I've been reading about Han Feizi and the Legalist tradition, after a coworker who knows I like thinking about incentive design recommended it. The part that attracts me is how much it resembles the way I try to build systems at work: don't rely on the good character of the people inside the system, because they will predictably chase what they're rewarded for and avoid what they're punished for. Governance, on this view, should be built around public, fixed law; techniques for checking that officials actually carry it out; and the positional power needed to make rewards and punishments attach to behavior rather than to a ruler's whims. As I understand it, the position was aimed squarely at the Confucian alternative — rule by cultivated virtue, where the ruler's own moral example is supposed to transform the people. Against that, the Legalist point is that sages are rare, and a system that needs one is fragile; a mediocre ruler with well-designed incentives will outperform a virtuous ruler with bad ones.

What bothers me is that I find this almost exactly right, and yet I have an intuition I can't get rid of. If a political order were fully realized along these lines — every relationship between ruler and subject, or manager and worker, mediated by encoded rewards and penalties — something would still be missing. I can't shake the sense that a society worth living in depends on at least some people actually caring about being just or kind, and not merely about avoiding penalties or earning rewards; and that a ruler who never appeals to character is failing at something important, not just choosing a different tool. I don't know whether this is a genuine insight or a sentimental leftover from the very picture the Legalists were attacking.

I don't want a survey of who won the historical debate. I want a negotiated verdict. Give me the strongest form of the Legalist-style position and the strongest form of the intuition that something like cultivated virtue is indispensable. Then tell me what it would actually look like for me to give up the intuition, what it would look like for me to amend or abandon the incentive-only view, and whether there is a reading on which both survive — for example, where virtue is something well-designed incentive systems gradually produce, or where incentive systems can only function against a background of character that they cannot themselves create.
