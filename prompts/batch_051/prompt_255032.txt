I direct the risk-estimation service at a large cancer center. Our team produces a personalized ten-year recurrence estimate for every newly diagnosed breast cancer patient, and that estimate is used in the visit where patient and oncologist decide whether to add chemotherapy; after being signed off by the treating oncologist, it is also printed in the patient portal. We produce several hundred of these estimates a month, each in under twenty minutes, and the staff who assemble them are registry analysts rather than statisticians. The stakes run in both directions: an estimate a few points too low can mean skipping chemotherapy that would have prevented a recurrence, and an estimate a few points too high can mean months of toxicity for little benefit. I have come to believe our central difficulty is the reference class problem. Every patient belongs to many classes at once — the 50–59 age band, the grade-2 group, the one-positive-node group, the ER-positive/HER2-negative group, the group diagnosed in the last decade, the group that completed endocrine therapy — and each class supports a somewhat different recurrence frequency. Our current choice of class is informal and varies with whichever oncologist is covering the service.

Please write a decision procedure our analysts could actually follow to choose, combine, or refuse to use a reference class when producing one of these estimates. It should pose the questions in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and specify whether the procedure ends in a single number, a range, or a refusal to give a number. It should be usable by staff with no formal training in statistics or philosophy: the distinctions that matter should survive as plain tests and rules, not jargon. In particular, tell us how to recognize when the largest and most precisely estimated class is nonetheless the wrong class for this patient, and how to handle classes too small to estimate reliably. Mark the steps where the procedure hands the decision to a person because the step is a judgment call, and be honest about what the procedure cannot settle. Then run the procedure on the following three cases, and identify any output your procedure would force us to report or act on that our clinicians will want to resist.

Case A is a 52-year-old woman with ER-positive, HER2-negative, grade 2 disease and one positive sentinel node. The largest classes she falls into point in different directions: her age band suggests a low recurrence number, the node-positive class suggests a moderately high one, and the class of patients who completed five years of endocrine therapy suggests a lower one still. Case B is a 68-year-old man with ER-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer; the class of male patients is tiny, and every other available class is defined almost entirely by women. Case C is a 44-year-old woman with a rare molecular subtype for whom the only adequately sized registry class was treated under a regimen we no longer use. For each, state the decision the procedure produces and note where the procedure leaves room for oncologist override.
