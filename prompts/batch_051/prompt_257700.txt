In my philosophy of science seminar, a classmate has taken to ending every debate about methodology by saying "anything goes." Last week I raised a question about whether there really is such a thing as the scientific method — whether anything actually holds together the mix of experimentation, modeling, statistical inference, and field observation that gets lumped together as "science" — and he replied that Feyerabend settled this long ago: "anything goes," so the question is pointless. I have the feeling this is too quick, but I can't yet say exactly what the slogan is claiming.

I want to be able to lay out, for discussion, the different precise theses that "anything goes" could be expressing. It might mean that no methodological rule holds across all scientific contexts; that every proposed rule has documented exceptions; that there is no principled line between science and non-science; that scientists do, as a matter of historical fact, use whatever works; that they should use whatever works; that any method is as good as any other; or something else entirely. For each candidate reading, I'd like to know whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or outright false, and whether it actually supports dismissing the question of whether there is a scientific method. I'm especially interested in which readings, if any, make the slogan self-undermining — for example, if "anything goes" is itself offered as a universal rule about method — and whether the readings that turn out true end up being too weak to shut down the original question.

Please end with a single sentence beginning "Bottom line:" that states which reading of the slogan, if any, would actually justify treating the question of whether there is a scientific method as settled.
