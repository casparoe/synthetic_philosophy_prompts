I am a retired research scientist — I spent my career in the physical sciences — and I have only recently begun reading philosophy and legal theory seriously. I have been working through material on promises, consent, and contractual obligation, and I keep coming back to a question I would once have thought too lawyerly to matter: whether clicking "I agree" on a website is genuinely agreement, or only the mechanical recording of a click.

In a recent issue of a general-interest law magazine, a practising solicitor wrote a short article arguing that it is not. Her summary of the case, as I recall it, ran roughly as follows. Nobody reads the terms and conditions, and everyone — including the companies that draft them — knows that nobody reads them. The terms are fixed unilaterally by one party, with no negotiation, and the user's choice is take-it-or-leave-it in practice, because every rival platform imposes materially the same terms. The button may say "I agree," but a recorded click is evidence only that a mouse was pressed or a screen tapped; it tells us nothing about whether the person understood, or even noticed, what the terms required. Since a contract requires a meeting of the minds, it follows that most clickwrap agreements are not contracts at all. Courts enforce them, she concluded, only because judges have been too ready to treat a click as the legal equivalent of a handshake.

I suspect much of this is broadly right — I confess I have never read a terms-of-service document in my life — but I have read enough philosophy to wonder whether the conclusion outruns the premises.

I would also like a view on how much of the argument, if sound, is about clickwrap specifically and how much is really about adhesion contracts in general.
