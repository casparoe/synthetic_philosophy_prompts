I'm a retired analytical chemist (retired three years now) and I've recently fallen down the philosophy-of-law rabbit hole, specifically how defamation law seperates statements into facts and opinions. In my old line of work you were always careful to distinguish the measured value from the inference you drew from it, so this looks to me like the same problem wearing a suit.

I've collected my gut calls on a bunch of cases, and I'm trying to figure out whether there's one principle behind all of them or just a vibe.

- A restaurant critic writes that the soup at a famously expensive place "tasted like dishwater with a cigarette in it." Fine, obviously. Nobody should get to sue over that, even if the owner loses business.
- A sell-side analyst writes that a pharma company's lead drug "will fail its phase III trial because the primary endpoint is mis-specified." If the drug succeeds and the stock soars, I still wouldn't let the company sue over the failed forecast. Fine.
- A credit rating agency downgrades a bank from A to BBB and writes that the bank is "overexposed to commercial real estate, at 40% of book." Suppose the 40% figure is wrong and the agency should have known. The bank's stock drops. Not fine. The rating is a judgment, but the underlying false number is still a false fact, and publishing it caused real damage.
- A magazine profile says a scientist "is not a serious researcher and, specifically, misrepresented the control conditions in her 2019 study." If the misrepresentation claim is false and the reporter didn't check it, not fine, even though "not a serious researcher" is loose evaluative talk on its own.
- A website just aggregates student ratings of professors and displays "Prof. M averages 1.7 out of 5 from 200 reviews." Fine, even though Prof. M hates it and thinks the reviews are unfair. The site is reporting what people rated, not rating him itself.
- An anonymous data shop publishes "There is a 70% chance that Congresswoman Y took bribes from a developer," with no method, no dataset, nothing. Not fine. That isn't a probability; it's an accusation wearing a decimal point.

And now the one I keep going back and forth on. A reputable analytics firm, one that actually publishes its models, calculates from a landlord's rental records that there is a 72% probability he discriminated against Black applicants. They publish the score, the model, and the residuals. Some days I think this is just a fancier version of the pharma forecast, which I called fine. Other days I think it's the anonymous "70% chance she took bribes" with better PR.

So: is there a principle that gets all six of the cases above (or nearly all), stated precisely? What does it say about the landlord discrimination score? And which of my gut calls, if any, does it force me to give up? I'd rather have a clean principle that overturns one of my calls than a gerrymandered one that saves them all.
