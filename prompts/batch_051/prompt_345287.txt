I've been trying to nail down what I actually think about punishing people who didn't mean to do the harm, and I've noticed my gut gives different answers to cases that look superficially similar. Having read a bunch of rationalist-adjacent arguments about desert, deterrence, and moral luck, I keep flipping between "blameworthiness is about what you could have controlled" and "the law needs strict liability sometimes, otherwise everyone just says oops." I'd like someone to extract the principle from my verdicts, if there is one, and tell me honestly which verdict, if any, the principle forces me to drop.

Here are the cases. (1) A guy gets drunk, drives home, and kills a pedestrian. He never intended to hurt anyone, but I think prison is right. (2) A careful pharmacist with a clean record makes a single dispensing error one afternoon—wrong vial, right label, patient dies. I don't think she should go to prison; losing her license or facing a civil suit seems fine. (3) A baby food company follows its testing protocols, but a supplier secretly sends contaminated ingredients and a baby dies. I'm fine with a big fine against the company but would be horrified if the CEO went to prison. (4) A man in a psychotic episode attacks a stranger because he believes the stranger is about to kill him. I don't want him punished, but I'm fine detaining and treating him while he's dangerous. (5) A person sleepwalks and strangles their spouse. I don't think any punishment is appropriate, and I'm not even sure about detention there. (6) A delivery driver misses a stop sign in an unfamiliar neighborhood and kills a cyclist; he wasn't drunk or texting, just a momentary lapse. My gut says no prison, maybe a short license suspension depending on his record. (7) A factory owner subcontracts waste disposal to a licensed company that secretly dumps the waste instead; the owner did due diligence and knew nothing. Fine the company, don't imprison the owner.

The seeming pattern is something like: punishment tracks whether the person chose a dangerous course of conduct, not merely whether the outcome was bad, and strict liability is okay for fines but not for blame-flavored punishment. But cases 2 and 6 both involve a one-off lapse of attention with a death, and I want neither punished, while case 1 involves no intent either and I do want prison—so the line can't just be "did they intend the harm." Is there one coherent principle behind all seven verdicts, or is one of them the odd one out? Separately, what does the principle say about a self-driving car whose sensor fails in an unforeseen way and kills someone: does anyone deserve punishment (the manufacturer, the safety driver, the company), or is that a case where liability should be purely civil? And should I count the company fine in case 3 as punishment at all, or as a price for engaging in a risky activity?
