The exam question was:

Both the Daodejing and the Zhuangzi treat wu-wei as central, but they are clearly not recommending idleness. Explain the paradox of trying to attain wu-wei, and discuss how the texts suggest one is supposed to reach it. Refer to at least one concrete story or image (for example, Cook Ding in the Zhuangzi), and distinguish wu-wei from mere passivity.

The student wrote:

Wu-wei literally means non-action, but most interpreters gloss it as effortless action or non-coercive action rather than doing nothing. The paradox is that the attempt to become wu-wei is itself a form of wei, that is, deliberate effort, so the whole project seems self-defeating: the harder you strain to be spontaneous, the less spontaneous you are. Both Laozi and Zhuangzi resolve this in basically the same way, by telling us to stop trying. Once you really give up your aims, effortless action is supposed to arise on its own, which is why the sage is described as not planning or deliberating. This is also what the story of Cook Ding is about: Ding does not rely on skill or technique, he just follows the natural grain of the ox, and that is why his knife never dulls. The paradox is resolved because renouncing effort is not itself an effort — it removes rather than adds. This also shows why wu-wei differs from passivity: a passive person is still refusing to act or avoiding something, whereas the wu-wei person acts fully, just without forcing a self-conscious agenda onto the situation.
