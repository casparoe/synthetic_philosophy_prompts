I am a policy adviser to the mayor of a midsized city in a country where corruption in public contracting is common, tax evasion is widespread and widely tolerated, and surveys regularly find that most residents say strangers cannot be trusted and public services are poor by default. The mayor, who trained as an economist, wants to introduce a well-run, universal preschool program: free for every family regardless of income, funded by a broad and highly visible property tax, and free of the means-testing and paperwork that burden our current assistance for the poor. Her case is that the current targeted programs split the city into payers and recipients; a universal program, she argues, gives middle-class families their own stake in the service, makes it obvious what their taxes buy, and over time can teach people to expect public institutions to deliver. My colleague in the finance office thinks this is backwards. In a low-trust place, he says, residents will not believe the program will be good, will resent paying for other people's children, and will evade the tax more aggressively; the trust has to exist before such a program, not after it.

The mayor wants a recommendation, and both of them want it concrete: is the mayor's confidence in a universal program as a builder of trust well-founded, is the finance director's fear of a backlash better supported, and which features of the program's design, staging, or communication would matter most for whether it moves the city toward a self-reinforcing cycle of compliance and improving services rather than a self-reinforcing cycle of skepticism and evasion?
