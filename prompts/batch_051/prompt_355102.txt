I've been running a small reading group with some friends from the local rationalist community, and after a long detour through possible-worlds semantics and structured propositions, we've stalled out on the unity problem. Here is the trouble as I now understand it. Suppose a proposition is not an unstructured set of worlds and not a primitive object, but a complex whose constituents — objects, properties, relations — must be combined in the right way for the whole to be about something and to be capable of truth or falsity. The moment you posit an instantiation relation to do the combining, a hostile question arises: what combines that instantiation relation with the constituents it was meant to combine? Whatever does the binding seems to require yet another binder, and so on without obvious end. The stock responses to Bradley's regress seem to fall into two camps. On one, the regress is vicious: it shows that no relational constituent can explain unity, so unity must instead be supplied by a primitive, operation-like binding that does not stand to what it binds as one more constituent. On the other, the regress is benign: once a relation is in place, its relating its terms is not an additional fact standing in need of a further relation, and the demand for one rests on a confusion about what relations are for.

I would like a dialogue between two invented philosophers, Elara and Kiran, at a workshop on the metaphysics of propositions. Elara defends the first camp and argues that the only stable stopping point is a primitive binding operation; Kiran defends the second and argues that the regress dissolves once you stop treating relating as an extra constituent. The dialogue should involve real back-and-forth, with each character first presenting their opponent's best version of the objection before answering it, and neither should be allowed to win by fiat. Make them push each other on at least the following: whether a primitive binding operation is actually distinct from an unanalyzable instantiation relation, or is merely the old relation renamed; what, if anything, distinguishes a fact in which a relation relates its terms from a mere set containing the relation and its terms; and whether the regress looks different when the item to be unified is a proposition rather than a state of affairs. End the dialogue after both characters have been forced to articulate precisely which explanatory demand a theory of propositional unity must meet, even if neither has converted the other.
