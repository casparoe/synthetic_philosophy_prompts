I have a rule about uncertain evidence. If I can't attach a likelihood ratio to a piece of evidence — how much more probable the evidence is under the claim than under its denial — I don't update on it at all, I just act like I never saw it. Last month a friend said she was about 60% sure she'd seen our old classmate at a rally, but when I pressed her she couldn't say how likely that sighting was if he really had been there verses if he hadn't. So I didn't budge my belief about whether he was there. My friend says this is absurb: a blurry photo, a half-remembered conversation, an uncertain ID are still information, and treating them as zero information is itself a substantive claim I'm not entitled to. I've read about Jeffrey conditionalization, and I get that it's supposed to handle uncertain evidence by shifting probabilities across a partition, but it seems to need numbers I can't honestly supply. So: is my rule defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle? If it's wrong, what's the right fallback when I genuinely can't quantify the evidence, short of pretending to have numbers I don't have?
