Locke's memory criterion makes a person at a later time identical with a person at an earlier time only insofar as the later consciousness reaches back to the earlier person's thoughts and actions through memory. Reid's brave-officer objection targets the logic of that relation: an old general remembers capturing an enemy standard as a young officer, and the young officer remembers being flogged at school as a boy, but the old general does not remember being flogged as a boy. Since identity is transitive but the memory relation is not, the criterion seems to force the result that the general both is and is not the boy.

Now add a development that was not available to the original debate. Memory research now indicates that episodic remembering is reconstructive rather than archival: each retrieval reactivates the trace and restabilizes it by reconsolidation, in a form that may incorporate information acquired after the remembered event. No untouched record of the earlier episode remains deposited in the mind to be played back; every memory link is partly a product of the intervening history between the event and the remembering.

Does Locke's memory criterion survive this second finding? Address the argument itself rather than announcing a verdict. Separate the two objections by the premise each one presses on: the brave officer presses on the transitivity of the memory relation, while reconsolidation presses on the stability and fidelity of each individual memory link. Assess whether the criterion can be repaired into overlapping chains of causally connected memory traces, and, if it can, whether the reconstructive character of memory makes the links in any such chain too labile and indeterminate to do the work of identity. Consider openly whether the strongest repaired view ends up locating personal identity in the continuity of the rewriting process itself rather than in any remembered content, and whether at that point the view has quietly ceased to be a memory criterion in Locke's sense.
