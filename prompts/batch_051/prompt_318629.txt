I've been caring for my dad since his frontotemporal dementia diagnosis, and something about it keeps getting tangled up with moral-responsibility stuff I used to read. The criteria I was taught for being an appropriate target of the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, forgiveness) are roughly: (1) the person can have intentions toward you, (2) they're responsive to reasons, including your demands and expectations, and (3) there's enough continuity of self that the person you resent is the person who did the thing. Here's the borderline case. Some days dad is lucid: he apologizes for snapping at me, says he hates being a burden, and seems to genuinely grasp that he shouldn't accuse me of things. Other days he's convinced I've been stealing from him, says things I cant repeat, and an hour later has no memory of any of it. In the clear spells he meets all three criteria; in the bad spells he fails all three, fairly cleanly.

So here's what I'm asking. Is dad still an appropriate target of reactive attitudes, or only on the lucid days? Does resentment track the person across the fluctuating episodes, or would it have to be re-earned each clear period (which feels absurd)? If he apologizes on Tuesday for something he did Monday and doesn't remember, is that forgiveness in anything like the normal sense, or is it something else, maybe just me deciding to drop the matter? And the bigger question: does this case show the criteria were wrong from the start? Maybe the whole participant stance — treating someone as a person to be held responsible rather than an object to be managed — isnt really about their capacities at all, but about being in an ongoing relationship, and capacity talk is just a proxy. Work through the criteria one by one and tell me where the framework breaks, or whether it survives this case.
