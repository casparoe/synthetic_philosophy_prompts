I have been thinking on my own about whether a harm prevented decades from now should count as less important than the same harm prevented today, just because it is later; the question keeps surfacing in family arguments about how much present sacrifice is justified to avoid slow-building environmental damage. In one of those arguments, my brother cut off my claim — that a fixed amount of future suffering matters no less than the same amount of suffering now, unless something other than its timing changes the calculation — by saying "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush," as if that settled the matter. I suspect that proverb is quietly doing several different jobs at once, and that only some of them would actually support his conclusion. Lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making about how much future goods and harms weigh against present ones, arranged from the trivial or merely descriptive to the radical or normative. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false or confused. Then say which of those readings would actually license my brother's move and which would not, and whether the proverb is doing any real work by specifying two birds rather than just saying an uncertain future bird is worth less.
