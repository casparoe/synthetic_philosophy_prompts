I write for a magazine about science and ideas, and for the past couple of weeks I've been trying to get a feature off the ground about moral worth: whether someone has to do a good thing for the right reasons for the action to reflect well on them. I pitched my editor two cases to show why it's messier than it sounds. The first: a woman donates a kidney to a stranger while a camera crew is in the room, and she's mostly thinking about how generous she'll look; the transplant goes ahead and saves a life. The second: a woman arranges the same donation quietly, with what genuinely seem to be the right reasons, but she's found medically unsuitable at the last minute and the surgery never happens. My editor listened for about ten seconds, said 'well, it's the thought that counts', and moved on to budgets.

His gloss, as far as I could tell, was that the inner state of mind is what matters, so it shouldn't matter much that one donation saved a life and the other never happened — and maybe that the self-conscious motive of the first woman is beside the point too. I think that sentence is smuggling several different claims at once, and the word 'thought' is doing a lot of blurry work. Before I go back to him, I want to seperate the readings.

Could you lay out exactly five distinct theses a person could be asserting when they say 'it's the thought that counts' in a debate about moral worth? Put them in order from least to most radical. For each, in a sentence or two, say what the claim amounts to, whether it's plausible, and whether it actually supports my editor's quick dismissal of the outcome difference in my two cases. No introduction or conclusion.
