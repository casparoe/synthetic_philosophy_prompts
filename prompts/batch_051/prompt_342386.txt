I'm a political theory PhD student, and my transitional justice reading group has spent the last three weeks stalled on the amnesty-versus-prosecution debate, and it's starting to feel like the impasse itself is the interesting object. The same fault line keeps re-forming. One camp, mostly the South Africa people, argues that the TRC's conditional amnesty—full disclosure in exchange for immunity—wasn't a compromise of justice but a different mode of it: truth-telling as a form of accountability, public acknowledgment to victims, a way to name perpetrators when trials would have been selective and maybe impossible. The other camp, mostly the Argentina and Rwanda people, keeps insisting that treating amnesty as anything other than a tolerated evil turns punishment into a policy variable that can be traded away against reconciliation, which corrupts the very idea of accountability: if prosecutions are owed, bargaining them away is a wrong, not a price.

What's weird is that nobody in the room disputes the basics facts—the scale of the violence, that the South African process surfaced things trials wouldn't have, that Argentina's prosecutions were frozen and then unfrozen, that Rwanda's gacaca courts were a makeshift attempt to process enormous caseloads. And we all agree that victims' dignity and truth matter. Yet nobody moves. My own hunch is that the real disagreement isn't about how much punishment is enough, or about the empirical effects of amnesty, but about whether justice is the sort of thing one weighs—a defeasible value that can be traded against other goods—or whether the response to mass atrocity involves duties whose failure can't be offset by epistemic goods like truth. But I might be misdiagnosing it: the impasse could be a hidden empirical disagreement about whether amnesty actually buys truth and prevents recurrence; a disagreement about whose justice is at stake, the individual survivor or the polity; or a difference about whether reconciliation is even a legitimate political aim rather than a euphemism for letting culprits off.

I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a verdict on whether the amnesty bargain was right, and not a survey of the transitional justice literature. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction people haven't drawn, a change of question, a specific comparative case, an empirical finding—or should we expect this particular standoff to survive any amount of new evidence no matter what?
