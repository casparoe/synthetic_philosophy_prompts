During my working life as a research scientist I employed dispositional language constantly and unreflectively: solubility, brittleness, catalytic activity, thermal stability, and a great many other properties were, as I then would have put it, just propensities to behave in certain ways under certain conditions. Since retiring I have begun to read philosophy of science and metaphysics, and I have found myself settling into a view that now strikes me as both natural and perhaps naive. The view is this: to ascribe a disposition is just to assert a counterfactual conditional. To say that a material is soluble is to say that it would dissolve if placed in a suitable solvent; to say that a sample is brittle is to say that it would fracture if struck in an appropriate way; and so on. On this picture, disposition talk is a compact way of expressing how things would respond across a range of relevant interventions, and a full statement of the disposition just is a full statement of those conditionals.

I would like you to tell me, honestly and in an organized way, what counts against this view. I am aware that philosophers have named certain kinds of counterexample -- I have come across talk of finks, masks, antidotes, and mimics -- but what I want is not a list of labels. I want a map of the position's exposure. Specifically:

1. Which of these cases (or which other cases I have not encountered) come closest to being fatal to the simple conditional analysis, and which are merely awkward? I want them ranked from decisive to minor, with the reasons for the ranking made explicit.

2. What, if anything, does the view get right that survives the counterexamples? Is there a repaired version -- one that appeals to normal conditions, ceteris paribus clauses, intrinsic causal bases, or a distinction between a disposition and its manifestation -- that a committed defender should retreat to, or do the repairs amount to abandoning the original insight?

3. As someone whose sense of disposition talk was formed in the laboratory, I am especially interested in two kinds of case. First, the case where we confidently ascribe a disposition even though no triggering condition is ever realized (a material that is never actually put in the relevant solvent; a structure that is never actually loaded to failure). Second, the case where we hold a disposition ascription fixed while intervening on the very categorical properties that arguably make the counterfactual true. What, if anything, do these facts about scientific practice show about the analysis?

4. Most important: I want to know whether the conditional analysis, as elaborated by its defenders, has been insulated against refutation. If every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by adding a further qualification about "suitable conditions" or "normal circumstances," is the view still risking anything? In the sciences I regarded a hypothesis that could not be made to fail as a bad hypothesis. Is that the situation the conditional analysis finds itself in? Or does it have genuine empirical and conceptual content against which the finks, masks, and related cases can genuinely cut?

Please give me a single, structured assessment: a ranking of the defeaters, a statement of which ones a thoughtful defender of the conditional analysis would themselves concede as counting against the view, and a verdict on whether the right conclusion is to abandon the analysis, to repair it, or to accept that disposition ascriptions cannot be reduced to counterfactual conditionals at all.
