I have spent long enough defending the autarky ideal in the classroom that it is awkward to admit how torn I am about its central claim. On one side stands a theory with real systematic virtues: the view that a life is going well only insofar as one's happiness depends on what is within one's control supplies a single criterion for deciding where to direct concern, unifies the virtues as ways of becoming invulnerable to fortune, and promises a stability that no arrangement depending on health, wealth, reputation, or the affections of others can provide. On the other side stands an intuition I cannot let go of: in the best friendships and loves, one's well-being is partly placed in another person's hands, and that exposure is not a regrettable vulnerability to be minimized but a constituent of what makes the relationship worth having; a life arranged so that no one's absence could touch one's happiness seems to me not secure but impoverished. The familiar reconciliation says that the wise person loves and values the other without needing her, because virtue alone is good and the beloved is a thing to be preferred but not depended upon. I suspect this is a redescription of love that preserves the word while conceding the point. I would like an adjudication: does the autarky theory, properly understood, actually contradict the intuition, or only one philosophical gloss on it? If they do conflict, what would justify amending the theory, debunking the intuition, or finding an interpretation on which they are compatible, and what would each move cost?
