I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the moral force of everyday conventions — chiefly tipping, queueing, and jaywalking, but also a few cases nearby. Please take my verdicts, find the principle or small set of principles that captures them, state each candidate precisely, and then tell me which of my verdicts I have to abandon, if any. I want the principle to face the facts, not the facts to be forced into the principle.

1. Tipping at a sit-down restaurant in the United States. I leave 15–20%, even for mediocre service. I think this is obligatory, not merely customary: the server's income depends on it, and the menu is priced on the assumption that the tip completes the payment.

2. Tipping in Japan. I do not tip, and I think I am right not to. But my reason is not just that locals don't tip. It is that, there, not tipping does not deprive a worker of expected income in the way it does in the United States.

3. A tourist from a no-tipping country dines in the U.S. and leaves only the face value of the bill. If they genuinely did not know about tipping, I do not regard them as blameworthy. But if the practice is explained to them and they still refuse to tip, I think they do something wrong — and the wrongness has nothing to do with which country they are from.

4. Queueing. In a city where the bus stop operates by a strict first-come, first-served line, someone who pushes in ahead of people who have been waiting does something wrong. But in a place where boarding is an accepted crush without lines, I do not think anyone who shoulders toward the door wrongs anyone, and I do not hold myself to forming a line no one else recognizes.

5. Jaywalking. In my own city, I cross against the light on an empty street and think nothing of it. I would not cross against the light through busy traffic; that seems wrong because it endangers others and models recklessness. In a city with strictly enforced anti-jaywalking rules, as a tourist I comply — but I take my compliance to be prudence, not a moral requirement.

6. Shoes at the door. Invited to a home in a country where shoes are always removed at the door, I remove mine. A visitor who refused would be rude, but I would not call it a moral failure the way shorting a tipped worker is; nothing and no one is directly harmed or deprived.

7. A convention that marks someone as lesser. Suppose I am visiting a place where, at meals, men are served first as a standing custom. I would not go along with it — if I were among the people served last, I would not accept the arrangement, and if I were among those served first, I would decline the priority. I do not think a visitor is morally required to comply with a convention whose point seems to be to mark a group's lower standing. But I also think the visitor is badly wrong who treats every local practice as optional and loudly makes an exception of himself, without regard for the relationships in the room.

Is there one principle behind all of this? In stating it, please address directly whether a visitor is ever bound by a local convention as such, or only by what the convention does in that place. I genuinely cannot tell whether the binding force of a convention comes from what it does for people — income, safety, the fair allocation of scarce space — or from the bare fact that it is the recognized local practice. Please work that out from the cases.

Then apply the principle to the case I keep going back and forth on. A sit-down restaurant in a U.S. city that has abolished the tipped minimum wage, with a 20% service charge the menu says goes to staff and an optional tip line below it. I leave the tip line blank and feel no guilt. A local friend says that by local standards anything less than an additional tip shortchanges the server, and a visitor from a tipping country would probably add another 15%. What does the principle say about whether I should tip, and whether the visitor should? And if the principle cannot accommodate all seven of my verdicts, tell me which one it overturns and why that is the one to lose.
