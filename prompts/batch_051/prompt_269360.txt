For a piece on whether the science of pleasure is converging or fragmenting, I want your actual credence — a number between 0 and 1 — in the following proposition: every experience correctly classified as pleasant, from the taste of sugar and the relief of escaping danger to the satisfaction of finishing a proof, the warmth of a loved one's voice, and the pride after winning an award, shares a single phenomenal property, hedonic tone, in virtue of which it counts as pleasant, and this property is a natural kind rather than a family-resemblance label stretched over a motley set of mechanisms. Decompose the estimate. I would like at least four components: the probability that bodily and attitudinal pleasures share a common feeling-quality; the probability that the affective-neuroscience distinction between liking and wanting tracks that shared feeling-quality rather than a confound; the probability that a simple feeling-tone theory of pleasure is correct rather than a functional or attitudinal theory; and the probability that, if such a shared property exists, it will turn out to be one neurobiological kind. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, show how you combine them, and identify the term to which your final credence is most sensitive. I put myself around 0.35 on the proposition; a philosopher I have been interviewing puts herself at 0.8. Where is the most likely locus of that gap, and what single empirical or conceptual finding would move your own estimate the most?
