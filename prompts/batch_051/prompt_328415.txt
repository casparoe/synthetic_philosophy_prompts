My friend Dana keeps a notepad of one-word verdicts on which systems she thinks are morally considerable, by which she means capable of actually suffering rather than just producing behavior that happens to read as pain. Her record so far: a thermostat, no; a 300-layer feedforward image classifier, no; a live rat, yes; a neuron-by-neuron simulation of an entire mouse brain running on a supercomputer with a simple body in a virtual environment, yes; the classic Blockhead set-up, where a lookup table reproduces that same simulation's exact outputs for every possible input sequence, no; a person in a warehouse hand-executing the same mouse-brain algorithm with paper and pencil, considered as a whole system, yes, even though the person doing the arithmetic feels none of it; and a slime mold solving a maze, no.

Next week I'm supposed to ask her about three cases she hasn't ruled on, and I want to get ahead of her answer. First, a high-fidelity simulation of the full 302-neuron C. elegans nervous system, wired to a simulated worm body. Second, just the pain subnetwork of the mouse simulation — the part whose firing tracks pain in the full sim — run detatched from the rest of the simulated brain and body, with only internal noise for input. Third, a recurrent network that was randomly mutated in a lab until it reliably avoided virtual damage and showed the usual pain-avoidance behaviors, but shares no architectural details with any real animal nervous system.

What do you think she'll say about each? More than the raw predictions, its the rule she's running that I'm after. Give me the rule that fits her old verdicts best, at least one other rule that fits them about as well but gives different answers on one or more of the three new cases, and the further case you'd run by her to most cleanly separate those rules.
