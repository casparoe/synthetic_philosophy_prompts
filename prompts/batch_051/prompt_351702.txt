Hume, in section X of his Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, offers this criterion for when a report of a miracle should command belief: "If the falsehood of his testimony would be more miraculous, than the event which he relates; then, and not till then, can he pretend to command my belief or opinion."

But the case I want to think about is not a bare report. A patient in an ICU is declared dead after forty minutes of flat EEG, no pulse, and no respiratory effort. The room is under continuous video recording, and the monitors log to hospital servers. A chaplain prays over the body; at the end of the prayer the EEG trace resumes, and the patient subsequently sits up and speaks. Hospital access logs record no one entering or leaving during the interval. Independent forensic video analysts and biomedical engineers later find no signs of tampering, editing, or equipment failure in the recordings or the monitor logs.

Does Hume's criterion govern this case? If the video and machine records are treated as a kind of testimony, does the "falsehood of his testimony" alternative carry the same weight it carries for a single eyewitness? Or does the fact that the evidence is multichannel and machine-generated change the comparison? More fundamentally, should a Humean conclude here that the patient was not really dead, and that the previous judgment of death was a mistake, rather than that a law of nature was violated? Argue from the text of the quoted passage, from the purpose of Hume's argument, and from what Hume would likely say if asked about this case. Then say which of those approaches should decide and why.
