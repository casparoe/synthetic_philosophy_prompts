I want a criterion for when a merely local convention gives a visitor a genuine moral obligation, rather than only a reason of prudence or politeness. By a local convention I mean a practice that is widely followed and expected in a place—tipping, queuing, dress, gestures of respect—but that the visitor has not consented to, and may not share. The cases below are meant to force the criterion to distinguish three things: what is morally required, what is merely good manners, and what a visitor may in some cases be required not to do. Some of my verdicts are firm, some are guesses, and some conflict with what I would do in practice; where that happens, tell me which to abandon.

1. A tourist in the United States, aware that servers are legally paid below the ordinary minimum wage on the assumption that tips make up the difference, leaves five percent at a full-service restaurant. I take this to be wrong, and not merely rude.

2. The same tourist gives an unusually large cash tip at a restaurant in Japan, where gratuities are not expected and are often returned or interpreted as a minor embarrassment. I do not think this is a serious wrong, but I also think it shows a failure to be a good guest. I want to know whether that failure is moral at all.

3. In a country known for strict queuing, a visitor walks to the front of a long line at a post office, knowing what he is doing; he simply thinks queueing is an irrational local obsession. I take this to be wrong, at least partly because the people behind him reasonably relied on their places.

4. In a city where jaywalking is strongly disapproved, a visitor crosses against the light on a completely empty street at two in the morning. No traffic is present, and I do not know whether anyone observes him. I have no stable view about whether he does anything wrong, or whether being observed should matter.

5. A non-Muslim woman enters a mosque as a tourist; covering her head is a condition of entry and a local sign of respect. She is an atheist who finds the requirement arbitrary, but covers her hair to avoid giving offense. Is covering her hair a moral requirement once she chooses to enter, merely an admission fee, or something in between?

6. At a dinner in a community where it is customary for women and men to sit separately, a visitor is seated apart from her male colleagues. She finds the custom demeaning and asks whether she is obligated to comply, obligated to decline the invitation rather than comply, or entitled to insist on sitting with her colleagues. Here I suspect compliance may actually be wrong, and want the criterion to explain why this differs from the mosque case.

7. A local resident cuts a queue in his own town, where the norm is strict; a visitor cuts the same queue with the same knowledge and the same effects. Is the local's act worse? If it is, state what that shows about whether the duty to follow a local convention rests on membership, on reliance by others, or on something else.

Propose the criterion—it may be disjunctive—and run it through all seven cases. Then give your least secure verdict, and say whether you think the contrast between moral obligation and mere convention survives the cases or collapses into a difference of degree in how strongly others' expectations and legitimate interests weigh.
