I am currently taking a module on epistemic injustice, and I have been collecting my own reactions to cases in which people are or are not believed. I would like to know whether any single principle captures them, or whether some of my verdicts have to be revised.

(a) A doctor assumes, on the basis of a racial stereotype, that a patient is exaggerating her pain and therefore withholds adequate pain relief. I take this to be a clear case of a credibility deficit and a clear injustice.

(b) A woman tries to explain a pattern of humiliating treatment by her manager to colleagues at a time when the vocabulary of sexual harassment has not yet taken hold in her workplace. They do not exactly disbelieve her; they lack a shared way of making her experience intelligible as a wrong. I think she suffers an injustice, but it does not feel like the same kind as (a).

(c) A seminar tutor gives an upper-class student more credence than he gives a working-class student who has made the same point. I think this is wrong, but the wrong seems to have two faces: the working-class student is under-heard, while the upper-class student's weak arguments go unchecked. Yet I hesitate to say the upper-class student has been treated unjustly.

(d) A patient with a heavily stigmatised diagnosis does not report new symptoms because she expects to be disbelieved. Nobody actually disbelieves her. I still want to say she has suffered an epistemic injustice, but if the injustice consists in a hearer deflating a speaker's credibility, this case does not fit.

(e) A teacher does not believe a child who reports abuse by a well-liked colleague, not because of any view about children as a group, but because the report cannot be reconciled with the teacher's settled picture of the colleague. I think the child is wronged, but the source of the failure feels different from (a).

Is there one principle about credibility, intelligibility, or participation in shared understanding that accommodates all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them? What does that principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: someone who is believed, but only after he has had to present his complaint in the idiom of the dominant group, which he experiences as a kind of self-estrangement? If no tidy principle captures every verdict, tell me which of my verdicts it would be least costly to overturn and why.
