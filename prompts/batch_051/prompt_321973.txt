You're a philosopher of mind who works on the unity of consciousness. I want you to think carefully about this, and start by brainstorming a couple of possible readings before you commit. Take the actual split-brain experiments — the commissurotomy patients from the 1960s onward, where a picture flashed to one hemisphere can't be verbally reported by the other, and the left hemisphere confabulates a story when the right hemisphere makes the left hand act on something the left hemisphere never saw. I know the basic facts, so don't re-explain the surgery to me. Analyze that episode through the lens of the unity thesis: the claim that a subject's simultaneous experiences are co-conscious, jointly attendable, and all belong to a single subject. What does that thesis predict or rule out in these cases, what does it handle fine, and where does it strain or just go silent? Be disciplined about the mapping — who or what counts as the subject, what counts as evidence of disunity, and wich reports you should trust. Especially: is the left-hemisphere confabulation evidence of two streams, or is it a post-hoc interpretation that makes disunity look cleaner than it is? Then run it the other direction: do the split-brain cases refute the unity thesis, are they an anomaly it can absorb, or do they show that unity isn't a fact we discover but something a cognitive system actively constructs — and could in principle construct twice? Commit to a position at the end, and say plainly where the unity thesis has nothing useful to say.
