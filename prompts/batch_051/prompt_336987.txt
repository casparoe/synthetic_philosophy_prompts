I got into an argument with a friend about self-sufficiency, and I tried to make my side into a tidy little argument. It goes like this:

(1) Self-sufficiency, if it means anything, means not depending on other people for what you need.
(2) Everyone depends on other people for what they need, at least across a whole life and at least in the background—the clothes on your back, the language you think in, the machines and knowledge that built the room you're standing in.
(3) So no one is self-sufficient except in tiny, temporary pockets.
(4) The ideal of self-sufficiency is supposed to be livable at least in approximation, not a state that's strictly impossible.
(5) So the ideal is incoherent: it tells us to move toward a state of zero dependence that nobody could ever occupy.

My friend told me the argument only works because I'm sliding between two senses of "depending on"—one sense being causally enabled or produced by others, the other being subject to someone's arbitrary will—and that once you keep those apart the ideal survives. I think he's just motivated by romantic homesteading and doesn't want to give it up. (I have the same suspicion about national autarky arguments, but let's keep this to the personal version.)

Which step, if any, actually fails? Is the equivocation charge fair, and what would a corrected version of the argument show—does it kill the ideal of self-sufficiency, force it to be redefined, or leave it alone? Also, please separate what's settled here from what's genuinely contested; I don't want "autonomy is really about non-domination" presented as if everyone who works on this agrees.
