I'm in a jurispudence class and we keep going round in circles on the usual Hart–Fuller examples, the retroactive-statute and wicked-regime stuff, so I wanted to run the two positions on a case that isn't pre-packaged with a verdict. The way I was taught it: for Hart (positivism), what makes something a legal system is a working union of primary rules of obligation and secondary rules — rules of recognition, change, and adjudication — that officials actually use; whether those rules are morally good is a separate question. For Fuller, law is a particular way of governing people through rules, and a system counts as law only if it respects what he called the inner morality of law: rules must be general, public, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, possible to obey, relatively stable, and actually administered as announced. A system that systematically fails those isn't law at all, regardless of how much it resembles a legal order.

Now the case. A friend who does development work described something like this, and I've been turning it over: Kern Valley, a company-owned mining town in a region where the national government has effectively collapsed, so no state courts or police operate there. The company has published a Civil Code covering contracts, property, torts, housing, and workplace safety, and there's a Magistrates' Court with published opinions and a three-level appeal structure. The rules are general, publicized, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, and have been stable for years. Sanctions are real: fines, docked pay, eviction, a company-run lockup. The officials' rule of recognition seems to be: whatever the Civil Code and Council's published rules say, as interpreted by the Magistrates, is law in the Valley. And the judges actually apply that test. But two features are ugly. The Code imposes a settlement lien: the company sets rates for passage, housing, and meals, and the debt tracks the person, so many residents can't leave because no neighboring community will take them while they owe. And the Council — entirely company-appointed — can issue emergency orders that override the Code without Magistrates review, which they've occasionally used to cut wages. So you've got a mostly well-ordered rule-system that is also a debt trap.

So: is Kern Valley a legal system? Run it through both tests, criterion by criterion, say where the two tests agree and where they pull appart, and whether the case exposes a gap in either list of criteria (does Fuller's inner morality actually catch the debt trap, or does it let substantively evil-but-procedurally-clean law through?). Give me a JSON object with these keys:

- "hart_criteria": array of objects, each with "criterion" (short label), "assessment" (one or two sentences applying it to Kern Valley), and "met" (true, false, or "underdetermined")
- "hart_verdict": an object with "verdict" ("law", "not law", or "underdetermined") and "justification" (one sentence)
- "fuller_criteria": same array structure as "hart_criteria", but using Fuller's eight desiderata
- "fuller_verdict": an object with the same structure as "hart_verdict"
- "where_they_diverge": a string explaining any disagreement between the two verdicts and what drives it
- "criteria_critique": a string saying whether the case shows either list of criteria needs changing, and how
