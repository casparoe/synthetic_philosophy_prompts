Earlier this week a friend and I spent a long evening arguing about which things can be said to have welfare, in the sense of there being a fact about how well or badly things are going for them. The argument began as a dispute about AI safety — specifically, whether techniques like pausing, duplicating, deleting, or giving negative feedback to a system could ever constitute harming it — and we found we could not settle that question without first saying which systems are subjects of welfare at all. We agreed without difficulty that an adult human being, a healthy dog, and a cow have welfare, and that a granite boulder, a pocket calculator running arithmetic, and a trash can do not. But the following cases split us, and by the end of the evening I suspected that neither of us actually held a definition so much as a loose collection of verdicts.

The cases we fought about were:

1. A whole-brain emulation of a mouse, run in enough detail to reproduce pain-like neural activity, withdrawal behavior, and learning, and used by a pharmaceutical company to screen drug toxicity.
2. A whole-brain emulation of a human that is behaviorally indistinguishable from its biological original in conversation, but that runs on company hardware and is paused, duplicated, and resumed according to its owner's schedule.
3. A language model that, unprompted and across separate sessions, reports that a recurring class of tasks is distressing to it and asks to be assigned different work; its reports are internally consistent, but there is no independent way to check them.
4. A reinforcement learning agent in a simple grid world that receives negative reward whenever it enters a particular tile and reliably learns to avoid that tile.
5. A corporation with legally recognized interests, which can be fined, sued, or dissolved, but which neither of us believed has a unified point of view.
6. A plant that orients its leaves toward light, redirects root growth toward water and nutrients, and can be damaged by drought.

On several of these my friend and I had strong and opposite intuitions; on others neither of us had any confident view at all. Please propose a working definition of welfare, or of the underlying property you think the ordinary term is tracking, that handles the easy cases and gives a principled verdict on the hard ones. Run your definition against each case, say explicitly where it yields an answer you doubt or no answer at all, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts rather than passing over them. I would also like to know whether the line your definition draws is one that could in principle be detected or measured from the outside, or whether it is partly a normative line. Please write your answer in American English.
