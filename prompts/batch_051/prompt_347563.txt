I help run a philosophy of probability workshop, and two of our longest-running participants—one a committed propensity theorist, the other a committed subjective Bayesian—have agreed, after years of talking past each other, to an adversarial collaboration rather than another exchange of position statements. The propensity theorist holds that probabilities are objective, mind-independent dispositions of chance setups or physical states to produce outcomes, and that such dispositions attach to single events rather than only to long-run sequences. The subjectivist holds that probabilities are degrees of belief, constrained by coherence and by evidence such as observed frequencies, but are not themselves features of the world; for them, talk of single-case objective probabilities is at best a convenient reification of stable frequencies or of stabilized expectations. Both have already conceded that strict frequentism is too narrow, so set it aside except for contrast.

Please draft the document this collaboration should produce. Structure it in four parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: substantive, not vacuous, and worded so neither side feels it has been drafted by the other camp. Second, a disagreements table; for each row, give a neutral statement of the point at issue, the wording the propensity theorist would sign, the wording the subjectivist would sign, and a note on whether the residual disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Third, a section on cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, each paired with a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Fourth, a closing note on which parts of their dispute, if any, are only about words and which are real. Throughout, the document must engage at least these: a coin tossed exactly once and then destroyed; two agents with identical information who assign different probabilities to the same event; the claim that propensities explain stable observed frequencies rather than merely re-describing them; and the question of what calibration to observed frequencies establishes, whether it is an independent norm of rationality, a theorem about coherent agents, or merely evidence that one has tracked an objective chance.
