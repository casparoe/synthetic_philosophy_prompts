Okay so I've been going down a rabbit hole on corruption, and something bugs me. Transparency International and a lot of anti-corruption programs treat clientelism—politicians handing out cash, jobs, or favors to voters in exchange for support—as a form of corruption, more or less on par with bribery. Fine. But then I look at, say, a U.S. representative who brings home a federally funded bridge for her district, or who spends half her staff's time on constituent services like leaning on a housing agency to hurry along a particular developer's permit. Or a mayor who makes sure the neighborhood that turned out hardest for him gets its potholes fixed first. These are widely treated as normal, even admirable, parts of democratic representation. What I can't figure out is whether that distinction is actually principled, or whether its just rich-country practices getting a pass while the same underlying logic gets criminalized when politicians in poor countries do it.

So here's what I want. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory of corruption that this pattern of classification presupposes: what makes a targeted distribution of benefits to supporters corrupt in one case and legitimate in the other? Then say whether the practice actually needs that theory, or whether it's compatible with several different theories and the practice is underdetermined by any one of them. And finally: if you replaced that presupposed view with one that treats clientelism and pork-barrel politics as the same kind of thing, what would actually have to change—in the laws, in what gets called corruption, in how we judge politicians?
