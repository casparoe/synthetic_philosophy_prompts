In debates about the replication crisis, one influential diagnosis held that the fragility of published research in fields such as psychology and biomedicine was largely a by-product of how null-hypothesis significance testing is actually practised. In its textbook form, a p-value below 0.05 controls the false-positive rate only when the analysis plan is fixed in advance and the reported test is the only test that was run. In real research, the argument continues, investigators face a large menu of individually defensible choices: when to stop collecting data, which covariates to adjust for, which transformations to apply, which outcome measure to emphasise, and which of many unreported paths through the data to follow. Because publication rewards significant results, the choices that happen to cross the threshold get reported while the others vanish, so the published p-value no longer means what the textbook says it means. On this view, the replication crisis is substantially a crisis of researcher degrees of freedom, and the remedy is procedural: preregister hypotheses and analysis plans before seeing the data, and adopt registered reports in which the planned analyses are accepted irrespective of outcome.

Suppose now that a decade of widespread preregistration and registered reports has passed, and replication rates have barely improved. Moreover, the remaining failures are concentrated in low-powered studies of small effects with noisy measurement, while studies in areas with ample analytic flexibility do not fail conspicuously more often than tightly preregistered ones.

Does the diagnosis survive this development? Identify the premise or step on which the development bears, explain whether the argument is refuted, in need of repair, or better restated in a deeper form, and say what a Bayesian framing of the same evidence adds, for example in terms of prior odds, posterior probabilities, or the distinction between evidential support and long-run error control.
