I've been turning over an idea in Schopenhauer's essay On the Sufferings of the World, from his Studies in Pessimism. Near the beginning he suggests that the right way to look at the world is as a kind of penal colony, a place of punishment, and that this attitude is a reliable compass for getting through life without being surprised or misled by how much pain and frustration there is. I can't recall the exact phrasing, but the picture is that existence is, in some fundamental sense, something we endure rather than something that goes well. I've noticed that this has pushed me toward a broadly negative utilitarian outlook: that the prevention and reduction of suffering is the only thing that matters unconditionally, and that what we call positive goods are mostly just the temporary relief of suffering, or illusions produced by the contrast with worse states.

I want to stress-test that view. Suppose someone like me is drawn to the penal-colony picture and to suffering-focused ethics. What would actually count against this position? I don't mean the lazy objections like "that's just your depression talking" or "it would make everyone miserable to believe this." I mean: what observations, arguments, formal results, or cases would, if true, show that the view is wrong or at least seriously overreaching? Which alleged counterexamples should a committed negative utilitarian concede as genuine problems, and which are they right to dismiss as confused or as attacks on a strawman? I'd also like an honest answer to whether the penal-colony picture can be qualified enough to survive any possible evidence — that is, does it risk anything at all? And if it is insulated in that way, is that more of a problem for this kind of view than it would be for, say, a practical claim about which charities reduce the most suffering? Please be specific about what would have to be different in the world for the view to lose its grip, and sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
