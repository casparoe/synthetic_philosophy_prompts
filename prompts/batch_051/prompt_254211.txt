I've been rewatching the reimagined Battlestar Galactica miniseries (and the first season) with a friend, and we keep getting stuck on the scene where Laura Roslin, the Secretary of Education, is sworn in as President after the Cylon attack kills everyone ahead of her in the line of succession. My friend's take is that the show is basically a designated-survivor fantasy: it treats the legal chain of command as if it automatically settles who gets to rule, and it wants us to accept Roslin's authority as legitimate just because the paperwork happened to reach her. I don't buy it. It seems to me the show spends at least as much time showing that her authority is actually improvised after the fact — she hides her cancer from the fleet, leans on Adama's military force, gets thrown in the brig when Adama stages his own coup, and then nearly loses an election to a candidate she's convinced will lead the fleet to disaster.

I'd like a reading of the miniseries (and the first season or two, if that helps) through the lens of continuity of government and the ethics of designated survivors. In particular: does the show defend the view that in an existential emergency, keeping a legally recognized chain of command intact matters more than whatever democratic or moral deficits the particular survivor brings with her, or does it treat the line of succession as a convienent fiction the survivors need to hold the fleet together? Which scenes or decisions are the crux of your reading, and where does the show resist a clean philosophical position? Please take a definite interpretative stance and argue for it rather than laying out both readings — and if you think the show is genuinely conflicted, make the case that the conflict itself is the interesting philosophical point, not a cop-out.
