In a working group on formal epistemology, one participant has been defending a strict Bayesian position by leaning on an analogy that I suspect is doing more work than anyone has acknowledged. The analogy is that a person's degrees of belief are the prices at which she would accept either side of a small cash bet on the proposition in question, much as a bookmaker posts odds on each claim it entertains. From this, and from the observation that a bookmaker whose posted odds are internally inconsistent can have money extracted with certainty by a customer combining the right bets, the participant concludes that a person whose degrees of belief violate the probability calculus is irrational in just the same way, because a Dutch book could be built against her. I want that analogy audited rather than taken for granted. How far does the credences-as-betting-odds analogy actually hold? First set out the correspondence explicitly, but do so in ordinary prose since I do not want tables: what in the target, an ordinary person's belief state, is being mapped to what in the source, a bookmaker's posted line; what coherence and sure loss correspond to; and which features of the bookmaking situation the analogy silently imports into the mind without argument. Then work through the places where the mapping breaks. I especially want you to scrutinize the assumptions that there is a single price at which the person would both buy and sell a bet, that small cash stakes measure strength of belief rather than risk attitude or the changing usefulness of money, that willingness to accept each bet individually commits the person to accepting the whole package of bets together, and that being offered a bet leaves the target belief itself untouched. For each disanalogy, say whether it is fatal to the specific conclusion that probability-rule violations are irrational, or whether it is a harmless imperfection in the comparison. End by saying whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy, or no analogy at all, would carry the argument better. Please write the whole response as continuous prose, like a careful email or letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
