Last night a friend and I spent the whole evening arguing about a problem in Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics*, and we parted without either of us having moved. The argument concerned what Sidgwick calls the dualism of practical reason. In the closing chapter, he presents the conflict without resolving it: from the standpoint of impartial reason, one ought to pursue the general happiness, while from the standpoint of what makes an individual life rational, one may with equal reason pursue one's own happiness even at the expense of the general happiness. Sidgwick admits that he cannot show either standpoint to be rationally superior, and he treats the resulting division as a fundamental unresolved contradiction within practical reason itself.

My friend took the position that this dualism is a pseudo-problem: the conflict only looks like a contradiction because we are using the word "reasonable" in two different senses, one impartial and normative and one self-regarding, and once the senses are separated nothing genuinely conflicts. I resisted that, partly because Sidgwick was too careful to have simply confused senses of a word, and partly because the problem seems to reappear even for a single agent asking what she ought to do all-things-considered: she can feel both the impartial claim and the egoistic claim as real normative demands, not as different definitions. My friend replied that this felt conflict is merely a psychological residue of altruistic training. We went in circles.

The two of us have now reproduced, in miniature, a debate that has run through the literature since Sidgwick. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right. Is the disagreement between my friend and me — and in the larger debate — verbal, substantive, or a matter of conflicting starting intuitions? Are the two sides answering different questions under one heading? Is the question ill-posed, or merely hard? I have a working hypothesis: the deadlock comes from the demand that practical reason be a single, unified court of appeal, and the dualism arises because egoism and impartiality presuppose different, equally non-derivative notions of what it is to have reason to do something. But I am not confident this is right, and I want you to say where the diagnosis is most likely to break down.
