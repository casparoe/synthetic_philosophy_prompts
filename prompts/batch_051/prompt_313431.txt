One effect of Bostrom's "Infinite Ethics" (2011) has been to make two problems look like outliers: expected value fanaticism and infinitarian paralysis. Fanaticism says that an outcome with arbitrarily tiny probability but enormous—perhaps infinite—value can rationally swamp all finite expected values. Paralysis says, as Bostrom writes:

"We can do only a finite amount of good or bad. Yet in cardinal arithmetic, adding or subtracting a finite quantity does not change an infinite quantity. Every possible act of ours therefore has the same net effect on the total amount of good and bad in a canonically infinite world: none whatsoever."

My proposal is that these two problems are two ways of making the same mistake: treating the value of an act as a quantity that gets added into a global total. In fanaticism, an infinitesimal probability times an enormous value is still supposed to produce a decisive expected value. In paralysis, a finite difference added to an infinite total is supposed to vanish. Both conclusions follow from the arithmetic, but both are, I argue, bad guides to what matters.

I introduce a distinction between "global difference" and "local decisiveness." An act makes a global difference if it changes some aggregate score by addition—pleasure minus pain over all worlds, say. An act is locally decisive if it changes the value of some bounded region, relationship, life, or sequence of events in a way that would be recognized as mattering from within that region, regardless of what happens outside it. I claim that neither fanaticism nor paralysis can get a grip unless we assume that only global difference matters.

So my essay argues for this principle: an act can be morally decisive without changing any infinite or finite global total. This is because decisiveness is a property of how the act relates to the local structure of value, not of its arithmetic contribution to a sum. If this is right, then the fanatic and the paralysis skeptic share a false premise: that the force of a reason must be representable as a marginal contribution to a total.
