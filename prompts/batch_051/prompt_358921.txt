A colleague in political theory and I have been going back and forth about statehood and recognition, and the dispute keeps sliding between a conceptual question and an empirical one. On the view often called declaratory, an entity is a state once it possesses a defined territory, a permanent population, an effective government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states; recognition by existing states merely registers a fact that already obtains. On the rival, constitutive view, recognition is not merely an acknowledgment—it is part of what makes a political community a state at all. The more I think about it, the less I can tell whether these two positions are genuinely in conflict, and whether any observation could ever choose between them.

Suppose a colleague says, as a serious claim, that statehood is constituted by recognition. Design the best test you can of that claim. You might compare entities that have functioned as states for a long time without broad recognition against broadly recognized states, or look at what changes for an entity before and after its recognition shifts. Tell me which comparisons would be most probative, what pattern of empirical findings would favor the constitutive reading and what pattern would favor the declaratory reading, and which confounds would need to be controlled for. Then be exact about what a result would and would not settle: if recognition turned out to have large causal effects on an entity's capacities, would that vindicate the constitutive theory, or would it only show that recognition matters for something less than standing as a state? And if recognition turned out to be causally inert, would that refute the constitutive theory or merely show that its claim was never empirical to begin with? Keep your answer to no more than 700 words.
