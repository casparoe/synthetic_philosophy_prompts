I spent the better part of four decades as an atmospheric scientist, much of it building and running deterministic models of circulation, runoff, and regional climate. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy — chiefly the older debates about determinism and freedom, but increasingly the more recent literature on chaos, predictability, and measurement — and I keep noticing that conceptual distinctions that now seem clear to me are rarely operationalized in practice. In particular, I have come to think that working forecasters tend to collapse several very different things into the single word *uncertainty*: sensitivity to initial conditions inside an otherwise adequate deterministic model; genuinely stochastic forcing from outside the system or from unresolved scales; and structural error in the model itself. The practical consequences differ — more and better observations mainly help with the first; ensemble design and stochastic parameterizations speak to the second; while only multi-model or scenario approaches touch the third — yet standard practice often does not track the distinction. I have also seen both characteristic failures at once: a model that reproduces the historical record beautifully is trusted for point predictions years ahead, while next door someone shrugs that the system is chaotic and therefore nothing at all can be said.

I would like you to turn these distinctions into a procedure for a specific kind of practitioner. Imagine a water resources engineer, with no specialized training in dynamical systems, who must decide, several times a year, what operating rules to recommend for a reservoir on the basis of seasonal streamflow forecasts. The stakes are high — flood control and water supply — the time budget is a day or two, and a management committee must sign off on the recommendation. The procedure should be a decision sequence: a checklist or small set of tests run in order that tells this engineer, for a given forecasting exercise, which of the three sources of uncertainty above is likely to dominate, whether the forecast is being used inside its predictability horizon (for example, whether the lead time is short relative to the Lyapunov time, the timescale over which errors from imperfect initial conditions grow by roughly a factor of e), when to prefer a point forecast rather than an ensemble rather than a scenario, and what the sign-off discussion should be forced to confront. Each step should be plain enough for someone who will not read the theory, while marking the classical distinction it carries — for instance, predictability in principle versus predictability given finite measurement precision. Please be honest about the places where judgment cannot be eliminated, and about what the procedure cannot do: it cannot guarantee skill, and it cannot expose structural error that no test has yet revealed.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order: (1) "The decision context" — a short restatement of the practitioner, stakes, and constraints, together with the specific failure modes the procedure is meant to curb, around 10 percent of the response; (2) "The procedure" — a numbered decision sequence with the branching points and the yes/no or graded tests at each step, including what to do when the needed numbers, such as a Lyapunov time or a clean estimate of model error, are unavailable, around 45 percent; (3) "Worked cases" — two or three concrete applications to the reservoir-forecast setting, at least one of which should produce an answer the practitioner will find unwelcome, around 25 percent; (4) "Where the procedure breaks down" — its boundaries, irreducible judgment calls, and cases outside its scope, around 20 percent.
