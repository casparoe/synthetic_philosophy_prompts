I'm a legal philosophy PhD student trying to get clear on Maine's treatment of legal fictions, and this passage from Ancient Law keeps bothering me:

"When primitive law has once been embodied in a Code, there is an end to what may be called its spontaneous development. Henceforward the changes effected in it, if effected at all, are effected deliberately and from without."

Later in the same chapter he says legal fictions are the first of three agencies (with equity and legislation) by which law is harmonized with social needs. But fictions seem to work precisely by pretending the law has not changed—they keep the old form while doing new work. So what does he mean by calling post-code changes like these "deliberately and from without"? Is the fiction itself supposed to be the deliberate thing, or is it a sort of unconscious self-disguise that only later gets recognized as change? And if its deliberate, what does that tell us about how he thinks the law's identity is preserved through change?
