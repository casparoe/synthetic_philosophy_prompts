Here's a wierd one I keep coming back to, and I keep wanting a version of it that says what the actual buried theory is rather than just which team someone is on. Take US labor law, specifically the union side. If a majority of workers in a bargaining unit votes to unionize, that union becomes the exclusive representative of every worker in the unit. You can vote no, campaign against it, think the union's a racket; it doesn't matter. You can't negotiate your own separate deal with the employer, the union's contract binds you, and outside right-to-work states you can be made to pay an agency fee for representation you never wanted. Meanwhile the surrounding legal culture treats consent as basically sacred: contracts bind only the people who assented to them, association is voluntary, and your employment is otherwise at-will, meaning either side can walk for almost any reason. So a worker has maximum exit in every direction except this one: once 51% of your unit votes in a union, you're in the union's collective voice wether you like it or not.

And then there's the strike asymmetry. If you strike over wages, the employer can permanently replace you; if you strike because the employer committed an unfair labor practice, you can't. The law talks about a "right to strike," but the more fundamental the strike's goal is (your own economic position), the more it's treated like an ordinary market move you make at your own risk, while the more it's about policing the legal rules, the more it's treated as protected. That looks backward if strikes are supposed to be a worker's economic weapon.

So my question is: what philosophy of freedom, association, or representation does this whole apparatus actually presuppose? Does exclusive representation require a collective-agent view, where a majority vote creates a "we" that can claim you even over your individual no? Or can a hard individualist contractarian story still explain it, maybe as a coordination device for solving a public-goods problem inside the workplace? Does the strike asymmetry force a purely instrumental reading of the right to strike, where it isn't a natural right at all but a statutory tool for making the scheme work? And what would have to change doctrinally if you replaced the hidden theory with a clean voluntary-association view, or with a full "the certified union is a mini-democracy and the losers are just democratic losers" view? I'd like the answer to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and to say so if it's underdetermined between several theories. And steelman both the "exclusive representation is coercion" and the "exclusive representation is how equals actually bargain" lines before landing somewhere.
