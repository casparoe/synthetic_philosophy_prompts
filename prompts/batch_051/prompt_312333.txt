After a long career in analytical chemistry, I kept returning to the fact that our classifications were operational rather than sharp: detection limits, purity cutoffs, the point at which a trace constituent counts as a contaminant. Since retiring, I have been reading philosophy, especially on vagueness, and I built an argument that now strikes me as proving too much. I would like to know exactly where it fails, if it does.

Here is the argument, as I wrote it out for a reading group:

(1) If a substance is accurately called toxic at a dose of D milligrams, then it is accurately called toxic at a dose of D + 1 milligram. One milligram cannot make the difference between toxic and nontoxic; any such difference would be arbitrary and scientifically unreal.
(2) By repeated application of (1), if a substance is toxic at 0 milligrams, then it is toxic at 1000 milligrams.
(3) Yet 0 milligrams is not toxic and 1000 milligrams is toxic.
(4) Therefore (1) is false: there must be some adjacent pair of doses D and D + 1 such that the substance is not toxic at D but is toxic at D + 1.
(5) But accepting such a pair is accepting a sharp cutoff on a continuous dose–response relation, which is precisely what the operational practice of threshold setting denies.

I would rather keep (5) than (1), but I do not see what rejecting (1) commits me to. If I reject (1), I seem forced to posit a hidden sharp boundary that no instrument could locate. If I keep (1), the argument dissolves everyday toxicity classifications. My instinct is that something happens between the ordinary notion of a difference too small to matter and the universal statement in (1), but I cannot name the mistake, and I suspect the same structure would infect any predicate applied to a continuous magnitude. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
