I have been trying to settle a question one could call the ontology of dead-letter law, and I want a genuine credence rather than a survey of the many things 'law' might mean. Suppose a statute prohibits an activity — think of the sort of low-level regulation that is openly and routinely ignored — and suppose this has been true for so long that everyone inside the system, including the officials nominally charged with enforcement, treats non-enforcement as a fixed feature of the landscape rather than a discretionary decision. The proposition I want you to assess is this: under those conditions the statute no longer imposes a legal obligation on the people it addresses, and someone who violates it does no legal wrong; the statute remains law only in an archival or bookkeeping sense. My own credence is around 0.55.

Give me your credence for that proposition with the reasoning decomposed. I would like at least the following five terms:

1. Practice versus pedigree. How likely is it that legal validity is ultimately a matter of current social practice — so that a rule ignored by both officials and the community can lose its status — rather than a matter of enactment and formal pedigree, so that its status persists until a court or legislature says otherwise?
2. Silent repeal versus dormant validity. Conditional on the practice-based view being broadly right, how likely is it that stable, common-knowledge non-enforcement amounts to desuetude — a kind of implicit repeal — rather than merely a dormant law that remains valid and could be revived by a decision to enforce it?
3. Is 'illegal' univocal. How likely is it that 'this conduct is illegal' has one literal truth value in this scenario, rather than being true in one register (the enacted record) and false in another (the lived practice), so that a sizable part of the dispute is merely verbal?
4. The rule-of-law pull. The rule of law can be felt pulling in two directions: toward the view that the statute has lost its force, because the real danger is arbitrary or selectively revived enforcement and lingering dead letters are corrosive; or toward the view that the statute remains valid, because officials must not be allowed to repeal law by simply neglecting it. What is your credence that the first pull is the correct reading of the rule-of-law ideal?
5. The threshold. How much of your answer hangs on the difference between never enforced and almost never enforced? What is the probability that there is a principled line between these at all, rather than a continuum along which a rule's status as law gradually degrades?

For each term give a rough probability and one or two lines of justification. Then state how you combined the terms, and whether you treated them as conditional on one another. Then identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive, and say what evidence or argument would move that term the most. If your final credence differs from mine, don't just report the difference — tell me where in this decomposition the disagreement lives.
