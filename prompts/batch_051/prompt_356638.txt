Please act as a professional philosopher who works on social epistemology and the design of epistemic institutions, and read what follows as a problem in mechanism design rather than as an invitation to moralise. Think carefully, take the analysis step by step, and begin with some explicit brainstorming before you settle on your conclusions. Write your answer in British English.

I am a retired natural scientist, recently come to philosophy, and therefore temperamentally drawn to proposals that would measure what is usually left unmeasured. Here is one such proposal, and I would like you to tell me how the institution would actually behave once strategic agents live inside it.

A consortium of broadcasters proposes a professional league — call it the Calibration League — that would turn argumentation into a spectator sport. Each fixture concerns a single proposition about some future, publicly observable state of affairs, with a resolution date and a resolution protocol registered in advance, so that within at most a year everyone can agree, without genuine controversy, whether the proposition has come out true or false. Two teams are assigned, one to the affirmative and one to the negative, with equal preparation time and a shared evidence packet.

So far, ordinary debating. The novelty is in the scoring. Two scoring panels of five hundred lay reasoners each are formed for every fixture; one panel hears only the affirmative case, the other only the negative case, and neither hears the other side. Each panel member reports a posterior probability q for the proposition after hearing the case. The prior p against which improvement is measured is not the panel member's own; it is taken from a pre-registered survey of a separate, matched sample, sealed before the sides are assigned. Let the Brier score of a probability r against an outcome o be (r − o) squared, where o is 1 if the proposition proves true and 0 if it proves false; the lower a Brier score is, the closer the probability sat to the truth. A panel member's improvement is defined as the prior Brier score, computed from p, minus the posterior Brier score, computed from q. A team's score for the fixture is the mean improvement across its own panel. Panel members are paid partly for the accuracy of their posterior Brier scores over the season. The public would be shown a joint session with cross-examination for the spectacle, but only the solo panels count toward the standings, which determine prize money, broadcasting share, promotion and relegation.

Now treat this as a mechanism. Walk me through what it incentivises: how would teams choose which propositions to place on the fixture list, and which kinds of proposition would come to dominate as the league matured? What should a team do on the morning of a fixture when its internal research says it has been assigned the false side, and what does that do to the spectacle, to recruitment, and to the very idea of a contest? How might panel members, or the people who run the pre-registered prior survey, game the numbers, and can those holes be plugged? Which styles of argument and which temperaments would ten years of selection actually favour? Where does the system get gamed first, and which of its failure modes are repairable without abandoning truth-scored argument altogether? Finally, after the analysis is complete, give your verdict: would this design improve public reasoning, or merely optimise a metric — and if it is broken, what is the smallest change that would rescue it?
