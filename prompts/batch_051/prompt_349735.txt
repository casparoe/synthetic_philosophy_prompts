Hi — I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, mostly) who has started reading philosophy of science in retirement, and I've been trying to get my views on the end-of-physics question into some kind of order. I'm writing a short discussion note for a reading group, and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to these claims. Heres roughly where I've landed:

1. There really is a final theory of fundamental physics — a compact set of dynamical laws that the universe actually obeys. I don't mean it also has to fix initial conditions or the values of constants; that's a stronger claim than I want to make.

2. I think underdetermination has real bite. For any finite body of evidence, however big, there will be infinitely many alternative theories that fit it just as well, so in a strict sense we can never be justified in believing that the final theory we have in hand is the true one rather than one of its lookalikes.

3. But I also think the nesting of our theories is strong evidence that we are converging on the truth. Newtonian gravity as a limit of GR, thermodynamics as a limit of statistical mechanics, the standard model as an effective field theory of something deeper — it would be an enormous coincidence if better and better approximations kept working at higher energies and shorter distances without there being something real at the bottom.

4. I also hold that simplicity is a genuine guide to truth in fundamental physics. Fewer free parameters, more constrained structure, less fine-tuning: those don't just make a theory more convenient, they make it more likely to be on the right track.

5. I don't require the final theory to explain the initial conditions of the universe, why the constants have the values they have, or why the laws are one way rather than another. Dynamical laws and boundary conditions are just different kinds of facts, and I'm only after the first kind.

6. If something like a landscape of vacua turns out to be right — so the low-energy constants are set environmentally rather than uniquely derived — I don't think that spoils the search for a final theory. It just means the bridge from the fundamental law to our particular observed world runs partly through selection effects rather than unique derivation.

7. Lastly, if two formulations turn out to be exactly equivalent under a duality, I don't think we're forced to say one of them is the real one and the other is just a representation. The final theory could just be the equivalence class of such formulations.

Do these hang together? Please don't just say yes or no. I'd like the tensions ranked by severity, with a note on which are only apparent and which are the real problems, and for each tension what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs. I want a genuine audit, not a pep talk.
