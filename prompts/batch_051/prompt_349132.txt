Lately I have been reading about the familiar contrast between an individualist West and a collectivist East Asia, and two proverbs keep getting produced as if they settled the matter: the nail that sticks up gets hammered down for the East Asian side, and the squeaky wheel gets the grease for the Western side. In a seminar last week someone used the pair to argue that they encode not merely different styles but opposed normative logics, one culture teaching that standing out invites correction, the other teaching that making oneself conspicuous is how needs get met, so that the relation between person and community is genuinely different in the two traditions. I am not convinced the proverbs settle as much as advertised, and I would like something precise to bring back to the seminar. Could you lay out, for each proverb separately and then for the pair together, the distinct precise claims it could be making, including but not limited to purely descriptive statistical claims about how people respond to conformity and complaint, prudential advice about how one should behave, normative endorsements or criticisms of those responses, claims about the mechanisms of reward and sanction involved, and any other importantly different readings; and say for each reading whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or one that is probably false? Then tell me which of these readings would actually support the seminar's claim that the two proverbs mark a deep East-West difference in how person and community are related, and which readings would leave that contrast overstated. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose in the manner of a considered email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
