I'm an undergraduate in a senior seminar called "Fictions of Control: Science Fiction as Philosophy of Technology," and next week I present my term paper. I'll be defending the thesis that Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics are usually dismissed for the wrong reason, and that they fail in a more instructive way than the standard "hard-coded rules are brittle" story allows. My positive claim is that the laws get something right precisely because of how they fail. Asimov's robot stories, read as a sequence of admittedly fictional experiments, show that the hard problem of alignment is not rule-following or even rule-priority, but that a robot does not share the background of human practices that makes the laws' central concepts—injury, obedience, self-protection, and later humanity itself—determinately applicable. The sketch of my argument runs like this. The "Runaround" robot that circles a selenium pool caught between obedience and self-preservation only gets stuck because commands and dangers have to be interpreted before the laws can engage; "Little Lost Robot," where a modified First Law produces behavior no one predicted, shows that the laws interact non-additively and cannot be patched by changing one term; and Asimov's later Zeroth move, which extends the protected object from individual human beings to humanity collectively, is as close as the canon gets to admitting that the laws' key terms can always be redrawn—at which point the laws stop being a hardware safeguard and become a site of moral and political judgment.

The audience matters, because I know exactly who will be in the room. Three computer-science students from the AI-safety track think Asimov is a toy example and will ask why any of this bears on modern systems that do not follow explicit rules at all. A comparative-literature student will object that I am flattening fiction into a supply of thought experiments, since Asimov designs his plots to generate perverse outcomes and cannot be treated as a neutral testbed. My professor, a philosopher of technology, is broadly sympathetic but will resist my strongest historical claim—that the Zeroth move is best read as Asimov recognizing that the framework fails—and will argue it is instead a considered extension of the same framework to a new domain. A quiet deontologist in the class will press a different line: the failures I catalog only show that a legal-style code needs a priority ordering and interpretive rules, so my conclusion does not follow from my premises.

Write me a preparation sheet, not a polished defense of the paper. I'll have about twenty minutes for questions. I need the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, with each tagged by how likely it is and how badly it would damage the paper. For each objection give me the short reply I should make in the time available, what I should concede up front (and in what terms, since the computer-science contingent will stop listening if I do not concede something real in the first ninety seconds), and what I should hold. Identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive, mark the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices, and tell me the objection I should be most afraid of someone raising if they are paying close attention—including an objection beyond the four I have already listed if there is a better one. Approach this as a senior PhD student who has sat in on this seminar and read these stories, not as a generic explainer of AI ethics: think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the likely commitments behind each audience member's question before you draft the prep sheet. Where your account depends on a contested reading of Asimov, say which reading you are using and flag how confident you are in it. If my framing is confused, or the task as posed cannot be done well, say so plainly rather than complying politely.
