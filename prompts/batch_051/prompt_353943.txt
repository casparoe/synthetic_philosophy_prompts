Here are the criteria I work with for something's being a rule of grammar rather than a rule of style. (1) Structural scope: it governs how expressions are put together—inflection, agreement, word order, argument structure—rather than which words or registers a speaker prefers. (2) Internalization: competent members of the community follow it without monitoring or explicit instruction, and violations strike them as ill-formed, not merely as unusual or infelicitous. (3) Systematicity: it is productive, applying to novel instances rather than to a fixed list of memorized phrases. (4) Communal stability: it is shared across members and acquired through participation in the community, not imposed by an editor or a style guide. Now consider a hypothetical message board, r/FirstPersonArchive, whose members post episodic reports of past events. Over several years the board has settled into a strict convention that all narrative main clauses are in the simple present, even with explicit past-time adverbials: members write "Yesterday I walk to the station and miss the train" and treat "Yesterday I walked to the station and missed the train" as a noticeable error in that context, correcting newcomers even when the content is fine. Veterans say they no longer have to think about the rule, produce the present tense spontaneously with novel verbs and in private messages, and some carry it into spoken conversation with other members. Evaluate whether this tense convention is a rule of grammar under the four criteria.
