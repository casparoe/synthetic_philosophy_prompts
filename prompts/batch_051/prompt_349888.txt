In a conversation about whether a decent person should be able to justify even their most personal actions by an impartial rule, someone offered the case of a parent who jerks their child back from the path of a car. The objection was that if the parent's thinking is "it's my child, and saving a child in these circumstances is permissible," the second part is "one thought too many" — that the extra thought itself weakens the act or shows that the parent's relationship to the child is not fully sound. I've since seen the phrase used loosely to end arguments, for example to dismiss the idea that a morally serious person should in principle be able to state the general rule that licenses even their most intimate choices. Lay out the distinct precise claims "one thought too many" could actually be making — about whether the defect is in the person, the action, the relationship, or the moral theory that demands such a thought; about the difference between what justifies an act and what should run through the actor's mind; about whether the thought can coexist with genuine love or commitment. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which reading, if any, actually supports using the phrase to reject the demand that a morally serious person be able to produce the impartial justification.
