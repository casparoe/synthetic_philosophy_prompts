I spent my working life as an experimental biologist, and since retiring I've been trying to get a grip on some philosophy of mind. One thing I keep tripping over is the idea that there can be knowledge that cannot, even in principle, be put into words. The mystics and a lot of people who write about contemplative practice seem to think it's not just a feeling or a state of mind; they call it a cognitive achievement — the person has learned something, and the learning is real even though it is ineffable. To me that sounds either trivially true (there are plenty of things I know how to do but can't fully describe) or close to contradictory (if it's knowledge, shouldn't it be the sort of thing that could in principal be said?). I'd rather not stay stuck in that armchair.

So here's what I'd like. Take this precise claim: a person can come to know something — not merely feel differently or acquire a skill — such that their judgements or discriminations reliably improve, but the content of what they know cannot be verbalized by them even with effort and training. A concrete version I keep chewing on is the meditator who says she now directly knows something about the constructed nature of her own experience, but can't say what she knows. Design the best concrete test you can for that claim; it could be behavioral, psychophysical, longitudinal, whatever you think is most probative. Tell me exactly what result would count as evidence for the claim, what would count against it, and — this matters to me as an old experimentalist — what the test would still leave open. In particular, I suspect no experiment can distinguish 'genuinely ineffable' from 'not yet articulated'; if that's right, say why, and if it's wrong, tell me what would separate them. Keep it to 600 words at most.
