Hey, I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm finishing a piece about how the old cold war debates on nuclear brinkmanship have started to feel less like  history. As a little sidebar I want a poem — a villanelle, I think, because the form just keeps circling back to the same two lines the way these arguments keep circling back to the same bind. Here's the bind as I understand it: a deterrent only works if the other side believes you'd really retaliate, but actually retaliating is the one move that's strategically insane — it's pure revenge, your own country is already ash, there's nothing left to defend. So a rational adversary should see the threat as a bluff, which guts it. The old strategists, Schelling more than anyone, said the fix is to make retaliation less fully deliberate: automate parts of it, lean on launch-on-warning, walk the edge so things could slip into war without anyone quite deciding to. You make the threat credible by giving up control, and the peace you buy rests on accepting a real chance of accident and miscalculation.

Could you write the villanelle so that one refrain carries the demand that the threat be credible, and the other carries the handover of control that credibility costs, and the two refrains meet in the final two lines in a way that makes the reader feel the tradeoff isn't a bug in the argument but the whole point? I'd love it to be precise enough that somebody who knows the deterrence literature recognizes the actual paradox rather than just getting a generic "nukes are scary" poem, but good enough that a reader who's never thought about this reads it as just a good poem. And maybe keep the rhymes a little strained in one stanza — like the form itself is under pressure — but dont overdo it.
