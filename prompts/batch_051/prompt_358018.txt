I sit on the grants committee of a small family foundation that funds high-school and university programs in argument and civic discourse. We are choosing between two finalists for this cycle's single $150,000 award. One is an established parliamentary debate organization requesting funds to add three regional tournaments. The other is a new organization proposing a national competitive reasoning league, which has asked for the full amount as seed funding.

The league would not run debates in the usual sense. Each match is a structured three-party inquiry into a live empirical or policy question, with teams rotating through three roles: a Claimant who defends a specified answer, a Critic who attacks it, and a Synthesist who must build the strongest all-things-considered position from the exchange. Scoring rewards the highest-fidelity steelman of an opponent's position, correct identification of the crux of the disagreement, prompt and appropriate concession when the balance of argument shifts, and calibrated confidence intervals on factual subquestions. A separate Bayesian score is computed from the gap between a team's stated confidence and the accuracy of its claims across the season. The founders—former debaters and tournament forecasters—describe the project as an attempt to make adversarial collaboration into a spectator sport with standings, rankings, and an annual champion.

The committee is split. Several of us, me included, are longtime readers of rationalist blogs and forums, and we are drawn to the proposal because it looks engineered to reward updating, calibration, and scout mindset rather than mere persuasion. The skeptics reply that competitive incentives will optimize whatever the judges can actually measure—poised hedging, confidence theater, rhetoric disguised as synthesis—and that the Bayesian score will be Goodharted by the second season. The chair has asked each committee member to draft a recommendation memo for the full board.

Draft that memo as an expert on argumentation theory, epistemic institutions, and incentive design who has thought carefully about how competitive scoring shapes behavior over many seasons. Steelman the position you ultimately recommend against. Before drafting, brainstorm at least three distinct failure modes of the league and at least one mechanism by which a competitive format could genuinely track truth-tracking.

Structure the memo with exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Recommendation — one sentence, no more than 50 words, landing on exactly one of: fund as proposed, fund with conditions, or reject with a path to resubmission.
2. The case for funding — roughly 250 words; give the strongest version of the proposal's logic.
3. The case against funding — roughly 250 words; give the strongest version of the skeptics' worry, not a strawman.
4. Conditions or a path back — roughly 300 words; if you recommend funding, specify what would trigger a cutoff; if you reject, specify what a revised pilot would need to show or change.
5. Bottom line for the board — no more than 75 words.
