I am a professor of philosophy, and next month I am presenting a seminar at a marine science institute. I will be defending the thesis that precautionary welfare standards for decapod crustaceans—lobsters, crabs, shrimp, and their relatives—in aquaculture are justified on the evidence we already have, and that the industry should be required to phase out two practices in the near term: eyestalk ablation in shrimp broodstock (removing a female's eyestalks to induce spawning) and chilling or boiling without prior electrical stunning, replacing both with electrical stunning before killing. My audience is not a philosophy audience: it is mostly comparative physiologists and aquaculture veterinarians, plus two economists who model seafood markets and a handful of fisheries policy staff. They know crustacean biology better than I do, they work with invertebrate nervous systems daily, and several of them regard claims about fish pain and crustacean pain as activist rhetoric rather than science. I need a question-period prep sheet, not a paper defense.

My argument sketch, so the objections can target it, runs as follows. First, the behavioral and physiological evidence makes it reasonable to assign a non-trivial probability to sentience in decapods. Second, where there is a non-trivial probability of large-scale suffering and affordable mitigations are available, a precautionary standard is warranted. Third, electrical stunning is technically feasible and cheap per animal once capital costs are amortized. Therefore, delaying action is harder to justify than adopting the standards now. I know the weak links are the first and third premises, and I expect the room to go at them hard.

Please produce the prep sheet in the following five sections, in this order, with the indicated rough lengths.

1. The audience's operating assumptions (about 150 words). State the commitments and methodological standards that will make this room skeptical, in their own vocabulary—for example, the distinction between nociception (neural response to noxious stimuli) and felt pain, and the charge that welfare talk about crustaceans is anthropomorphic projection.

2. The objections, in order (about 1,400 words). Give me seven or eight objections in the approximate order they will actually arise. For each: identify who in the room raises it; phrase it as they would phrase it, without caricature; say whether it has real force; and give a short reply that states what I should concede and what I should hold, with a note on where the reply is soft. Make sure to flag the two places where my own argument is weakest and tell me how to defend them rather than hope the audience misses them.

3. Concessions to make up front (about 150 words). The points I should grant early, both because they are true and because granting them will buy credibility with this audience.

4. The load-bearing reply (about 250 words). The single point that must land for the proposal to survive, what it costs if it does not land, and how I will know from the room whether it has landed.

5. The question I should hope nobody asks (about 200 words). The hardest objection this particular audience could put to me, phrased the way they would put it, and what I should say if it comes anyway.
