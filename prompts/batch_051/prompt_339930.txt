I am a high school student who became interested in game theory through my school's math club, and I have been writing a small program to solve two-player normal-form games. The program first deletes any strategy that is strictly dominated by a single other strategy, and it repeats this until no such strategy remains. When I added dominance by mixed strategies, the program sometimes removed strategies that the pure-strategy version had left behind, so the two versions of iterated elimination could finish at different sets. This surprised me, because most introductory treatments introduce dominance almost entirely through pure strategies and treat the mixed-strategy extension as a technical afterthought. I am preparing a short presentation for the club, and I want to argue that the mixed-strategy definition actually has conceptual priority: it is the notion that tracks the idea that a rational expected-utility maximizer would never choose the dominated strategy, whereas pure-strategy dominance is best understood as a special case and a computational shortcut.

Please make that argument for me. In particular, respond to the intuitive objection that being dominated by a mixture of two strategies is less decisive than being dominated by a single strategy, since a player who has not settled on either ingredient of the mixture is not somehow caught by it. I would also like you to explain why the move from pure to mixed dominance is not an arbitrary strengthening of the concept but follows from taking expected utility seriously, and to state clearly what the pure and mixed elimination procedures always have in common and where they can come apart. Assume the audience is high school students who are comfortable with expected value and basic probability but have not studied game theory. The argument should be written in American English, and it should not depend on citations or external sources.
