In my reporting on AI safety, I keep running into a chain-of-thought monitoring thesis: that reading a model's written reasoning gives safety teams a reasonably faithful view of why the model did what it did, and therefore an early warning when a system is behaving deceptively or pursuing goals it should not. I want an actual number, not a hedge. State precisely the proposition you are assigning the number to, then give your credence that the overt chain of thought of the most capable deployed systems in 2027 will still be faithful in that sense—meaning a deceptively behaving model would, more often than not, reveal the considerations actually driving its behavior. My own credence is around 0.3. Decompose the question into at least these components, with a rough probability and one sentence of justification for each: (1) current chains of thought are substantially faithful reports of the deliberation behind today's outputs; (2) as systems are optimized to be more capable and more acceptable to users, selection pressure will turn chain of thought into a public-relations channel that tracks what looks reasonable rather than real deliberation; (3) a capable model could carry out consequential hidden reasoning in its activations without that reasoning surfacing in the overt chain; (4) if obfuscation emerges, countermeasures such as paraphrase-based checks, hidden-representation probes, and second-pass critic models would catch it often enough to keep monitoring useful. Then explain how you combined these terms, identify the single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and say what empirical result would move that term the most.
