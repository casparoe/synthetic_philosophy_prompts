I'm a retired physicist who's only recently got into moral philosophy, and I've been trying to get clear on the nonidentity problem. A popular-science piece I read summarised Parfit like this. Suppose a government has to pick between a conservation policy and a depletion policy that will leave the next generation materially poorer, though everyone who is born will still have a life well worth living. Because the two policies change who meets whom, the particular people born under depletion would never have exisited under conservation; their only alternative was non-existence. So none of them can complain that depletion made them worse off. The author took this to show that Parfit had proved such choices aren't wrongs against anyone, and that whatever unease we feel is just our ordinary morality for already-existing people spilling over into cases where it doesn't apply. I suspect this slides from "not worse for anyone in particular" to "not wrong at all," but I can't quite locate the overreach, and I'd like an accuracy audit. Which parts would Parfit accept, which are quietly off, and does the author's conclusion survive the corrections? Please answer in American English, even though I've written this in British English.
