Two people on a Discord server I'm in got into a long argument last week, and I keep going back and forth on who's right. It started when one of them said that after reading about conceptual engineering, they no longer think there's a useful difference between analysing what a concept means and deciding what it should mean. I'll call them A and B.

A: Any analysis has to make choices: whose usage counts as "the" concept, which cases are central and which are defective, whether we defer to experts or to ordinary speakers. Those choices are normative. So conceptual analysis is just conceptual engineering that hasn't admitted what it's doing.

B: That's the standard underdetermination argument, and it proves too little. Drawing a map involves choices of projection and scale, but map-making isn't road-building. You can aim to describe the concept a community actually uses even if you need theory to do it.

A: The map metaphor assumes there's an independent road network for the map to be right or wrong about. For concepts like "woman" or "marriage" or "addiction", what counts as "our" concept is partly settled by whose usage gets treated as the default. An ameliorative defintion — say, defining "woman" in terms of social subordination — isn't discovering a hidden road, it's laying one and then calling it a map.

B: But the people who do ameliorative analysis are explicit that this is a separate project. They distinguish the descriptive/empirical question of what a term currently picks out from the ameliorative question of what concept we should use for some purpose. The whole point is that they aren't pretending to do neutral description — but that doesn't show neutral description is impossible.

A: For contested social terms it basically is impossible. Press on what "our" concept of marriage or addiction is and you get several incompatible clusters, each with its own history and defenders. There's no fact about which cluster is "the" concept, so what's left is a choice about which to adopt — which is engineering.

B: "Several incompatible clusters" is still an answer to the descriptive question: the answer is that the term is disjunctive, or context-sensitive, or split across communities. That's an ordinary empirical finding, not a collapse into engineering. And for natural-kind terms like "water" or "species", how the world is does a lot of the fixing, independent of our usage.

A: Sure, the world has joints. But with something like "species" there are multiple real joints — phylogenetic, morphological, ecological — and which one we take the concept to track is a value- and project-loaded choice. Even the natural-kind cases are engineering with extra steps.

B: Choosing which real biological relation to track for a given purpose is a choice between candidate descriptions of the world, not a decision to change the concept. If someone says "let's make 'species' mean any morphologically similar cluster", they're changing the subject — and we can say so precisely because we have a grip on the previous concept.

A: That just pushes the normativity back a step. Calling one usage "the previous concept" privileges a status quo and then makes it look discovered. The people whose usage lost get told their meaning was a defective case. That's advocacy wearing analysis's clothes.

B: I'm not saying description is value-free. I'm saying the aims are distinct: (1) what concept does this community in fact employ, and (2) what concept should it employ going forward? You can answer (1) fallibly and interest-relatively without it turning into (2). Your view erases the difference and lets an advocate claim their proposal is just what analysis reveals.

A: And your view lets an analyst claim their reconstruction is just what the community meant, when actually they've smoothed over disagreement, picked favourites, and hidden the pick behind "in fact employs". If the descriptive question rarely has a determinate answer exactly where people bother to argue, then in the cases people actually care about, (1) does collapse into (2) in practice.

B: "Rarely determinate" and "collapses into" are different. Even where the honest descriptive report is "indeterminate between these clusters", that's still a different verdict from "we should henceforth use cluster C". And huge stretches of language are determinate enough that the distinction does real work.
