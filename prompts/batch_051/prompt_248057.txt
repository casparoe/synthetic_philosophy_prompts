In his essay "Of Cannibals" (Essays, Book I, ch. 30), Montaigne seems at first to deny that the Tupinambá are barbarous. He reports what he has learned from a man who lived among them and says, in effect, that he finds nothing savage or barbarous in that nation except the universal habit of calling barbarous whatever differs from one's own usage; our only test of truth and reason, he suggests, is the opinions and customs of the country we are raised in. Yet shortly afterward he concedes that we may properly call these people barbarous in respect to the rules of reason, though not in respect to ourselves, because Europeans surpass them in every kind of cruelty—for instance, by torturing and burning living men under pretext of piety and religion, whereas the Brazilians merely roast and eat the dead.

I want to know whether these two moments can be reconciled. What exactly is the standard of "the rules of reason" that licenses the second passage's concession, and how does it relate to the first passage's claim that custom supplies our only test of truth and reason? Is Montaigne contradicting himself, distinguishing two senses of "barbarous" (one tracking difference from custom, one tracking a trans-cultural standard of cruelty), or doing something else? Which reading requires the least special pleading, and what does it leave of his broader relativism?
