The topic has recently become personally salient because the board of the community hall where I volunteer has been deadlocked over whether to rent space to an organization that several board members believe would not extend tolerance to others in return; the dispute has turned on exactly the question this exam addresses. Exam question (set by the instructor):

Reconstruct Karl Popper's paradox of tolerance as he presents it in The Open Society and Its Enemies. Explain the sense in which it is a paradox rather than an ordinary policy trade-off. Then contrast Popper's treatment of intolerance with John Rawls's account, in A Theory of Justice, of how a just society should treat the intolerant. Finally, assess whether Popper's paradox licenses the legal suppression of intolerant speech in a democracy, taking care to distinguish legal suppression from private refusals to associate with or platform a speaker.

Student's answer:

Popper's paradox of tolerance begins from the observation that if a society tolerates everyone without limit, the intolerant will exploit that tolerance to organize, gain influence, and eventually destroy tolerance itself. Unlimited tolerance therefore undermines its own precondition. To prevent this, Popper argues that a tolerant society is entitled to withhold tolerance from the intolerant and, if necessary, to suppress them. The paradox lies in the self-referential structure of the principle: tolerance, in order to preserve itself, seems compelled to become intolerant of intolerance. That is why the problem is not an ordinary trade-off between competing goods but a paradox internal to the principle itself.

Popper, following Mill's harm principle, holds that intolerant speech may be restricted only when it directly harms others, for instance by inciting violence; short of that, the intolerant must be left alone.

Rawls reaches a similar practical conclusion by a different route. In A Theory of Justice he argues that an intolerant sect cannot consistently complain if its own liberty is restricted, because it would deny that same liberty to others. But Rawls adds an important qualification: a just society should restrict the intolerant only when doing so is necessary to protect equal liberty itself and the stability of just institutions, and not merely because the intolerant hold repugnant views. The contrast is thus between Popper's concern for the survival of an open society and Rawls's concern for the stability of a just scheme of equal liberty. Since the intolerant cannot consistently complain, Popper and Rawls together establish that the state is justified in preemptively banning intolerant parties and their publications before they can do real damage.

One argument for legal suppression is that democratic institutions cannot wait until an intolerant movement has already taken power; the paradox shows that tolerance must be defended before it is too late. The strongest argument against legal suppression is procedural: once the state can ban speech it deems intolerant, that power can be redirected against any unpopular group, and the cure may damage the open society more than the intolerant movement itself would have. Private refusals to associate or platform are different in kind, since no one is owed a platform, but legal suppression must clear the higher bar of overriding the general presumption in favor of free expression.
