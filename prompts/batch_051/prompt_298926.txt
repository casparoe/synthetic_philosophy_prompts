I've settled on a resolution of the two-envelope paradox, and I want to know how exposed it is. The resolution is this: the switching argument is invalid because its central step—assigning probability one-half to "the other envelope contains twice my amount" and one-half to "the other envelope contains half my amount," whatever amount I observe—smuggles in an improper uniform prior over possible envelope sums, or over their logarithms. On any proper prior, those two conditional probabilities cannot both be one-half for every observable amount, so the apparent dominance of switching rests on credences no agent with a coherent prior could actually hold, and the paradox dissolves into a misapplication of expected value.

Tell me what would count against this resolution. I do not want the standard rival diagnoses recapitulated; I want a map of its exposure. Work through at least the following pressure points and rank them from fatal to merely awkward.

First, consider a proper prior with infinite expectation: for example, a prior on the smaller amount that falls geometrically with each doubling, with a decay ratio strictly between one-half and one. I believe such a prior can make switching conditionally expected-value-positive for every amount one might see, even though the one-half assignment itself fails. If that is right, does it show that my diagnosis conflates "the one-half step is unjustified" with "the always-switch conclusion is unwarranted," and that the real culprit is infinite expectation rather than impropriety? How damaging is that to the resolution as I stated it, as opposed to a qualified descendant of it?

Second, consider finite, bounded variants in which a uniform prior over orders of magnitude is perfectly proper. There the one-half assignment holds for every interior amount, and switching is conditionally favorable across most of the range, with only the boundary cases balancing it out. Does the survival of the core reasoning in a setting where no improper prior is available show that the resolution overstates what is wrong with the switching argument, or does it confirm the resolution's point that the one-half assignment is at most locally valid?

Third, does the resolution risk overgeneralizing from a defect in this particular expected-value calculation to a ban on flat-prior reasoning more broadly? Would that be a counterexample to the resolution itself, or only a warning about careless statements of it?

Finally, I would be moved in either direction by the following: a fully specified game, with finite expectation and a defensible prior, in which a rational agent who sees the amount in her envelope should switch no matter what she sees would be close to fatal to my position. If the only cases that force switching-for-every-amount require infinite expectations, I would treat that as an awkward qualification rather than a refutation—but say whether that attitude is justified, or whether it is precisely the kind of insulating move that makes the resolution unfalsifiable.

So: rank these defeaters, distinguish the ones a defender of the resolution should concede as genuine anomalies from the ones that should be dismissed as changing the subject, and end with an honest assessment of whether the resolution, in its best form, still risks anything at all.
