So I'm a software engineer who got pulled into long-range planning, and I keep having to make calls off probability estimates, and there's this pattern I've fallen into that I don't know the name of, or whether it's even considered coherent.

When I ask people for a probability — say, the chance that a service we depend on gets deprecated before our replacement lands — nobody will commit to a number. But they will commit to a range, like twenty to forty percent. If I force them to pick a point, it's obviously made up. So I started taking the range literally: instead of one probability distribution, I keep all the distributions consistent with the range, and when I compare two options I first ask whether one beats the other under every distribution in that set. Most of the time that rules nothing out, so I end up adding a second move: compare options by their worst case across the set, and pick the one with the least bad worst case.

Concrete version. Migrating off this service costs a fixed 50 engineer-weeks. Staying costs zero if the service survives and 200 if it gets deprecated. The range they gave is a .2 to .4 chance of deprecation, and the break-even point is .25, which sits right in the middle of the range. So the set alone just says undecided: at .2, staying is better (40 vs 50), and at .4, migrating is better (50 vs 80). Then the worst-case rule kicks in and says migrate, because 50 < 80. But notice the whole decision got made by the most pessimistic end of the range, even though nobody endorsed that end specifically; they only said the range.

I hit the same thing estimating whether a vendor will drop support for a language we run in production — people will give a range, never a point, and then the worst-case rule makes a much more conservative choice than any single number would've.

I've also noticed that for easily reversible calls I'll just use the midpoint of the range and move on, but for hard-to-reverse ones I use the worst case. Same range, same two options, different answer. That makes me suspect the two halves are actually seperate commitments: (a) beliefs are sets of distributions, and (b) decisions are by worst case. But I don't know the landscape.

So my question is whether there's an established name for this whole way of doing things, and for the two halves separately. Where are the edges? A quant friend keeps saying the whole set thing is just confused Bayesian reasoning: I should put a prior over the possible values of the probability (including outside the range) and integrate them out, which would collapse everything back to one distribution. But that seems to smuggle in precision I don't actually have, and it would make the range stop being the load-bearing object. I keep seeing people gesture at the difference between risk and uncertainty, but that doesn't seem to be the distinction I'm using. If there's no single name, tell me the nearest concepts and how what I'm doing differs from each; a bare label won't help because I actually want to know which parts I can commit to independently.
