I run the modeling team at a municipal water utility, and every few months we have to set priors for pipe failure rates, demand growth, and extreme rainfall, then defend them to our board, who signs off on a capital plan in the tens of millions. Last quarter two consultants both called their priors "non-informative" on the same flood model and landed on budgets 40% apart, so I've been reading up on objective Baysianism and the maximum entropy principle. The bit I keep coming back to is roughly: given only the constraints you actually have, use the distribution that adds the least extra information. That sounds like it should be a procedure, but everything I read stops one step before telling me how to run one.

Write me a reusable procedure for setting a prior, not a essay on the theory. Assume the person running it has our background: some statistics, no philosophy, about half a day per decision, four or five decisions a year. The deliverable should be a checklist or ordered sequence of steps: what to ask first, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "no, you don't have enough to set a prior this way," and where it hands the decision to a human because the remaining choice is a value or policy call rather than a technical one. Flag the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a prior for the annual failure probability of a levee segment, where the only thing the engineers agree on is that it's between 0.2% and 1.5%; (2) a prior for demand growth, where the only number everyone accepts is a long-run mean of 2% per year; and (3) a case where the constraints look precise but the procedure should still refuse or produce an answer we won't like—you can construct that one.

Keep it under 1,000 words, not counting any table, and be honest about what the procedure can't do.
