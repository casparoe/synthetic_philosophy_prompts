I retired from physics a couple years ago and started reading the causation literature for fun. Pearl's do-calculus and structural causal models have their hooks in me, but I've backed myself into an argument that seems to show the whole approach only works for systems that don't exist. I'd like a diagnosis: which numbered step should I reject, and what does rejecting it force me to give up?

In a structural causal model, each variable gets an equation saying how it is produced by its direct causes. The core move is:

(1) The effect of an intervention do(X=x) is computed by severing X from its parents and replacing its equation with X=x, leaving all the other equations untouched.
(2) This computation mirrors a real intervention only if, when you actually force X to x, the other mechanisms keep operating just as they did before.
(3) So the framework assumes modularity: each mechanism can be changed on its own without altering the rest.
(4) But the systems we care about in biology and medicine are not modular in that way. Disable one gene or one pathway and other pathways compensate, reparameterize, or reorganize; the remaining "equations" dont stay fixed.
(5) Therefore, for exactly the systems we most want to understand, the do-calculus describes a surgical fiction, not anything real.
(6) And since almost all systems worth modeling outside physics are like this, the framework is broadly inapplicable.

I strongly want to reject (6), and I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than give up on the do-calculus being about real interventions. So: where does this argument actually go wrong, and what do I have to believe about mechanisms—or about what an intervention is—to get out?
