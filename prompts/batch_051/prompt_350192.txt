I have been developing a notion I call a modal illusion, and I want an honest assessment of it rather than encouragement.

Here is the idea. In debates about whether conceivability is a guide to possibility, the usual move is to distinguish kinds of conceivability — prima facie versus ideal, or imaginative picturing versus a priori coherence — and then ask which kind, if any, tracks which kind of possibility. I want to cut across that debate with a psychological category. A modal illusion, as I use the term, is a case in which a proposition remains conceivable even after the thinker has accepted that it is impossible. The conceivability persists the way an optical illusion persists after you know the two lines are equal. For instance, I can accept the proof that there is no largest prime and still find myself entertaining, almost seeing, a scenario in which the primes eventually give out; I can accept that water is necessarily H2O and still hold a watery, non-H2O scenario before my mind. My working claim is that these are not just leftovers of ignorance or sloppy imagining. They reveal something structural: conceivability tracks what our cognitive apparatus can represent as a scenario, not what is genuinely possible. If that is right, then the conceivability-to-possibility inference is less a guide than a standing cognitive artifact, the modal analogue of an optical illusion, and much of the debate has been spent refining an instrument that is the wrong instrument.
