My history teacher is running a unit on the origins of mathematics, and each week she has ruled on whether a particular ancient artifact counts as mathematics or as merely counting/recording. Her rulings so far:

- A notched bone of the Ishango kind, with marks grouped in a way that some archaeologists read as a lunar tally: not mathematics.
- A tally stick on which someone kept a running count of sheep: not mathematics.
- A set of Sumerian clay tokens, and the clay envelope that held them, recording a shipment of grain: not mathematics.
- A Roman shop's wax tablet on which someone worked out the total of a bill, with the arithmetic written out step by step: arithmetic, not mathematics.
- An Old Babylonian clay tablet containing multiplication tables copied by apprentice scribes: mathematics.
- Plimpton 322, an Old Babylonian tablet now usually read as a table of Pythagorean triples: mathematics.

She has now given us the next case and asked us to predict her verdict. The case is a private notebook kept over decades by a land measurer who was never trained in a scribal school. It contains carefully organized tables of square numbers and Pythagorean triples, with marginal notes indicating that the tables can be reused for any field of the same shape rather than only for a particular field. When she dismissed the wax tablet, she said that doing a particular calculation for a particular sale is not yet mathematics.

Predict how she will rule on the notebook, and walk me through the reasoning. In your answer: identify at least two plausible rules that fit all six of her earlier rulings; state what each rule predicts about the notebook; state which rule she seems to be implicitly running, with your reasons and a best-guess prediction; and specify one further case, beyond the notebook, that would cleanly settle which of those rules she is actually using.
