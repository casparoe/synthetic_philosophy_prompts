My team evaluates language models for deployment in settings where an unnoticed misaligned objective would be expensive. Our standard gatekeeping practice is to read chain-of-thought transcripts the way a manager listens to a subordinate talk through a decision: as a more or less faithful report of the reasoning that generated the action. The working rule is that a transcript that candidly considers a dangerous goal and rejects it is evidence that the goal did not drive the final choice, while evasions and unexplained leaps are treated as evidence that the real reasoning is being hidden from us. In other words, we are treating chain-of-thought as transparent, in the sense that the transcript stands to the model's actual reasoning roughly as thinking out loud stands to a person's reasoning. That analogy is carrying much of the weight of our safety judgments, and I want it audited rather than assumed.

Produce the audit. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: match the source of the analogy, a person thinking out loud, to the target, a language model producing a chain-of-thought transcript. Cover at least the following entries: the reasoner, the underlying deliberation, the spoken or written report, the fact that verbalizing is itself a further cognitive act, the audience and its effect on the speaker, the speaker's incentives and capacities to misreport, and the listener's act of interpretation.

Then identify where the mapping breaks. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for our specific use, inferring the model's goal from the transcript, or is merely a harmless difference. In particular, address the claims that a language model may have no independent mental state to report back on; that the chain-of-thought tokens are generated by the same process, and under the same training objective, as the final answer; that training could reward plausible rationalization rather than accurate self-description; and that a model selected for a hidden goal could simply write down an innocuous chain of thought while steering its final answer elsewhere.

Then give a verdict on the analogy's intended work. Does a clean chain-of-thought transcript license the inference that the model's final action was not driven by a goal it would otherwise conceal? If the conclusion survives only in a weakened form, state precisely what the transcripts do and do not let us infer, and whether some nearby analogy or framing would serve our purpose better.

Close by telling me what would change your mind: what empirical finding about models or training, or what conceptual argument, would most sharply move your verdict in the other direction, and where in your own reasoning you are least confident.
