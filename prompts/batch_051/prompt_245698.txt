Arthur Schopenhauer, in volume 1 of The World as Will and Idea, describes the will as thing in itself: one, indivisible, outside space, time, and causality, and therefore incapable of being altered or abolished by anything that occurs within the world of representation. Yet in the same work he presents the denial of the will to live, arrived at through ascetic resignation, as the only genuine path to salvation. On their face, these claims seem to pull against each other: if the noumenal will is indivisible and unaffected by phenomenal events, what exactly is it that an individual human being denies or turns against in the denial of the will? Are these two claims reconcilable, perhaps because the denial is itself merely phenomenal and concerns only the will as it appears in a particular body, or do they require a more revisionary reading of Schopenhauer's distinction between will as thing in itself and will as phenomenon? Lay out the most plausible reconciliation, and assess whether it is honest to the text or special pleading.
