Take Reid's objection to Locke's memory criterion of personal identity, standardly presented through the brave officer, as a base case, and consider a variant in which a missing autobiographical memory is restored externally. First set out the base case in your own words: a child is flogged for robbing an orchard; as a young officer he remembers being flogged; as an old general he remembers the officer's deed of bravery but has completely forgotten the flogging. Reid's argument is then that Locke seems committed to saying the general is the officer and the officer is the child, while denying that the general is the child, which conflicts with the transitivity of identity.

Now vary one assumption. Add a lifelogging device that records first-person experience from the wearer's point of view and, when played back, induces in the viewer a vivid, first-person-style memory of the recorded event. The old general, having lost his memory of the flogging exactly as in the base case, plays back a recording of the original flogging made from the child's point of view and thereby acquires a full, first-person-style memory of being flogged. Distinguish two sub-cases: in the first, he knows the state came from the recording and experiences it as borrowed; in the second, he cannot distinguish it from his native memories.

Run the variant through three readings of a memory-based criterion: a strict causal reading, on which a present state counts as memory of a past action only if it is the genuine continuation of the original experience in the person's own memory; a phenomenal reading, on which any first-personal, vivid seeming-memory counts as memory regardless of its source; and an overlapping-chain reading, on which personal identity consists in the transitive closure of overlapping real memory connections and does not require the last stage to remember the first stage. For each reading, say whether the general is the same person as the flogged child, whether the sub-case changes that verdict, and whether Reid's transitivity objection still goes through, is blocked, or is merely relocated to a different premise. Then say exactly which premise of Reid's original argument the variant puts pressure on. End with the sharpened puzzle: suppose the same recording is played to a stranger, who acquires the same first-person-style memory of the flogging; would each reading make that stranger the same person as the child, and what does the asymmetry between the old general and the stranger show about whether a criterion of personal identity should track continuity of consciousness or merely the preservation of the information in a life?
