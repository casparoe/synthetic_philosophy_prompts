For the past few years I have been covering the science of consciousness and time perception, and I have settled into a working rule that I now apply almost automatically. The rule is this: first-person reports about the timing or temporal order of conscious experience are confabulation-prone, and I discount them severely. If a study's headline result depends on subjects reporting when a stimulus entered awareness, how long it seemed to last, or which of two events they consciously experienced first, I either drop the finding from my coverage or report it only with heavy caveats. I applied the rule last month to a paper on the felt onset of a voluntary decision, which I declined to cover because the central claim rested entirely on participants' retrospective reports of when they had decided. The color phi phenomenon is what originally pushed me toward the rule. In that setup, a red dot is flashed briefly in one location and then a green dot is flashed briefly in another; under the right timing conditions, observers do not report two separate dots. They report a single dot that moves from the first location to the second and changes from red to green roughly halfway along its path. But no dot ever occupied that midpoint, and no red-to-green transition was ever presented. The report of the color change happening mid-trajectory looks like a retrospective construction, as if the brain waits for the second dot and then fills in an earlier moment with information that arrived later. Depending on how one frames it, the later information either overwrites a memory of the first experience before the subject can report it, or the first experience is held back and edited before it enters awareness. My understanding of the two live options is that one is Orwellian and the other Stalinesque, and both made me suspicious of any introspective claim that assigns a time to a mental event.

An editor recently pushed back. She pointed out that some temporal self-reports are stable across repeated testing, align with objective measures such as EEG signatures or reaction-time behavior, and form the evidential basis for studying illusions rather than simply falling for them. She accused me of dressing up a sensational bias as a methodological scruple. I want to know whether the blanket rule is defensible, or whether there is a principled distinction among kinds of temporal self-report — onset, duration, order, simultaneity, the felt moment of a decision — such that some deserve near-zero weight and others deserve moderate trust. I am also unsure whether reports given immediately after a trial differ importantly from recollections given much later, and whether judgments of order are more trustworthy than judgments of duration. What I want, in the end, is a repair of the rule rather than only a verdict on it: a version that keeps the lesson of the color phi without demanding that I, a journalist who cannot run these experiments myself, estimate the reliability of every individual paper from scratch.
