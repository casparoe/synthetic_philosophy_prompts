I'm the secretary of a historical fencing club that's been around since the early 80s, and we're haveing a fight about a clause in our constitution. It reads: "A member shall not refuse a challenge to a fair trial of arms from a fellow member, save for injury or absence, lest he be deemed to have acted from cowardice and be asked to resign." When the clause was written, everyone understood "trial of arms" as a bout with blunted steel in the salle. Now one member, who took a bad hand injury and can't spar in person anymore, has gotten extremely good at a motion-capture VR longsword league—ranked matches, judged technique, the works—and he's formally challenged one of our older members to a best-of-nine series on the platform. The older member refused, saying it's a video game, not a trial of arms, and there's nothing brave about loseing in a headset. The VR member argues the point of the clause was never the specific weapon; it was that a member meets a challenge from a peer at full intent and risks being beaten in front of the community, which is exactly what he's offering. I have to write the committee a recommendation: does the clause reach simulated-bout challenges? Give me the reading from the text itself, the reading from the purpose of the rule (what was the courage it was trying to protect?), and the reading from what the founders would probably have said if you'd asked them about photorealistic simulated blade combat. Then say which approach should actually decide the matter, and if you think they diverge, what further thing would need to be settled first.
