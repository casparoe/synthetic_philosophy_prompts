Six months ago I became the associate editor at a cell-biology journal, and I am now the person who decides, for every new submission, whether it goes to peer review or receives a desk rejection. I handle about a dozen papers a week and can give each one no more than fifteen minutes before deciding. The journal's stated bar is that a paper must provide, or at least credibly promise, a mechanistic explanation of its phenomenon; we return studies that report only correlations, phenotypes without any causal story, or models whose parts have not been tied to anything in the cell. A wrong desk rejection can waste a laboratory's best work, and a wrong send-out burdens reviewers, so the standard needs to be explicit and defensible rather than a private editorial intuition—especially since the editor-in-chief audits my rejections. The account I want to anchor the standard in is the one I half remember from graduate school, by Machamer, Darden, and Craver: a mechanism is composed of entities and the activities they engage in, organized so as to produce regular changes from set-up conditions through intermediate stages to termination conditions, with productive continuity from one stage to the next, and with the description allowed to bottom out at whatever lower level the field can take for granted for the question at hand.

Turn that account into a reusable checklist I can run in fifteen minutes, in the order I should follow, with every step where judgment cannot be eliminated marked as such. Then apply the checklist to three hypothetical submissions: (1) a paper showing that protein X phosphorylates protein Y, which then binds and activates protein Z, and that deleting any of the three blocks a hormone-induced change in the cell; (2) a paper using a large genome-wide association study to identify forty loci associated with a psychiatric condition, with no experiment showing how any locus exerts its effect; (3) a paper presenting a mathematical model in which two coupled positive feedback loops generate bistable cell-fate switching, with no molecules identified for either loop. After working through all three, tell me which of the three I am most likely to misclassify if I ignore the checklist, and say what the checklist cannot tell me even when it is used correctly.
