Ok so I'm in a media law class and we've been picking at the fact/opinion distinction in defamation law, and I built an argument that seems to prove something absurd. I can't find the flaw, and my study group just argues in circles. Here it is:

(1) Defamation liability attaches only to false statements of fact. Pure opinion is protected no matter how nasty or damaging.

(2) The standard way to draw the line is that a factual statement is one that can be verified or falsified. "The mayor took a bribe" makes a claim about the world that an investigation could confirm or refute. "The mayor is a louse" doesn't, and that's why it's protected.

(3) Ratings and forecasts aren't verifiable in the relevant sense. A credit rating, a stock "sell" rating, a review that a contractor is "unreliable," a forecast that a CEO "will run the company into the ground" — these are predictions about the future or evaluative judgments. A forecast can turn out wrong (you said it would rain, it didn't) without having been a lie when made, and a rating is more like a grade than a claim about what happened.

(4) So ratings and forecasts can never be defamatory, no matter how destructive they are.

(5) But any factual smear can be repackaged as a prediction or a rating while communicating the excact same thing. "The mayor took a bribe" becomes "I predict the investigation will show the mayor took a bribe." "The doctor botched the surgery" becomes "I rate this doctor's competence 0 out of 10." If (1)–(4) are right, the repackaging alone buys immunity.

(6) That's absurd. A person who knows the mayor took no bribe can ruin him with the "prediction," and the law would have to shrug. A paper could write "our analysts forecast that this restaurant serves spoiled food" instead of "this restaurant serves spoiled food," dodge liability, and still lose the restaurant its customers.

(7) So either the fact/opinion distinction, applied to ratings and forecasts, collapses into a loophole you can drive any lie through — or one of the early steps has a hidden mistake.

I have a vauge feeling the problem is somewhere around (2) or (3), something about "predict" not actually changing the content of what's being communicated, but I can't make the feeling precise without just asserting the answer. Which step do I reject? And if the repair is that a prediction with a factual backside is still treated as a factual assertion, then what stops credit ratings and teaching scores from being actionable too, since they also rest on facts? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) and give up whatever has to go, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying.
