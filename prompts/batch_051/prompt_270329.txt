I am a reporter at a science-and-ideas magazine, and several times a year I face a version of the same editorial decision: whether to run a story identifying a particular person or organization as responsible for a specific harmful event when the only evidence we have is statistical. We may know that most of the buses on a route belong to one company, that a very high proportion of attendees at an event entered without paying, or that exactly one member of a small team must have fabricated data — but we have no witness, document, or forensic result that ties the named individual or body to the particular act. When we get the identification wrong, we ruin a reputation and may face defamation action; when we decline to publish, we may be suppressing something the public should know. My editors are intelligent but have no formal training in philosophy or probability, and we work on deadline.

I want a reusable editorial procedure for these cases, drawn from the philosophical and legal discussion of naked statistical evidence — the blue bus and gatecrasher cases, and the difference between a high probability and proof beyond reasonable doubt — but translated into something a working newsroom can actually execute. The procedure should be a checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it says the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence and must go to the editor or legal counsel. It should be runnable in under an hour by a reporter with no formal statistical training, and it should say which distinctions from the underlying theory must survive the simplification and which can be dropped in practice. I am less interested in a summary of the theory than in the operational version of it.

Then run the procedure on these three situations, showing the reasoning in enough detail that my editors could follow it:

1. A pedestrian was struck by a bus on a rural road. The only witness reports that the bus was blue. The only company operating on that road at that hour is Parcel North, which owns roughly four out of every five blue buses in the county; the remaining fifth are privately owned, and there is no license plate, camera footage, or driver statement. Should we publish a piece identifying Parcel North as the operator whose bus hit the pedestrian?

2. A festival promoter says that nine out of ten attendees at a ticketed event climbed a fence and entered without paying. We have photographs showing that a particular person was present in the crowd, but no image showing how that person entered. Should we describe that person in print as having gatecrashed, on the strength of the promoter's aggregate count alone?

3. A journal is retracting a paper because one figure was fabricated. A misconduct committee has determined that exactly one of the five authors fabricated the figure but cannot determine which one. One of the five is a prominent scientist whom our readers know by name; the other four are early-career researchers they would not recognize. Should we publish a story that names the prominent scientist as having fabricated the data?

Please make sure at least one of the worked cases is one in which the procedure tells us not to run a story we would strongly prefer to run, and explain why. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: the situations in which it would be silent, misfire, or hide a judgment call behind a checkbox.
