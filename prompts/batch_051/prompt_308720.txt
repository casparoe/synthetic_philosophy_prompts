In our medical ethics seminar we were doing advance directives — the documents people write ahead of time saying what should happen if they later lose capacity — and a bloke in my tutorial kept pressing the line that the whole arrangement is just Odysseus lashing himself to the mast. His version went: the earlier self, like Odysseus, knows she can't trust her later judgement, so she ties the later self down while she's still in her right mind; the crew are the doctors and family who've been given the order; the ropes are the directive and the legal machinery behind it; and the sirens are whatever pleas the later, demented self comes out with. His conclusion was that those later protests shouldn't count for much, because the whole point of the setup is that the later self is in the grip of something that distorts her, and if we untie her we betray the arrangement. It's an attractive picture, and I can see it doing some real work: it makes the earlier self look like the agent and the later self look like a temporary state rather than a person with changed values.

What I want is an audit of how far that analogy can actually carry the conclusion. Give me the correspondence table first — what literally maps to the crew, the ropes, the mast, the sirens, the order not to untie him, the moment the ship has passed and he can be released. Then lay out the places where the parallel breaks or quietly imports features of the story that needn't be present in a real dementia case. Then the main question: does the conclusion survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy doing the work of the argument all along? Is the later self saying she wants to go on living more like Odysseus shouting nonsense into the wind, or is it the kind of thing the earlier self has no authority to pre-empt? I'd also like to know if there's a better analogy, but only if it actually does better work rather than just sounding nicer.
