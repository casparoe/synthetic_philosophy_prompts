I'm not an academic, just someone who reads a lot of social-science and medical papers on my own, and I keep running into a pattern I can't square with itself. In the fields I read, a study is usually treated as a real finding — published, cited, reported to the public — if it clears the bar of p < .05. The official story is that this means: if there were no effect, you would see data at least this extreme less than 5% of the time, so you reject the null hypothesis and go on as if the effect exists.

But the same researchers will insist, often in the same breath, that a p-value is not the probability that the hypothesis is true, and that p < .05 does not mean there is a 95% chance the result will hold up. They say significance is about the long-run behavior of a testing procedure, not about how much anyone should believe this particular claim.

And yet the practice seems to do exactly what the official story says cannot be done. The moment a study crosses the threshold, the field starts believing the specific claim — it enters meta-analyses, textbooks, and press coverage. Then a replication with a larger sample comes along and finds p = .40, and suddenly everyone talks as if the original effect has been debunked, even though the failed replication is itself just one more piece of data with its own long-run error rates. If the p-value was never a statement about whether the claim is true, why does one failed replication flip the verdict so decisively? And if a single low p-value licenses belief in a claim, why doesn't a single high p-value license disbelief just as firmly?

I also wonder where the base rate of true effects enters. If a field tests hundreds of barely plausible ideas, then even an honestly run procedure should produce mostly false positives among the findings that cross p < .05. The practice seems to acknowledge this and then ignore it.

What I am asking is this: reconstruct the theory of evidence that this practice quietly presupposes. Is it a frequentist view, where evidence is about the long-run error rates of procedures and one is never entitled to a degree of belief in any single finding? Is it a Bayesian view, where evidence updates degrees of belief and priors and base rates have to enter somewhere? Or is it an incoherent blend of the two? I would like you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, and to say where it is underdetermined — that is, where a clear-headed frequentist and a clear-headed Bayesian would endorse the same practice, such as preregistration or replication, for different reasons.

Then tell me what would have to change in how findings are reported and treated if the presupposed view were replaced by a coherent alternative, whether consistently Bayesian or consistently error-control.
