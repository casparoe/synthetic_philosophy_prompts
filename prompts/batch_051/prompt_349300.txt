I have been working through the Hilbert's Hotel argument in a seminar on the mathematics and philosophy of the infinite, and I want to test how much of its force comes from one unstated assumption: that the manager may rearrange infinitely many guests in a single act. In the familiar story, a hotel has one room for each natural number, all occupied, and a new arrival is accommodated by moving the guest in room n to room n+1 for every n simultaneously. Consider a variant governed by what I will call finitist house rules. At any one time, the manager may issue an instruction only if it names finitely many particular rooms and prescribes, for exactly those rooms, a permutation of their current occupants among themselves; every guest not named stays put. The manager may issue further instructions later, but no single directive may quantify over all infinitely many guests at once.

Under these rules, analyze two cases. In the first, the manager may issue only finitely many instructions overall. In the second, he may issue a countably infinite sequence of instructions, one after another, with no final instruction. Can the hotel accommodate a single new arrival in either case? Can it accommodate a countably infinite busload? Pinpoint where the standard argument breaks down: is the difficulty that there are infinitely many rooms and guests, or is it that the standard solution requires an infinite act of rearrangement rather than merely an infinite collection? In the sequential case, does the sequence of configurations converge to a state in which a room is genuinely free, or does the free room exist only at infinity, never realized at any finite stage? I am especially interested in what a finitist about completed infinities should, and should not, be licensed to conclude from the breakdown.
