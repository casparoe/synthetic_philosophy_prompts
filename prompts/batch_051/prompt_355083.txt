In a reading group on vector-space models of meaning, a colleague cut off a debate about whether cosine similarity between word embeddings really deserved to be called semantic similarity by quoting J. R. Firth's old slogan: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." His claim was that the matter was thereby settled: if two words occur in sufficiently similar contexts, they are similar in meaning, nearest neighbours in embedding space can therefore be read as near-synonyms, and a vector offset like king − man + woman landing near queen can be treated as a genuine analogy rather than as a parochial artefact of the model. To me the sentence seems to be doing several different jobs at once — a methodological rule about how to build representations, an empirical generalisation about corpora, and a philosophical claim about what meaning consists in — and I doubt one precise thesis can do all of them.

I would like you to unpick Firth's sentence, together with its modern glosses such as "the distributional hypothesis", into the distinct precise theses it might be used to assert. For each reading, say whether it is roughly trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and say which of those verdicts are settled within computational semantics and which are genuinely contested (for example, disputes over antonymy, reference, or what gets lost when only distribution is modelled). Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually license my colleague's inference from distributional similarity to semantic similarity, and which common reading would make his inference a non sequitur. Where the field is divided, please present the disagreement as disagreement rather than implying there is a consensus; I want to leave the meeting able to say precisely which part of his argument survives which interpretation.
