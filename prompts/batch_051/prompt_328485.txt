I'm a software engineer, and when I think about decision problems I tend to think in terms of explicit probabilities, likelihood ratios, and expected values. I want to work through a puzzle that I can't resolve cleanly.

Take the standard Pascal's mugging scenario. A stranger asks me for $20 and says that if I refuse he will inflict an astronomically large amount of suffering — specifically, 10^20 years of torture on 10^20 people, which is 10^40 person-years. Count the disutility of the threatened outcome linearly as one unit of disvalue per person-year, so the threatened outcome costs 10^40 utils. The ordinary expected-value calculation says I should pay if the probability that the threat is genuine is greater than about 2 × 10^-39, since that probability times 10^40 exceeds 20. The familiar replies are that utilities should be bounded, or that the prior probability of such a specific, complex claim is (or should be) far smaller than one over the harm, or that tiny probabilities of enormous harms should not be treated literally.

Now vary the setup: the mugger can produce a bounded amount of verifiable evidence before demanding the money. He says he will predict the outcomes of n flips of a fair coin, writes his predictions down in front of me, and is correct on all n. Then he makes his threat. I model the coin flips like this: under the hypothesis that he has the power he claims over those 10^40 person-years, the probability of a correct prediction on each flip is 0.99; under the null hypothesis that he is an ordinary person, it is 0.5. Before seeing the flips, I assign probability 10^-60 to the power hypothesis (deliberately far below one over the harm, to be generous to the skeptical reply).

Please work through the following. (a) Give the posterior probability of the power hypothesis after n correct predictions as a function of n. (b) Evaluate the expected cost of refusing at n = 20, 80, 160, and 200, using both linear utility and a bounded utility function in which the disutility of an outcome is log(1 + x), with x measured in person-years for the threat and in dollars treated as disvalue for the $20 payment (any consistent log base is fine; the threshold does not depend on it). (c) Find the smallest n at which the clean expected-value calculation says to pay under each utility function, and compare them.

Now the philosophical problem. At the n where linear expected value recommends paying, the demonstration itself has probability 2^-n under the ordinary-person null. By the time the bounded-utility version recommends paying, the demonstration is more extraordinary still. At that point, aren't alternative explanations — a rigged coin, a hidden-camera prank, a hallucination, or a glitch in my perception — overwhelmingly more likely than the mugger hypothesis? The best of those alternatives predict the observed run nearly as well as, or better than, the power hypothesis does, and they start with vastly higher priors than 10^-60. If I include them, the mugger hypothesis can get drowned out even though it fits the flips perfectly. Work through whether and when the expected-value calculation self-undermines in this way. What does this variation show about the standard replies to Pascal's mugging — are they ad hoc patches, or is the revised case the norm once a claim can be partly tested?
