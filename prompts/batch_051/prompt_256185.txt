I have been thinking about a line of reasoning on my own, without any formal training in philosophy or decision theory, and I would like someone to check it carefully. It concerns whether it can be rational to cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma — a situation where each person is better off refusing to cooperate no matter what the other does, yet both would be better off if both cooperated — with a stranger, where there is no reputation, no punishment, and no prospect of meeting again. The conclusion feels too strong, so I suspect one of the steps fails, but I cannot find the mistake. Here is the argument as clearly as I can state it.

(1) What I do in a single anonymous prisoner's dilemma is not an isolated fact about that moment; it is the expression of a standing disposition or general decision rule that I carry with me into all similar situations.

(2) The world is large, and there are very many people who are importantly similar to me: they reason in roughly the way I do, occupy roughly similar circumstances, and face the same sort of one-shot dilemma.

(3) Therefore, my cooperating in this case is evidence that those similar people are cooperating in their cases, and my defecting is evidence that they are defecting; by choosing one way rather than the other, I raise or lower the expected number of cooperative acts performed across that entire group.

(4) I am better off living in a world where the people similar to me cooperate than in one where they defect, because in many of their dilemmas I — or people whose welfare I depend on — would be on the receiving end of the cooperation, and I would also benefit indirectly from living among more cooperative people.

(5) Because the group of similar people is very large, the total expected benefit to me of their being moved toward cooperation across all those encounters outweighs the direct cost of cooperating in this particular encounter while my counterpart defects.

(6) So, even in a single, anonymous, never-to-be-repeated prisoner's dilemma, I ought to cooperate rather than defect.

Is this argument valid as it stands? If not, I want to know exactly which step fails and what goes wrong at that point. I am especially suspicious of step (3): if those other people's choices are already fixed before I decide, it is strange to say that my choice changes how many of them cooperate, yet conditioning on my choice does seem to change what I should expect about them. But the mistake may lie somewhere else. If the argument is valid, then I want help understanding how to accept the conclusion, since it seems to recommend cooperating with people who will take advantage of me.
