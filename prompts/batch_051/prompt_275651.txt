I'm putting together next term's lecture on meaning and interpretation, and I want to hang the whole thing on the frame/outer/inner distinction from Gödel, Escher, Bach, which I still think is genuinely usefull for students. My rough gloss for the lecture is this: the outer message is the physical marks or sounds themselves; the inner message is the thought or content being expressed; and the frame message is the implicit signal that tells the receiver this is a message at all, and roughly how to decode it — the bottle and the handwriting as much as the sentence. None of the three does the work alone, which is rather the point.

But I keep toying with an argument in my head that seems to show the whole picture collapses into a regress, and I can't decide weather it's a real problem or the same old trick in new clothes. The argument, set out as I'd put it on the board: (1) The meaning of a message can't be identified with its outer message, since the very same marks can mean different things under different conventions. (2) What settles which convention is in play is the frame message; without it the inner message isn't available. (3) Yet a frame message is itself a message — one can miss it entirely, misread it, or fail to take it as a message. (4) So the frame message's own meaning must likewise be fixed by a further frame message, and so on without end. (5) Since no finite act of interpretation can run through an infinite chain of frame messages, no message ever has determinate meaning. (6) But plainly we do understand notes in bottles; therefore meaning must be supplied from outside the system of messages altogether, by something that interprets without itself being a message.

My instinct is that (4) is where the cheat is, but I want to know exactly what to write on a handout, and I also half-wonder whether (2) and (3) are already more tendentious than they look. So: say which step or steps fail, give the strongest reading of the questionable step on which it might still be true, and sketch the positive picture of where a message's meaning is located that survives the critique. And if you'd answer, could you do it in British English?
