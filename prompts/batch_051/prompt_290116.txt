In the essay where she calls for the abolition of political parties, Simone Weil argues that a political party is not a setting in which independent thought can survive: a party is organized for collective pressure, for winning and keeping power, and for imposing a party line on its members, and therefore every party, just by being a party, moves toward totalitarianism and should be abolished. In her discussions of rootedness and the needs of the soul, she argues that one of the deepest human needs is real, active participation in a collectivity that keeps a past alive and opens toward a future, and that this rootedness has to be restored. These two claims look in tension: one says a certain kind of collective membership is intrinsically corrupting, while the other says genuine participation in collective life is a basic need. Are they reconcilable, and at what cost? Give the strongest readings on which they come apart, distinguishing among different senses of collective life, different contexts of judgment, and a possible change in Weil's view. Then say which of those readings are honest rather than special pleading, and give a verdict on what Weil would have to say if pressed on why a party could not provide rootedness while a workplace, a neighborhood, or a country could.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. One of the five items should state how confident you are in your overall verdict and which point you are least sure of.
