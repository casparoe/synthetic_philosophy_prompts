A friend of mine from my old institute — he was an X-ray crystallographer, so he likes symmetry arguments — has got very into combinatorial game theory since he retired and has invented a board game he keeps calling "provably fair." The game is finite, both players have identical pieces and the same moves available, there's no dice and no hidden information, and the rules are written so draws cannot happen. But he also claims something stronger: in *any* game of that kind the first player can never be worse off. His proof goes roughly like this. Suppose the second player had a winning strategy. The first player opens with a meaningless move in an out-of-the-way corner, then simply pretends to be the second player and follows that strategy. If the strategy ever tells him to play on the square the meaningless move already occupies, he plays another meaningless move somewhere else and keeps going; since the position the second player would have faced is otherwise unchanged, the borrowed strategy should still win. Therefore the second player can't have had a winning strategy, and with draws impossible that seems to hand the first player the win. I've heard this style of argument before — I can't rememeber what it's called — and it always felt faintly like a shell game.

Here's what bothers me. In his game, a piece on the board isn't automatically an asset. Some squares are poisonous in the sense that a piece you placed there can later be turned against you, and there are positions where you'd be happier if an earlier move had never been made at all. If that's right, then the "meaningless" first move isn't guaranteed to be harmless; it might be a small time bomb. Does the proof still go through? I want to know what exactly the argument needs to assume about the game for the trick to work, and whether a symmetric, perfect-information, no-draw game can even in principle favor the second player, or whether the argument rules that out. I care less about the verdict on his particular game than about pinning down where the reasoning quietly grabs its premise.
