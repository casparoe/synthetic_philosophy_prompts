I'm auditing a philosophy of biology course on biological individuality, and one move keeps coming up in seminar: people call ant colonies (or honeybee colonies) 'superorganisms' and treat that as more than a metaphor. The analogy gets made to do real work. I've heard someone argue that since natural selection acts on the colony as a unit, the colony is the genuine individual; and I've heard someone else brush off worker sterility by saying workers are just somatic cells of a colonial body, so an ant that doesn't reproduce is no more puzzling than a liver cell that doesn't reproduce. I half-buy it, but I also suspect the organism side of the comparison is quietly importing features a colony might not have; its a heavy load for one word to carry.

What I'd like is an audit of the analogy. Push the mapping as far as it'll go: what corresponds to what — queen as germline, workers as soma, pheromone trails as a nervous system, colony fission as reproduction, maybe the nest as a body? Then say where the mapping actually breaks, which of those breaks matter for the conclusions people want to draw, and which are just loose ends. Does calling a colony a superorganism commit you to treating it as an organism in the same sense as an ant is, or is it vivid shorthand for very tight cooperation? And if the colony-as-organism argument works, does it also make a holobiont like you-plus-your-gut-microbiome an organism, or is that a different case? Ultimately I'm trying to figure out whether this analogy can carry the weight it's being asked to bear, or whether it just names the thing it's supposed to prove — and if it can't, is there a better analogy that captures what's interesting without begging the question?
