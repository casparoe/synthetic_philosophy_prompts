I'm a novelist, and I've been trying to write down what I actually believe about moral progress — the expanding-circle business — because it's the unstated engine of most of my books, and I want the machinery to actually cohere. Before I show this list to my reading group I'd like a consistency audit. Here's where I've landed:

(1) The long arc of moral progress is real, and it mostly consists in widening the boundary of who matters: kin, tribe, nation, strangers, animals. That widening is the main kind of moral improvement there is.

(2) And the widening is a discovery, not an invention. When people finally registered that enslaved people or women or animals could suffer, they weren't deciding to count them; they were noticing something that had already been true all along.

(3) But I also think the boundary is drawn by attention, and the main instrument of attention is narrative. A novel can put you inside a life you'd otherwise pass over. So the circle grows mostly through stories, not through arguements.

(4) The engine of that growth is sentiment. You don't get someone to include a stranger by handing them a better principle; you get there by making them feel the stranger's life from the inside.

(5) At the same time, sentiment is biased toward the near and familiar, and narrative empathy can be hijacked — I've wrote charming characters who should not be sympathized with, and watched readers do it anyway. So narrative empathy needs some impersonal check, some idea of counting everyone equally, or it curdles.

(6) But I don't want the endpoint of that process. A world where a mother feels exactly as much for a stranger's child as her own is not the moral ideal; it's a world without novels worth writing. Particular loves are load-bearing.

I genuinely can't tell where the strain is, if there is any. Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and which single revision would clean up the most? Rank the tensions by severity. And give it to me bottom-line-first: open with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
