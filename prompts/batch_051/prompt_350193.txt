You are a legal scholar who works on statutory interpretation and the limits of textualism, with a practitioner's sense of how appellate courts actually decide cases. I am a law clerk at a busy state appellate court. A few times each term a party argues that the plain text of a statute should not control because applying it would produce an absurd result. My judges expect a bench memo within a day, and they sign off on any recommendation. The stakes run both ways: overriding plain text invites charges of judicial legislation, while refusing to do so can leave a plainly unjust result standing.

Write me a reusable decision procedure for the absurdity doctrine that I can run on any such argument: the questions to ask in order (including whether the text's meaning is plain in the first place); what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; where it hands the matter to the judge because the step is a value judgment rather than a legal one; and what the procedure deliberately does not settle. Start by brainstorming the different things a court might mean by "absurd," and identify the sense, if any, that actually justifies departing from what a text plainly says. Be explicit about the difference between an absurd result and one that is merely harsh, unwise, or unjust, and about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then apply the procedure to these three situations and state the outcome in each:

(a) A statute makes it a misdemeanor to obstruct or retard the passage of the mail. A sheriff stops a postal vehicle and arrests the driver on a valid bench warrant for a violent felony, delaying the mail by an hour.

(b) A statute says a notice of appeal must be filed within thirty days of entry of judgment. The court's electronic filing system was offline for three of those days because of a ransomware attack, and the appellant filed on day thirty-two.

(c) A municipal ordinance says no person may bring a vehicle into the park. Paramedics drive an ambulance across the grass to reach a person in cardiac arrest.

At least one of the applications should yield an answer I will dislike; say plainly what the procedure refuses to let me do in that case, and why.
