I'm a retired physicist (fluid mechanics, mostly), and I've been using my retirement to get into philosophy of action, which I'm finding harder than turbulence ever was. The Daoist idea of wu-wei is what pulled me in, but the paradox has me stuck: the advice seems to be that you act best when you don't try, but the moment you set out to not try, you're manifestly trying in a all too familiar way. I want to get underneath that by getting clear on the word "trying" itself.

Please act as a professional philosopher of action, maybe one who works on skill and expertise, think carefully, and start by briefly brainstorming two or three candidate analyses before committing to one. Then give me your best working definition of "trying" — or "effortful trying," if you think that's the better category — precise enough that someone could in principle sort hard cases with it. My physicist's first pass would be that trying is what a control system does when it holds a set point and spends energy to reduce the error; but that makes a thermostat a tryer, which seems wrong, so the definition has to beat that. Then run your definition through my cases and say explicitly where it misclassifies, gives no answer, or makes you bite a bullet, and revise or defend as needed. Cases: (1) a concert pianist in performance, playing a passage that a year ago she could barely get through; (2) that same pianist a year ago, grinding out the passage in the practice room; (3) an insomniac trying to fall asleep; (4) a meditator who has been told simply to let go, and is now trying not to try to let go; (5) a housecat stalking a bird; (6) a nervous speaker who takes a beta-blocker before the talk so that staying calm won't require effort; (7) a procrastinator who says he has been trying to start his paper for six hours but has written nothing. Once the verdicts are in, tell me whether wu-wei is best understood as skilled automaticity, as a special kind of trying after all, or as something the whole trying/automaticity distinction can't capture.
