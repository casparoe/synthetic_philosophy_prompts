I am a psychiatry resident, and over the past few months I have been keeping notes on what I actually think when I have to write phrases like "the patient expressed a delusional belief." In the interests of consistency, I have been collecting my own verdicts on a set of cases, and I would like to know whether there is a single account of what it is to believe a delusion that captures them — or whether some of my verdicts must be revised.

Here are the cases.

1. Capgras with conduct. A patient tells me, with evident distress, that her husband has been replaced by an identical impostor. She offers small differences: the impostor's hands are wrong, his manner slightly unfamiliar. She has twice telephoned the police and now refuses to sleep in the same bed as him. I cannot help but say that she believes this.

2. Capgras without conduct. Another patient with the same Capgras delusion will say, when I ask, that his son has been replaced. He delivers the sentence calmly, then returns to talking about television. He continues to live with the son, accepts meals from him, and has never once acted on the claim. This feels to me less like a belief than like something he assents to only inside the interview.

3. Cotard. A patient states that she is dead and has been dead for several months. She says this with apparent conviction, yet she eats, complains about the temperature, and asks about her discharge date. I want to call this a belief, but it does not seem to do anything in her life, and "believing she is dead" does not feel like the same kind of state as "believing the train is late."

4. Mirrored-self misidentification. A patient insists that the person he sees in a mirror is not him but a stranger who resembles him and follows him from mirror to mirror. He avoids reflective surfaces and has once smashed a mirror. I call this a belief.

5. Fregoli-type misidentification. A patient is convinced that several nurses and visitors are actually her former husband in disguise, monitoring her. She confronts them, warns other people, and writes letters about it. The claim is wildly improbable, but it regulates her fear and planning the way beliefs do; I call it a belief.

6. Denial of paralysis after a stroke. A patient with a paralyzed left arm does not spontaneously complain about it. When I ask him to move it, he says it is fine, or that he is tired, or that the arm on the bed belongs to someone nearby, and the explanation changes each time. I do not want to call these delusional beliefs; they feel like improvised rationalizations under questioning rather than anything he held before I asked.

The rough principle I have been using is functional: a delusional claim counts as a belief when it is stable, held with some degree of conviction, and plays the ordinary causal role of a belief in the person's reasoning and behavior; otherwise it is something weaker — an avowal, a passing report, or a confabulation. That principle gets cases 1, 4, and 5 as beliefs and case 6 as a non-belief, and it leaves me uneasy about cases 2 and 3. But I can see that it may be ad hoc, and it has a troubling consequence: the very same sentence — "my son has been replaced" — would cross from non-belief to belief if the patient started acting on it tomorrow, which sits badly with the idea that what distinguishes belief from non-belief should be a state the person is in, not their downstream behavior.

I have also been reading about two-factor theories of delusion formation — the idea that a monothematic delusion typically requires both an anomalous experience (for example, a felt loss of familiarity) and a second, impairing factor that prevents the person from rejecting the strange explanation of that experience. I would like to know whether this sort of account can be extended to address my actual question: whether, and in what sense, the person believes the delusion. Is there a principle, possibly a two-factor one, that delivers all of my verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I overturn? And what does the resulting account say about a further case I have been reluctant to decide: a patient with a fixed guilt delusion who says he has caused a terrible catastrophe but cannot specify what it was, is untroubled day to day, and never mentions it unless the delusion is raised?

Please state the principle precisely, connect each clause to the cases, say explicitly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and defend the tradeoffs rather than leaving the matter undecided.
