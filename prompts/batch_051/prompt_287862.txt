For a school project on decision-making under uncertainty, I have been reading about the disagreement between Rawls and Harsanyi over whether rational parties behind a veil of ignorance would choose principles by maximin or by expected-utility reasoning. I would appreciate help thinking through a concrete counterfactual.

Suppose that in 2026 a medium-sized parliamentary democracy with an independent constitutional court amends its constitution so that every public decision meeting three conditions—(1) it is expensive to reverse, (2) it affects more than 10,000 people, and (3) no broadly accepted scientific probabilities are available—must be made by choosing the option whose worst possible outcome is least bad. The amendment explicitly forbids justifying such decisions by expected utility, cost–benefit analysis, or minimax regret whenever these methods rely on probability estimates. Courts are empowered to strike down decisions if the official record assigns probabilities to outcomes.

I am less interested in whether this rule is, in principle, better than its alternatives than in a reasoned prediction of how the next forty years would unfold. Please trace the mechanisms rather than just give a verdict. Specifically:

What kinds of environmental, public-health, and infrastructure decisions would look different under this rule, and which would look the same? What incentives does the rule create for agencies to frame probabilities as unknown in order to trigger it, or to dress up expected-value reasoning as worst-case analysis? How might courts police the boundary between decisions under risk and decisions under ignorance? Would the rule make policy uniformly more cautious, or could the maximin option sometimes be riskier along less salient dimensions? Finally, would the rule likely survive, be narrowed through litigation, or be formally repealed?

Please make your confidence explicit for each link in the causal chain. Which effects follow mostly from the rule's logic, and which depend on surrounding facts—such as judicial independence, the degree of scientific consensus, or politicians' fear of blame for rare disasters? What would have to be true for your central prediction to be wrong?
