My employer is considering replacing annual performance reviews for our operations team with a forecasting-based bonus scheme. Each quarter, every employee would assign probabilities to a shared set of company outcomes: customer churn, warehouse error rates, on-time delivery, and quarterly revenue. Each forecast would be scored by the Brier score at the end of the quarter, and bonuses would rise monotonically with average score, with no cap. Forecasts may be revised as often as one likes; only the final pre-resolution forecast counts. The stated rationale is that this pays people for the accuracy of their beliefs rather than for how managers perceive them. Provide a reasoned analysis of how this system would actually behave. What does it incentivize beyond simply forming accurate beliefs, who is best placed to exploit it first, and what moves do they make? Pay particular attention to the fact that the people being scored can also act on the outcomes they are predicting. Which failure modes would persist even if the scoring rule were strictly proper in the technical sense, designed so that one's expected score is maximized by reporting one's true probabilities, and what does that reveal about the assumption that accuracy is the only epistemic good worth rewarding?
