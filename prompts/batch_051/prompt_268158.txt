In my previous life as a bench scientist I was used to concepts that either applied cleanly or could be operationalized, and I find the concept of corruption frustratingly resistant to both. I have been working through it by way of the 1997 East Asian financial crisis. The standard Western and IMF diagnosis was that the affected economies suffered from crony capitalism: relationship-based lending, government-directed credit, and personal ties between business and state that bred inefficiency and corruption. Several Asian leaders and some scholars replied that these practices were long-standing institutions of reciprocity and mutual obligation, closer to gift exchange than to bribery, and that the Western critique treated unfamiliar social forms as corrupt only by assuming that Western standards are universal.

Analyze this episode as a case study in what counts as corruption. I want a disciplined mapping rather than a retelling of the crisis, whose broad economic history I know. Distinguish bribery, clientelism, gift exchange, and influence, and say which features of the episode each concept genuinely captures and which it distorts. Then work out what a universalist account of corruption would have to claim to make the IMF diagnosis legitimate, what a locally bounded account would claim instead, and what the episode in turn shows about whether a cross-cultural standard of corruption is defensible.
