In an aesthetics seminar I have been working through a debate about whether photographs are transparent: the claim that when you look at a photograph of, say, a late relative, you do not merely see a picture of her—you see her, in something like the way you see a face in a mirror, because the image is formed by a mechanical, light-sensitive process that carries information from the scene rather than being routed through an artist's beliefs and decisions. The rival view is that "transparency" is at best a metaphor: a photograph is a representation the viewer interprets, and its causal dependence on the scene is just a fact about how the representation was produced, not a kind of seeing. I find myself pushed around by examples—a live parking-garage security feed feels like seeing, a heavily composited advertisement plainly does not—and last week's session got completely derailed once someone raised synthetically generated photorealistic images and the question of whether a camera with no photographer behind it still shows us the world. I want to stop litigating cases one at a time.

Write me the complete desiderata for an adequate theory of photographic transparency: what such a theory has to explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the empirical facts about optical transfer and image formation it cannot contradict—with the reasoning behind each requirement, and a marking of which are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one side of the debate. Then flag any pairs or larger sets of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say why. Finally, in brief, score the three usual starting positions—the literal-seeing-through view, the photograph-as-ordinary-depiction view, and the view that photographs are a distinctive kind of representation with one foot in perception—only to show which requirement does the most work against each. Keep the focus on the requirements themselves; I care much more about the criteria than about the scoreboard.
