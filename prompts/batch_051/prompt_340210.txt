Suppose a country's social assistance legislation contains the following provision, headed "Work search condition":

"18. — (1) A person to whom an out-of-work allowance is payable shall, as a condition of entitlement, take all reasonable steps to obtain paid employment. (2) A person who fails, without good cause, to comply with subsection (1) is liable to a sanction reducing the allowance, in whole or in part, for a period not exceeding thirteen weeks."

The provision was drafted in the mid-1990s, when the drafters' picture of a job search was a claimant telephoning employers, filling in paper application forms, and attending a job centre.

Now consider this case. A claimant, R, has received the allowance for five months. He has written a computer program that scrapes job advertisements from the internet, rewrites his curriculum vitae for each vacancy, and submits applications on his behalf. R checks the program's log once each evening and has set criteria by which the program accepts or declines interview invitations; beyond that he does no job searching himself. His work coach decides that R has not personally taken any steps to obtain paid employment and applies a sanction. R appeals, arguing that the statute requires only that the steps be taken, not that they be taken by human hand, and that building and running the program was itself a step — indeed the most effective step available to him.

Does section 18 apply to R? Argue the case from three angles: the ordinary meaning of the text, the evident purpose of the condition, and what the provision's drafters would most likely have said if asked about automated job applications. Then tell me which of those approaches should decide the case, and state plainly whether R should be sanctioned. I want a committed answer: take a definite position and give the reasons for it, rather than laying out the possible readings and leaving me to choose.
