I have been following an argument in a philosophy of mathematics reading group about whether mathematical objects really exist. The Platonist line is that ordinary mathematical statements should be read at face value: a sentence such as there are infinitely many primes appears to assert the existence of primes, and the practice of mathematics is hard to make sense of without treating numbers and functions as real, abstract objects. The nominalist I keep arguing with counters with an analogy she returns to constantly: mathematical objects are like fictional characters. Just as the claim that Holmes lives on Baker Street is true in the story without there being, in reality, a detective named Holmes, the claim that there is no largest prime can be regarded as true in the story of arithmetic without there being, in reality, any prime numbers. Mathematics, on her view, is a useful fiction, and the analogy is doing real argumentative work: it is meant to let her keep the usefulness, rigor, and apparent truth of mathematics while denying that its apparent objects exist.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than simply defend or attack either side. Begin by making the mapping explicit: what corresponds to what, comparing mathematical statements with statements inside a fiction, existence claims taken at face value with existence claims relativized to a story, axioms and definitions with an author's stipulations, and mathematical practice with the practice of reading and reasoning about a fictional world. Then identify the points where the mapping strains or breaks. Candidates you should assess include: fictions typically involve concrete particulars at spatiotemporal locations, while mathematical objects are supposed to be abstract and causally inert; fictions are created by authors at particular times, while mathematical results are usually taken to be discovered and to hold necessarily; there is ordinarily a canonical text for a fiction but no single canonical story of mathematics; and, perhaps most seriously, mathematics is applied in physics in a way that no work of fiction is. Separate the disanalogies that are merely incidental from the ones that do damage to the intended conclusion. Say what the analogy quietly imports into mathematics—for example, a story-relative notion of truth without an actual story-teller, or the principle that engaging with a discourse does not commit one to its objects—and assess whether those imports are defensible. Finally, state whether the nominalist's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy available to her.
