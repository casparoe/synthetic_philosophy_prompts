Lately I’ve been trying to use Nietzsche’s essay On the Use and Abuse of History to make sense of the statue-toppling stuff, especially the 2020 toppling of Edward Colston’s statue in Bristol. This is the passage that’s been stuck in my head:

We do need history, but quite differently from the jaded idlers in the garden of knowledge, however grandly they may look down on our rude and unpicturesque requirements. In other words, we need it for life and action, not as a convenient way to avoid life and action, or to excuse a selfish life and a cowardly or base action.

I don’t want the usual culture-war takes. I want you to actually run with this as an analytical lens. Take the Colston statue case: statue of a slave-trader, pulled down and dumped in the harbour, then later recovered and put in a museum with the protest signs and the graffitied version of the statue. Use Nietzsche’s claim to analyze what the defenders and the topplers were each doing with history, and where the framework gets it right or starts to crack. For example, does the museum display count as “history for life” in his sense or just a more tasteful version of the same bad historicism? Does the “don’t erase history” side actually fit his criteria any better than the iconoclasts, or is it closer to the jaded idlers in the garden of knowledge thing? I want a disciplined mapping: who or what is serving life, what counts as the “superfluous luxury” here, and where Nietzsche’s lens genuinely has nothing to say about democratic memory, pluralism, or the fact that a statue’s meaning depends on who’s walking past it. Then tell me whether the episode ends up making Nietzsche’s position look useful or too thin.
