I sit on the governing board of a small scholarly association. Our constitution includes an amendment clause stating that any provision of the constitution may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the board, and this clause itself is explicitly subject to amendment like every other. At our last meeting, a faction proposed replacing the two-thirds requirement with a stricter procedure: henceforth, constitutional amendments would require a three-quarters vote plus review by an outside parliamentarian. The proposal was handled under the existing rules and received exactly two-thirds of the votes cast — sufficient under the old standard, but short of the three-quarters it sought to introduce. The chair ruled the motion adopted.

One member objected that the ruling cannot be right, because if the motion really was adopted, then the stricter threshold governed at the moment of its own adoption, so it should have needed three-quarters; yet if the stricter threshold did not apply to the motion, that seems to commit us to saying the amendment clause can never be amended, which is no more plausible. Another replied that the objection conflates the validity of a rule with its content, and that the chair was plainly correct. I have been asked to write a short analysis for the next board meeting. I am less interested in what a parliamentary manual or a particular legal system would dictate than in the underlying conceptual problem: how should one reason about a rule that is being used to change the very procedure by which it is changed, and is there a coherent account of which majority requirement applied to this vote?
