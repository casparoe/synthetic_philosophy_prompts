Philosophers and game theorists working on program equilibrium have spent years at an impasse over a basic point. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma where each player's strategy is a program that can inspect and simulate the other player's source code, there are equilibria in the space of programs in which both players cooperate: each program cooperates just in case the other program would cooperate against it, so neither can unilaterally improve by switching to a different program under those conditions. One side takes this to show that cooperation is compatible with self-interested rationality, and that the standard result — defection as the unique rational move in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma — was an artifact of modeling strategies as atomic choices. The other side replies that this changes the subject: once a strategy can condition on the opponent's entire decision procedure, the game is no longer genuinely one-shot, and the counterfactual about what the other program would do were one to switch is doing question-begging work. The dispute has not moved much. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical or mathematical but constitutive: it is about what may legitimately be held fixed in a formal model of a strategic situation. Diagnose the impasse — is it verbal, substantive, or something else; do the sides differ about facts, about standards of modeling, or about starting intuitions; and is there a hidden formal question about simulation, self-reference, well-definedness, or equilibrium existence doing the real work? Then, given the diagnosis, say what would count as progress and whether we should expect it.
