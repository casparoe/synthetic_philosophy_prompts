I'm a novelist poking around cognitive science for material, and I keep coming back to Bongard problems — those little puzzles with six boxes on the left and six on the right where you have to state the rule that seperates the two groups. The thing that hooks me is that the examples never pin the rule down: any finite set of examples is consistent with wildly different rules, and the rule a person actually finds says more about the priors — the assumptions they walked in with — than about the boxes. I want to smuggle that feeling into a story. Write me a short story in exactly five numbered scenes, no introduction or conclusion, each scene a sentence or two. In each scene a character is infering a rule about someone (a person, a town, a family, whatever) from the examples they've met so far, and a new example arrives that doesn't just complicate the old rule — it shows the old rule was never forced by the old examples to begin with. Make it concrete and human, not about the puzzles themselves; the Bongard structure should be the skeleton, not the subject. By the last scene I want the reader to feel that seeing a pattern in a few cases is always a bet, and that the data never leaves only one story on the table.
