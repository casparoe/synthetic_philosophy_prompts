I'm hoping you can referee a dinner argument between two friends of mine, Mia and Dev, because I'm a software engineer whose never taken a philosophy class and I could follow each of their points but not how they added up. The case they were arguing about was in the news: a developer who, in his early twenties, planted a backdoor in a hospital's billing software. He never used it himself and nothing bad happened as a result, but it was discovered years later, by which time he was a respected security researcher with an otherwise clean record. A judge gave him 18 months anyway.

Their exchange went roughly like this.

(1) Dev said the sentence is punishment theater. The guy obviously won't reoffend, and the case is so rare that it won't change what any actual developer decides — the people who plant hospital backdoors already assume they won't get caught. So there's no deterrence and no incapacitation payoff, just 18 months of ruining someone's life for the sake of a symbol.

(2) Mia said the guy deserved it. Getting his act together later doesn't cancel what he did. A sentence isn't a tool for maximizing good outcomes; some things are owed because of what you did, not because of what they'll produce.

(3) Dev said "owed because of what you did" is just a feeling with a costume on. He only accepts deliberately hurting someone when it stops something worse. If the whole justification is "he deserves it," you're claiming there's a moral requirement that he suffer even when nobody's safer and no future crime is prevented, which is vengeance with a thesaurus.

(4) Mia said that's a dangerous game. If stopping something worse were the whole justification, a judge would be right to sentence an innocent scapegoat whenever that would prevent a riot. Dev doesn't actually believe that, so he's already committed to a backward-looking constraint: only punish the guilty, because they deserve it.

(5) Dev said fine, guilt can be a filter. But desert can't set the length. It tells you the guy may be punished, even that he should be, but not whether it's a fine, six months, or six years. Between "he did wrong" and "18 months" there's a gap, and the judge fills it with deterrence and public fear — so desert is doing far less work than Mia pretends.

(6) Mia said the length is part of desert, not a gap you fill from outside. A hospital backdoor is worse than shoplifting a sandwich, and not because it deters more people. Wrongs come in degrees of blame — the harm risked, the trust violated — and a proportionate sentence tracks those degrees. If Dev admits that ranking exists before deterrence enters the picture, he's admitted desert does more than pick who's eligible.

(7) Dev said okay, take two devs who plant identical backdoors. One gets caught immediately, loses his job, marriage, savings, and spends years unemployable. The other gets caught late and is thriving. Same wrong, so same desert, so on Mia's logic they should get the same sentence. But almost nobody — including most people who consider themselves desert types — would pile another prison term onto the already-ruined first guy. If the judge gives him a break for suffering he's already endured, that's a consequence-based adjustment, not a measurement of blame.

(8) Mia said that's a reason to show mercy at sentencing, not a reason to pretend the sentence was never deserved. A judge can think the ruined guy still deserves the same 18 months but decide the state shouldn't collect every last bit of what it's owed. That doesn't make punishment a prediction problem; it just means desert isn't the only thing a decent judge thinks about.

Please referee this. Go through the exchange move by move and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a one- or two-sentence justification for each rating. Then say whether Mia or Dev is more right overall, and what, if anything, they're both missing. Also, if you think any move is fine on its own but goes wrong as a response to the previous one, say so seperately.
