I have been reading Nietzsche’s The Antichrist next to Schopenhauer’s essay On the Basis of Morality, not for a course but because I wanted to think through a disagreement that keeps bothering me. Schopenhauer argues that an action has genuine moral worth only insofar as it comes from pity — from being directly moved by another person’s suffering and trying to lessen it. Nietzsche says something close to the opposite: pity is a kind of contagion that increases suffering rather than subtracts from it, because it weakens the person who feels it and drags the sufferer’s pain into another life. My question is why they do not at least agree about the basic bookkeeping. Before asking whether pity is noble or degrading, shouldn’t there be a plain causal fact about whether an act of pity leaves the total amount of suffering in the world lower than it was? And wouldn’t that fact simply settle which of them is right? If Nietzsche means that pity adds to the stock of suffering and Schopenhauer means that pity removes some of it, then one of them must have the causal claim wrong, and I keep trying to figure out which one, as though a careful enough observation of people helping others could resolve it.
