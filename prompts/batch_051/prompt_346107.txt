I've settled into a habit in settings where people state their views one after another: hiring panels, comment threads after a controversial post, group chats following a talk, live-tweeted verdicts on a debate. I treat the first few expressions of agreement as a single piece of evidence, roughly equivalent to the first speaker's view, unless one of those early speakers gives an independent-sounding reason, breaks ranks, or noticeably qualifies their agreement. My reasoning is that once someone has committed themselves publicly, the next few people are not really producing fresh evidence; they are partly reacting to the social fact that a position has already been staked out, and once an apparent majority starts to form, further agreement becomes cheap and almost uninformative. So I wait, and I give most of my weight to the first mover and to any later person who visibly departs from the emerging consensus, because departing is costly and therefore carries more information.

I have caught myself applying this even when the group is small and the topic is one where the people involved are genuinely well-informed rather than merely performing confidence. I would like a committed assessment of this policy, not a survey of ways one might think about it. Specifically: under what conditions is treating early agreement as one observation more reliable than treating each stated view as an independent datapoint, and under what conditions does it throw away real information? Is the asymmetry defensible, that is, is agreement generally safer to discount than dissent? And given all that, should I keep the habit, revise it, or drop it, and in what form?
