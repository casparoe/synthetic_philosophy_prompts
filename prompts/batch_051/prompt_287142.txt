I've ended up somewhere in the illusionist neighborhood about phenomenal consciousness, and I want you to push back on it in a serious way. Not the straw version where illusionism says no one is conscious in any sense and there's no experience at all. The version I mean grants that there are access-conscious states, reports, a global workspace, all of that; what it denies is that, over and above that, there is an extra what-it's-like property, the sort of thing people gesture at when they say the lights are on. On my view introspection misrepresents access-conscious processes as having a private, intrinsic, nonrelational feel, and that misrepresentation is itself something the access machinery does, which is why the denial feels so impossible from the inside. The fact that it feels impossible is, as far as I can tell, exactly what the position predicts rather than evidence aginst it, and that is the part that worries me, because I don't want to be holding a view that has eaten all of its own counterevidence.

So here is what I'm after: tell me what would actually count against illusionism, not what the internet treats as a devastating one-liner. I want the empirical results that would be awkward or fatal (the overflow debate, blindsight, anesthesia, psychedelics, split-brain cases, whatever you think is genuinely in the weeds), the thought experiments that bite (zombies, Mary, fading qualia, the meta-problem if it cuts the other way), and the theoretical results that would make the position collapse into either reductive realism or outright eliminativism about consciousness. Sort them from fatal to merely awkward, and for each, say whether a committed illusionist of the Dennett-ish sort, not a caricature, would grant that it counts against them or would just say that's the illusion speaking again. I'd also want to know what illusionism predicts about the mind that its rivals don't, so it could come out looking good rather than merely unfalsifiable — and an honest verdict on whether it has been insulated so heavily that nothing could count against it, and whether for a view of this kind that is a serious defect or just what a philosophical position is. Personally I'd be most moved by a clean dissociation where the access machinery is degraded or absent and the what-it's-like-ness demonstrably stays, or where someone with no access at all to some content still shows all the downstream hallmarks of having a phenomenology; but tell me if I'm weighting that wrong.

And keep it plain prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just write it like an email.
