Consider the color-phi phenomenon. A red dot is flashed briefly on the left, and shortly afterward a green dot is flashed briefly on the right. Observers typically report a single moving dot that changes from red to green roughly halfway along its path—a moment before the green dot has actually appeared. Two kinds of account compete for this result. On a Stalinesque account, the brain delays the relevant experience until the second stimulus has been processed, then inserts the color change into the constructed trajectory: the moving, red-to-green dot is genuinely experienced, only too late. On an Orwellian account, experience runs more or less in real time and contains no such moving, color-changing dot; the coherent report is a memory edited after the fact, a false recollection of what was experienced.

A colleague insists that this is a substantive empirical distinction about the timing of consciousness, not merely a difference in how we choose to describe the same reports. I suspect the two accounts may be indistinguishable in principle, because anything a subject says or does about the display could be attributed either to an earlier experience rewritten afterward or to a later experience constructed before awareness. Is there any observation, experiment, or neural measurement that could actually decide between the Orwellian and Stalinesque accounts? Design the strongest test you can, specify exactly what pattern of results would favor each side, and be explicit about what a confirmatory result would and would not settle. If you conclude that no test can split the two, say precisely which residual difference, if any, keeps them from collapsing into one account.
