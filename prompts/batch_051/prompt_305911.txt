I'm working through Karl Popper's philosophy of science, and I've become stuck on something I read about his treatment of Darwinism. As I understand it, Popper argued at one point that Darwinian theory is not a genuinely testable scientific theory so much as a metaphysical research program. The core worry was that natural selection, stated as the claim that organisms with greater fitness tend to survive and reproduce more successfully than their competitors, can always be shielded from refutation, since any organism that survives can be called fitter after the fact and any organism that fails to survive can be called less fit. The principle would therefore hold no matter what the world is like, and that sounds to me like a devastating objection. I know Popper later qualified the view, but I have never been able to see how the qualification actually repairs the underlying damage.

What I would like to know is this: how should evolutionary theory be restated, at the level of its central explanatory principle, so that it stops being so close to a tautology and becomes the kind of empirically risky theory Popper said science ought to be? A friend pressed back that I am confusing the abstract schema of natural selection with the concrete and falsifiable hypotheses biologists actually derive from it, such as claims about the fossil record, biogeography, or the outcomes of selection experiments. But that response has always felt somewhat evasive to me, because a theory built on an unfalsifiable core would seem to inherit a problem at its foundation even if its upper floors happen to be testable. I would like an answer that genuinely works through this worry instead of simply reporting that Popper changed his mind.
