After our seminar on early modern political thought, two of my classmates got into a dispute about a fragment in Pascal's Pensées — the one often paraphrased as saying that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and that because we have never been able to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just. I listened to the whole exchange and honestly could not referee it. Could you go through it and adjudicate point by point?

(1) Elena began by saying the fragment is, at bottom, an admission that justice is whatever the powerful can enforce. She said the giveaway is the order of explanation: Pascal does not propose making the just strong; he reports that we make the strong just. That means just is a retrospective label the winning side gets to wear, not a standard that constrains who should win.

(2) Marcus replied that this ignores the very claim about force without justice being tyrannical. That claim is normative: it condemns something. If justice were nothing over and above effective power, tyranny would have no content, and Pascal could not use it as an accusation. So he must be keeping an independent standard of justice, even if he is pessimistic about realizing it.

(3) Elena answered that an independent standard that can never be realized is doing no work. Pascal himself says justice is endlessly disputable whereas force is recognized by everyone and not subject to dispute. In practice the only currency that counts is power; calling some of it tyrannical is just disapprobation, and it does nothing to distinguish legitimate force from raw violence in a way anyone could actually apply.

(4) Marcus came back by saying the standard does work, but negatively. Force becomes tyranny when it has cut all ties with law, custom, and inherited title — that is, when it rules as undiluted coercion. Pascal's whole point is to join the two, so that authority rules through recognized right rather than by the sword alone. That joining is a real accomplishment even if the justice side of the alloy is conventional rather than natural.

(5) Elena replied that this just redescribes the problem: the winning faction will always be the one whose claim is afterward certified as lawful, customary, and continuous. If you cannot make the just strong and instead make the strong just, the content of justice will track strength — whichever side prevails writes the justifications. Pascal's remark that custom determines what is accepted as equitable shows he knows this; he is a conventionalist who thinks there is no justice, only settled power with good manners.

(6) Marcus answered that on that reading Pascal would be incoherent, because he could not make the judgment of tyranny at all — a settled constitutional order that coerces would be indistinguishable from a bandit state. Pascal's pessimism about founding justice on reason alone does not mean justice is nothing; it means that in a fallen world the best available thing is an order in which force is constrained and given form by received norms. That is better than civil war, not a fig leaf over it.

Please rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, explain who wins which sub-point and why, say what if anything both parties are missing, and then state who is more right overall.
