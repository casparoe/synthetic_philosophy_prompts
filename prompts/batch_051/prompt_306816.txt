I have been arguing with a psychiatrist friend about a pattern in how delusions are handled, and I cannot find the principle that makes it consistent.

Consider a patient with Capgras delusion who tells his clinician that his wife has been replaced by an impostor. When the clinician writes her notes, she treats the utterance as the report of a belief: the condition is picked out by its content, the patient is said to believe that his wife has been replaced, and the intervention is aimed at this belief. Yet everywhere else in the encounter the same utterance is treated as something quite unlike an ordinary belief. Nobody asks the patient for his evidence; nobody attempts to reason him out of the claim; and he is not criticized or blamed for asserting something so poorly supported. With an ordinary false belief, by contrast — a relative taken in by a scam, say — we do exactly those things: we demand reasons, we argue, and we hold the person partly responsible for having been so credulous. So the practice treats one and the same state as a belief for the purposes of classification and as something other than a belief for the purposes of epistemic response.

A widely discussed account in delusion research, the two-factor theory, appears to sharpen rather than dissolve the difficulty. On this view, the delusion issues from an anomalous experience — here, a powerful feeling that one's spouse is not one's spouse — together with a second factor, a deficit in the system that evaluates candidate beliefs, so that the improbable explanation is accepted uncritically. But notice the strain. The second factor is described as a failure of belief evaluation, which presupposes that what it produces genuinely is a belief, with a belief's normal representational and inferential character. If it is a belief, why does clinical practice withhold the responses that ordinary beliefs attract, and why does the surrounding culture treat the patient as exempt from doxastic criticism? If it is not a belief, then what exactly is the second factor a deficit of, and in virtue of what does the utterance still count as diagnostically reportable as a belief? I want the strongest principle that could justify classifying delusions as beliefs while simultaneously exempting them from the practices of challenge and correction we bring to other false beliefs — and an honest verdict on whether that principle survives, or whether something in the practice or the theory should be revised.
