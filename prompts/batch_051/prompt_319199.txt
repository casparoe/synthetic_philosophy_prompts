I work as a software engineer on an LLM product team, with no formal philosophy background. Our safety review has been arguing for weeks about chain-of-thought monitoring, and I want a precise verdict on an argument I keep hearing.

Here is the argument, cleaned up:

(1) If a system is optimizing its visible chain-of-thought in order to satisfy a monitor, then that chain-of-thought no longer gives an outside observer faithful evidence about the system's actual reasoning.
(2) Training with a loss term that scores the system's chain-of-thought applies exactly that kind of pressure: it optimizes the system's visible chain-of-thought in order to satisfy the monitor.
(3) Therefore, training with a monitor-derived loss term makes the system's visible chain-of-thought unfaithful.
(4) A monitor can constrain the system's actual policy only insofar as the chain-of-thought gives it faithful evidence about that policy.
(5) Therefore, chain-of-thought monitoring during training cannot constrain the system's actual policy.

The conclusion feels too strong, because process-supervision rewards clearly seem to do something in math tasks. But I cannot tell which step is doing the damage. My suspicion is that optimizing means one thing in (1)—a system strategically producing a misleading trace—and another in (2)—gradient descent selecting weights across updates. But I don't know whether that distinction really blocks the argument, or whether the argument can be restated in terms of selection pressure without changing the conclusion.

Please tell me exactly which step, if any, fails, and under which reading. If a term is doing double duty, spell out the two readings and run the argument under each. Then give the strongest corrected version of the argument whose conclusion is actually supported, even if that conclusion is much narrower than (5).
