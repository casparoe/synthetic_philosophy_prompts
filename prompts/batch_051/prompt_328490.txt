I'm a philosophy professor, and I've got a rule I use in arguments that I'm no longer sure about. When somebody supports a claim about what I (or anyone) should do or believe by telling me what I'd conclude if I were fully informed, ideally rational, or scrubbed of my biases, I discount that move all the way to zero and ask them for an argument I can actually check from here. Last week at a reading group, a visitor told me I'd accept his skeptical view about moral responsibility if I had all the relevant psychology and weren't in the grip of folk intuitions. I held the line: the imaginary ideal me is not evidence of anything, so give me the facts or the argument. He said I was refusing the only standpoint that matters. My reasoning for the rule is that nobody, me included, can run the idealized deliberation from where we actually are, so claims about what my perfect counterpart would think are cheap to make and impossible to check — and in practice everyone's idealized interlocutor conveniently ends up agreeing with them. The rule has kept me safe from a lot of bad armchair psychology, but i realize it also puts me at odds with a whole family of views in metaethics and decision theory that cash out what I ought to do or believe in terms of what I'd endorse after full information or ideal reflection. And sometimes I suspect the real reason I like the rule is that it protects my current, biased self from correction by a standard it can't currently appreciate. So: is my policy defensible? Are there conditions under which an appeal to my idealized self is legitimate evidence after all, or should I just demand the ordinary reasons every time? Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion; make the fifth item a note on how confident you are in the first four and which one you're least sure of.
