Draft the document that should emerge from a year-long adversarial collaboration between two scholars of sovereignty who have been arguing past one another.

The two positions are these. The corporate-form theorist holds that the modern state is best analysed as a corporation: an artificial legal person constituted by constitutional instruments, holding bundled powers and assets, run by officeholders, and continuous with the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century chartered trading companies that raised troops, levied taxes and administered justice. On this view, the apparent differences between a state and a firm are differences of degree and of constitutional drafting, not of kind. The public-authority theorist holds that the state is not a corporation except by loose analogy. The state holds a territorial monopoly on legitimate force and is the source of legal personality rather than one legal person among others; a chartered company exercised delegated powers under a continuing sovereign, and when it came to rule a territory it became a state only by ceasing to be a mere company. Citizen and shareholder are different relations.

The document they have agreed to produce should contain the following. First, a list of numbered claims both accept, about the historical record of chartered trading companies and about the institutional technology of the corporate form; these claims should be neither vacuous nor slanted, and should include formulations each side would have resisted in its polemical moments as incomplete. Second, the residual disagreements, each stated once in neutral wording and once in each side's preferred wording, so that each side would sign only its own formulation. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about the case recorded in advance. The statement should separate disagreements that are genuine from disagreements that were only about words, and where a dispute dissolves on clarification, the clarified claim should be folded into the agreed claims.

I suspect the cruxes include at least these four:

1. Winding-up and exit. Whether a state can be dissolved by a decision of those subject to it in the way shareholders may wind up a company, or whether every such decision presupposes a public-law power that no corporate resolution can create. The case both sides accept as bearing on this is the negotiated dissolution of a federation into successor states, with Czechoslovakia's division into the Czech Republic and Slovakia as the paradigm. The corporate-form theorist predicts the process will track a corporate division: partition of assets, apportionment of debts, and transfer of treaty memberships and currency arrangements, with any residual continuity being that of an artificial person. The public-authority theorist predicts the case shows the opposite: borders, nationality, and the extinction of one legal order and the creation of others are acts of public authority with no corporate counterpart.

2. Jurisdiction over non-members. Whether statehood lies on a continuum with the chartered company, or requires a categorical step. The accepted case is the East India Company's exercise of civil and criminal jurisdiction in India over inhabitants who neither held its stock nor contracted with it. The corporate-form theorist predicts this is best explained as a point on a continuum from firm to state, with no non-arbitrary line between them. The public-authority theorist predicts the Company ruled only because it was at once a delegate of a sovereign and in the process of becoming something else; at the point where its rule stabilised, the entity doing the ruling was no longer a mere company.

3. The author of legal personality. Whether the state can be a corporation in the same sense as the bodies whose personality it confers, or whether its role as the author of corporate personality undermines any claim that it is itself a corporation. The accepted case is a sovereign's revocation of a chartered company's charter and the transfer of its governmental powers to a colonial office. The corporate-form theorist predicts revocation will look like the exercise of a principal's reserve powers under a corporate constitution. The public-authority theorist predicts it will look like an act of public law that no corporate instrument could authorise, because the author of legal personality cannot be the creature of the charter it cancels.

4. Historical continuity. Whether the early modern state consolidated by adopting the chartered company's institutional technology, or whether the companies were always instruments of a public authority that preceded and outranked them. The accepted case is the transfer of a chartered company's administrative apparatus, tax rolls and courts to a newly created colonial government. The corporate-form theorist predicts that organisationally little will change, because the underlying legal technology is the same. The public-authority theorist predicts that a structural change occurs: the source of the power shifts from a charter to general public law, and that shift is exactly the difference the continuity thesis misses.

Work with these four, refining the wording as the parties would. If a refinement requires splitting one crux into two, do so within the same schema. Do not include footnotes, references, bibliographies or suggested reading; the document must carry its own warrant.

Produce the deliverable as a single JSON object with the following schema. This JSON object is the statement itself, not a summary of a statement.

- `preamble`: a string of no more than 150 words stating what the collaboration is and what the signatories hope the document will be used for.
- `agreed_claims`: an array of strings; each string is one numbered claim the two parties sign without reservation, with the number included in the string.
- `disagreements`: an array of objects, each with the keys `issue`, `neutral_statement`, `corporate_form_wording` and `public_authority_wording`. `issue` is a short label. `neutral_statement` is a one-sentence formulation of what is at stake that neither side would reject as a description. The two remaining fields give each side's considered view in wording that side, and only that side, would sign.
- `cruxes`: an array of objects, each with the keys `crux`, `bearing_case`, `corporate_form_prediction` and `public_authority_prediction`. `crux` is the sub-question phrased so that both sides agree a settled answer would move at least one of them. `bearing_case` is the concrete case or body of evidence both accept as relevant. The two prediction fields record, in advance, what each side expects the case to show.

Use British spelling throughout.
