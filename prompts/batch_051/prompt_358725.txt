I am giving a paper next term to a departmental colloquium in epistemology, and I want to prepare for the question period rather than improvise. The thesis I am defending is that Nozick was right in outline but wrong in detail: knowledge of ordinary empirical propositions requires a sensitivity-like modal condition on belief, yet his full truth-tracking conditions, with both sensitivity and adherence, are neither jointly sufficient nor unproblematic. I defend a restricted, method-relative sensitivity condition, applied only to ordinary non-inferential empirical beliefs and understood with a natural ordering on possible worlds so that distant trickster set-ups do not automatically defeat knowledge. My central argument is that the familiar counterexamples to unrelativised sensitivity, such as the grandmother case in which one would still have believed because a solicitous family would have concealed the truth, trade on an unindividuated method; once the method is fixed as, say, direct visual inspection, the alleged failure disappears. I then argue that the remaining counterexamples are largely driven by an unrestricted closure principle, and that abandoning unrestricted closure is a smaller theoretical cost than accepting safety, which I claim lets opportunistic true belief pass as knowledge.

The audience is not friendly. It will include three epistemologists who regard closure under known entailment as close to non-negotiable, two formal epistemologists who are convinced that safety is the correct modal condition and that sensitivity was decisively refuted by the classic counterexamples, and one knowledge-first theorist who suspects that any modal analysis of knowledge replaces an explanatory primitive with a set of conditions that fail in both directions. I would like a question-and-answer prep sheet, not a sympathetic commentary. Please lay out the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones a Nozick-friendly reader would raise; order them roughly by how much damage they would do if I answered badly; phrase each one in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it; give a two-minute reply for each; and mark honestly where that reply is weakest. End with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain what makes it so dangerous. Please write your answer in British English.
