Last year I made a promise to my dying aunt that I would keep her small farm in the family and let my cousin live there rent-free. I made it chiefly to comfort her, not because anyone was relying on me in an ordinary way: she would not be alive to be disappointed or to rearrange anything if I failed. I have since been reading about why promises bind, and two friends I argue with have settled into opposite camps. The first takes the expectation view: roughly, a promise binds because it leads someone to form expectations, trust, or plans, and the promisor wrongs the promisee by failing to make good on that induced reliance. The second takes the normative-power view: roughly, promising is the exercise of a moral power to place oneself under a new obligation, more like making a vow or giving consent than making a prediction, and its binding force does not reduce to whatever reliance one happens to produce.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher working on promissory obligation, one who has been asked to moderate between these two positions rather than to choose a winner. Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the expectation view and the normative-power view on the question of why promises bind. Begin with informal brainstorming notes in which each side states its own view in its strongest, most plausible form and characterizes the other side without caricature. Then write the deliverable: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims that both camps can sign, covering the paradigm data about promising that any theory should explain and the bad arguments both sides disown; (2) a disagreements table stating the residual differences with enough precision that each side accepts the wording of its own position; (3) a list of cruxes, meaning sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side, including whether a promise can bind without inducing any actual expectation or reliance and whether the promisee's acceptance or actual reliance is necessary or a communicated intention to undertake an obligation is sufficient; and (4) a set of imagined cases or tests that both sides agree in advance would bear on each crux, with each side recording its own prediction before the evidence is in. Where a disagreement turns out to be merely verbal, say which part and restate it in neutral terms. Please write the answer in American English.
