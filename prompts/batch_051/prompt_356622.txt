I am preparing a discussion exercise for an adult-education class on Christian theology, and I would like you to write the instrument itself: a sorting quiz that places respondents among three theories of the atonement by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which label they find appealing. The three positions the quiz must sort people into are the penal substitution view, the satisfaction view, and the moral influence view. Please include a scoring key, and write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, with each question introduced inside the prose and numbered only within the sentences.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions. Each question should present a short, concrete scenario drawn from ordinary social life, such as a judge and a broken law, a parent and an unforgiven child, a ruler and an unpaid debt, or a community and an unrepentant wrongdoer, followed by two or three possible responses. None of the question stems or answer options may use the names of the theories or their technical vocabulary; someone who has never heard the labels should be able to answer every question purely on the basis of the scenarios. The questions should be diagnostic rather than direct, so none may ask, even indirectly, whether the respondent believes in one of the named positions.

In designing the questions and the key, work from the underlying logical space rather than from the list of names. The important distinctions are these. First, whether the atoning event primarily changes something in the sinner, such as the sinner's heart, trust, repentance, or love, or something in God, such as God's honor, God's justice, or God's grounds for forgiving. Second, among the views on which something in God is changed, whether what is satisfied is a debt of honor or a legal penalty. The moral influence view treats the cross chiefly as a demonstration that changes the sinner; the satisfaction view treats it as a payment that restores God's honor; the penal substitution view treats it as Christ bearing the penalty that God's law demands from sinners. Include at least a couple of questions engineered specifically to separate the two objective views, since those are the pair respondents and writers most often blur.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto the three named positions, and it should also describe the underlying combinations of commitments, change in whom, debt versus penalty, demonstration versus transaction, so that the key locates the three theories inside the full logical space rather than merely labeling them. Where a cell of that space is not occupied by any of the three theories, say so and give a brief diagnosis of why: because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply not a developed position. If the answer patterns reveal a fourth stable position, you may add it as a leaf, but the three named theories must remain separately sortable. Flag any pair of theories your questions cannot reliably separate rather than pretending the instrument is sharper than it is.

The audience is intelligent adults without formal theological training, and the point is to sort them by what they actually affirm when responding to cases, not by how they would describe themselves.
