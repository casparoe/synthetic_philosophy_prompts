I am drafting a historical novel set during the Italian Wars, and I have been trying to let the episodes of my captain's career show the difference between virtù and fortuna rather than having the narrator explain it. As I revised the outline, I wrote down how I classify each episode. Now I want to know whether there is a single principle behind my classifications, or whether I am fooling myself.

1. Giovanni, my captain, wins a siege because an early autumn storm swells the river and floods the enemy camp downstream. I call this fortuna: the weather did his work, and he did nothing to bring it about.

2. Giovanni loses a battle in a narrow valley because he insists on deploying his heavy cavalry where it cannot charge, against two lieutenants' advice. I call this a defect of virtù, not bad fortune: the defeat followed from his choice of ground.

3. Giovanni changes his allegiance from a duke to the king of France one season before the duke is destroyed by a coalition. He survives and his condotta doubles. I call this virtù: he read the signs and moved before others did.

4. Giovanni attacks a larger force at dawn, uphill, against every counselor's advice, and wins because the enemy's mercenaries, unpaid for months, break at the first shock. I call this virtù, because he judged the enemy's morale and chose the moment. But I waver: had the mercenaries stood, he would have been annihilated, and that they broke was not something anyone could have known for certain.

5. Ercole, a rival captain, banks the river with dikes and raised earthworks; a flood still destroys his camp because the river shifts its bed upstream, in ground no one had had reason to survey. I call this fortuna, not a failure of Ercole's virtù: he prepared for the river he could see.

6. The duke whom Giovanni abandoned in (3) had fortified his cities, paid his troops, and kept good intelligence, yet he was ruined by the simultaneous betrayal of two allies he had treated well. I call this fortuna; but another part of me wants to say that a man of greater virtù would have foreseen even that, and this is the verdict I am least sure of.

7. Giovanni abandons a strong position without a fight when sickness breaks out in his camp; the sickness burns out before it reaches the army he was to meet, and he is later blamed for timidity. I call this virtù, not cowardice: he accepted dishonor to preserve his force. But in hindsight the danger was smaller than he believed, which leaves me uncertain.

8. I cannot classify this last one. Giovanni plans a whole campaign around a treaty he has been promised by a foreign secretary known to have deceived others. The treaty is never honored, but Giovanni has already placed his army so that it can turn to raiding, and he profits handsomely. Is this virtù, because he built a plan with a fallback, or fortuna, because the fallback worked only because the enemy's granaries happened to be full that autumn?

Is there a coherent principle that delivers these classifications, or close to them? If so, state it precisely, and tell me what it says about (8). If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what is the cheapest revision? I would also like to know how much of the resulting principle Machiavelli would actually accept: in particular, whether his image of fortune as a river that can be banked in advance, his preference for boldness over caution, and his claim that a man of fixed nature fails when the times change fit my verdicts or cut against some of them.
