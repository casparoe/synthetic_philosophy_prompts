I am a high school senior presenting a paper at a regional philosophy conference for high school students, and I want to be prepared for the question period. My paper defends a Rawlsian view of religion in democratic politics: when citizens advocate for laws that will coerce everyone, they may introduce religious reasons in public debate, but they must also be prepared to offer reasons that citizens of other faiths, or of none, could reasonably accept — at least for constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, and at least in due course, as a kind of proviso.

The audience I expect is mixed, and I know the same thesis will draw different objections from different parts of it. The largest bloc is a group from a Catholic high school's philosophy club, whose members worry that this requirement tells believers their deepest convictions cannot count publicly and forces them to translate their faith into a neutral vocabulary they do not actually occupy. A second bloc is from my own school's secular humanist society, and they think the proviso is much too weak: in their view, religious reasons should not enter legislative justification at all, because finding a public reason afterward is just rationalization that lets sectarian motives drive the law. The faculty respondent is a political philosopher broadly sympathetic to Rawls, and I expect her to press from inside the view: what makes a reason genuinely public rather than merely secular in disguise; why religious doctrines are asked to translate themselves into public terms while nonreligious comprehensive doctrines, such as a thoroughgoing naturalistic atheism or a comprehensive libertarianism, are not put through the same test; and whether the proviso is a real constraint or a face-saving formality.

The argument I plan to give runs like this: coercive laws are legitimate only if they can be justified to all who must obey them, not only to the coalition that enacted them; reasons drawn solely from one religion cannot be expected to move reasonable people outside that religion, so a law resting only on such reasons has not been justified to them; therefore a good-faith public justification must be available. The proviso is my answer to the worry that this excludes religious citizens: religious arguments may be offered openly, provided the supporting public reasons are supplied as well.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they could do, phrased the way each audience member would actually phrase them, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections have real force, what I should concede in my opening remarks or early in the discussion, what I should hold firm on, and the objection I should most hope nobody raises — including the reply I would need in order to survive it.
