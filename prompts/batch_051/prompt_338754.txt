In my philosophy of mind section two people got into it after the split-brain lecture, and I cant figure out who's right. It would help if you could walk through their exchange move by move and adjudicate it — who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and what overall position you'd defend.

Mara started by saying the standard split-brain experiments make it most natural to describe the patient as having two conscious subjects, or at least two separate streams, because each hemisphere has its own perceptual field and its own intentions that the other hemisphere can't access. Erin said that's too fast: two streams of consciousness isn't the same thing as two subjects. One subject can have disunified consciousness, and outside the lab the patient has a single body, a single life, and mostly acts as one person.

Mara came back with: what makes something a subject if not a unified first-person point of view? If there's no single what-it's-like that includes both hemispheres' experiences, you don't have one subject left over, you have two partial ones. Calling it "one subject with divided consciousness" is just relabeling the thing you're trying to explain. Erin replied that counting streams doesn't by itself count subjects — a person can have conflicting desires or memories they can't access without becoming two people — and the surgery leaves a lot of subcortical integration intact, so the split is partial rather than total.

Mara then said the experiments show divided agency, not just divided input: one hemisphere can make the left hand do something and the talking hemisphere will confabulate a reason for it, which looks exactly like two agents where one doesn't know what the other did. Erin said the confabulation actually cuts the other way: the speaking hemisphere takes ownership of the action instead of treating it as someone else's, which sounds more like one subject with a broken self-narrator than two agents. She added that if you ask the patient, the left hemisphere says "I meant to do that," so first-person reports support her side.

Mara answered that first-person reports are exactly what you can't trust here, since it's only one hemisphere doing the talking; ask the right hemisphere by letting it point, and you can get a different answer. At that point Erin said maybe the lesson is that the question "how many subjects are there?" is one our ordinary concept of a subject isn't built to answer — not clearly one and not clearly two. Which, she admitted, is itself a third position.
