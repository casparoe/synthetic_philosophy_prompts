Two friends of mine, Rafa and Mina, got into an argument after a reading group on biological individuality, and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. Rafa was defending a line about ant colonies: he said a colony is an organism, not just by analogy. The queen and the drones is the germ line, the workers are the soma; each generation the colony passes through a bottleneck of one or two reproductives; and since most workers are sterile and can't reproduce on their own, there's no internal conflict over who gets to breed. So he says selection acts on the colony as a unit, and some traits—like the colony's age structure or how many foragers it fields—are adaptations of the colony itself, not of individual ants.

Mina pushed back that this is exactly the problem with the superorganism idea: it picks one criterion, being a unit of selection, and quietly drops all the others. Organisms, she said, are also physiologically integrated, develop from a single cell, and have a shared genome. An ant colony is a family group living in separate bodies; calling it an organism just makes the word fuzzy. Rafa said fine, but "develops from a single cell" is a vertebrate prejudice—plenty of organisms bud, or fragment, or clone themselves, so that can't be a requirement. Mina said: your criterion either proves too little or too much. If shared reproductive fate is the test, then an aspen grove—one clone connected by roots—is an organism too, and so is a Portuguese man o' war, which is a colony of zooids budded off one founding individual. Maybe you like those results, but I doubt you'd say a whole aspen grove is one organism. And in the other direction, a cow with its rumen bacteria looks like a better candidate on physiological grounds than an ant colony: the bacteria live inside the cow and do its digestion. If you choose integration, the cow-holobiont wins and the ant colony loses; if you choose reproductive bottleneck, the colony wins and the holobiont loses. The criteria don't line up, so the word "organism" isn't tracking one thing.

Rafa came back that this cross-cutting is exactly why he doesn't choose physiological integration: the scientifically productive notion is evolutionary individuality, and the questions that matter are about shared reproductive fate and how strong a bottleneck filters out cheaters. Colonies have strong bottlenecks and aligned interests; cow microbiomes are reassembled each generation from the environment, so the bacteria have their own interests and the coalition isn't a unit of selection. Mina said that won't work either, because some endosymbionts are vertically transmitted just like mitochondria are: aphids get their Buchnera bacteria from their mothers, and the bacteria make amino acids the aphids can't make themselves. If you're consistent, you either have to say mitochondria aren't really part of the organism—since they're vertically transmitted bacteria—or admit that some holobionts are organisms. Rafa said mitochondria are different in degree: they've lost most of their genes and can't live outside the host, and matters of degree can still have real thresholds. Mina replied that now the threshold is doing all the work, and it's been picked to match his favorite cases. She said the whole question "are ant colonies organisms?" is a boundary dispute about a word, not an empirical question about evolution. Rafa said no—within a pluralism about the word "organism," you can still ask the sharp empirical question whether colonies are evolutionary individuals, and that's the question worth asking. Mina said: then why insist on calling them organisms? You've just renamed the interesting question to make it sound like it's already answered.

I stayed out of it, but I want to know: who's right about which specific moves, and what are they both missing? Adjudicate the actual arguments as made—which claims survive, which don't, and whether the original question is being answered or dissolved.
