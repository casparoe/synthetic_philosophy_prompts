Each year I teach a required first-year course on how science works to physics majors. I select about a dozen canonical thought experiments — the falling-bodies argument usually credited to Galileo, Maxwell's demon, Schrödinger's cat, Einstein's elevator — and for each one I have to decide what I am entitled to tell students it actually establishes. I am a physicist, not a philosopher, and I have about twenty minutes per thought experiment to produce a slide and a short framing note; other instructors use my notes and my course coordinator has to sign off on them, so an overstated or understated claim propagates. Please write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in whatever philosophy of science can genuinely defend about how thought experiments can yield knowledge, that tells me for any proposed scientific thought experiment: (1) what kind of epistemic claim it is being used to support — an impossibility claim, a proof that some set of assumptions is internally inconsistent, a claim that something is physically possible, a novel prediction, or merely an illustration; (2) what tests to run, in order, before I may present it as supporting that kind of claim; (3) how to tell whether the idealizations in the scenario are harmless or are doing all the work; and (4) where the procedure stops with a no and where it hands the judgment to a person. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, and mark every point where a judgment call is dressed up as a mechanical test.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: the falling-bodies argument against the heavier-falls-faster rule, Maxwell's demon as a challenge to the second law, and Schrödinger's cat as a way of bringing out the measurement problem. I want at least one application where the procedure forces me to present something more weakly than I would like. End by saying explicitly what the procedure cannot settle — the kinds of scientific thought experiments it will leave genuinely open.

Begin with the procedure itself, with no preface or commentary, and only then give the reasoning.
