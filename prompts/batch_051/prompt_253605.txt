I'm putting together an application for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea is this. Historians and historically minded scholars increasingly write sentences like "the volcano made the revolution possible," "plague did more than any general to decide the war," and "the Little Ice Age tipped the empire into crisis." Read literally, those sentences treat germs, climate, and volcanoes as actors — as things that do things. But the moment a historian says an eruption acted, a familiar objection appears: either the phrase is a harmless figure of speech (in which case it adds nothing to plain causal explanation) or it is the first step toward environmental determinism, with human decisions and contingency washed out of the story. I suspect this dilemma is false, and that the argument only works because "actor" is doing at least three jobs at once: (i) a causally salient factor, (ii) an intentional agent with something like goals and reasons, and (iii) the element an explanation is organized around — a protagonist in the narrative rather than a mere background condition. The project I want funded asks which of these senses, if any, lets a non-human process be a historical actor without committing the explanation to environmental determinism, and — more sharply — under what conditions a historical explanation earns the right to narrate a climate regime or a pathogen as a protagonist rather than as scenery.

Turn this into a proposal with the following sections, in this order, with rough word counts. (1) Framing Question: state the question so that it has a determinate answer and avoids the three-way ambiguity I just described; about 200 words. (2) Gap: explain why the existing debates leave this question open, naming positions and arguments as needed but without citations or bibliography, since I will add those myself; about 200 words. (3) Approach: say what I would actually do, quarter by quarter across the twelve months, and why the right method is conceptual analysis tested against a small set of historical cases rather than pure theory or pure historiography; about 350 words. (4) Anticipated Results: what the deliverable would look like if the project works, including an explicit criterion for separating substantive actor-talk from decorative actor-talk; about 150 words. (5) Failure Conditions: what would show the project had failed on its own terms; about 150 words. (6) Scope and Risks: what would make the project too big for twelve months and which parts you would cut first; about 150 words.
