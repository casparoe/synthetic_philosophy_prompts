I've been trying to get straight on what it actually means to be free—the usual distinctions between liberty as non-interference, non-domination, and self-mastery all make sense in the abstract, but when I run cases my own reactions don't line up neatly with any of them. So I just want to report my verdicts and see if there's a principle behind them.

- My landlord can refuse to renew my lease for no stated reason, though she's never come close to doing so. I think of myself as free—it's just what renting is.
- A friend works in an at-will state where his boss can fire him for any reason or none. The boss has never so much as reprimanded him. Yet I think of my friend as meaningfully less free than me, even though nothing is actually happening to either of us.
- I stop at a red light at 3 a.m. on an empty road. I don't experience that as an infringeement of my freedom; I had a vote in the law and can campaign to change it. But it is the state stopping me.
- Another friend lives in a country whose ruler is personally decent and leaves everyone alone, but could lock them up at will. I can't bring myself to call that person free.
- My cousin has severe panic disorder and can't leave the house. No one is stopping her, but calling her free sounds obscene to me.

Is there one account of liberty that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which should I give up? The first two especially bother me, since arbitrary power over a person's housing and over their income seem structurally similar, so either I need a principled difference between them or I should treat them the same. And say what your preferred account predicts for a case I haven't listed: someone in a company town where the same family owns the only real employer and the only rental housing, and has never abused either position.
