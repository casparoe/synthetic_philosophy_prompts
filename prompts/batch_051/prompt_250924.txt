Take the Münchhausen trilemma—the idea that any chain of justification ends in infinite regress, circularity, or a dogmatic stopping point. The usual way of setting it up assumes support is all-or-nothing: a reason either grounds the next link or it doesn't. Change just that one thing: let support be probabilistic, so a link only gives the next claim a credence of .9, and let chains be allowed to run infinitely long. Now work through each horn with that twist. A detail to be careful about: if each of infinitely many links only confers .9, those confidences might multiply out to zero—so an infinite probabilistic regress could be worse, not better, than the ordinary one. What would need to be true for an infinite chain to leave the original belief with a well-defined, decent probability instead? Can a probabilistic circle bootstrap itself to high confidence, or does that confidence just get imported through whatever priors you started with? And what about the foundationalist who stops at a foundation that's only probable: is he less dogmatic, or just dogmatic about his choice of priors? In the end, is probabilistic justification a genuine escape from the trilemma, or the same three horns with numbers attached?
