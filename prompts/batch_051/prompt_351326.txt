I have been arguing with a friend for months about what kind of thing a self is, and the argument has narrowed to a single claim. On the table is the view associated with Daniel Dennett: that a self is not a substance or a soul, nor even a part of the brain, but something like a center of narrative gravity — an abstraction that emerges from the stories an organism tells about its own past and future, together with those others tell about it, and that is real in the way a center of gravity is real (useful, objective, causally explanatory) but not locatable inside the skull. I find myself pulled toward this view; my friend thinks it is a subtle way of refusing to answer the hard question, namely the existence of the subject who is doing the telling.

Here is the proposition I want your credence in: there is no self over and above the center of narrative gravity. For purposes of the question, read self as whatever is picked out by I in ordinary self-reference — the subject of experience and the bearer of identity over time, if such a thing exists at all. You may treat the proposition as true if you think what exists is only an abstract, narratively maintained construction with no further substantial owner or teller; treat it as false if you think something more substantial is required. My own credence is roughly 0.7; my friend's is roughly 0.2.

More important than the number is the decomposition. Give me the sub-claims your credence depends on — for example, whether there is in fact a unified subject of experience at any given moment, whether identity over time is psychological continuity rather than persistence of a substance, whether the narrative-gravity account can handle hard cases such as split-brain patients, dissociative identity disorder, advanced dementia, or gradual whole-brain replacement, and whether the abstraction analogy itself does explanatory work or merely relabels the problem. Assign a rough probability and a sentence of justification to each sub-claim, say how they combine into your final credence, and identify which term your number is most sensitive to. Then tell me what evidence or argument would move that term most. Finally, and this is the part I care about most: say where the disagreement between a person at 0.7 and a person at 0.2 most likely lives, and which of the two of us you think is closer to being right about that particular term.
