So I just spent like two hours arguing with a friend about whether delusions are beliefs, and I came away with a bunch of verdicts I can't quite make hang together. My friend's line was the standard two-factor thing: a delusion needs (1) some weird anomalous experience and (2) a failure of the normal belief-evaluation machinery, and because of factor (2) the person never really "accepts" the content the way you accept an ordinary belief. I kept saying formation history shouldn't matter for whether a state is a belief right now, and that some delusions sure look like beliefs. But then I had to admit some don't.

Here are my verdicts on specific cases:

1. Cotard: a man insists he's dead. He stops eating because "dead people don't eat," and when asked why he's talking he says "the body is just a machine now." Verdict: definitely a belief. It's global, action-guiding, and he'll give reasons for it.

2. Capgras: a woman says her husband has been replaced by an identical impostor. She calls the police twice, but she also keeps living with him, sets him a place at dinner, and when he brings flowers she says the impostor is "trying to win her back." Verdict: also a belief, but a weirdly compartmentalized one — it guides some behavior and not other behavior.

3. Anosognosia: after a stroke, a man denies his left arm is paralyzed. Asked to raise it, he says "sure," it doesn't move, and then he says he didn't feel like doing it. Verdict: not a belief. It's a confabulation produced on the spot to protect his sense of himself; there's no standing representation that his arm works.

4. Passivity delusion: a woman says her hand isn't hers and "does things on its own." She doesn't try to stop it or plan around it; she reports it the way you'd report a strange sensation. Verdict: not a belief either, or at most a belief-like report of an experience.

5. Alien organ delusion: a man insists his internal organs have been removed and replaced with plastic replicas. He'll tell you this with total conviction, but he still eats, exercises, and takes his heart medication "just in case." Verdict: this is the one that broke me. By avowal and conviction it's a belief; by action-guidance it isn't.

6. For contrast: a conspiracy theorist with no obvious brain damage who believes the moon landing was faked. He argues for it, spends money on it, and plans around it a little. Verdict: obviously a belief, even if false and irrational.

So: is there a coherent principle that captures all of these verdicts at once, or do some of them have to go? If you give me a principle, tell me (a) exactly what it says about each case seperately, (b) which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and (c) what it predicts about a patient who avows a delusion, acts on it in therapy sessions, but never acts on it outside the clinic. And can the two-factor theory be stated so it's consistent with that principle, or does the two-factor theory force a different verdict somewhere?
