At an interdisciplinary seminar on concept learning last term, two of my colleagues — Dr Levin, a philosopher of cognitive science, and Dr Okafor, a computational psychologist — became tangled in an argument that has since troubled me. It concerned what Bongard problems actually demonstrate. For context: a Bongard problem presents two panels of six simple line drawings; the six drawings on the left exemplify a rule, and the six on the right either violate it or conform to a contrasting one, and the task is to identify that rule. Many people take success on such problems, with only twelve examples available, to show that a mind or a machine can induce a concept from a handful of cases. Dr Levin and Dr Okafor disagreed sharply about whether that is what the problems show.

Dr Okafor began from underdetermination. Any twelve drawings are consistent with an enormous number of rules, so the intended rule is not fixed by the examples alone; success therefore reveals only that the solver's prior preferences happen to align with the designer's taste for symmetry, connectedness, and easily expressed partitions, rather than an ability to extract the concept from the data. Dr Levin replied that this is not a defect but a description of induction itself: learning from few examples just is the use of a prior to choose among hypotheses all consistent with the data, and to demand otherwise is to demand a form of learning no finite system could perform. The problems are revealing precisely because the rule is underdetermined, she argued, since a system that nonetheless succeeds has priors rich enough to generalise from six positive and six negative cases. Dr Okafor answered that this concedes the crucial point: if success depends on matching the generator's aesthetic, the strong claim that a general-purpose learner can track novel regularities without collusion with the designer is unsupported. Dr Levin responded that transfer to rules the solver has never seen is exactly the faculty at issue — the solver and the setter are not the same person, and such transfer is what distinguishes a concept learner from a look-up table. Dr Okafor rejoined that the novelty is illusory, because setter and solver share a description language and simplicity is relative to a vocabulary; with a different vocabulary, a different rule would seem equally natural, and nothing in the drawings marks one candidate rule as the concept. Dr Levin then said that this argument, pressed home, would make a sceptic of every inductive achievement — the child learning a word from a few referents, the scientist extracting a regularity from noisy data — since there too the selected regularities are the ones a human mind finds natural. One cannot confine the scepticism to Bongard problems, she concluded, without giving up on concept learning altogether.

I did not referee the exchange at the time and could not have done so cleanly, and I genuinely do not know which of them is more right. Please adjudicate the argument rather than merely summarise it. Go through its stages, say what each move establishes and where each overreaches, and then take a committed position on the underlying question: is few-example concept induction in Bongard problems a real epistemic achievement, or an artefact of matched priors between setter and solver, or does that contrast itself rest on a confusion? State who is right about which specific claim, what both parties are missing, and where the stronger overall position lies. I would rather have a definite, defended verdict than a balanced survey of options.
