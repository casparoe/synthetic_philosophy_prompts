I am on the rules committee of a small but unusually formal debating league, and we have been arguing for weeks about a proposal meant to make matches more truth-tracking by forcing presuppositions into the open. We start from the familiar scorekeeping picture of conversation: at every point in a debate there is a body of propositions that all participants are treating as taken for granted — call it the board, or the common ground — and speakers add to it not only by asserting things outright but by presupposing them through their choice of words. The usual triggers are definite descriptions, factive verbs such as regret and realize, aspectual verbs such as stop and continue, clefts such as it was the chair who tabled the motion, and particles such as even, too, and already. In ordinary conversation, hearers tend to accommodate these presuppositions quietly: a presupposition that is not objected to simply becomes part of what everyone takes for granted. The proposal codifies exactly this habit into the rules.

Here is the mechanism. A publicly displayed board records every proposition currently in the debate's common ground. Whenever a speaker presupposes a proposition that is not yet on the board, that proposition is posted as pending. If no speaker on the opposing team issues an explicit challenge — formally, by saying something of the form 'I deny the presupposition that P' and thereby undertaking to argue against P — before the end of the opposing team's next turn, the proposition becomes seated. Once seated, it is available to both sides as a premise and may no longer be denied, except by a motion to amend the board, which costs one of the team's three challenge points and must be approved by the chair.

We are not asking whether the rule is desirable in the abstract. We are asking how it would actually behave. What would skilled opening speakers presuppose in their first speech, and what would they deliberately avoid presupposing? How should an opposing team spend its finite attention and challenge points — hunting for false presuppositions while they are still pending, or conserving objections for later? Where does the game get played first: presuppositions hidden under negation or inside questions, presuppositions that hold on one reading but fail on another, or presuppositions about the state of the debate itself? And are the obvious failure modes fixable by small changes to the seating rule, or are they already baked into the idea?
