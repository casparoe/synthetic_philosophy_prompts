I have been arguing with a colleague who works on risk modelling about the reference class problem, and I would like a thorough audit of the analogy she keeps reaching for. The running case is the familiar one of attaching a probability to a singular event: what is the probability that this particular 52-year-old woman, a non-smoker with a family history of breast cancer who works as a solicitor, will develop breast cancer within ten years? Depending on whether one places her in the class of all women, of women aged 50–55, of non-smoking women in that age band, of women with that family history, or of the intersection of all these, the numbers shift sharply, and the more the class is refined the smaller the sample behind the number becomes. My colleague takes this to be a familiar estimation problem rather than a deep philosophical one. Choosing a reference class, she says, is like choosing the magnification on a microscope. At low magnification one sees a broad, well-populated field but only coarse structure; at high magnification one sees the individual case in fine detail but almost no usable signal, only noise; and the statistician's craft is to find the intermediate resolution at which the class is relevant enough to the case while the frequencies remain robust enough to support inference. On her view, the puzzle dissolves into something like a bias–variance trade-off, with no fact of the matter beyond it.

I am unsure whether the analogy is doing innocent expository work or covertly settling the hard question, and I would like that settled rather than assumed. My inchoate worry is that a microscope keeps one and the same object in view at every magnification, whereas reference classes seem to define the object differently at each level; but I have not managed to make the objection stick, and I do not want to lean on it if it is not where the real problem lies. Please audit the analogy in detail: spell out what corresponds to what (the magnifications, the field of view, the object on the slide, the noise, the skill of the operator), then identify where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for her intended conclusion from those that are merely superficial, and saying which features of the microscope picture the analogy quietly imports that the statistical case lacks. Finally, say whether her conclusion — that the reference class problem reduces to a manageable precision–reliability trade-off — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If some version of it survives, state what further premises it needs, for example concerning causal homogeneity, relevance, or natural kinds. If it does not, say whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along. Please write your answer in British English.
