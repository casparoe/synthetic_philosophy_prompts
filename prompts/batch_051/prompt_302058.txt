I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about whether the state should ever license biological parents. In the background reading I keep coming back to Joel Feinberg's essay The Child's Right to an Open Future. The idea I'm turning over from him, roughly paraphrased because I don't have the book in front of me, is that a child has certain autonomy rights that can't be exercised yet but have to be protected now, the way you manage a trust fund for a beneficiary who isn't old enough to touch the principal. Feinberg calls the upshot something like the child's right to an open future: the child should arrive at adulthood with a reasonable range of life-options still available rather than having them foreclosed by other people's decisions during childhood. He seems to treat this as a reason for restricting what parents, schools, and the state may do to kids, especially around things like religious upbringing or pigeonholing education, where a choice made now permanently closes a door the adult might have wanted.

But here is the practice that puzzles me. In every jurisdiction I've looked at, you have to be licensed to cut hair, drive a car, run a daycare, prescribe drugs, and in some places even to fish with a rod. You do not need any license, training, or demonstrated competence to create and raise a biological child. At the same time, adoption is intensely regulated: home studies, financial checks, criminal background reviews, sometimes marriage or age requirements. Someone who would fail an adoption home study can generally go home and have a biological child the next year with no state review at all. And then society spends enormous public resources educating that same child, protecting them after the fact if things go wrong, while doing almost nothing to screen the parents before the fact.

What theory of children's rights, parental rights, or state authority does this two-track practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can. Does it presuppose that biological parents have a kind of natural entitlement that adoptive parents lack, and that this entitlement is weighty enough to be allowed to foreclose the child's open future even when the same foreclosure via adoption would be blocked? Or is it better explained by a bare appeal to biology or to the state's limited ability to predict parenting quality? Does the practice strictly presuppose any view about the child's right to an open future, or is it underdetermined by the available theories, consistent with several incompatible ones? And if we took Feinberg's trust idea seriously as a moral principle, what would actually have to change in family law, in the licensing of parents, or in adoption practice?

I don't have the philosophical vocabulary for this, so please act as an expert in applied ethics and political philosophy. Take your time and think carefully. Start by brainstorming at least three different candidate theories the practice could presuppose, then assess which candidate best fits the full pattern, including the weird asymmetry between biological and adoptive parenting. Be explicit about where the practice is a coherent implementation of a theory and where it's just an inconsistency. Then give me the bottom line on what a policy built on the open-future idea would have to change.
