I've been arguing with a friend about risk and blame, and we keep getting stuck on the same knot. Suppose two managers at the same firm make the same call with the same data: they both greenlight a costly expansion on the assumption that a new regulation will fail. For one, the regulation dies in committee and the expansion prints money; for the other, the regulation passes and the division is gutted. Afterward almost everyone says the first manager showed good judgment and the second was reckless, even though nothing about what they knew or did differs. Only the outside event did.

But here's the asymmetry. Those same people, when you ask them how to write history, will insist that a good historian never makes outcomes look inevitable and should reconstruct how things looked before the result was known. They'll say writing the story backwards is the narrative fallacy or hindsight bias, and its a cheap trick. So in everyday evaluation we treat a decision as better when it turns out well, but in historical explanation we demand that people refuse to do exactly that. I want to know whether anything actually justifies the asymmetry. Maybe judging contemporaries and explaining the past have different aims, and a heuristic that's a vice in one is a practical necessity in the other. Or maybe that's just special pleading, and we should either evaluate our coworkers the way we ask historians to evaluate the dead, or admit that history is only fair to people because they aren't around to sue.

The harder version of the puzzle: the same general sends troops through the same narrow pass on two consecutive days. The first day the enemy happens to have moved its cannons, and the attack succeeds. The second day the enemy happens to have moved them back, and it is a massacre. Same plan, same information going in, same everything except what the enemy did. I'd like the strongest possible case for treating the two decisions as different in quality, or an honest concession that consistency would force us to give up one of the judgments we're attached to.
