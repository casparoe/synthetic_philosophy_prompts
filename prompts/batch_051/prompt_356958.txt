I'm a software engineer, and in my field a perfect copy is, for nearly every purpose, as good as the original; I want to know how far that principle extends to art. Imagine a painting in a museum is covertly replaced by an atom-for-atom duplicate, and the original is then destroyed. The swap is never detected and changes no one's behavior. Holding aside market value, legal title, and personal attachment, consider the proposition that the world is aesthetically worse off after the swap than before it — where aesthetic value is whatever makes a work beautiful, moving, or worth looking at. I want your credence in that proposition as a committed number, not a range, plus the decomposition. Assign probabilities to at least these terms: (a) physical identity guarantees aesthetic identity, so the duplicate is exactly as good as the original; (b) an object's history or origin can be part of its aesthetic properties rather than mere biographical context; (c) our tendency to care about originals is a misfiring of a general human heuristic rather than a sensitivity to a real aesthetic property; and (d) the proposition has a determinate answer at all, rather than dissolving under analysis. Add any further terms you think carry weight, show how you combined them, and identify the single term your final credence is most sensitive to. Then tell me which empirical finding or argument would move that term the most, and roughly how far it would move your overall number.
