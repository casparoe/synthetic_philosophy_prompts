My friend and I just burned an hour and a half on a bench arguing about imaginative resistance, and I want you to write up the argument as a dialogue between the two positions with each side done as strong as we ever got it. The phenomenon: people swallow all kinds of wild descriptive facts in fiction (dragons, faster-than-light travel, whatever), but if a story says a character was morally right to do something awful, and the only reason it gives is something like because it was a girl, readers don't treat that as fictionally true the way they treat the dragons as true. The disagreement is about why. Her view was that it's purely a matter of willingness: we can imagine a world were infanticide is right, we just won't, because accepting it in the fiction feels too much like endorsing it, and we don't want the imagined moral judgment to export back into our real ones. My view was that willingness can't be the whole story, because there are cases where we try and still fail. A story that stipulates a flatly impossible mathematical result, or that a character knew something on no evidence at all, seems not to produce a fictional truth in the first place rather than producing one we refuse. The author can make up descriptive facts, I said, but she isn't licensed to just stipulate evaluative or epistemic facts into being.

Theres a specific case we kept coming back to. Imagine a two-hundred-page novel that slowly builds an internally consistent but horrendous value system, and then treats a character's killing of her baby as right; both of us found our resistance largely vanished by then. I said that's because the moral claim had become a consequence of a richly described world rather than a brute stipulation; she said that just proves it was willingness all along, because the novel had talked us into the perspective and we eventually consented to play. Write the dialogue. Make the characters respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past each other, and don't strawman either side. If it ends in an impasse, fine, but locate exactly which moving part the impasse is about.
