Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is deciding whether to leave a stable job for a startup. I found myself resisting every attempt to reduce her situation to numbers: she wanted to assign a probability to the venture's success, multiply through by the payoffs, and treat the result as a verdict. My policy, as I came to see it, is this. I refuse to let a bare probability carry weight in a decision unless the person offering it can also say something about the evidence behind it: how much information it rests on, whether the situation is more like a gamble with stable odds or a one-off case for which no well-defined frequency exists, and whether the reference class is well chosen. Without that, I treat the number as pseudoprecision and discount whatever conclusion is drawn from it. I take this to be in the spirit of Keynes's view that probability is often not numerical and that the weight of evidence matters separately from the probability itself. What I cannot tell is whether this is a defensible epistemic norm or a bias that protects my vague uneasiness from quantification. In this argument, I may have discarded useful rough estimates merely because they were not accompanied by an account of their basis. Is this policy defensible? If it is partly defensible, what is the corrected version—one that preserves the protection against pseudoprecision without rejecting numerical estimates outright? And how could I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying the norm in a particular case or merely rationalizing my reluctance to commit? Please tell me how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
