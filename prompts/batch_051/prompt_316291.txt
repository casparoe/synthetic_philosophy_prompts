I'm a novelist, so I spend a lot of time thinking about what people actually do with their freedom, and I keep coming back to a fight between a theory I find beautiful and an intuition I can't shake.

The theory is Keynes's famous bet about our grandchildren: that the economic problem of scraping together enough to live would eventually be solved by compounding growth, and once absolute needs were met people would stop wanting more stuff and start wanting more time — something like a fifteen-hour week — and then we'd finally face the real, harder problem of learning to live well, using leisure for friendship, the arts, the present. It has a lot going for it as a theory. It's simple, it makes a definite prediction, it explains why our obsession with moneymaking feels pathological, and it gives a satisfying endpoint: grow until scarcity is solved, then the actual civilisation begins.

But my intuition keeps pointing the other way. Everywhere I've looked — including at myself and other writers — every increase in productivity or money gets converted into more work, more consumption, more status competition, not time. The people I know who win the freedom to write less take on more obligations; the ones who get an advance buy a bigger apartment and chase the next thing. Ask people whether they'd take an extra free day or a extra day's pay and they say one thing but live the other. It's not that the absolute needs aren't met; it's that the wants don't behave like needs. They're positional, or performative, or just restless. So my intuition is that the theory rests on a false picture of human wanting: not satiable absolute goods but a moving social target.

So which yields? Should I debunk the intuition as an artefact of a transitional phase — maybe we haven't yet outgrown the moneymaking instinct Keynes thought we'd shed, and one century isn't long enough to test the bet? Or should the theory be amended so the good life isn't something individuals just drift into, but something that requires collective action to change the relative-position game? Or is there a reading where Keynes was describing a possibility we keep collectively declining, so the intuition and the theory are compatible but the theory was never really a prediction? I want to know what each move costs, and whether 'the intuition is just status anxiety' is a real argument or a way of not listening to it.
