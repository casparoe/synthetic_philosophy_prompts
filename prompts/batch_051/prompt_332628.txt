i keep hitting the same wall in arguments about moral compromise. One side says a compromise is just a deal: each person gives up something they care about less to get something they care about more, so both walk away better off by their own lights. The other side says that is exactly what selling out looks like when the thing being traded is a value rather than a preference. Neither camp can tell me, before I pick a theory, what the theory actually has to get right. So I want the spec. The theory is for deciding, in real cases, wether a compromise is a legitimate moral achievement or a failure dressed up as one.

Treat the central phenomenon as moral gains from trade: two people or groups with incompatible value systems reach an arrangement where each judges themselves better off by their own lights, without either having first changed their values. If a theory cannot say when that is actually happening, when it is an illusion, and when it is disguised capitulation, I do not want to read it.

Write the desiderata for a theory of moral compromise, not a scorecard of existing views. For each requirement give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable for any adequate theory or just a preference of one camp. Keep the following pressures in view:

- the difference between compromising over preferences (what to fund) and compromising over values (what is owed to whom), and whether gains from trade even makes sense in the second case;
- compromise as a one-off deal vs compromise as a durable change in what a person or institution will stand for;
- the ex ante/ex post split: agreeing on a procedure before we know who wins vs cutting a deal after the chips are down;
- whether the theory must show the parties are each better off by their own lights, or must also show the arrangement is justifiable from outside, and what happens when those come apart;
- the edge cases: a minority group accepting weaker legal protection than it is owed because the alternative is nothing; a person trading away a political principle to fund a program that saves lives; a party too awful to compromise with; two neighbours swapping shifts so each can keep their own holy day.

Then seperately flag any subsets of the requirements that look jointly unsatisfiable. im especially interested in whether "each party is better off by its own lights" and "neither party has violated a non-negotiable value" can both hold in deep conflicts, or whether one has to give. Finish by saying which single requirement does the most work to filter out lazy theories.

Use these sections in this order, roughly:

1. The core phenomenon (~120 words): what a theory of moral compromise is about, and whether there is one unified thing to theorize.
2. The requirements (~600 words): the list, each with its motivating case and the why behind it.
3. Negotiable vs non-negotiable (~200 words, a table is fine): which requirements are load-bearing and which are camp preferences.
4. Tensions (~200 words): the subsets that look jointly unsatisfiable, and where the real disagreement lives.
5. The one that does the most work (~150 words): the single requirement you would use to reject the most theories, and why.
