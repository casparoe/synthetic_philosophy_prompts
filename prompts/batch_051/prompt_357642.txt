I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, Anya Kelleher, is a retired appellate judge who now teaches a seminar on responsibility. In the early chapters she states no general theory; she simply rules on one hypothetical after another, and the reader is left to infer the principle behind her rulings. I have fixed the following five rulings and do not want to revise them.

1. A woman, under no compulsion, betrays a close friend's secret to win a promotion, after thinking the matter over for a night. Kelleher holds her responsible.
2. A man deliberates about injuring a rival. Unknown to the man, a neurosurgeon who wants the injury to happen has installed a dormant implant that would produce the decision to injure if the man were about to decide not to injure. The man briefly considers walking away—he feels the pull of restraint as a live option—then decides in his ordinary way that the injury is worth it. He never nears a contrary decision, so the implant never activates. Kelleher holds him responsible.
3. A sleepwalker knocks over a child and breaks the child's arm; he has no awareness of what he is doing. Kelleher holds him not responsible.
4. Through a wireless neural interface, an experimenter directly commands a subject's arm to snatch a brooch while the subject, horrified, tries to keep her arm still; the interface overrides her. Kelleher holds her not responsible.
5. A teenager, after a long afternoon of taunting, lashes out and strikes his taunter. He could have walked away, and he later says he simply gave in to his anger. Kelleher holds him responsible, though she calls the blame diminished.

I can see at least two principles that fit these rulings. On one, she has quietly abandoned the idea that responsibility requires an alternative possibility; what matters to her is only whether the actual sequence that led to the act was the agent's own, reasons-responsive deliberation. On the other, she still requires that some alternative—even a very slight one—have been available, and she treats the man in case 2 as having had exactly that: a first stirring toward restraint that the implant allowed, even though a fully formed contrary decision was blocked. There may be further readings that fit just as well, and I would like to know if there are.

The chapter I am now writing turns on a new hypothetical. Cora is deciding whether to testify truthfully at an inquiry. Unknown to Cora, a neuroscientist who wants her truthful testimony has installed a device keyed not to a formed contrary decision but to the earliest neural precursor of any alternative course of action: if any precursor of deciding to lie, to delay, or even to reconsider so much as begins, the device will immediately produce the decision to testify truthfully. Cora deliberates in her ordinary way, finds the reasons for truthfulness decisive, and testifies truthfully. No precursor of any alternative ever occurs, so the device never activates.

What should Kelleher say about Cora if she is to remain consistent with her five fixed rulings? Please lay out the candidate principles that fit those rulings, say whether they diverge on Cora, and give your best guess about Kelleher's verdict and the reasoning behind it. If the earlier rulings underdetermine her principle, say so and tell me what each possible verdict in Cora's case would reveal about it. Finally, describe one further hypothetical—different from the cases already used—that would settle which principle she holds if the Cora case does not.
