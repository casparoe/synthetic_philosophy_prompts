Pretend you're a comparative philosopher who works on East Asian moral psychology and contemporary philosophy of emotion, and who has a reputation for drafting extremely fair joint statements between rival camps. Think slowly. Before you write anything, brainstorm the strongest formulations each side would accept and the places where they secretly already agree.

This one is a friendly, modernized reconstruction of the old Korean Four–Seven debate, so don't worry if you don't know the history — everything you need is right here.

Two camps are arguing about what the moral emotions are. Both camps accept the same rough picture of the phenomena. They agree that people have 'moral sprouts' — call them the four beginnings: compassion at someone's pain, shame and aversion at one's own bad acts, deference toward someone deserving, and a felt sense of right and wrong. They also agree that people have ordinary passions — call them the seven emotions: joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, dislike, and desire. The fight is about how those two lists relate.

Camp A (the principle-first camp, loosely following Toegye) says the four beginnings are not a variety of ordinary passion at all. They issue directly from principle, while the seven emotions issue from our embodied, material constitution. That's why the four beginnings can be trusted as moral guides and the seven can't, even when one of the seven happens to feel nice. On this view, moral emotion is a different kind of thing from ordinary emotion, not just a better-behaved specimen of it.

Camp B (the material-condition camp, loosely following Yulgok and his side) says no. The four beginnings are just the seven emotions when they're properly ordered. Compassion is the ordinary feeling of being moved by another's harm when that feeling has the right object and proportion; there isn't a second, special pipeline from principle into the heart. Principle is the norm the emotions answer to, not a separate source that manufactures a special batch of moral feelings. On this view, moral emotions are ordinary emotions done correctly, not a distinct species.

The camps have spent years accusing each other of a fatal flaw. Camp A says Camp B can't explain the special authority of moral emotion. Camp B says Camp A multiplies sources of motivation for no reason.

Your job is to draft the joint statement these two camps would produce if they hired a hyper-careful mediator. It must be something both could sign without either side silently thinking 'that wording is rigged.'

Structure it with these exact sections:

1. Agreed claims (200-300 words). Number them. Make them substantive — not just 'emotions exist.' Include claims about the phenomena, about what any theory of moral emotion needs to explain, and about what both camps reject.
2. Disagreements table (150-250 words). Two or three rows. In each row, name the issue, then give the principle-first camp's wording of its position and the material-condition camp's wording of its own position, in each side's own voice.
3. Cruxes (100-150 words). The sub-questions that, if one of them were settled, would force at least one camp to change its view. For each crux, record each side's prediction in one sentence.
4. Verbal vs. real dispute (100-150 words). Which part of the fight, if any, is mostly about words (for instance about what 'source' or 'emotion' means), and which part is a genuine difference in commitments.
5. What would move each camp (50-100 words). One or two sentences per camp on what evidence or case they would each have to treat as real pressure against their own view.

Don't adjudicate. The deliverable is the document itself, not a verdict. And dont use cop-outs like 'both sides are partly right' anywhere in the agreed claims — the agreed claims should be sharp enough that someone could build something non-trivial out of them.
