i've been using a term in a reading group on ontology identification and I want it stress-tested before I keep using it. I've been calling it 'ontological inertia': a system — an agent, a science, a trained model — holds onto its current way of carving up the world not because the data best support that carving, but because the carving is load-bearing. Its predictions, plans, and memory are all indexed by it, and the cost of rewriting the carving often exceeds the cost of living with the mismatch. So a high-inertia system can look exactly like a system that has the right ontology, right up until the mismatch gets expensive, and then it rewrites in a rush (Kuhn-ish). I think this bears on why ontology identification isn't just curve-fitting: if several internal ontologies fit all the accessible data equally well, inertia may be part of why a system settles on one, and why the question 'which one is about the real world' can't be answered from fit alone — the deciding information isn't in the data.

I know this is in the neighborhood of Goodman's grue and Kuhn's paradigms, and it's obviously tangled up with the alignment problem of identifying which latent variables a model actually uses as its ontology. What I want to know: is the distinction doing work beyond those, or am I just relabeling switching costs with a fancier metaphor? What is the strongest version of the claim — is it about agents, theories, or something narrower? And where does it break: what would zero ontological inertia even look like, and is the implicit claim that high inertia can be rational rather than merely path-dependent actually defensible?
