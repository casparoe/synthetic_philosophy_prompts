I'm a second-year astronomy PhD student and this has come up because I made an offhand comment in journal club that we finally know dark matter is real, and a philosopher friend basically rolled his eyes and walked me through an argument that's been stuck in my head since. I keep trying to find the hole in it and failing, which is embarrasing because I'm pretty sure it's the kind of thing people in my field just wave off.

The argument he gave goes like this. (1) History is littered with scientific theories that were hugely successful at predicting and explaining things by the standards of their day, and that competent people believed, but that we now regard as false: phlogiston, caloric fluid, the luminiferous ether, Ptolemaic astronomy, and so on. (2) So, by induction, the track record gives us strong reason to think that most of our current successful theories will also turn out to be false. (3) If we have strong reason to think a theory will turn out to be false, we shouldn't believe it is true now. (4) Therefore we shouldn't believe our current best theories (general relativity, the standard model of particle physics, the standard model of cosmology, whatever) are true.

I can feel that the word "false" might be doing different work in step (1) and step (4): phlogiston being dead wrong feels different from Newtonian mechanics ending up as a limiting case, and maybe the sample of past theories is biased toward the ones we've already discarded. But I can't make the objection sharp. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and why. If the argument is valid as stated, say which premise a scientific realist should deny and what the most defensible version of realism looks like after doing so. I'd rather not just be told the argument is old; I want the precise diagnosis.
