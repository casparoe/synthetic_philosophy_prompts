I'm a retired biologist who's been reading philosophy for a few years now, and I keep getting tripped up by Sartre's slogan "existence precedes essence." In a reading group the other week someone used it to shut down a question about whether a person's temperament or biology sets any limits on what they can become—the claim was that there's no fixed essence to fall back on, so any talk of inborn constraints is just bad faith. I'm sure the slogan doesn't have to mean that, and I suspect it's doing several jobs at once and being stretched. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "existence precedes essence" might be making—about human nature, about choice, about responsibility, about what actually explains the kind of person someone becomes—and for each say whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or just false? I'm especially interested in wich readings would actually license ruling out biological or temperamental constraints, and which readings are compatible with there being real constraints that we still choose what to make of. I've also seen people say Sartre grants something called facticity—the stuff we're stuck with—so if you can, be precise about what exactly the slogan is denying when it denies an essence.
