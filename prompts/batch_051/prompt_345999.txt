A colleague in my department fit a model with a variable he constructed after looking at the outcomes, and found a striking pattern. I brushed off the result without much thought, which led to a genuine fight about it. My policy, which I'd never stated out loud before, is that an induction doesn't count if the predicate it's stated in was cooked up after the data arrived — grue-style — because any finite data set can be dressed up to fit such a predicate, so the past fit is no reason to project it. What I actually trust is regularities stated in vocabulary I'd have accepted before seeing the data. But I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science and I know I can't give a principled account of why pre-data availability should do that work; my colleague says it just smuggles in my favorite theory-laden categories and calls them natural. Can the policy be made defensable, or is it a shortcut that only works when I already agree with the conclusion? If it can be repaired, what is the strongest version of it, and what would count as evidence that I'm using it as a shield instead of a filter?
