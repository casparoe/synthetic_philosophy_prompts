I am a retired physical chemist who has recently begun attending a philosophy of science reading group, and the group has asked me to lead a single ninety-minute session on why probability is a problem in the many-worlds, Everettian picture of quantum mechanics. I can set out the technical ingredients without difficulty — unitary evolution, entanglement, decoherence, the absence of a collapse postulate — but I have never had to teach a foundational problem rather than a settled result, and I find that audiences either do not see that there is a problem at all or dissolve it with a move they would not accept elsewhere. I want to arrange the session so that the difficulty appears forced rather than asserted.

The audience has no formal training in quantum theory: they know the popular Schrödinger's-cat story and several of them can follow complex amplitudes treated as vectors in a plane, but they have not thought about measurement, and a few are more interested in whether the puzzle is epistemic or metaphysical than in the formalism itself. I will have a whiteboard and the whole ninety minutes, with the last twenty reserved for open discussion. I am not asking for readings or references, and the plan should not depend on the audience having prepared anything in advance.

Please design the explanatory arc for the session. What concept has to be in place before the Born-rule probability question even becomes intelligible as a problem, and what should be built on that foundation, in what order — where each idea presupposes the previous one — so that the puzzle comes to seem unavoidable? What single concrete scenario should carry the session: a spin measurement with unequal amplitudes, a short sequence of repeated measurements, a wager an observer must accept before branching, or something else? Say what that example must have and what it must avoid. Which misconceptions should I expect, in roughly the order in which they will arise — for instance, the branch-counting thought that two branches means equal odds, or the thought that decoherence quietly restores a single outcome — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than argued with after it has hardened? What should I openly decline to explain at this level, and how should I mark the omission? Finally, what question or short exercise in the closing ten minutes would tell me whether someone has grasped the problem itself rather than merely acquired the vocabulary of branches and amplitudes?
