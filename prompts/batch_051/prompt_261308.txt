You are a decision theorist and social epistemologist with expertise in rational choice and the logic of information aggregation. I'd like you to think carefully, and to begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before committing to the one you can defend best. I'm not looking for references or a reading list; I want the reasoning done in your own words.

I've been collecting my own gut verdicts about when it is rational to follow the crowd, and I'm trying to extract a single principle from them — or to learn that no such principle is available. Here are the cases.

1. Two side-by-side restaurants in an unfamiliar city. One has a queue of locals out the door; the other is empty. I know nothing else. I join the queue. Verdict: rational — the queue is real evidence of quality.
2. Same city, but before choosing I receive a text from a friend who lives there and eats out constantly, telling me the empty restaurant is the best in town. I join the queue anyway, telling myself that this many people can't be wrong. Verdict: not rational — I had better-informed private evidence and abandoned it for a crowd whose individual judgments may largely be copies of one another.
3. A bank run. After a speculative news report, I see depositors lining up outside my bank. I believe the bank would almost certainly survive if everyone refrained from withdrawing, but I withdraw anyway. Verdict: individually rational, though the collective result is self-defeating and may leave everyone worse off.
4. A stock I have researched. My own reading of the filings tells me the company is overvalued, but the price keeps rising and others keep buying. I buy anyway, telling myself the market must know something I don't. Verdict: not rational in the way the restaurant case is — here I'm not extracting independent information from others so much as riding momentum, because the people I'm following may be deciding on the same second-order reasons I am.
5. A hiring committee. The first two speakers give a lukewarm endorsement based on a skim of the file. The third member has read the entire file and found serious problems, but, seeing the room's direction, softens her remarks. Everyone then reads the absence of strong dissent as confirmation, and the candidate is hired. Verdict: the group's decision is not rationally justified, even though each member's behavior is psychologically understandable; I blame the later member most, because her silence helped manufacture the very consensus she read off the room.

Is there one principle precise enough to predict these verdicts and to tell me what to say about a case I haven't considered? Or do I have to abandon one of my verdicts to keep the principle simple? Please state the principle, or a short list of candidate principles, as exactly as you can, tie each component to the case that forces it, apply it to a further case — for example, a long line of cars all following a detour sign into an empty dead-end parking lot, or a group of hikers who all turn back one after another as the weather worsens — and say honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate.
