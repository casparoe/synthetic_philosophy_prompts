Ever since I started reading rationalist blogs and forums, I have been in the habit of watching for the point in an argument where a word quietly changes its sense or a claim quietly changes its strength. Lately I keep noticing a particular move in arguments about copyright and patents, and I cannot find a clean name for it.

The move is easiest to see in a typical exchange about a student who downloads a textbook. The defender of copyright begins by saying that the student has stolen the author's work, or that the work is the author's property and using it without permission is like taking someone's bicycle. When I point out that copying a file leaves the author's possession untouched, the defender shifts, without marking the shift, to saying that if people could copy freely, authors would not write textbooks. When I then point out that this is a disputed empirical claim, and that it would at most justify some limited legal incentive rather than a moral prohibition on an individual reader, the defender shifts back to the language of property and rights, as if that had been established all along. I have seen the same back-and-forth in arguments about drug patents, fan fiction, and music streaming. What strikes me is that the two claims are not a strong claim and its weakened version. Each is a substantive, full-strength claim; what changes is the kind of claim being made. One is the claim that the author simply owns the work, in roughly the way a person owns a bicycle, before and apart from any statute. The other is the claim that we grant authors and inventors legal rights only because, and only insofar as, those rights are useful for getting books and drugs produced.

I keep coming back to a passage from Thomas Jefferson's letter to Isaac McPherson, dated August 13, 1813:

"Stable ownership is the gift of social law, and is given late in the progress of society. It would be curious then, if an idea, the fugitive fermentation of an individual brain, could, of natural right, be claimed in exclusive and stable property."

Jefferson goes on to say that society may choose to grant an exclusive right to the profits of an invention as an encouragement to invent, but that this is a matter of social convenience rather than the recognition of a pre-existing right. What I keep seeing is debaters crossing this line in both directions while talking as if they were standing still.

My question is whether there is an established name for this specific alternating pattern: defending copyright or patent by appealing to ordinary property-type ownership, shifting to an incentive rationale when the ownership claim is challenged, and shifting back to ownership language when the incentive rationale is challenged, all without acknowledging that the basis of the claim has changed. It is not quite motte-and-bailey, because neither rationale is a fallback or a stripped-down core; and it is not quite plain equivocation, because the two claims are not being confused simply because they share a word. If the exact pattern has no established name, I would like you to say so and to coin one, supplying a definition, a statement of how it differs from the nearest existing concepts, and an account of its boundary cases — for example, someone who openly holds a two-level view and says which rationale is doing the work in which context is not doing the thing I mean. I am less interested in the label than in getting the carving right. Please also say how confident you are in your identification, and single out the specific point of your answer you are least sure of.
