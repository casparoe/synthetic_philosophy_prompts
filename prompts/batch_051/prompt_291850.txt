I recently overheard a disagreement between two colleagues, Maya and Daniel, about a third colleague's answers to two hypothetical choice problems. I stayed out of it, because I found each of them persuasive at different points and could not tell who was right at each stage. Their exchange is reproduced below, as closely as I can remember it.

Sam had been asked:

Problem 1: Choose between
(A) $1,000,000 for certain;
(B) a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing.

Problem 2: Choose between
(C) an 11% chance of $1,000,000, otherwise nothing;
(D) a 10% chance of $5,000,000, otherwise nothing.

Sam chose A in Problem 1 and D in Problem 2.

The argument went as follows.

(1) Daniel said Sam's two answers cannot stand together. In Problem 1, A and B both contain an 89% chance of the same outcome, $1,000,000. What distinguishes them is the remaining 11%: certainty of $1,000,000 in A, versus a lottery in B that gives a 10/11 chance of $5,000,000 and a 1/11 chance of nothing. Problem 2 is produced by replacing that common 89% chance of $1,000,000 with an 89% chance of nothing while keeping the distinguishing 11% component unchanged. The independence axiom says that replacing an identical component of two options with some other identical component should not reverse one's preference between them. Since Sam prefers A to B, he ought to prefer C to D; instead he prefers D to C. Daniel concluded that Sam's choices cannot be represented as maximizing expected utility, and that this is a decisive mark against them.

(2) Maya replied that Daniel had shown only that Sam violates an axiom of expected utility theory, not that Sam is irrational. She said there is a defensible difference between the two problems. In Problem 1, the only way to obtain the 10% chance at $5,000,000 is to accept a 1% chance of ending up with nothing, and a certain $1,000,000 may reasonably be worth more than that package. In Problem 2, both options already carry an 89–90% chance of nothing, so Sam is effectively choosing between an 11% chance at $1,000,000 and a 10% chance at $5,000,000—and at that margin the larger prize can reasonably win. Certainty, she said, is not merely one more probability; the move from 89% to 100% can matter to a rational person even if the independence axiom has no place for it.

(3) Daniel answered that Maya was describing a psychological tendency, not a justification. If Sam's ranking genuinely depends on which common component is present, then Sam is vulnerable to a money pump: a sequence of trades, each acceptable given his own stated preferences, that leaves him with something he strictly disprefers and a fee paid along the way. A person whose choices permit this, Daniel argued, has no stable ranking of prospects at all. He is choosing in a frame-dependent way and therefore is not maximizing anything—so the failure is deeper than merely failing to maximize expected utility in particular.

(4) Maya replied that the money pump assumes Sam evaluates each trade in isolation and never sees the sequence as a whole. Once Sam is shown the entire schedule of trades in advance, he can simply decline the first trade, because he can see that it is the opening move of a losing sequence. She added that Daniel had smuggled in the very point at issue: that replacing the common 89% chance of $1,000,000 with an 89% chance of nothing is merely a re-description of the same choice. Sam can deny that it is irrelevant, and the independence axiom is precisely a stipulation about which re-descriptions count as irrelevant. It is a modeling convention, she said, not a dictate of rationality, and there are coherent alternatives to expected utility theory in which certainty may carry extra weight without exposing the agent to exploitation.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication of this exchange. For each of the four moves, identify which claims are correct, which are overstated, and why. In particular: (i) Is Sam's pair of choices irrational in any defensible sense of "irrational," or is it merely non-representable by expected utility? (ii) What role does the independence axiom actually play—is it a condition on rational preference, a condition on a certain kind of representation, or a modeling convention? (iii) Does the money-pump objection survive Maya's reply that Sam can see the whole sequence in advance? (iv) What, if anything, are both parties missing? I want the verdict tied to the arguments as they were actually made, not a general survey of decision theory.
