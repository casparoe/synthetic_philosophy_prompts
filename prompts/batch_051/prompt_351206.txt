I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on twentieth-century political thought, and we have just spent several weeks on Simone Weil's argument that political parties should simply be abolished. I find myself persuaded by the core of the position, which I would state in my own terms as follows: a party is not a neutral instrument that can be used well or badly; it is a structure organized around the conquest and defense of power, and that orientation makes party members systematically substitute collective interest, group loyalty, and manufactured passion for independent judgment about justice and truth. On this view parties do not merely risk degrading political thought. They are constitutively unsuited to be organs of political thought, and the apparent civic goods they provide — mobilizing citizens, simplifying choice, training politicians — are produced by exactly the mechanisms of group discipline that do the damage. The conclusion, that parties should be abolished rather than reformed, seems to follow, but it is a very strong conclusion, and I want a clear account of what would really count against it as opposed to what merely looks like a problem at first glance.

Please map the position's exposure for me. Specifically: what kind of observation, historical episode, or institutional arrangement would, if it stood up, disconfirm or at least substantially weaken the claim? Would a party that demonstrably transformed public common sense about justice — rather than mobilizing a preexisting bloc — count as a counterexample, or only show that the mechanism does not universalize? What should I make of party systems with open primaries, strict internal transparency, deliberative procedures, or proportional representation: does their success, if any, count against the Weil-ian line, or are they simply the least-bad cases the view already predicts? I would like the candidate defeaters ordered from the most damaging to the merely inconvenient, and for each a note on whether a committed defender would concede that it genuinely counts against the view or whether they could reasonably set it aside as accommodated, irrelevant, or misdescribed. Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the view, stated as strongly as I have stated it, can be reshaped to absorb every apparent counterexample, and whether that flexibility is itself a problem — or whether a weaker version, something like parties are always a structural risk to political judgment rather than always a disaster, is the strongest version the evidence will actually support.
