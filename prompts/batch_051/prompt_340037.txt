I've been reading William James's chapter on Habit in The Principles of Psychology, not for a class, just because a friend kept quoting it at me, and one of his practical maxims is stuck in my head. He lays out something like: when you are trying to establish a new habit, make the start as strong and decisive as possible, rearrange your circumstances so the new pattern gets supported, and then never allow a single slip until the habit is solidly rooted. His deeper point, as I read it, is that habit is a kind of flywheel or conservative force that keeps life running without conscious effort, which frees up the higher parts of the mind for the hard stuff.

I couldn't stop applying this to the whole rationality thing. People talk about red teaming your own views, noticing when you're rationalizing, checking the base rate, doing premortems. These seem like they would benefit from becoming automatic. If checking the base rate were as deeply drilled as a pianist's scale, it would not cost scarce attention in the moment. But then I thought about martial arts. A lot of traditional training uses katas, fixed sequences you repeat thousands of times to make them automatic. Yet plenty of fighters say kata by itself doesn't transfer to a real fight, because a real opponent doesn't move like the form. What you need is live sparring, unpredictable conditions, keeping an opponent from doing what he wants. So I genuinely can't tell which model fits rationality better. Is William James's habit-training picture closer to the kata idea, where you drill the right moves until they fire automatically? Or is it closer to sparring, where the only thing that transfers is practice under messy, resistant conditions? Please compare his account of habit with the kata versus sparring distinction as two models of training. In particular, what would the rationality equivalent of sparring be, and does his no-single-exception rule maybe produce rigid automaticity rather than the flexible judgment rationality needs? Also please say what would change your mind: which argument or empirical result would make you switch from one model to the other, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
