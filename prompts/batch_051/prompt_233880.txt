Set up a spectrum. There are 500 hospital policies, numbered 0 through 500. Moving from policy n to policy n+1 adds one second to the average ER wait and, in exchange, lowers each patient's annual chance of a fatal charting error by one in a trillion. Judged pairwise, every single step looks like an obvious improvement — a second of waiting for that risk reduction is clearly worth it. But policy 0 has a negligible wait and a 1-in-10,000 fatal-error risk, while policy 500 has a six-hour wait and essentially zero fatal-error risk; at the endpoints, policy 0 looks clearly better than policy 500. So if 'better than' is transitive, at least one of those obvious-looking adjacent judgments must be wrong.

I want the components seperated: the probability that apparent intransitivities are artifacts of vagueness or context-shift in 'better'; the probability that adjacent steps are merely roughly equal or on a par rather than determinately better, so the chain never forces the endpoint verdict; the probability that the endpoint judgment itself is the mistake; and any other component you think belongs. Attach a number and a one-line justification to each, show how you combined them, and flag which component the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me which consideration or evidence would move that number the farthest up or down, and where you think your reasoning most likely falls apart.
