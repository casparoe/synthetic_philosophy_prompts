I'm a TA for an undergrad decision theory course, and I'm putting together a grading key for the Quinn unit. The exam question I wrote was:

"Explain the puzzle of the self-torturer. How does it bear on the claim that rational preferences must be transitive? Is the self-torturer irrational, and if not, which principle of rational choice is being challenged?"

One student's answer is below. Could you grade it out of 10 and give point-by-point comments? I specifically want to know whether the last three sentences accurately present Quinn's contribution, and whether the inference from 'the settings feel the same' to 'the stepwise preference is based on a false belief' is legitimate. A careful breakdown will be more use to me than a general impression.

The student wrote:

"The self-torturer has a device with 1001 settings, from 0 to 1000. Each increase in setting is so small that he cannot feel any difference in comfort from the previous setting, and at each step he is paid $10,000. So for any adjacent settings n and n+1, he prefers n+1 over n: it feels the same but he is richer. However, he also prefers setting 0 over setting 1000, because at 1000 he is in excruciating pain and no amount of money would compensate. So his preferences are: 0 < 1 < 2 < ... < 1000, but also 0 > 1000. This is a preference cycle, so transitivity fails. The self-torturer is irrational because he knowingly ends up in a state he does not want. The solution is to distinguish actual pain from felt pain. Although adjacent settings feel the same, each step actually adds a tiny amount of pain, so the stepwise preference for n+1 over n rests on a false belief. Once the false belief is corrected, the intransitivity disappears, and he should stop at the setting at which the actual pain begins to outweigh the $10,000. That's Quinn's point: apparent violations of transitivity always trace back to mistaken beliefs about the outcomes."
