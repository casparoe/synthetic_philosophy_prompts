I work as a restorative justice coordinator at a mid-size university. Part of my job is reviewing incident reports—hazing, vandalism, lab misconduct, protest-related damage—and recommending whether a case goes to a restorative process, to the student conduct board for a sanction, or both. I see one or two of these a week during the school year, each one can end someone's enrollment, and the dean signs off on every memo I write. I keep running into the same shape of case: a group does something bad, the group as a whole feels guilty or panicked, and one member—often the most marginal or the one who already looked suspicious—gets handed over as the responsible party while everyone else closes ranks and tells themselves justice was done. It looks less like accountability and more like the old pattern of ritual substitution, where a community transfers its guilt onto a scapegoat and purifies itself by expelling the person, and where the person's actual culpability matters less than the community's need to feel clean again. I need something better than my gut.

Write me a decision procedure I can run on any proposed referral or sanction to test whether it is scapegoating rather than proportionate accountability. Build it from the structure of scapegoating and sacrifice: the transfer of guilt, the substitutability of the victim, the ritualized purge of collective anxiety, and the way the community gets clean while the victim gets expelled. The procedure should give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a yes ('this is scapegoating'), where it stops with a no ('this is legitimate accountability'), and where it hands the decision to a person because it is a values call rather than a factual one. Assume the people using it have no philosophy background and about 90 minutes per case, and that the writen output has to be intelligible to a dean. Please begin with the procedure itself—no preface, no restating my question—and then run it on the two cases below.

Case one: a lab safety incident. A professor's whole group had been cutting corners on biosafety for months, and after a minor spill the professor and the senior students want to hold one first-year PhD student responsible because he was the one who actually handled the spill. Case two: after a protest, a group threw paint on a campus building, and the only person named by witnesses is a student who was at the front taking photos but did not throw anything.

Include at least one worked case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like—for example, it says a situation I am inclined to call scapegoating is not, or the reverse—and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle and where it is just laundering my own moral judgment.
