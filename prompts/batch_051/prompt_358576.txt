Consider the following mechanism.

A national research council and the principal historical journals in its country jointly establish a Counterfactual Certification Panel. From next year, any claim in council-funded work that an event was inevitable, unavoidable, bound to happen, or strongly overdetermined may be published only if the author first submits a counterfactual appendix to the Panel. The appendix must specify two distinct branch points before the event, each one a point at which the historical path could plausibly have diverged, and must argue under stated assumptions that the event, or a close analogue of it, would still have occurred under both alternatives. The Panel issues one of three certificates: Certified Inevitable; Robust but Not Inevitable, in which case the claim must be softened accordingly; or Insufficient, in which case the claim may not appear in its original form in funded work. Journals that publish funded research agree to enforce the certificates, and the council will not renew grants to authors who repeatedly publish uncertified inevitability claims elsewhere. The Panel's deliberations are public, include dissents, and its members rotate every four years; authors may appeal only on procedural grounds. Ministers and public bodies may cite a certified claim with the certificate attached, while an uncertified inevitability claim is not banned but must be accompanied by a statement that it has not been counterfactually assessed.

Analyse how this scheme would actually behave, rather than judging it in the abstract. What does it incentivise historians to argue, avoid, and measure? How would smart actors work around it — by relabelling inevitability, by choosing branch points so close to the event that the alternatives are trivial, by gaming the appeal route, or by moving strong claims into outlets the scheme does not reach? What second-order effects would it have on the status hierarchy of the discipline, on which historical episodes get treated as contingent, and on the public use of history? Identify the failure mode you judge most serious and defend that choice; then say whether a concrete amendment — for instance, letting critics nominate the branch points, anonymising submissions, or permitting only a limited number of certificates each year — would fix that failure without gutting the scheme. Give a committed forecast of the equilibrium you expect, with reasons, rather than a menu of possibilities.
