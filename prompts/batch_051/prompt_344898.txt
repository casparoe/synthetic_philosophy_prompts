The exam question (25 points): Does the Buddhist doctrine of anattā entail that there is no personal identity over time? Distinguish anattā from the view that persons are unreal, and say how a Buddhist account can make room for the continuity involved in rebirth.

The student's answer: Anattā is the teaching that there is no permanent, unchanging self or essence underlying experience. The Buddha analyzed the person into the five aggregates—form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness—and argued that none of these, singly or together, qualifies as a self, since they are all impermanent and conditioned. This rules out a substance view of the self, but it does not by itself rule out personal identity in every sense.

The key point is that Buddhism does not deny that persons exist at the conventional, everyday level; it denies that the conventional person corresponds to some separate, enduring thing. And the tradition's commitment to rebirth confirms this: if there were no continuity at all, there would be nothing to be reborn. So the right conclusion is that Buddhist no-self is basically a version of Locke's memory criterion of personal identity. Just as Locke says personal identity consists in continuity of consciousness and memory rather than sameness of substance, Buddhism says what makes the reborn person continuous with the earlier one is the causal continuity of the aggregates, especially the stream of consciousness. Because both views locate identity in psychological continuity rather than in an underlying substance, anattā does not entail that there is no personal identity over time; it just relocates identity.

Someone might object that the Buddha is more radical than Locke, since he denies the self as such, whereas Locke only denies that substance is what matters. But that is really a difference in emphasis. Locke and the Buddhists are both bundle theorists before the term existed; both hold that the self is a collection of mental states held together by causal and memorial links. So the answer is no: anattā is compatible with personal identity over time, provided identity is understood as psychological continuity.
