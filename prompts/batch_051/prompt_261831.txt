I find myself unable to settle a conflict between a theory I am drawn to and an intuition I cannot shake. The theory is that the badness of pain—its unpleasantness, its power to demand relief, its status as a reason to avoid what causes it—is not intrinsic to the sensory quality of pain but is a separable affective and motivational component. Pain asymbolia seems to display this: patients correctly locate a stimulus and rate its intensity as a sharp, burning pain in the hand, yet report that it does not feel unpleasant, do not withdraw from it, and may even laugh at it. If the same sensory quality can persist while the badness vanishes, the badness must be something the subject contributes rather than something in the sensation. The intuition is that such patients are not in pain at all, but only in a pain-like state plus a pathological deficit: a pain that no one minds is not a pain in the full sense, and a deficit that strips away the minding shows only that the patient has lost contact with the pain's nature, not that the nature was never in the sensation. Present the strongest version of each side, and then arbitrate: should the intuition be debunked, should the component theory be amended or abandoned, or is there a reading on which they are compatible—and what would justify each of those three outcomes? In particular, say whether the conflict is about the state itself or only about the word pain and the taxonomy built around it.
