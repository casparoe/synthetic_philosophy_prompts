I have been trying to sort out what I actually think about greed and acquisitiveness, and my reactions to concrete cases refuse to line up into any principle I can state. I am going to give you my verdicts first, as honestly as I can, and then ask you to find the principle behind them, or to tell me which verdicts have to go.

A trader spends weeks reconstructing a company's accounts, concludes the company has been booking phantom revenue, shorts the stock, and profits when the fraud unravels. I think this is fine, even valuable: the gain tracks real insight, and the trader bore risk.

A mid-level employee happens to see an internal message indicating that her own firm is about to miss earnings, and quietly sells her shares before the announcement. I think this is not fine. It is also profiting from an informational edge, but the edge comes from breaching a confidence and a duty.

A mechanic in a remote town, the only garage for forty miles, quotes a stranded family double his normal price to fix their car, and they pay because towing would cost more and take days. I think this is not fine.

A consultant with an unusual skill quotes a large multinational five times her ordinary rate, because she knows the engagement is worth millions to the company and it can easily pay; the company negotiates only a little and agrees. I think this is fine, though it is plainly acquisitive.

A landlord raises the rent by a quarter in one year on a longtime tenant with health problems and no realistic alternative, purely because market rents have risen. I think this is not fine, even though it is legal.

A junior academic negotiates a much higher salary with a university that has told her she is its only viable candidate and must sign by a deadline. She demands far more than the initial offer and gets it. I think this is fine. I can see that it resembles the landlord case: both press a bargaining advantage against a party with few options, and my reactions split anyway.

A company buys the rights to an old generic drug, improves nothing about its manufacture or distribution, and raises the price several hundred percent over a few months; hospitals and patients must pay the new price or go without. I think this is not fine.

The case I keep going back and forth on is ticket resale. A person buys four tickets to a sold-out concert intending to go, then cannot attend, and resells them on an exchange at three times face value. I cannot decide whether that is closer to the mechanic or to the consultant, or something else entirely.

Two pairs bother me in particular. The mechanic and the consultant both raise their price because of scarcity, yet I condemn one and excuse the other. The landlord and the academic both press a bargaining advantage against a counterparty with limited options, yet my reactions differ. My rough rule is that seeking more is legitimate when the gain comes from creating value, from absorbing risk, or from a bargain between parties who can walk away, and becomes vice when it extracts payment from another person's necessity or from information obtained by a breach of confidence. That rule, however, has too many clauses, and I suspect it is just a list of my reactions with more abstract names.

What I want is the simplest principle that captures the pattern in these verdicts, or a small set of candidate principles if one will not do. State it precisely, not as a slogan. Then tell me: which of my cases motivates each part of the principle; what it implies about the ticket-resale case and about two further cases I have not listed; and which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the only principle that fits all of them is gerrymandered past the point of being a principle, say so directly and tell me where I should change my mind.
