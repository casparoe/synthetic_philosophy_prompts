In my philosophy class we read a handout on family ethics that summarized the philosopher Jane English's friendship model of filial obligation. The handout says this: English argues that adult children do not owe their parents anything, because you can only owe someone something if you freely accepted a benefit or made a promise; since no one chooses to be born, the care parents provide cannot create a debt. Any obligations grown children do have toward their parents, the handout continues, come from the same place as obligations between friends: an ongoing relationship of mutual care and trust. So if you and your mother are close, you should help her when she needs it, not to repay her for raising you, but because that is what friends do. If the relationship has broken down, however, even parents who sacrificed enormously have no claim on their children.

I am inclined to think this is mostly right, but something worries me: it seems to make whether I should help my parents depend on how warm my current feelings are, and to erase every obligation the moment a relationship goes badly, even if a parent has done a great deal for me. Before I rely on this for my paper, could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts someone familiar with English's actual view would accept, which are subtly unfair or overstated, and whether the final claim about broken relationships actually follows from the accurate parts?
