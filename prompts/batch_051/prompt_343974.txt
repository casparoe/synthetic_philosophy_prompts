I keep trying to work out what philosophy is hiding inside the way asylum law actually works, and I can't shake the feeling that the whole thing presupposes a very particular, rarely-spelled-out picture of the relation between a person and their state. Take the standard definition: a refugee is someone outside their country of nationality who has a well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership of a particular social group. The things this definition rules out are what puzzle me. Someone whose village is slowly swallowed by the sea and who can never go back isn't a refugee. Someone fleeing a civil war in which the danger is generalized rather than aimed at them as an individual isn't a refugee either, unless they can squeeze themselves into one of the five categories. Someone who fled the same death squads but only made it two provinces over is classified as an internally displaced person—same killers, same danger—but the law treats them as a different kind of problem. And in practice, many rich states will bounce a person back to the first country they passed through as if that country's protection were interchangeable with their own. What does this whole apparatus presuppose about what persecution is and about what it means to be protected by a state? It seems to assume that persecution matters only when it targets you for being the sort of person you are, rather than because it is simply dangerous to be in a place, and that there is something specially broken about the bond between citizen and state that only becomes a refugee problem once the person has crossed an international border. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind this as charitably as you can, say whether the pieces—the five grounds, the outside-the-country requirement, the first-safe-country practice—really presuppose the same picture or are just historical compromises bolted together, and then tell me what would have to change in practice if we replaced that picture with a plain harm-based standard: serious, ongoing threat to basic rights and no adequate protection where the person is, regardless of cause, category, or location.
