I'm a retired hydrologist — spent forty years expecting every surprising model output to have a identifiable mechanism — and lately I've gotten into the philosophy of explanation, computational irreducibility, the idea that some behavior has no shorter explanation than just running the simulation. Now I chair a small technical advisory panel for a water district. Maybe ten times a year we have to decide whether a contractor's flood or water-quality model is good enough to base a permit on, and we cant run our own models from scratch. The staff keep demanding that modelers produce a mechanistic story for every odd output, and the modelers keep shrugging and saying the output just is what the simulation does. Turn computational irreducibility and the limits of explanation into a reusable procedure I can actually run in a two-hour panel meeting: ordered tests or a checklist for when to keep pushing for a mechanism (and what kind), and when to stop and fall back on coarse statistical validation, scenario ranges, fit to the historical record, that sort of thing. It has to be usable by people who are never going to read the primary literature. Be explicit about the hard cases — especially where you cannot tell true irreducibility from 'we just havent found the short explanation yet', or from chaos, underdetermined parameters, or a plain buggy model — and mark the steps that are really judgment calls. Then work it through two or three cases, at least one where the procedure tells me something I dont like (I already suspect it will tell me my instinct to always demand a mechanism is part of the problem), and say plainly what the procedure cannot do.
