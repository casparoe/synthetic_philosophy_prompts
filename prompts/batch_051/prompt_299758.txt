You are a political philosopher who works on comparative church–state arrangements and on the history of political thought, and who is used to teaching these issues in both law-and-religion and political theory courses. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on church and state in modern democracies, and I am writing the final exam. I want one essay question on James Madison’s argument in the Memorial and Remonstrance against Religious Assessments (1785) that cannot be answered by reciting Madison’s reputation or the two religion clauses of the First Amendment. The question must force students to reconstruct Madison’s equality argument from the text and then test it against four actually existing regimes: England, with its established Church of England; Germany, with its state-collected church tax; France, with laïcité; and the United States, with formal disestablishment plus religious exemptions and accommodations. The exam question itself should give students a one-sentence institutional sketch of each of the four regimes, so that performance does not depend on recalling, say, the German church-tax rate or the details of the 1905 French law.

The question must turn on the following passage, which is Article 4 of the Memorial. Keep it verbatim in the handout:

Above all are they to be considered as retaining an "equal title to the free exercise of Religion according to the dictates of Conscience." [Virginia Declaration of Rights, art. 16] Whilst we assert for ourselves a freedom to embrace, to profess and to observe the Religion which we believe to be of divine origin, we cannot deny an equal freedom to those whose minds have not yet yielded to the evidence which has convinced us. If this freedom be abused, it is an offence against God, not against man: To God, therefore, not to man, must an account of it be rendered.

Before you write anything final, think carefully about what a strong answer must establish and what weaker students most often confuse, and let that diagnosis shape both the exam question and the marking scheme. In particular, do not let the question collapse into "was Madison right about religion?"; the point is to make students argue which regime best honors, and which most strains, the equality principle in the passage.

Then write all of the following, in American English:

1. The exam question itself, exactly as it would appear on a handout: one essay prompt with the passage included, for a three-hour final in which students answer one of three questions, worth 40 marks.

2. A model answer of about 650 words, in American English, that engages the passage line by line where needed rather than summarizing the four countries.

3. A marking scheme that separates A, B, and C answers. For the A, name the specific moves it must contain—at minimum: (a) distinguishing Madison’s equality of free exercise from mere toleration or non-persecution; (b) separating the two dimensions on which the regimes vary, establishment/status and money/assessment, and locating the regimes on each rather than ranking them by how secular they look; (c) taking a definite position on at least England, Germany, and France by arguing from the passage rather than from national stereotypes; and (d) confronting the sentences “If this freedom be abused, it is an offence against God, not against man: To God, therefore, not to man, must an account of it be rendered” and explaining what they entail about the state policing religious expression—this bears directly on whether French laïcité is Madisonian. For the B, say which of those moves are present but underdeveloped. For the C, name the specific misconceptions the marking scheme catches—for example, treating laïcité as automatically Madisonian; treating Germany’s church tax as obviously refuting or obviously satisfying Madison because membership can be exited; equating English establishment with persecution of dissenters after the nineteenth century; or using “separation of church and state” as a slogan without analyzing institutional mechanics.

Do not ask for or provide a bibliography or reading list; the passage is the only source the question requires.
