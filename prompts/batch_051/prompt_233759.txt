I work as a software engineer, with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to settle a question about what makes a life good. The question is whether novelty and variety are valuable in themselves, or whether they matter only instrumentally, for example because they prevent boredom, produce memories, or enable growth. My starting assumption was something like a function that treats the value of a life as depending on how it feels from the inside plus what it achieves. But when I test that assumption against my own judgements about particular cases, I get inconsistent results, and I would like an account that either reconciles them or tells me which ones to abandon.

Consider these cases and my verdicts on them. (1) Two lives contain the same overall enjoyment, health, and close relationships, and comparable achievements; one is highly routine while the other is full of travel, new projects, and changed circumstances. I judge the varied life better, and I do not think this reduces to a difference in moment-to-moment enjoyment. (2) A master woodworker performs essentially the same craft every morning for forty years and finds it absorbing and satisfying; I do not think his life is missing anything important simply because it is repetitive. (3) A person who chases novelty for its own sake, switching hobbies every few months and never completing anything, seems to me to be living worse than the woodworker, not better. (4) For a holiday, I would pay somewhat more to visit somewhere unfamiliar than to return to a resort I know I will thoroughly enjoy, even if the new place is expected to be slightly less comfortable. That preference weakens by the fourth consecutive holiday; novelty seems to have diminishing appeal. (5) Offered a life that repeats one very pleasant day forever, with no memory of the cycle, I would refuse, even though each iteration would feel as good as the first.

I cannot see what single principle handles all of these: the first and fifth cases suggest variety has value of its own, but the second and third pull against that, and the fourth suggests it matters with diminishing marginal value. The pair I find hardest to reconcile is my preference for variety in my own life and my conviction that the repetitive craftsman is not missing anything. As an engineer I keep wanting to model a life as a set of momentary states and aggregate them, and these cases feel like a test suite that my model fails. I would like you to identify the best available principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts; state precisely how each clause is motivated by a case; say which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate; and tell me what it predicts about cases I have not listed, such as adventures someone can never remember, or a varied life lived entirely alone. Please write your answer in British English.
