I'm the metadata editor for a digitization project that catalogs manuscript and early print material from the Italian peninsula, roughly 800–1450. Our catalogers tag every record with a language label, and the great majority of records are tagged either Latin or Italian. The catalogers are trained in descriptive cataloging but not in Romance historical linguistics, and they process about thirty records a week, spending ten to fifteen minutes per record. The stakes are medium: the labels drive faceted search, corpus queries, and some public-facing exhibits, so a mislabel is visible and persistent, but nothing legal or commercial turns on it.

The working view I'd like you to operationalize, rather than argue for, is roughly this: there is no moment at which Latin became Italian, and there is no hidden fact about the "real" language of a transitional text. Language identity is partly continuous grammatical and lexical transmission, partly a social fact about how a text and its community described themselves, and partly a retrospective labeling decision made for a purpose. A decision procedure should therefore not pretend to detect the true language of a text; it should produce a defensible label for our project, attached to the evidence, and it should mark where another project with different purposes might reasonably label the same text differently.

Please write a reusable checklist-style decision procedure that a cataloger without specialist training can run. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, when the procedure stops, and when it hands the record to me or to a consulting Romance philologist. The procedure should cover at least: (a) the metadata to record before any linguistic judgment — date, region, genre, whether the text is a copy or original, and any language the scribe or rubric actually names; (b) a short battery of surface markers a nonspecialist can reliably spot — presence of definite articles like *lo* or *la*, whether nouns show case endings, how the future and conditional are formed, and whatever else you think carries the most weight; (c) a rule for what to do when markers conflict or are too sparse, including a mixed or "not assignable" outcome instead of forcing a Latin/Italian binary; and (d) an explicit step that records whose purposes the label serves and flags cases where a different user would reasonably want a different label.

The constraints are these: the procedure must be runnable in ten to fifteen minutes per record; it must not require parsing the text or using specialist tools; it must not treat mutual intelligibility with modern Italian as evidence, since that simply reads today's boundary back into the past; and it must state plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It should also require the cataloger to record the label and the supporting marker profile together, so a future project can re-label the same record without re-examining the physical item.

Then run the procedure on these three records and show exactly what it would output:

1. A ninth-century northern Italian contract in a formulaic legal register. The scribe describes it as a *chartula* in Latin; the text has no definite articles and nouns show case endings, but some spellings are non-classical (for example, *e*/*i* confusion).

2. A twelfth-century Tuscan sale record, short and plain. It has definite articles (for example, *la casa*), no noun case endings except in frozen phrases, and a Romance future/conditional construction. The scribe says nothing about the language.

3. A late thirteenth-century Umbrian didactic poem that the project has been publicly describing as the earliest Italian in the collection. It has vernacular articles and verb forms but also heavy Latin-style spellings, and the scribe calls the language *vulgare*.

Make sure at least one of the three comes out in a way you expect I won't like, and say briefly why the procedure returns that answer anyway.

Finally, please end the procedure itself with an honest note on what it cannot do — for example, settle what these texts "really" are, adjudicate claims about linguistic patrimony, or replace the judgment of a historical linguist in borderline cases.

One more thing, and I mean it. If you think this request rests on a confused premise — for instance, that the honest answer to "when does Latin become Italian?" is to dissolve the question rather than to operationalize it, or that a checklist like this will inevitably manufacture false precision — tell me that directly and give me the nearest workable alternative, rather than complying politely with a procedure you think is misleading. If only parts of the task can be done well, say which parts and what you'd need in order to do them well.
