I'm trying to write up my views on simple living for a small magazine, and before I send it in I want a consistency audit of the whole package. Here's the set of things I think I believe, roughly as I'd put them:

1. Voluntary simplicity is a real ideal. A life with fewer possessions, less buying, and more time not spent acquiring and maintaining stuff is not just a valid preference but a morally better life, and we'd be better of if more people aimed at it. Its point is a fuller, more enjoyable, less encumbered life — I'm not in this to be worse off, and I don't care about purity for its own sake.

2. I'm suspicious of asceticism as a thing in itself. Denying yourself something genuinely good just to prove you can, or because you think suffering is inherently purifying, usually looks to me like ego or scrupulosity wearing a hairshirt. If a practice makes you miserable and produces nothing besides the self-denial, I don't think it's admirable.

3. Cheapness is often waste in disguise. Buying the expensive repairable tool, the boots that last a decade, the pan your grandkids will fight over, is more frugal than buying the cheap thing three times. Durability and repairability count as frugality, not just spending.

4. People in rich countries have an obligation to reduce their personal consumption because of climate and resource use. That obligation isn't optional and it isn't offset by recycling.

5. I also believe my own reduced consumption — one person flying less, one person eating less meat — makes no difference to the climate outcome. The damage is done by aggregate demand and policy, not by any one of us, and the 'what if everyone did that?' argument rests on a false premise, because everyone won't and my choice changes no one else's choice.

6. Status-motivated luxury — buying the branded thing to be seen with it — is one of the least respectable uses of money, and a culture that admired people for consuming less would be better than one that admires conspicuous consumption. But I also don't think there's anything wrong with good tools, good coffee, or a beautiful pot you use every day.

I can't tell where the real fault lines are. Some of these feel like they might be in tension, but I've held them long enough that they all feel natural. Please go through the pairs — all of them, not just the ones that jump out — and sort them into genuine tensions, apparent tensions that dissolve, and pairs that are just compatible. For each genuine tension, tell me how bad it is and what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me. I don't need a lecture on the history of asceticism or any reading list.

Format the audit as a table, listing all fifteen pairs. Put genuine tensions first, sorted by severity, then apparent tensions, then no-tension pairs. Columns:
- Pair: which numbered commitments are involved
- Kind: genuine tension / apparent tension / no tension
- Severity: 1 to 5, where 5 means the set can't stand as written
- What's actually pulling: one or two sentences
- Cheapest repair: the smallest change or clarification that resolves it
- Cost of that repair: what I'd have to give up or weaken

After the table, give me a one-sentence verdict on whether the whole set survives with minor repairs or needs surgery.
