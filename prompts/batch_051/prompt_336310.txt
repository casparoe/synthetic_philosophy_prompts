I'm preparing to teach Stigler's law of eponymy to first-year undergraduates in a one-off 50-minute "science in society" seminar. The students will have taken at least one science course but none in the history or sociology of science, and they will have done no assigned reading. The core content I want them to grasp is that the law, as Stephen Stigler stated it in the 1980 paper that gave the law its name, says: "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer." I also want them to absorb the self-exemplifying coda: Stigler attributed the law to the sociologist Robert K. Merton, so the name "Stigler's law" obeys Stigler's law. My aim is not for the students to collect a list of misnamed discoveries; it is for them to understand why eponymy systematically detaches from priority, and to be able to say what an eponym in science is and is not evidence of.

Please plan the explanation rather than write the explanation itself. Specify:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and what depends on what. I suspect the students need a working distinction between discovery, publication or promotion, and naming before the law can mean anything, but do not take that order for granted: argue for whatever dependency structure you choose.

2. The single carrying example around which the session should be built. Say what this example must do and must not do: it should have an uncontroversially earlier discoverer and a later namesake whose contribution was nonetheless real, so the point is not "the namesake was a fraud"; it should hold up if a student tries to rescue the eponym by redefining "discoverer"; and it should not require students to master specialized historiography. Justify the choice of example against those constraints.

3. The misconceptions students predictably bring or form, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. In particular, address the likely slide from "the name does not track priority" to "eponyms are just credit theft" or "the named scientist contributed nothing," and the conflation with the Matthew effect, where credit flows to the already eminent regardless of priority. Include any other misconceptions you expect.

4. A check for understanding that would show the idea has landed rather than showing that they have memorized examples. It should require them to apply the distinction between priority and eponymy to a new case, or to predict when an eponym is likely to stick.

5. What should be left out at this level, and what to say to the students about why it is being left out. I want the session to remain honest about the limits of the law without turning into a historiography lecture or a systematic treatment of the sociology of scientific credit.
