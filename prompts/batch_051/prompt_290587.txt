I sit on an appeals panel for a state that brought in parenting licences back in the eighties. The Act says an applicant has to satisfy the Board that they will raise any child in their care 'so as to keep open for the child, as far as reasonably practicable, a fair range of substantially different life paths, and not to foreclose options that the child might later reasonably wish to have.' The clause was drafted in 1987.

Now we've got a couple who run a family channel with about three million subscribers. They live-stream most of the day and have already signed a multi-year deal that they describe as 'bringing the baby on from birth.' The contract commits any child in their care to appear in sponsored content until sixteen, pays a cut into a trust the parents control, and if the child refuses to appear there's a financial clawback. The parents say the clause doesn't bite: the kid could still become a doctor or a welder and just show up on camera sometimes; the money opens options rather than closing them; and the provision was aimed at closed sects and single-career hothouses, not a kid who happens to have a camera in the house.

Does the clause reach them? I want you to run it through the main approaches: the ordinary meaning of the words, the purpose it was enacted for, what the 1987 drafters would have said if you'd shown them a live-streamed childhood, and what a reasonable person reading the provision today would think it covers. If the approaches come apart, whcih should decide the appeal, and why? And does the clawback or the fact the child is monetized before they can consent matter to the lisence, or am I just smuggling in my own views about what childhood should be?
