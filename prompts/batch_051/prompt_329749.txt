I'm taking a decision theory class and we've been working through the St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if the first heads is on toss n the payoff is 2^n dollars, which gives an infinite expected value. Our instructor had us tweak the parameters, and one variation has been bugging me all week. Suppose the coin is biased so heads comes up with probability 0.51 rather than 0.5. With a little algebra the expected value turns out to be finite — exactly 51 dollars, since it's 2p/(2p − 1) when heads has probability p. But here's what's weird: about 42.5 of those 51 expected dollars come from outcomes where the first heads is on toss 10 or later, and that whole tail has a combined probability of only about 0.16%. So with about 99.8% probability you win at most $512, and conditional on being in that likely case, the expected payout is only around $8.50. And yet the expected-value "fair price" for the game is $51.

Can you walk through how this version should be analyzed? Specifically, does the paradox actually dissapear just because the expectation is finite, or does it get relocated into a tiny-probability tail? I'd like you to go through what the standard resolutions — diminishing marginal utility, finite bankroll or a house cap on payoffs, risk aversion, and the thought that repeated play changes the calculation — would say about this particular biased version, and then say which (if any) gives a satisfying account of why almost nobody would actually pay 51 dollars to play it.
