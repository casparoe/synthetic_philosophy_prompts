Linguists and laypeople alike sometimes lean on the analogy that a writing system is a theory of the language it writes — an alphabet, the story goes, is a phonemic analysis: it commits to which sound contrast are linguistically real and treats the rest as sub-phonemic noise. The analogy gets used to beat up on English spelling: if a script is a theory, then English orthography is a bad theory because its letter-to-sound mapping is so inconsistent, while Finnish or Spanish spelling is a good one. I want you to audit how far this actually holds. Start by laying out the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to what (letters, digraphs, spelling rules, silent letters, punctuation)? Then say where the analogy breaks. For instance, does it smuggle in the assumption that a script's only job is to represent sounds — what about morphophonemic spellings like electric/electricity, or etymological letters like the b in debt? What does it predict about abjads, which leave out most vowels, or logographic systems like Chinese characters, and does the analogy quietly take alphabetic writing as the paradigm case? Finally, say whether the conclusion people draw — that English spelling is a badly designed theory — actually survives the audit, or whether it just assumes scripts should be phonemic transcriptions. And if there's a better analogy once this one gives out, say what it is.
