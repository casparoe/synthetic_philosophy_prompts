Last night my friend and I spent the whole evening arguing about my job, and I still don't know if I'm the bad guy. I work at a company that builds "legacy avatars" — chatbots trained on a dead person's texts, voice notes, and social posts, which a family member or close friend can talk to after the death. I review requests: there are maybe a dozen a week, and each one goes to me and two other people on a 30-minute trust and safety call. We have no real protocol — one of us asks "would they have wanted this?", someone else says "the widow is grieving, how can we say no?", and eventually the strongest personality wins. My friend, who is a hospice nurse, argued the whole product is unethical: a griefbot dresses up an inability to let go as a product, the dead can't meaningfully consent, and the comparison to reading old letters or watching home videos is a sleight of hand, because a photograph is quiet and you do the interpreting, while a bot talks back and invites you to treat it as the person. When I pushed back that some people seem genuinely helped and a blanket ban would be paternalistic, he agreed, which just made the argument worse. So now I have a pile of objectons and no decision rule, and next week's cases are going to be decided by whoever had more coffee.

I need you to turn the best version of both positions into a procedure my team can actually run on a request. Give me a ordered list of questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the process stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the facts can't settle it. We don't have a clinician on staff, so anywhere the procedure needs an actual guess about someone's mental state, say so and tell me what kind of outside review to flag. Then run the procedure on three requests we will probably see some version of: (1) a widow wants an avatar of her husband, who never said one way or the other, built from their private texts; (2) a mother wants an avatar of her 16-year-old who died by suicide, built from the kid's own messages, so the younger siblings can "say goodbye," and she is the only requester; (3) a man wants an avatar of a friend he had a bitter falling-out with, so he can "finish the conversation." Make sure at least one case ends with the procedure telling me to do something I will not like. And finally tell me which cases the procedure will still get wrong and which questions it simply cannot answer, so we don't pretend we have removed the judgement from the decision.
