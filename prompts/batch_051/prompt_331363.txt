I am trying to decide whether a colleague is making a testable claim or only re-describing the same phenomena in teleological language. He puts a great deal of weight on this passage from Aristotle’s *Physics*, Book II, Chapter 1, in the Hardie–Gaye translation:

“Each of them has within itself a principle of motion and of stationariness (in respect of place, or of growth and decrease, or by way of alteration). On the other hand, a bed and a coat and anything else of that sort, qua receiving these designations i.e. in so far as they are products of art-have no innate impulse to change.”

His gloss is that natural things, unlike artifacts, possess an internal source of change that cannot be exhaustively decomposed into the arrangement of their material parts and the external efficient causes acting on them. From this he infers that at least some organic changes are genuinely directed toward an end in a way that a purely material-efficient description would leave out. I want to know whether this last inference is vulnerable to empirical test. Design the strongest test you can of the claim that a biological or physical process has an end-directed character not reducible to material and efficient causes, specify what result would count for and against it, and say honestly what a positive result would and would not settle about the standing of the final cause in Aristotle’s four-cause scheme.
