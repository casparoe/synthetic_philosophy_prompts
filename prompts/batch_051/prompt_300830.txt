I keep noticing that my commitments about the geometry of semantic embedding spaces are doing different things, and I would like an audit of whether they actually hang together. Here is the set of positions I find myself wanting to hold:

(1) Semantic similarity is graded, and distance in a high-dimensional distributional embedding space is our best general model of it: a word's nearest neighbors are, for the most part, the words most similar to it.

(2) The arithmetic of embeddings is semantically meaningful. The offset that takes one from "man" to "king" is approximately the offset from "woman" to "queen", and more generally, linear directions in embedding space can correspond to interpretable features or relations such as gender, age, or formality.

(3) But I am not a naïve geometrical realist. The coordinate system itself is arbitrary up to rotation, and there is no privileged axis that is the gender axis or the formality axis. What is real, I want to say, is the relational structure represented by the geometry, not any particular coordinate frame.

(4) I accept the empirical fact that antonym pairs such as "hot" and "cold" are typically close in embedding space, and I also do not want to say that hot and cold are semantically similar — they are opposites. I likewise do not think proximity in embedding space is sufficient for semantic similarity, let alone synonymy; co-hyponyms and other tightly related terms cluster together as well.

(5) Meaning is use, and context is not a correction to a fixed core but part of what fixes meaning. I therefore regard contextual embeddings, which assign "bank" one vector in "river bank" and another in "central bank", as a better model of word meaning than one fixed vector per word.

(6) At the same time, I think that interpolating between two sentence embeddings, or continuously moving a vector along an interpretable direction, yields something like an intermediate or modified meaning. I take this to be evidence that embedding geometry tracks genuine semantic structure rather than mere co-occurrence artifacts.

(7) For natural categories I hold a prototype view: conceptual membership is graded, most boundaries are vague, and there are no crisp necessary-and-sufficient conditions for category membership.

Is this a consistent package? I am not looking for a general lecture on embeddings, but for a structured audit: which pairs are in real tension, which apparent tensions are spurious, how severe each conflict is, and which one or two commitments would have to be revised — and in what way — for the rest to cohere.
