I've adopted a standing policy in my own thinking that I privately call my Meno rule. I'm persuaded that knowledge is worth more than mere true belief, and that the difference has something to do with being able to give an account of why the belief is true. So whenever I notice that I hold a belief I take to be true but cannot even roughly articulate its basis—what evidence it rests on, what pattern I'm responding to, or how someone would go about checking it—I demote it. For purposes of assertion and decision, I treat it as no better than a guess and refuse to lean on it, even when it came from a source I trust or feels like the product of trained judgment rather than luck.

In practice this makes me slower, and it sometimes makes me worse at tasks where I used to rely on inarticulate pattern-recognition; more than once I have declined to act on a strong hunch, only to be proved right later and reconstruct the reasons afterward. I've kept the rule anyway, because a true belief I cannot explain looks to me like it is stuck: I can't teach it, I can't pass it to someone who doubts me, and I can't tell whether I'm tracking something real or just rehearsing a lucky prejudice. But I worry the rule is a rationalist prejudice of its own—that it confuses the ability to verbalize a justification with actually having one, and that it penalizes tacit knowledge, sound instinct, or reasons I once had and have since forgotten while keeping the lesson. Is my Meno rule a defensible way of honoring the difference in value between knowledge and true belief, or is it too strict? If it is partly misguided, is there a version of the rule that keeps the benefits without the costs?
