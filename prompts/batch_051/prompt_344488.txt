So Ive been trying to think through Origen — the third-century Alexandrian exegete — not as a museum piece but as a live voice. You probably know the rough picture: he thinks all of scripture is divinely inspired and that every passage has a meaning worth mining, but he also thinks taking every passage as literal history is exactly wrong. He reads the bits that would come out silly or unworthy of God if taken flat-footedly — the six days of Genesis, God walking in the garden, the commands to wipe out whole cities — as deliberate snags the Spirit left there to force readers toward a deeper sense. But he's not just an allegorist either; he has a whole multi-level scheme and he thinks the straightforward sense does real work for simple people.

Heres the armchair game. Drop him into a debate between a modern fundamentalist and a modern progressive Christian. The fundamentalist holds an inerrancy line: scripture makes no false claims about history or science, Genesis 1 is six literal days, Adam is a historical individual, the resurrection is a physical event. The progressive says Genesis is basically mythic poetry and doesn't much mind if the resurrection is metaphorically true rather than an event that happened. Where does Origen land? I don't want a summary of his hermeneutics. I want a prediction: which of his commitments would push him toward each side, where exactly he would break with both, and whether he would even think the literal-vs-allegorical frame the debaters assume is the real issue. Would he find one side's anxieties more intelligible than the other's, and why?
