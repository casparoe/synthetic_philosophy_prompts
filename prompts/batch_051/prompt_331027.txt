For the past year I have been developing a criterion for deciding when a piece of information about a person belongs to them in the strong, property-like sense that matters for data protection. I call it the Practical-Identity Criterion, and I would like a candid assessment of it.

The criterion has two conditions. A datum about a person P belongs to P in the property-relevant sense just in case it satisfies both: (1) a production condition — the datum would not exist, or would not have the content it has, without some activity of P; and (2) an ascription condition — the datum is used, or is readily usable, as a premise in practical inference about P, where such inference bears on what P will do, receive, or deserve. When both conditions are met, P has a legitimate complaint against others who collect or use that datum for such inferences, even if the datum is public or was computed by someone else. When only one condition is met, the complaint is correspondingly weaker.

The appeal is that it handles cases that simpler accounts mishandle. A person's browsing history passes both conditions and feels strongly theirs. A background figure in someone else's holiday photograph passes the production condition but, in ordinary use, not the ascription condition, so there is little reason to treat the image as theirs. Most importantly, a shoplifting-risk score computed by a store from a person's public movements passes both conditions even though the store did the computing — which is exactly why such scores feel contestable: the prediction is about them and built from them. Yet the criterion has a consequence I find both right and unmanageable: the store and the person would each hold some claim over the resulting score, because each satisfies a production condition. The view thus implies joint ownership of an inference.

I would like four things. First, restate the criterion in your own words and say whether it is coherent, or whether the two conditions ultimately collapse into one. Second, say whether this is already worked out under another name in debates about treating personal data as property, and what, if anything, my version adds. Third, construct the strongest counterexample you can: a case where the criterion assigns a claim that is clearly wrong, or denies one that is clearly right. Fourth, tell me whether the joint-ownership implication is fatal, or whether it can be handled by distinguishing the underlying datum from the inference built on it.
