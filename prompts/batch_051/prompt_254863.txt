Write the joint statement that a Burkean conservative and a reform-minded rationalist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about Chesterton's fence: the principle, roughly, that an inherited institution or practice should not be abolished until the reformer understands what it was for. The document should neither paper over the real disagreement nor leave it as a clash of slogans.

Structure it as follows. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what the fence principle actually says, as against the caricatures that it forbids all change or that it only asks reformers to be polite; what both sides accept about the fallibility of present reasoning and of inherited practice; what both sides accept about the real costs of both removing and retaining institutions; and the conditions under which both agree reform is justified. Second, a disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, state the conservative's view and the reformer's view in wording each side would accept for itself, and mark whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal. Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I expect at least these three, but add any others both camps would accept as genuine: (a) when a practice has no known justification, is that absence evidence that it lacks one, or only evidence of the reformer's ignorance; (b) is the mere survival of an institution across generations evidence of its usefulness; (c) can the knowledge carried by an institution be articulated and tested without destroying it. Fourth, for each crux, give at least one concrete case, natural experiment, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it — for instance, a case where a practice was abolished and the predicted harm did or did not appear, or a case where the original rationale was articulable and the reform succeeded — and record in advance what each side would predict and what observation would make each side revise.

The measure of success is that both parties, on reading the document, feel their own view is stated as strongly as they would state it, that no agreed claim is vacuous, and that the residual differences are precise enough to be investigated rather than reasserted. Finally, tell me which part of the document you are least confident both camps would actually sign, and for any place where you had to adjudicate — for instance, in judging whether a disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, or in deciding that a proposed crux would really move both sides — say what argument or consideration would change your mind about that judgment, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
