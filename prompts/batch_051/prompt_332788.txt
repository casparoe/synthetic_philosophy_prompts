Last week I listened to two teachers in my school’s philosophy club argue about Bernard Williams’s essay The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality. One teacher, Mr. Ellison, said Williams’s Makropulos case shows why heaven, conceived as endless bliss, is incoherent: in the essay, Williams describes a woman who has lived for 342 years and no longer cares about anything because every possible desire has been satisfied so many times that nothing is left to want, and he argues that a life without categorical desires—desires that are not conditional on already wanting to go on living—cannot be a life we would want. The other teacher, Ms. Rahman, replied that Williams’s argument only works against a heaven that is just more worldly time stretched out, not against the classical idea of heaven as a timeless or perfected relation to God, where boredom could not arise. Mr. Ellison answered that this just relocates the problem: if the saved person cannot be bored, cannot long for anything, and cannot fail to be satisfied, then that person lacks the kind of forward-looking concerns that make our lives ours, so the result is not us living forever but some other kind of being carrying our name. Ms. Rahman said that on many theological accounts, identity is preserved not primarily by memory or the continuance of our current concerns but by the same person being loved and sustained by God, so the not-us objection assumes a secular criterion of personal identity that the heaven-proponent need not accept. Mr. Ellison ended by saying that this makes heaven a claim about divine activity rather than about the survival of human persons, which is exactly what Williams predicted: immortality would be meaningful only if we stopped being recognizably human. I could not tell who was right at each step. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each claim from clearly mistaken to clearly right and saying which parts are underdetermined. Where you can, connect the moves to the passage in Williams’s essay and to the idea of categorical desires. Finally, say how confident you are in your overall verdict and single out the point you are least sure of.
