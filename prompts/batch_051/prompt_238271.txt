Consider the following parallel and tell me whether it is deep or superficial. In programming-language theory, two syntactic forms are said to be notational variants, or syntactic sugar for one another, when they differ on the surface but are translated by a mechanical procedure into the same core language; a quarrel about which form is really primitive is then dissolved, not settled, because both sides are, at the level of the core language, saying the same thing. The deflationist strand in metaontology seems to offer a similar diagnosis of disputes such as whether numbers exist or whether every two objects have a sum: the parties speak different linguistic frameworks, and once the rules governing their words are made explicit, the appearance of a factual disagreement is supposed to disappear. In programming, the dissolving move works because there is a canonical core language that fixes what the surface notation means. In natural-language metaphysics, no such core language is given.

I want to know whether the analogy can be made precise, and if not, exactly what is missing on the philosophical side. What would have to be true of an allegedly verbal ontological dispute for the syntactic-sugar diagnosis to transfer, what does the parallel predict that turns out false, and is the absence of a canonical core language a repairable technical gap or the point at which the analogy ceases to be illuminating? Please write your answer in British English.
