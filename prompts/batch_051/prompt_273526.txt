I sit on the curriculum committee of a cognitive science department. A colleague regularly objects to proposals by saying they "bring back the ghost in the machine." At our last meeting she used the phrase to dismiss a proposed module on executive control in attention—the idea that some central system selects, coordinates, and integrates the outputs of more peripheral processors—saying the field "exorcised that ghost decades ago." I know the phrase originates with Gilbert Ryle's attack on Cartesian dualism in The Concept of Mind (1949), and that Ryle's diagnosis was that a category mistake was doing the work. But I am no longer sure what precise claim the phrase is supposed to express when it is turned, as it now is, against computational and functionalist views that are plainly not Cartesian substance dualism. Before I reply, draft a short briefing note for the committee that lays out the distinct theses "the ghost in the machine" could be asserting—from the trivial to the radical—and, for each, states whether it is plausible, whether it is substantive or merely rhetorical, and whether it actually condemns a central-executive account of attention. In particular, separate the readings that target an immaterial substance, the readings that target a homuncular style of explanation, and the readings that target mental vocabulary itself. The committee needs a menu of readings with verdicts, not a single interpretation, and it needs to be told which reading, if any, would really justify my colleague's dismissal.
