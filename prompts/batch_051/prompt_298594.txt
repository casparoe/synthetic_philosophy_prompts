OK here's something I've been chewing on. Hegel is weird about war. On one side, he doesn't treat it as just a misfortune that happens between states; he treats it as having a kind of ethical function. It stops a community from sinking into selfish private concerns, it forces individuals to identify with something larger than their property and families, and he seems to think it's one of the ways the world-spirit works itself out. States relate to each other the way individuals do in a state of nature: there is no higher court to appeal to, international law is only as binding as states let it be, and war is one way their differences actually get settled.

He died in 1831, so I want you to reconstruct for me. Take three things that came after him: (a) the post-WWII ban on aggressive war, enforced through the UN's collective security machinery; (b) humanitarian intervention, where a state or coalition goes to war mainly to stop mass atrocities inside another state rather than to protect its own interests; and (c) nuclear deterrence, where great-power war is prevented by the threat of mutual annihilation rather than by fighting. For each one, tell me whether Hegel would have seen it as a natural extension of his view of war, a distortion of it, or something his framework simply wasnt built to handle. Then tell me which of the three you think he'd have the hardest time digesting, and why. I want the actual reasoning he'd have to go through, not just a label like "he'd support it" or "he'd reject it."
