I've been in a consciousness studies reading group for the better part of a year, and every meeting ends up back in the same ditch. The two camps are the heterophenomenologists and the first-person-methods people. As I understand it, heterophenomenology (Dennett's method) says the science of consciousness should treat a subject's verbal reports — say, that she sees a red afterimage drifting left — as data to be explained, but without taking those reports as authoritative readouts of an inner theater; the subject is more like a novelist than a measuring instrument. The other side says this throws away the one thing we actually care about: there is something it is like, and the person having the experience has a kind of priviledged access to it that no third-person description can replace. So the science should train subjects to report better (neurophenomenology, contemplative protocols, that sort of thing) instead of quarantining their reports as fictions to be interpreted.

Everyone in the room agrees on what the subjects say. Everyone agrees the reports are caused by brains. Nobody has moved an inch in a year. My working theory is that the disagreement isn't about consciousness at all but about what a scientific explanation of consciousness has to do — whether explaining the reports is the whole job or leaves the job undone. Is that right? Or is there a real empirical or formal disagreement buried in it — for example about whether trained introspection changes the reports in a way that matters — that could actually be settled? I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, grounded in what each side actually says, and an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress.
