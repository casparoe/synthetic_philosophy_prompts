Benjamin Libet's experiments have long been treated as one of the central empirical challenges to free will. In the standard design, subjects watch a rapidly rotating clock and report the moment at which they first become aware of an urge to flex their wrist, while EEG recordings reveal a readiness potential that begins roughly half a second before the movement and several hundred milliseconds before the reported moment of conscious intending. Libet took this to show that the brain has already initiated the causal chain leading to movement before the subject consciously wills it, leaving consciousness at most a late power of veto. The result has become a familiar exhibit in arguments that our experience of deciding is a retrospective confabulation—or at least that conscious intention is epiphenomenal with respect to action.

A later line of work gives a different account of the readiness potential. In a task that asks subjects to move spontaneously at unpredictable times, the brain's slow ongoing fluctuations can be modelled as a stochastic accumulator that drifts until it crosses a decision threshold, at which point the movement is triggered. Such a model predicts that, when trials are aligned to movement onset and averaged, a ramping signal will appear before the threshold crossing even though nothing before the crossing itself constitutes a decision to move. On this reading, the apparent pre-conscious ramp is an artefact of the averaging procedure and of the spontaneous-movement design, not a trace of a covert neural decision made several hundred milliseconds early. What Libet counted as the beginning of the brain's decision would then be, in effect, the accumulating noise out of which an eventual threshold crossing emerges.

What does this reinterpretation do to Libet's challenge to free will? Does it refute the original argument, or merely force it to be reformulated—for instance, by saying that threshold crossings and their unconscious antecedents are just as damaging to free will as the original readiness potential was supposed to be? Which premise of the Libet-style challenge does the accumulator model bear on, and what would a defender of the conclusion now have to show in order to restore the challenge? And is there a version of the original worry that survives intact even if the readiness potential is discarded as evidence?
