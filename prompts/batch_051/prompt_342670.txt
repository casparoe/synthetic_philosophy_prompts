i'm a philosophy of mind PhD student who keeps drifting into pain neuroscience seminars, and I keep argueing with myself about pain asymbolia. On one side, there's a view I find increasingly compelling: pain breaks into a sensory-discriminative component and an affective-motivational component, and the badness lives in the second. The attraction isn't just the asymbolia cases themselves—people who can say that yes, that's a painful sensation, but they don't mind it, don't withdraw, don't try to stop it—but that this one distinction also explains morphine analgesia (sensation left, suffering gone) and why anxiety or catastrophizing can make the same noxious stimulus worse without changing the sensation. That's a lot of unification from a simple split. On the other side, I have a stubborn intuition that pain is essentially bad: if you genuinely don't mind it, you're not in pain, you're just having a painlike sensation. So the theory seems to say asymbolics are in pain without pain being bad, and my intuition says no—they've got the sensory event minus the thing that makes it pain. I can feel myself wanting to say 'so they don't feel pain' and also wanting to say 'no, they do, pain just isn't what I thought.' Give me the strongest version of each side, then tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there's a reading of 'pain' or 'badness' on which both survive—and what that reading would cost. I don't want just a verdict.
