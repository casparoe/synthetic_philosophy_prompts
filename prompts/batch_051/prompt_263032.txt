"Describe the basic asymmetry found in standard Knobe-effect vignettes, and discuss what this asymmetry may or may not show about the relation between judgments of intentional action and moral evaluation. In particular, consider whether the asymmetry is best explained by the idea that moral evaluation influences intentionality ascriptions, or whether some other factor might be doing the explaining."

The student wrote:

"In the standard Knobe study, participants read about a corporation chairman who is deciding whether to adopt a new policy. The policy will increase profits, but it will also either harm or help the environment. In both conditions, the chairman says he cares only about profits and does not care about the environmental side effect. When the side effect is harmful, most participants say the chairman intentionally harmed the environment; when the side effect is helpful, most say he did not intentionally help it. This asymmetry is the Knobe effect. A common reading is that a person's moral assessment of a side effect shapes whether they ascribe intentional action: because harm is bad, people are disposed to see it as intentional, while because help is good, they are disposed not to see it as intentional. Knobe himself takes the effect to challenge the traditional view that intentionality judgments are made in a purely descriptive way before any moral evaluation enters. Since the two vignettes differ only in replacing 'harm' with 'help,' the only thing that could account for the asymmetry is moral valence, so the effect proves that the folk concept of intentional action is evaluative rather than descriptive. The main objection to this reading is that participants simply want to blame the chairman in the harm case and credit him in the help case, but this objection really just re-describes the result in terms of blame and praise rather than offering an alternative explanation."
