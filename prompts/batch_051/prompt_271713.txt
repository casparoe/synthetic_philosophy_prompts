I keep going back and forth on Kavka's toxin puzzle, and I want to try a variation I haven't seen spelled out. In the original, a billionaire offers you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a harmless but miserable toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money is paid before you'd have to drink, and the toxin is pure downside, so the standard line is that you can't rationally form the intention, because you know you won't drink once the money is in hand.

Now change it. The billionaire runs this offer once a year for exactly ten years, but each year's offer is only made to people who actually drank the previous year's toxin. Tonight is round one. The amounts and the toxin-illness are the same every round, and after the tenth round the billionare dies and the game ends for good; everyone knows this. I want to know whether the one-shot impossibility survives the repetition. If the tenth round is just the original puzzle, you can't intend in round ten, so that money is unattainable. Does that then make drinking in round nine worthless, and so on all the way back, so that even round one's intention is impossible? Or is there something about the iterated setup—expected future offers, the fact that drinking round one makes you eligible for round two—that gives round-one drinking enough instrumental value to make the intention possible? Work through the backward-induction argument carefully and say whether and where it breaks, if it does.
