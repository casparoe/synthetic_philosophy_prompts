I've been turning over a modification of the original position for an essay and want it stress-tested before I lean on it. Standard presentations of the veil of ignorance hide your social position, talents, and conception of the good, but they leave in place a particular theory of how you choose under uncertainty: in Harsanyi's version the parties are expected-utility maximizers with determinate utilities and priors, while in Rawls the maximin rule is treated as the rational response to the situation rather than a psychological taste. I want to draw the veil tighter. Imagine a two-level veil: behind it you also do not know what kind of decision-maker you will turn out to be once the veil lifts — an expected-utility maximizer, a maximin-er, a minimax-regret-er, or someone with no settled risk attitude at all. The principle of justice must be one that you can endorse from behind this deeper uncertainty, knowing the choice may land in any of those hands.

My conjecture is that under the two-level veil only maximin survives. Expected-utility reasoning is unavailable because you do not yet have the determinate utility function and subjective probabilities it presupposes; minimax regret, once the state space includes facts about which decision-theoretic type you reveal yourself to be, collapses toward maximin anyway. If that is right, the familiar Rawls–Harsanyi standoff is an artifact of letting the veil hide the wrong parameters: hide not only your place in the distribution but your risk preferences and decision rule, and Rawls's conclusion follows without his more contested premises. But I can already see one worry — that ignorance of your own decision procedure is either ill-posed or silently reintroduces Harsanyi at the meta-level of choosing one's type. I'd like a candid assessment: is the construct coherent, does it already exist under another name, what is the strongest version of it, and where exactly does it fail or stop being distinctive?
