I am a retired physical chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been working through formal epistemology on my own. For most of my career my conclusions were only as good as my handling of noisy evidence, so the question of how to revise beliefs when an observation does not settle any single proposition outright feels like unfinished business from my working life rather than a textbook curiosity. The tentative rule I have been testing is this: when my credences shift over a set of exclusive and exhaustive alternatives without any of them becoming certain, I should leave my old conditional probabilities given those alternatives unchanged and average them using my new credences over the alternatives. I believe this is what is called probability kinematics, or Jeffrey conditioning, though I have only seen it stated abstractly. My problem is that my own verdicts on concrete cases pull in different directions, and I cannot tell whether one clean principle delivers all of them or which of my verdicts has to go.

Consider four cases. (1) A curtain is either blue or green, and I begin 40 percent confident that it is blue. In weak light I look at it and afterward I am 70 percent confident it is blue, not certain. I am 90 percent confident that if it is blue it will match the adjacent wall, and 10 percent confident it will match if green. The rule gives (0.70)(0.90) + (0.30)(0.10) = 0.66 for matching, rather than the 0.90 I would have had on learning blue outright; that seems right to me. (2) A fair die is about to be rolled and a friend who is truthful 90 percent of the time peeks and says it will be six. When she is wrong, her wrong report is equally likely to be any of the other five faces. I do not become certain of six; I go to 0.90 on six, and the rule then gives 0.94 for the outcome being even, because the residual 0.10 of not-six keeps its old internal ratios and two and four were two-fifths of not-six. That surprises me, but I accept it. (3) Same friend and same truthfulness, except that when she errs she always reports the face immediately below the true face, with one below one wrapping around to six. She says six. A direct calculation gives the same 0.90 posterior on six — the only ways she can say six are truthfully about six or erroneously from one — but my credence for even now comes out 0.90, not 0.94, because the residual 0.10 sits entirely on one. If I apply the rule with the coarse partition six versus not-six I get the wrong answer, while with the fine partition of the six individual faces I get the right one. I do not yet see the general criterion that tells me how fine the partition must be.

(4) A diagnostic test has 95 percent sensitivity and 90 percent specificity, and my prior for the disease is 10 percent. If I hold the lab report in my hand and learn positive outright, Bayes gives me about 0.51 for the disease. If instead a nurse gives me the result over a crackly phone line and I become only 80 percent sure she said positive and 20 percent sure she said negative, applying the rule to the positive/negative partition gives me about 0.41 for the disease. But after the die case I no longer trust myself to know whether positive versus negative is the right partition, or whether the crackly sound itself has to be modeled as its own uncertain observation. Is there one precise principle — probability kinematics or something near it — that yields all four verdicts? What does it say about the crackly phone case, and about a further case I have not settled: a die I regard as biased, with a one-third probability on six and the remaining two-thirds spread evenly over the other five faces, where the same symmetric 90-percent-reliable friend says six? Please write your answer in American English, and separate what is settled among people who work on this material from what is genuinely contested; do not present a contested view as the consensus.
