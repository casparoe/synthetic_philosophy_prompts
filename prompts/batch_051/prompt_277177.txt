For a seminar on the philosophy of consciousness I am organizing, two participants have spent the term defending rival interpretations of the same experimental finding. In the color-phi display, a red dot is flashed briefly at one location and a green dot at a second location shortly afterward, with no dot ever appearing in the intervening space. At the appropriate timing, subjects unhesitatingly report seeing a single dot move continuously from the first location to the second and change from red to green roughly midway along the path. Nothing in the stimulus itself presents such an object or trajectory, so the brain has evidently produced a coherent stream of events that the display never supplied. One participant takes what we are calling the Stalinesque view: the account is edited before it ever reaches conscious experience, the way a show trial enters the official version into the record before the court is convened. The other takes what we are calling the Orwellian view: the subject is briefly conscious of something closer to the raw sequence of flashes, and this conscious episode is then rewritten after the fact, before or during the act of reporting, the way a ministry of truth alters the archives after the events have occurred. The two accounts are introspectively indistinguishable, and each side suspects the other of smuggling in an untenable picture of where and when consciousness is completed.

They have agreed to produce an adversarial collaboration. I would like you to draft the document they could both sign. It should contain, first, a numbered section of claims about the stimulus, the subject's report, and the explanatory task that both parties accept; second, a disagreements table giving, in neutral wording, the exact point where they diverge, followed by a formulation of each position in language its own proponent would accept, and an explicit note on whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal. It should conclude with the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, and for each crux a concrete experimental or conceptual test, case, or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. They suspect one crux is whether there is a determinate fact of the matter about the content of consciousness at each instant, prior to any report or memory probe; please assess whether that is in fact a crux for them and, if it is, specify what each side would predict about it—and whether the collaboration itself reveals any shared presupposition whose rejection would dissolve part of the dispute.
