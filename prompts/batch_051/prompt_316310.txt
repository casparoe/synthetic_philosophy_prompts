Having recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and turned to philosophy, I have been trying to make sense of my own intuitive verdicts about musical sadness, and they do not obviously add up. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. A slow, stepwise descent ending on a minor chord, heard without words, title, or any knowledge of its composer, sounds sad to me as a property of the music itself, not merely as an effect on my mood.

2. A minor-key piece from a musical tradition entirely unfamiliar to me often sounds tense, alien, or ceremonial rather than sad; the minor mode alone does not seem to carry sadness.

3. Rain on a window or wind in the trees can put me in a melancholy frame of mind, yet I would not say the sound of the rain is sad in the way the music's sound is.

4. Learning that a piece was written in mourning changes how I hear it; afterward I cannot entirely unhear the sadness, even though the acoustic signal has not changed.

5. A fast, buoyant, major-key song with harrowing lyrics can strike me as ironic, manic, or chilling rather than sad; the words do not simply override the musical character.

6. A recording of a person genuinely weeping disturbs me, and something like the emotion seems to pass into me, but I do not experience it as an expression of sadness residing in the sound the way I do with music.

Is there one account of how sounds can be sad that delivers these six verdicts, or close to it, perhaps an account built on resemblance to the contours of sad speech, or on learned convention? If delivering the whole pattern requires revising a verdict, which one should I revise, and why? Finally, what does the resulting account say about a case I have deliberately not settled: a piece that traces the pitch and timing of weeping but at a quick tempo and in strict meter?
