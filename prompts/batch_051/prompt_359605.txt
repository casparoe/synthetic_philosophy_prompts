I've become convinced that the revealed-preference approach to utility is the only one that gives "utility" genuine scientific content: define a preference ordering as a summary of choice behavior satisfying the usual consistency conditions, let utility be whatever numerical scale represents that ordering, and refrain from smuggling in introspective magnitudes no one can measure. At the same time, I can't shake the conviction that there is a fact of the matter about the difference between what I chose and what I really wanted — the smoker who says she wants to quit, the agreement signed under duress, the choice made on a false belief, the person who sincerely changes her mind. Walk me through what each option would cost. Is the intuition a pre-theoretic residue I should debunk and give up? Is the theory too austere, and should it be amended or abandoned so that utility can run ahead of choice? Or is there a reading — for example, a division of labor between decision utility and a distinct notion of welfare or experienced utility — on which both survive rather than talking past each other? I don't want a verdict from on high; I want to know what would justify each of those moves, and where informed people who know the topic still genuinely disagree. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, don't present a contested view as the consensus, and write in American English.
