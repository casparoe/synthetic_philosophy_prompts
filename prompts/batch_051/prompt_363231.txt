I am a high school student in an animal behavior elective. My teacher gave us the following rule for interpreting animal behavior, which she attributed to the comparative psychologist C. Lloyd Morgan: 'In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development.' She then showed a research video that I have been turning over since. In it, a common octopus that had never encountered a screw-top jar watches a keeper hold a closed jar containing a crab against the glass, unscrew the lid once in plain view, screw it back on, and lower the jar into the tank. Over the next twenty minutes, the octopus pulls the jar into its den, grips the lid with several arms while anchoring the jar against its body, and rotates the lid in the same direction the keeper used until it comes off; it then eats the crab.

I want to know what Morgan's canon actually commits an interpreter to in a case like this. Read strictly, the canon seems to say that if any lower-level interpretation can be constructed—for instance, that the octopus's exploratory arm movements happened to unscrew the lid through trial and error, with the demonstration merely drawing its attention to the jar—then we must adopt that interpretation rather than saying the octopus represented the goal, or learned the lid's causal role from a single observation. But the word 'fairly' may matter: a lower-level account that must assume a long run of unobserved lucky movements may not fairly interpret what the video shows. What does each approach yield here: the words, the canon's purpose as a guard against armchair anthropomorphism, and what Morgan himself would probably have said, given his own example of a dog that appeared to open a gate by insight until Morgan recorded the slow trial-and-error learning behind it? Does the canon's talk of a 'scale' of psychological evolution even apply cleanly to an octopus, whose nervous system does not sit anywhere on the ladder of vertebrate minds Morgan was imagining? Which approach should govern here, and does the canon, read that way, still have a point?
