After a career in the natural sciences, where I learned both to respect expected-value reasoning and to distrust models whose conclusions outrun anything that could ever be observed, I have recently come to accept a position that still sounds to me, when stated plainly, faintly unhinged. I accept expected-value fanaticism: for every positive probability p, however close to zero, and every finite value v, there is some finite value w such that a p chance of w has higher expected value than a certainty of v, and rational choice should in principle follow that expectation. The algebra is trivial, so I accept the practical upshot as well: a one-in-a-billion chance of preventing an enormous but finite amount of suffering can, other things equal, outweigh the certainty of preventing one person's suffering, and I regard that as a sound implication rather than a reductio.

What I want is a sober map of what would count against this position, sorted by how decisive each consideration would be. My instinct is to look for defeaters in three places. First, formal results: could fanaticism, made fully precise, yield a preference ordering that is intransitive, exploitable across a sequence of choices, or indistinguishable from a rival such as bounded utility in every case we could actually decide? Second, the standard decision-theoretic objections: are St. Petersburg games, Pascal's mugging, the suspicion that the view is an artifact of an unbounded value scale together with an Archimedean axiom, and the fact that most people operate with de facto probability thresholds merely awkward, or are some of them fatal? Third, practical commitments: what would the view have to recommend about allocating one's time, about cause selection, or about policy under deep uncertainty that, if I found I could not accept it, would count as evidence against the view rather than evidence of my own weakness of will? Please also say which of these a committed fanatic should already concede counts against the view at least somewhat, and give an honest verdict on whether the position has been, or can be, qualified so far — by bounded utility, non-Archimedean values, finite value scales, or otherwise — that it no longer risks anything; if it has, tell me whether that insulation is a defect in a normative theory of this kind.

Finally, please say how confident you are in the assessment itself — not in the truth of fanaticism, but in your map of its exposure — and single out the point on which you are least sure.
