I've been working with a four-part definition of incorruptibility and I cant tell if it's any good: (a) the person won't trade a decision for private gain; (b) their standards don't drift when the incentives around them change; (c) the trait is stable across contexts rather than a one-off; and (d) it has actually been tested, meaning they've felt the pull of a real temptation and held anyway. Now here's the case bugging me. A friend's uncle sat on a municipal zoning board for twenty years and never took a bribe, never did a favor for a developer, never even accepted a bottle of wine. But the closer you look the less it looks like self-control: money genuinely bored him, he had no dependents and no expensive tastes, and he didn't much care what people thought of him. The offers didn't land as temptations; they just seemed like noise. The colleague at the next desk spent years white-knuckling it through real desire for the money, and she never bent either. On my criteria, does the uncle count as incorruptible, or does (d) rule him out? Go through the four criteria honestly. And is (d) right in the first place — should the term be reserved for people who feel the pull and refuse, or does the person with no price count too? Im also starting to doubt whether incorruptibility is always good: should we admire the uncle's trait the way we admire the colleague's, or is my definition missing a difference between them that matters?
