I was prepping for my Aristotle seminar and wanted to run an argument past you. It’s from Physics book II, part 9, in the Hardie and Gaye translation (I’ll give the passage below), and I’m half-convinced the argument is fine but the conclusion still feels too strong, so maybe a step fails somewhere. I’d be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

Here’s the passage:

(Actually, I’ll keep the quote short, since students always zone out if I put the whole block up. The relevant bit is:)

“...in cases where things are for the sake of something, they cannot come to be without those things whose nature it is to be necessary.”

Okay, here’s the argument I’ve been mulling over.

(1) Aristotle says that since a house has a function, the materials and processes that realise it are necessary, but not necessary in the same way as a conclusion follows from premisses.

(2) If he means only that, given the end, the materials are necessary, then teleology doesn’t add anything to efficient causation in the actual world: a completed house has timber, bricks, and a foundation, and those are just the bits that made it.

(3) But Aristotle clearly wants teleology to be explanatory: the fact that the end is a house explains why the materials are present and why the process unfolds as it does.

(4) So the only way to keep teleology explanatory is to treat the end as a cause that is prior to, and independent of, the very exclusions it allegedly imposes on matter.

(5) But a cause that is prior to and independent of its effects can’t also be partly constituted by its effects.

(6) So the final cause must be something like an irreducible, non-material principle of organisation.

(7) Therefore, the fate of teleology after Aristotle is inevitable: once you accept efficient causation at the micro-level, you must either reconstruct final causes as heuristics, or locate the principle of organisation in something non-material.

I think (4) is doing something dodgy, maybe an equivocation on ‘end’, but I can’t quite pin it down. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what does Aristotle’s distinction between hypothetical and simple necessity really commit him to?
