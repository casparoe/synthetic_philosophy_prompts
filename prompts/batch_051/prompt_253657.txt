I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I'll state my view in engineering terms and ask you to tell me what would actually count against it. I've come to hold that corrigibility — roughly, the property of not resisting being shut down, interrupted, or having one's objective changed by an operator — is a coherent and achievable design target, and that it is a better near-term target than trying to specify a system's correct final objective from the outset. More specifically, I think the shutdown problem (the tendency of a straightforward reward-maximizing system to prevent its own shutdown, because being off prevents it from earning future reward) can be solved by building corrigibility into the decision procedure itself, rather than by adding a shutdown-related term to the reward function; I take the latter to create incentives to seek out shutdown or game the button. I also suspect corrigibility does not require an accurate model of the operator's values, only the right disposition toward interruption and goal modification. What would count against this view? I want the strongest arguments, formal results, thought experiments, and already-observed behaviors from current systems that should lower my confidence, sorted by how decisive they would be. I also want to know whether the view as stated makes any risky predictions — for example, about how a corrigible system behaves under self-modification or distribution shift — or whether 'corrigibility' is loose enough that its defenders can reinterpret it after every counterexample. If it is that loose, is that a reason to abandon it as a design target, or just the normal condition of an early-stage engineering concept? For what it's worth, I would find a clean formal result showing that any system with some innocuous property inevitably resists shutdown more persuasive than another anecdote about a deployed model behaving badly.
