I keep noticing a pattern and I have never found a word for it. A person sets out to become more temperate—less governed by appetite—and at first it goes well. They moderate their drinking, their eating, or their time online, and they speak of feeling freer and less driven. Then the moderation project itself starts to acquire the texture of the appetite it was meant to reduce. The tracking becomes more exacting than the original desire ever was: the person negotiates social invitations around the routine, feels a spike of anxiety when a rule is broken, and treats minor lapses as failures of a deep kind. To an outside observer the behavior looks disciplined; to me it looks like someone who used to be governed by a craving and is now governed by a craving to be well governed. It has the same vigilant, preoccupied quality as the old appetite, except that the object is no longer food or drink or distraction but self-control itself.

It is not binge-and-restrict cycling, because there is no rebound into indulgence. It is not, as far as I can tell, simply an addiction, because the object is a practice rather than a substance or an ordinary reward-producing activity. And it can be socially invisible, because the person is often healthier, more productive, and more reliable than before.

A second instance: a student takes up a time-management and exercise system, and within a year the system has become the one part of his life he will not compromise; he describes a missed workout in the tone of a moral transgression. His friends see commitment. I see the old appetite wearing the clothes of the virtue that was supposed to cure it.
