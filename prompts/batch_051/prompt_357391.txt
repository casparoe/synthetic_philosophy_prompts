At a decision theory seminar last term, I overheard an argument I have not been able to settle. The question was whether you can coherently assign probabilities to your own choices while you are still deliberating about them. Mara opened with the claim that you cannot, or at least not in the way you assign probabilities to ordinary events. If, while deciding whether to accept a job, you say you are 70 per cent likely to accept, then either that figure is really a report of constraints you have not fully articulated — money, distance, family pressure — or it is itself a move towards deciding, half an intention dressed up as a forecast. There is no settled fact about what you will choose for the probability to track, because you are the person who has not yet chosen it.

Owen replied that this is contradicted by everyday experience: people predict their own choices all the time and are often well calibrated. He said he could announce, before reaching the restaurant, that he would probably order the vegetarian dish, and that this is not a half-intention; it is simply his best estimate. The estimate exists alongside the deliberation and is updated until it goes to 0 or 1. The mistake, he thought, is to suppose that a probability must track an already settled fact. Probabilities routinely describe uncertainty about facts that are not settled at all, such as tomorrow's weather.

Mara answered that the weather is not the right analogy, because the weather is not trying to decide about itself. A forecast about my own choice feeds into the very process it predicts; once I hear my own forecast, I am responsible for it, since I could make it true, or make it false, simply to vindicate it or to defy it. With an ordinary forecast, the forecast does not enter the process it describes; with self-prediction, it does. So the only genuine cases, she argued, are those in which the prediction concerns what is outside my control, or in which I have replaced choosing with the deliberate adoption of a mixed strategy. Owen said this was exactly the shape of the disagreement between evidential and causal decision theory. The self-referential worry — that one's act becomes evidence about one's own dispositions, so that one chooses acts partly in order to manage what the choice reveals — is the smoking-lesion problem. Causal decision theory blocks that move by refusing to treat the act as evidence about a hidden trait, and instead evaluates the causal consequences of acts. By analogy, he argued, one can maintain a probability distribution over one's possible acts during deliberation, because the probability is only a summary of one's current deliberative position and is updated as that position changes.

Mara countered that the causal decision theorist has not shown that the act can appear as a variable in a probability distribution in the required way. Causal decision theory assumes a set of acts and compares the expected utility of each; that is conditional reasoning about what would happen if one performed a given act. An unconditional probability that one will in fact perform that act — which is what Owen wants to assign — is a different thing, and the self-reference has not been removed. She added that Owen's own comparison failed: a programmer can assign a probability to whether the program she has written contains a bug, because the code is already fixed and the uncertainty concerns what the code in fact does. A forthcoming choice is not fixed by anything prior to the choice itself; if it were, it would not be a choice. Owen replied that this proves too much, since we also assign probabilities to things that are fixed not by anything prior but by the process that produces them, such as the result of a computation that has not yet run; and in any case the demand that an act be fixed by something prior assumes a kind of determinism about the self stronger than anything decision theory needs. At that point the seminar ended, and I left genuinely unsure who was right about what.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication of this exchange: at each stage, say who is right about which specific point, whether the point is fully right, partly right, or mistaken, and how it stands in relation to the earlier moves. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and whether there is a coherent position that combines their insights. Please write your answer in American English.
