I'm putting together a grad seminar on reduction and emergence, and I keep running into the same wall: each theory we read was engineered around a different stock example, and everyone means something slightly different by "emergent," so the arguments collapse into people restating their favorite definition. Before I read another theory, I want to write the spec first. What does a theory of emergence have to get right to count as adequate? Not a survey and not a definition — a requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The theory is supposed to do real scientific work: it should tell us when a higher-level phenomenon is emergent, whether being entailed by the microphysics means it's "nothing over and above" the parts, and whether we'd ever need new laws or new causal powers above the micro level.

Build the list around at least these, and say for each why it's a genuine requirement or else argue that it's not. The cases: temperature and pressure of a gas; phase transitions and critical phenomena, including universality (magnets and liquid-vapor systems having the same critical exponents, where the micro details seem to drop out); the glider in Conway's Game of Life; protein folding or some other case where the macro configuration seems to constrain its own parts; and consciousness as the standard hard case. The distinctions I don't want collapsed: weak versus strong emergence, epistemological versus ontological emergence, multiple realizability versus reducibility, and the difference between "emergent" as a surprising-to-us prediction problem and "emergent" as a failure of derivability in principle. It also has to deal with the causal exclusion worry — if the micro-level causes are already sufficient, what work is left for a higher-level cause to do? A theory shouldn't get to just ignore that; it should either dissolve it or meet it.

Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or just the preference of one camp. The part I actually want is the tension map: tell me which subsets of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied. The obvious candidate is wanting both genuinely novel downward causation and full microphysical causal closure, but I suspect there are less obvious clashes too. And tell me which single requirement, if you really enforced it, would rule out the most popular positions — the old British emergentists, identity-theory/reductionist views, functionalism, and the weak-emergence-is-enough dynamical systems camp. Be committed: I'd rather you defend one specific list and take a side on the clashes than get a balanced menu. Keep the scorecard of the named views brief; spend your effort on the criteria themselves.
