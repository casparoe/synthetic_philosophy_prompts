I have been collecting my verdicts about when a speaker is entitled to assert something flat out, rather than hedging it with “probably,” “almost certainly,” or “as far as I know,” and my reactions to particular cases have begun to pull in different directions. I want you to help me find the principle behind them, if there is one, and to tell me which of my verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases and my reactions.

1. The stopped watch. A colleague glances at a wall clock that, unbeknownst to her, stopped exactly twenty-four hours earlier. The clock reads 10:40. She turns to me and says, “It is 10:40.” By coincidence, it is exactly 10:40. My verdict: her assertion was defective. She had decent evidence and happened to say something true, but she did not know it was 10:40, and she was right only by luck. She should not have asserted it outright.

2. The lottery. A friend holds one ticket in a fair lottery with two million tickets. Before the draw, purely on the basis of the odds, he tells his family flat out, “My ticket is not going to win.” The ticket loses. My verdict: the assertion was defective. “It is practically certain to lose” would have been fine, but the flat-out version claimed more than he was in a position to claim. The fact that the odds were overwhelming does not settle it for me.

3. The weather forecast. Another friend checks a reliable weather service the evening before an outdoor event. The service puts the probability of rain at 90 percent. She says flat out, “It is going to rain tomorrow.” It rains. My verdict: I cannot bring myself to call this defective, even though I would not say she knew it would rain and there was a real chance she would have been wrong. If I have any complaint, it is only that she should also have conveyed the uncertainty, not that she made the assertion. The contrast with the lottery case disturbs me: both speakers were going well beyond anything they could be certain of. Why is overwhelming probability enough in the weather case but not in the lottery case? Perhaps because the weather evidence is genuinely diagnostic of tomorrow’s outcome while the lottery odds are not diagnostic of one’s own ticket, but I cannot state that distinction without hand-waving.

4. The mechanic and the oncologist. A mechanic runs a diagnostic that yields strong but not conclusive evidence—say a 99.7 percent diagnosis—that the alternator, not the battery, is at fault. The repair is cheap and low-stakes. He tells the customer flat out, “It is the alternator.” My verdict: this is fine. Now take an oncologist with structurally identical evidence: tests that jointly indicate a serious disease with 99.7 percent probability, with a definitive biopsy scheduled for the next day. She tells the patient flat out, “You have the disease.” My verdict: this is not fine; she should say that the tests strongly indicate the disease until the diagnosis is settled. I therefore have two judgments that differ only because the stakes differ, and I am not sure I can defend a formulation of the underlying principle that gives stakes that much power.

5. The honest mistake. A speaker consults three clocks around the building and her phone, all of which read 10:40, and asserts, “It is 10:40.” In fact a system-wide fault has shifted every one of them, and it is 10:35. My verdict: she was entitled to assert it; she did everything that could reasonably be asked. I would not call this a violation that happens to be excused. I would call it a proper assertion that merely turned out false.

6. The self-undermining assertions. A manager says at a meeting, “The client visit is at noon, but I don’t believe that the client visit is at noon.” Separately, someone else says, “It is raining, but I don’t know that it’s raining.” My verdict in both cases is the same: the utterance is defective in a particularly deep way, even if the first half is true. Notably, I have no problem with, “The client visit is at noon, but I’m only 90 percent sure.” That last sentence strikes me as a perfectly decent assertion.

The principle I keep reaching for is the knowledge norm: assert that P only if you know that P. It handles the stopped watch, the lottery, and the self-undermining assertions elegantly, but at the price of my verdict on the honest mistake, which it calls defective when I want to call it proper; and it cannot accommodate the forecaster or the mechanic unless I say those speakers literally knew, which I am reluctant to do. A justified-belief norm handles the honest mistake, the forecaster, and the mechanic, but it wrongly licenses the stopped-watch and lottery cases. A high-probability norm has the same problem in sharper form. A stakes-sensitive threshold view would split the mechanic from the oncologist, but it seems to license the lottery as well, since the lottery is low-stakes and its probability is overwhelming. Nothing I try keeps all of my verdicts.

Please give me a principle—or a small set of candidate principles—stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Apply it to the following further cases, which I have deliberately not yet considered: a sports analyst with a well-calibrated model giving a 75 percent chance that one team will win, who tells a television audience flat out that the team will win; a parent who tells a child, “There is no ghost in the house,” on the basis of very good but not conclusive evidence; and a witness in a criminal trial who testifies flat out that the defendant was in the bank, when the witness has only 80 percent confidence in his recognition memory and the stakes are extremely high for the defendant. Finally, say honestly which of my recorded verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should revise those verdicts or instead prefer a more complex principle that saves all of them.
