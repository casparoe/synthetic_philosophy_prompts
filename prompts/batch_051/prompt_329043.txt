I am a retired theoretical chemist who has come to philosophy late, and I now convene a small reading group on the limits of scientific explanation. Two of its members have spent the better part of a year arguing over what computational irreducibility implies. One takes the strong view: for a computationally irreducible system, the only way to learn the outcome is to trace the dynamics step by step, so there are principled limits on prediction and on the kind of explanation we can hope for. The other regards this as an overstatement: science rarely needs exact shortcut prediction, and explanation is normally secured by coarse-graining, stability, and statistical regularities, which computational irreducibility does not obviously undermine. Please draft the joint statement these two should produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration—one both could sign. I would like a numbered agreed-claims section, including what they both accept about the formal notion of computational irreducibility; then a disagreements table stating the residual disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own position; then the cruxes, each accompanied by a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it (turbulence, the three-body problem, an elementary cellular automaton such as Rule 30, protein folding, or climate are all candidates), with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Where the disagreement turns out to be verbal, or to rest on different senses of explanation, identify that explicitly.
