P. F. Strawson argued that our practices of holding one another responsible rest on a family of reactive attitudes—resentment, indignation, guilt, gratitude, forgiveness—rather than on a settled theoretical judgment about free will. On his account, to feel resentment toward someone who has wronged you, or gratitude toward someone who has helped you, is already to participate in a practice in which persons confront one another as fellow agents; by contrast, in the objective stance one suspends such attitudes and treats a person as something to be managed, diagnosed, or avoided. He further distinguishes the attitudes we feel on our own account, those we feel on another's behalf, and those we direct at ourselves. I want to run a counterfactual through that machinery to see which parts of the practice of responsibility actually depend on which attitudes.

Imagine a society of a few hundred thousand people in which every child, from birth, receives a drug with one precise effect: it eliminates the reactive attitudes felt on one's own account—the felt sense of being personally wronged, resentment toward the person who wronged you, gratitude toward the person who benefited you, and the distinctive softening that comes with reconciliation—while leaving everything else intact. People retain ordinary non-reactive emotions such as fear, sadness, joy, disgust, sympathy, and affection; they retain full cognitive capacities, including the ability to judge that an act was wrong, that someone violated a norm, that someone caused harm, and that someone deserves censure or thanks. They also retain the vicarious and self-directed reactive attitudes: they can be genuinely indignant when a third party is wronged, and they can feel guilt and remorse for their own misconduct. But no one in this society has ever once felt personally wronged, personally grateful, or resentful in the second-personal sense. If you hurt them, they may regret that a harm occurred and may regard you as dangerous to others; they do not feel that you wronged them, and they feel no resentment toward you. If you help them at real cost to yourself, they register the benefit and may judge you praiseworthy; they feel no gratitude.

Suppose the society keeps its inherited vocabulary and institutions—its members still say that actions are wrong, still demand apologies, still praise, blame, punish, and protest. Over the next three generations, predict what happens to four domains: apologies and forgiveness; friendship and intimate relationships; criminal law and punishment; and social movements and moral protest, which characteristically run on third parties adopting indignation on behalf of victims.

I want a chain of reasoning about mechanisms rather than a story. Which of your predictions are robust across plausible assumptions about human psychology, and which hinge on testable claims—for instance, about whether guilt is internally generated or depends on reading another person's resentment, about whether vicarious indignation can motivate as reliably as first-personal resentment, or about whether gratitude does relational work that approval plus affection cannot? Where does a Strawsonian expect collapse, and where only transformation or reinvention? Finally, what would have to be true about human beings for your most confident prediction to fail?
