Consider the standard centipede game in the following explicit form. Two players, A and B, alternate moves across ten decision nodes, with A moving at nodes 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 and B moving at nodes 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10. At node k the pot contains 2^k dollars. The player whose turn it is chooses either to stop or to pass. If she stops, she receives 70 percent of the pot and the other player receives 30 percent. If she passes, the pot doubles and control passes to the other player. If both players pass through all ten nodes, the final pot is split equally. In this game backward induction implies immediate stopping: at node 10 B prefers stopping, which yields 0.7 × 2^10, to the equal split, which yields 0.5 × 2^10; at node 9 A prefers stopping, which yields 0.7 × 2^9, to the 0.3 × 2^10, or 0.6 × 2^9, she would receive if B stopped at node 10; and the same preference recurs at every earlier node until the first move.

Now modify the setup in one way. Before play, nature independently assigns each player a type. With probability q, known to both players and set to 0.1 for concreteness, a player is a joint-benefit type whose utility is the undiscounted sum of the two monetary payments; with probability 1 − q the player is a self-interested type with the payoffs described above. Each player observes only her own type, and all moves are public. A joint-benefit type always weakly prefers to pass: before the final node, passing doubles the total available surplus whereas stopping freezes it, and at the final node stopping and passing preserve the same total.

Give the equilibrium analysis of this modified game and compare it with the baseline. At q = 0 the standard unraveling result returns. For q = 0.1, determine as precisely as you can what a self-interested player should do at each node, whether a self-interested A has reason to pass at node 1 in order to mimic a joint-benefit type, and at which node the equilibrium begins to unravel. How does the answer change as q varies and as the number of nodes is lengthened? Finally, identify which ingredient of the original paradox the variation disables: the certainty of the last round, common knowledge of the other player's motives, or the assumption that both players care only about their own payoffs, and explain whether the modified game dissolves, softens, or merely relocates the backward-induction paradox.
