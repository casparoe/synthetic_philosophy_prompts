Recently I noticed a structural resemblance between the particularism/generalism debate in moral philosophy and the eager/lazy-learning distinction in machine learning, and I am trying to determine whether it is a real isomorphism or merely a loose analogy. The mapping would go roughly as follows: a moral principle is an explicit, bounded-complexity hypothesis mapping situations to verdicts, so generalism claims moral competence can be captured by a finite set of such principles, while particularism treats judgment as case-based, closer to instance-based learners that assess a new situation by its relation to particular exemplars without committing to a compact global function. Holism of reasons—the claim that a given feature can count for or against an action depending on the surrounding context—then looks exactly like the non-additive feature interactions that make a decision boundary unrepresentable by simple rules. If the parallel is substantive, does the bias–variance tradeoff transfer to the philosophical debate: is the objection that particularism is unprincipled the same error as complaining that a lazy learner has high variance, while the objection that generalism is rigid is the same as pointing to the bias built into its principles? And what result in machine learning would the parallel predict for moral inquiry that seems false, or at least unsupported, once we look at actual moral practice?
