I work at a small environmental NGO and I'm the de facto climate person, which means I've drifted into a rule for reading model output that I'm no longer sure I can defend. When I need a projection—say regional precip or extreme heat for a particular city—I take the multi-model ensemble mean as my point estimate and treat the across-model spread as the uncertainty, with every model getting an equal vote and no weighting. I know some models have a much better track record for the variable I'm looking at, and I know a lot of them share code and biases (the geneologies are poorly documented but definitely not independent). My argument for the rule is: weighting by past skill is a good way to overfit a short observed record when the climate is non-stationary, and weighting by independence is impossible in practice because I can't reconstruct who copied what from whom. So equal-weight model democracy plus a sanity check, where I throw out anything obviously broken, feels like the least bad option. Last month this mattered: for one region the models split on the sign of precipitation change, the ensemble mean landed near zero, and I wrote "no robust change" in a briefing. A colleague said I was averaging away a real signal— the subset of models that get the relevant large-scale circulation right all point the same way, and the others are just adding noise from a known common bias. So I'm wondering: is my equal-weight rule actually defensible, or is it a shortcut I'm too busy to improve? If it's not defensible as stated, is there a version that keeps the simplicity but doesn't fool me, given that I have to make decisions on an NGO's timeline and can't turn every projection into a statistics project?
