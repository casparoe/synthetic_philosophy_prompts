Suppose a subject's neurons are replaced one at a time by artificial components designed to be fully functionally equivalent: same input–output mapping, same timing, same plasticity. After each replacement the subject is tested and reports that nothing feels different. At the end she is an entirely artificial system that passes every behavioral and cognitive test, insists she is the same person, and has continuous memories of the procedure. One camp takes this as evidence that the original consciousness was never interrupted: there is no step at which the light plausibly goes out. The other camp replies that these reports are exactly what a successor—or a non-conscious system that inherits the relevant memories and dispositions—would produce, and that the original subject may have been silently extinguished at some threshold and replaced.

Design the best test you can of the claim that consciousness is preserved across gradual replacement rather than extinguished and replaced. Be concrete about the measurements and comparison conditions (for example, all-at-once destructive uploading; replacement by components that match behavior but not causal fine structure; tasks that require a single conscious episode to be sustained across a replacement event), and say what result would favor each reading. Then say what no such test could settle even in principle. Close by saying how confident you are in what you claimed and single out the point you are least sure of.
