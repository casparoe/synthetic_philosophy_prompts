You are an experimental philosopher brought in to advise a cognitive neuroscience lab that studies perception, memory, and judgment. The lab director is convinced that human perception contains a Given: a preconceptual layer of sensory experience that is directly present to the mind and that can justify one's most basic perceptual beliefs without itself depending on any prior beliefs or categories. I think this is a myth. The underlying worry, which Sellars pressed, is that a state can justify a belief only if it has conceptual content and can stand in rational relations such as implication, inconsistency, and support to the belief it is supposed to justify; a merely preconceptual state could at best cause a reliable belief, not entitle anyone to it. The lab has two years of funding, standard psychophysics equipment, access to adult participants and preverbal infants, and enough computing power to train small artificial neural networks on raw visual or auditory input. Design the strongest feasible test of the claim that a nonconceptual sensory Given justifies perceptual belief rather than merely causing one. Before settling on the design, brainstorm at least three candidate operationalizations of the two key notions—a nonconceptual sensory state and epistemic justification—and say explicitly why you reject all but the one you choose. Then specify the chosen test in enough detail that another lab could run it: the manipulation, the groups or models, the measurements, the pattern of results that would count as evidence for the Given, and the pattern that would count against it. Finally, say what no test of this kind could settle no matter how clean the data come out, and where your operationalization of justification falls short of the full philosophical concept. Take the lab director's strongest argument seriously; do not caricature it.
