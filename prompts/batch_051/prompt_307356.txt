Here's a thing I keep going back and forth on. Augustine's argument in Confessions XI is roughly this: the past is no longer and the future is not yet, so neither of them has being; the present, meanwhile, has no duration, because any chunk of it would fall apart into what's already past and what's not yet. So time can't be a real feature of the world out there — it has to be a stretching of the mind between memory and expectation. Now give me the B-theory of time, the block universe that special relativity is often taken to suggest: every time exists tenselessly, there's no objective cosmic now, and past and future are just relations to wherever a speaker is. Does Augustine's argument survive that? Maybe it does — maybe the first move is even stronger once being-no-longer and being-not-yet aren't ways of failing to exist but just positions in a block. Or maybe the whole thing dies, becuase the argument needed the present to have a special kind of existence, and the only bit left standing is the psychological story about memory and anticipation. I want to know what happens to the argument, not which metaphysics of time is true: which premises break, which ones can be repaired, and wether the conclusion — that time is in the mind — is still licensed on the block picture.
