ok so in my econ/philosophy club we're going back and forth about this idea people call the alignment tax. The rough version is: in a competitive ai race, any money a company spends on safety/alignment that doesn't show up as benchmark performance is a cost its rival doesn't have to pay, so the company that actually tries to make the thing safe gets outcompeted. I turned it into a numbered argument and I want to know if it's actually valid, and if not, exactly which step breaks and why. My teacher says step 4 is a fallacy of composition but i think thats a cop out.

(1) In an AI race, a company survives only if its model stays competitive on the capabilities customers actually pay for.
(2) Alignment work (making the model actually do what people want, not lie, not scheme) costs time and compute and doesn't improve those benchmark scores much in the short run.
(3) So, other things equal, a company that spends heavily on alignment will get less capability per dollar than a rival that skips it.
(4) Therefore, in a competitive market, each company is forced to cut alignment/safety spending to the bare minimum just to survive.
(5) So the companies that end up winning the race will be the ones that spent the least on safety.
(6) So competition itself pushes everyone toward unaligned AI, even if every single company would rather build a safe one.

Am i wrong to think 4-6 actually follow fine and the fallacy of composition objection only works if you read 'forced' weirdly? Or is there a specific step where it quietly assumes that safety and capability are completely seperate, or that users can't tell the difference? Point at the step, give the strongest reading of it, and tell me what version of the conclusion genuinely follows from the premises.
