I am an undergraduate in a course on analytic philosophy from Frege to Quine, and I am trying to work out whether Quine's attack on analyticity still does the work it seemed to do in 1951. As I read it, Quine's argument in Two Dogmas of Empiricism has two load-bearing parts. The first is the circularity objection: every candidate account of analyticity — truth by definition, truth by interchangeable synonyms, truth by semantical rule — ends up presupposing the very notion of meaning it is meant to explain. The second is the revisability point: confirmation holism gives us no reason to treat any statement as in principle unrevisable, so no statement deserves a special analytic status. Suppose we now accept two later developments that Quine himself would have resisted: first, a broadly Chomskyan picture on which a speaker possesses an internally represented linguistic competence, and second, possible-worlds semantics, which defines intensions model-theoretically as functions from worlds to extensions and thereby offers an apparently noncircular notion of sameness of meaning and truth across all possible worlds. Does Quine's argument survive this changed landscape? I would like a committed answer, not a survey of possible readings. Tell me which specific premise of Quine's argument the new machinery puts pressure on, whether the circularity objection is dissolved or merely relocated — for instance, to the question of how internal competence fixes one intension rather than another — and whether an analytic-synthetic distinction can now be repaired into a weaker but still useful form.
