Here's an argument I can't find the flaw in, though the conclusion can't be right. (1) A bet is worth taking when it raises the expected value of your wealth. (2) If a bet is worth taking once, it must be worth taking repeatedly—the edge just compounds. (3) A coin flip that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 on heads and 0.6 on tails raises expected wealth by 5% each flip, since 0.5·1.5 + 0.5·0.6 = 1.05. (4) So someone who repeatedly stakes their whole bankroll on this flip should get rich in the long run. Yet with probability 1 their wealth goes to zero: in a long sequence of n flips, roughly half are heads, so the typical wealth is multiplied by (1.5·0.6)^(n/2) = 0.9^(n/2), which shrinks without bound. Which step fails, or is the argument valid and both facts compatible? If the problem is step (1), say exactly what "worth taking" has to mean once you distinguish the time average from the ensemble average.
