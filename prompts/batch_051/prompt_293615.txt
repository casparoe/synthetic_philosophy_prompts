Over the last few years I've absorbed a lot of the logical induction / logical uncertainty stuff from the rationalist corner of the internet, and its left me with a policy I now apply to actual arguments. When someone uses a mathematical or conceptual claim I haven't verified myself, I refuse to set my credence at exactly 0 or 1. I assign it a number that combines my prior, their track record, and a rough sense of how surprised I'd be if further thought overturned it, and I make myself state one concrete thing that would raise that number and one that would lower it before I lean on the claim in my own reasoning. Last week a colleague invoked a scaling-law result I'd never read to justify a design choice; I set my credence around 0.7 and then couldn't explain to myself why it wasn't 0.6 or 0.8.

My friend thinks this is a con. He says either I'm doing ordinary plausibility judgement and the no-0/1 rule is just decoration, or I'm pretending to run a logical inductor in my head, which nobody can do in real time. His line is that in my position the honest options are to verify, tentatively accept, or say I don't know, and that my policy mostly turns vibes into fake precision.

Is the policy defensible as a standing habit for someone who isn't a proof-checker and rarely has time to verify? Under what conditions would it beat his verify/accept/agnostic trichotomy, and when would it be worse? Is there a restated version that keeps the useful bits—staying calibrated about future revisions, avoiding false certainty—without pretending I'm a logical inductor? Please answer in British English, say how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the point you're least sure of.
