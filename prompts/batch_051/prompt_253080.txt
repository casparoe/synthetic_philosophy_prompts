I am a communications engineer with a working knowledge of information theory and coding theory. I think comfortably in terms of sources, channels, entropy, mutual information, source coding versus channel coding, error-correcting codes, and rate-distortion trade-offs, but I have only a layperson's grasp of the philosophy of biology. I keep encountering the claim that there is a substantive dispute about whether DNA is really a code and whether talk of genetic information is literal or metaphorical. I want that dispute restated in my own vocabulary, with particular attention to what gets lost in the translation.

Please first reconstruct the main positions in the debate in plain terms, without turning it into a history of who said what. Then restate each position systematically in information-theoretic language. Make sure you cover at least the following moves, or explain clearly when one of them resists translation:

1. The literal-code position: the genetic code maps nucleotide triplets to amino acids through rules implemented by tRNA and the ribosome; the mapping is underdetermined by chemistry, which makes it genuinely code-like; DNA stores information about the phenotype; mutations are transmission errors.
2. The anti-code position: calling DNA a code imports a sender, a message, and a semantic interpretation that biology does not actually supply; DNA is better described as a template, a reactant, or a set of causes rather than an encoding.
3. The intermediate position: DNA plainly carries Shannon information and replication involves something like error correction, but semantic information, including any notion of what a sequence is supposed to produce, should be kept out of the description.

For each position, give me an honest item-by-item translation. Which claims map cleanly onto source, channel, encoder, decoder, and error-correcting vocabulary? Which claims require you to stretch the vocabulary, for example by treating the whole cell as the decoder, development as the channel, or a phenotype or trait as the output message? Which claims have no information-theoretic counterpart at all?

Then provide the residue report. Is what fails to translate best explained as a defect in my vocabulary, or as evidence that the original position was importing notions that information theory rightly refuses to honor? I want to know whether, once the debate is put in my terms, it remains a real disagreement, dissolves into a shared technical picture, or turns out to be largely a quarrel about which metaphors should be permitted.
