So in my statutory interpretation seminar, the professor kept quoting "the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life" to shut down a question about whether courts should ever ignore a statute's plain text when applying it would produce a genuinely absurd result. He said it like it settled things, but I know that line from Sunday school, and there it was not a rule about legal interpretation. I can tell this is the sort of thing textualists and purposivists fight about, but I want the slogan itself unpacked. What are the different precise claims that one-liner could be making—about what a statute is, what a judge owes the enacted text, and how the words relate to the point of the rule? Lay out the readings as a menu with a verdict on each (trivial, defensible, false, or just rhetoric), and then actually commit: which reading is the strongest, and which one does the professor need to make his absurdity-doctrine move work? I'd rather get a defended position than a survey.
