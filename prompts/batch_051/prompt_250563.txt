For the past few months I've been arguing with a friend about when a lie should invalidate consent. We aren't academics, and we keep talking past each other, partly because we mean different things by consent. One of us holds what we've been calling a will-based view: valid consent is ultimately an inner act of agreeing, so deception matters only when it stops the person from actually agreeing to what really happened, or when it touches something the person themselves had made a condition of their agreement. The other holds what we've been calling a permission-based view: valid consent is something you do for another person, a permission you hand over, and a permission obtained by deceiving someone about a feature of the encounter that a reasonable person would regard as material is no permission at all, even if the person might have gone along anyway. We agree on the easy cases: force, threats, unconsciousness, severe intoxication, and clear incapacity invalidate consent, and lying can be wrong even when it doesn't. But we're stuck on the hard middle: lying about your job, your marital status, your fertility, or whether a condom was actually used — cases where the person says yes, is competent, and isn't coerced, but has been misled about something that might or might not have mattered to them.

I'd like you to draft the document we should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two positions. It should begin with a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table. For each genuine disagreement, phrase the two sides so that each side would accept the wording of its own view. Then list the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would actually move at least one of us — and for each crux, give a concrete case or line of reasoning we would both agree bears on it, and record in advance what each of us predicts it will show. I'm not asking for a verdict on who is right. I am asking you to be careful about confidence: separate what is actually settled among people who have studied this carefully from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view of consent as if it were the consensus. If part of our disagreement turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which part and why.
