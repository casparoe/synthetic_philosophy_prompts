I am a senior planning officer in a regional local authority. My team assesses roughly thirty proposals a year — estate demolition and rehousing, road and rail schemes, the closure or merger of schools and community centres, hospital relocations, regeneration plans — where the standard impact assessment says residents are "fully compensated" by alternative housing or facilities of equal or better standard within the same travel-time budget. At public consultation, however, residents repeatedly raise an objection our framework has no entry for: that something distinct is being lost, namely the community itself, and with it their sense of belonging. Some of these objections are plainly romantic or tactical, but some track a real loss that our current tools cannot see, let alone weigh. I need a way to tell the two apart case after case, without a philosophy degree on the team and within the time we actually have: most assessments must be turned around within five working days, and our output is a recommendation to an elected committee that takes the final decision.

Write me a reusable decision procedure my team can run whenever a "loss of community or belonging" objection is raised. It should be a sequence of questions in a fixed order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a clear "no" — meaning the objection does not identify a loss the decision must account for — and where it hands the question to the committee as a judgement of values rather than a matter of fact. In translating the idea that community and belonging can be genuine goods into something my staff can apply, you must decide which distinctions have to survive and which can be dropped. The procedure should at minimum separate:

(i) a community in the relevant sense — shared practices, mutual recognition, settled trust, and patterns of life tied to a particular place — from a mere residential concentration, a shared service catchment, or a network of acquaintances;
(ii) belonging as something members themselves value and live, from belonging as something attributed to them by outsiders, including planners and elected members;
(iii) losses that can be compensated in kind because the social goods can be rebuilt elsewhere, from losses that cannot be — practices, standing, recognition, and identity bound to a particular place and perhaps a particular history;
(iv) a community that is good for its members from one whose value lies mainly in its continuing to exist, or in the eyes of outsiders.

Where a distinction cannot be applied without the planner importing their own values, the procedure should say so and mark the step as a judgement call. It should also state what to do when members of the affected community disagree among themselves about what the community is and what it is worth, and when the most vocal claimants may not speak for the quieter members.

Then run the procedure in full on three proposals we are currently considering:

(1) The demolition of a 1960s social-housing estate with very high tenancy lengths, very low turnover, and strong informal mutual aid, to rehouse each household separately in better-quality flats scattered across the city.
(2) The closure of a village primary school that also functions as the only shared civic space in an otherwise dispersed rural parish, with pupils bused to a larger school in a nearby town.
(3) The redevelopment of a busy shopping street whose older daytime regulars recognise one another, exchange small everyday favours, and describe the street as their community, where the same seating and retail functions would be provided in a new building a short walk away.

At least one worked application should reach a result my team and I will not like; identify that result and make its reasoning explicit. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: what it cannot measure, what it cannot compare across cases, and which part of the decision remains irreducibly political rather than procedural.
