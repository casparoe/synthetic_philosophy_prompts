Read Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the lens of the inefficacy objection to individual contributions to collective harms. The objection, applied to abstaining from meat, flying, or voting, says that since a single person's choice makes no difference to the outcome, there is no point in acting otherwise, and perhaps no duty to do so. Le Guin gives us a limit case: walking away from Omelas neither frees the suffering child nor dims the city's happiness, so by the objection's own test the act is causally inert. I'd like a reading that asks whether the story thereby indicts efficacy-based moral reasoning, or instead exposes the self-indulgence of a refusal that changes nothing for the child. Take a position, and support it by pointing to specific elements of the story: the narrator's account of those who stay, the description of those who leave without destination, the fact that the child's condition is public knowledge and yet the city persists. Also say explicitly where the story resists the reading you favor and where it cannot be made into a clean fable either for or against the inefficacy objection. Conclude by saying what Le Guin's treatment adds to the philosophical debate over individual responsibility in overdetermined harms that an argument about marginal contribution would miss.
