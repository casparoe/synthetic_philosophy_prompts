I've been reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums for years, so I come to questions about belief with a fairly Bayesian and functionalist default: beliefs are graded, action-guiding representations that earn their keep by constraining anticipated experience, and I get suspicious when one word is made to cover several different functional roles at once. That default is exactly why the dispute in the philosophy of psychiatry over whether delusions are beliefs has always seemed to me both important and strangely frozen. The influential two-factor account starts from monothematic delusions such as Capgras, where a patient insists that a close relative has been replaced by an identical impostor, and Cotard, where a patient insists that she is dead or no longer exists. On this account, two things go wrong together: a first neuropsychological factor supplies the strange content — in Capgras, roughly, the loss of the normal affective response of familiarity when seeing the spouse, which surfaces as the thought that this person cannot really be the spouse — and a second factor, some failure in the belief-evaluation system, lets that content be accepted and retained instead of rejected. The doxasticists stress how belief-like delusions are: patients assert them sincerely, defend them, elaborate them inferentially, and sometimes act on them in costly and consistent-looking ways. The anti-doxasticists stress how unbelief-like they are: the Cotard patient who says she is dead still eats, dresses, and avoids traffic; the Capgras patient may denounce the replacement while continuing to live with and depend on the person he denounces; and the delusion often remains encapsulated, failing to propagate through the rest of practical reasoning the way an ordinary belief would.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the two sides are using one word for several different functional profiles and disagreeing about which profile deserves the label. A delusion may score high on sincere avowal, inferential elaboration, and its role in explaining some pieces of behavior, while scoring low on integration with the rest of the agent's commitments, on steering behavior across contexts, and on sensitivity to new evidence. The anti-doxasticist, on this reading, is really saying that these states do not play enough of the full belief-role in the economy of the mind, and that calling them beliefs grants them a kind of authority they have not earned; the doxasticist is really saying that they play enough of the role, and are produced and maintained enough like beliefs, that refusing the word obscures how continuous delusions are with ordinary opinionated cognition. But I would not bet heavily on this. The impasse could instead be substantive — a disagreement about how much behavioral inconsistency a belief can tolerate before it stops being a belief, or about whether encapsulation is disqualifying — or it could be a hidden empirical question about what patients would do under forced choices, real-money bets, or surprise probes of their stated commitments.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure rather than a survey of the literature and rather than a verdict on whether delusions are beliefs outright. Is this a verbal dispute, a difference in standards of evidence or in which cases count as central, two different questions being run under one heading, an ill-posed question, or something else? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a particular kind of experiment or observation, a change of question? And be self-critical at the level of the diagnosis itself: which argument or finding would most likely change your mind about what kind of disagreement this is, and where is your reasoning about it most likely to fail?
