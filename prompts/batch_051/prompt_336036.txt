In a discussion with a colleague about whether a newly published clinical-trial result made a small biotech company's shares undervalued, he dismissed the suggestion with the remark that 'the market has already priced that in.' I suspect that this one-liner is doing genuine argumentative work, but I cannot tell what precise claim it is being used to make; I take it the plausibility of the reply depends entirely on how the slogan is precisified.

Please disambiguate the remark by laying out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, as a menu rather than selecting one and defending it. I would especially like the readings distinguished along the following lines: whether 'the market' is short for the price mechanism, for the average investor, or for the marginal or best-informed trader; whether 'priced in' means that the news is reflected in the price fully, partially, or only probabilistically; whether the claim concerns how fast prices adjust, how accurately they incorporate public information, or how little can be gained by trading on public information; and whether the remark tacitly invokes something like the idea that the very willingness of others to trade at the current price is itself informative. For each candidate reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical claim, or something contested or likely false, and say what the reading would imply about my colleague's dismissal of the biotech suggestion. Please finish by identifying the reading that the dismissal actually needs in order to succeed, and whether that reading can in fact bear the weight he placed on it; if the reading people most often intend when they utter the slogan casually is different from the one the argument requires, say so as well. I am not asking for a summary of the efficient market hypothesis for its own sake; what I want is the slogan separated into the distinct theses hiding inside it, with each thesis made to answer for itself.
