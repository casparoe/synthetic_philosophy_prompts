I hold a theoretical commitment and a firm intuition that seem flatly incompatible, and I want you to adjudicate the clash rather than just pick one.

The theoretical commitment is roughly this. I am a Bayesian in the usual rationalist sense: my own future choices are events in the world, and a rational agent should be able to assign probabilities to events in the world. Other people can often predict my choices at better than chance; a prediction market on my own behavior seems no more incoherent than one on the weather; and if I refuse to attach any credence to the proposition that I will choose A over B, I am leaving a suspicious hole in my map of the territory. Forecasts of my own choices also pay rent in practice, for habit design and commitment devices, and they refuse to treat me as a special exception to the physical order.

The intuition is that, when I am actually deliberating between A and B, adopting a credence about what I will do is not a neutral report on an independent fact. Deliberation seems to be a matter of making one option true rather than discovering how likely it already is, and any probability I assign from inside the deliberation becomes one of the causes of the choice it purports to predict: a high credence that I will choose A can push me toward A or away from A depending on my psychology, so the prediction feeds back into its own object. The slogan that deliberation crowds out prediction captures this sense that I cannot occupy the forecaster's stance and the decider's stance toward the same act at the same time without one stance swallowing the other.

So the question is whether I can honestly assign probabilities to my own choices while I am still making them. Present each side at its strongest, then give me a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be debunked as an artifact of how agency feels from the inside, and dropped? Should the Bayesian principle be amended so that first-person probabilistic self-prediction is legitimate only before deliberation begins, or only from a third-person standpoint, and is forbidden from inside the act of choosing? Or is there a compatibility position that preserves both probabilism and the phenomenology of open options -- and if so, what exactly does it require me to give up? For each candidate resolution, say what would justify it, not merely that you favor it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
