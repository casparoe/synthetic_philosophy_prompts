Write a Petrarchan sonnet about the problem of verisimilitude as it arises for Popper's account of scientific progress: if our best scientific theories are, strictly speaking, false, then progress must be understood as replacing one false theory with another that is somehow closer to the truth. The octave should build the hope that this idea can be made precise. Present the natural measuring thought in terms of consequences: one false theory would outrank another if it had at least as many true consequences and no more false ones, and let the octave make that definition feel like the obvious way to rescue progress. The volta, placed exactly at the turn into the sestet, should record the discovery that the measure is empty among false theories: it never ranks one false theory above another, because a false theory that gained true consequences over its rival would also carry false consequences the rival lacks, so the two comparisons the ranking needs cannot both hold. The sestet should hold the resulting tension rather than resolve it: the intuition that some false theories are plainly better than others remains vivid, but the formal device meant to vindicate that intuition has nothing to say. The closing couplet should state, in compressed form, what has to be rebuilt: an account of scientific progress toward truth that does not abandon the intuition the failed measure was meant to serve. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of science who knows the verisimilitude debate would recognize the specific collapse and not wince, and intelligible enough that a reader who has never encountered the problem can follow the movement from hope, to collapse, to unsettled aftermath.
