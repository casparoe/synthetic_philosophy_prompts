Two mates of mine, Dan and Maya, got into this at the pub on Friday and I honestly couldn't referee it. Dan had shown us a transcript from a customer-support model he's been fine-tuning. He'd asked it whether it was raining in Manchester, and it came back with: it is raining in Manchester, but I don't believe it. There was a pause, and then this kicked off.

(1) Maya: "That's textbook Moore's paradox. The two halves aren't contradictory — both can be true — but a person who said that would be doing something self-undermining. You can't sincerely assert p while saying you don't believe p, because asserting p presents you as believing p. So the model has picked up a genuinely incoherent way of answering, not just an odd sentence."

(2) Dan: "But Moore's paradox is about belief plus assertion, and the model has neither. It doesn't believe anything, and it isn't asserting anything — it's just predicting likely tokens after the prompt. The sentence is only paradoxical in the mouth of a rational agent whose words are supposed to track its beliefs. A parrot that made those noises wouldn't be Moore-paradoxical, so neither is this."

(3) Maya: "The parrot isn't being put forward as answering a question, though. This one is. We asked it for information, and it produced an answer that any reader will take as a claim about the weather. Once a system is in the informant role, the commitments come with the role. If a road sign said 'bridge out ahead, but I don't believe the bridge is out', you wouldn't care that signs have no minds; you'd call the sign defective. It's the content plus the role, not the psychology."

(4) Dan: "I don't think the sign works — a sign has a sign-maker standing behind it. Here there's nobody answerable for the message. And calling the output Moore's paradox doesn't diagnose the actual bug, which is just that the model failed to keep two clauses mutually consistent over the sequence. In fact the machine case shows what's special about the real thing: Moore's paradox is a failure in someone whose beliefs ought to constrain their assertions. A text generator has no such ought. You're re-describing a prediction error in mental language."

(5) Maya: "That's too narrow a notion of 'ought'. Any system used to answer questions has to not be self-effacing: it can't present p as true while simultaneously removing its own standing to vouch for p. Whatever is going on inside, users can't rely on a source that does that. I'd even flip it: the paradox shows the norm attaches to the informant role first, and to believers only because believers occupy that role. If you think the model's output isn't defective, you can't explain why you'd still want to stop it answering like that."

(6) Dan: "I'd stop it because it makes the product look broken — that's a consumer preference, not logic. The reason the human case matters is that a human who says it is either lying, confused, or being ironic, and we can ask which. The model gives us none of those options, so the same words aren't the same speech act. But I'll admit the example keeps nagging at me, and we got cut off before I could say why."
