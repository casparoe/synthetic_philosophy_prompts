I have been working through the Kosslyn–Pylyshyn debate about mental imagery after seeing it cited repeatedly in the rationalist-adjacent forums I read, usually as an example of two capable researchers looking at the same data for decades and never converging. Let me set out my understanding before I ask my question. Kosslyn's side holds that imagery is carried by a depictive representational format: a spatial, picture-like medium in which distances and orientations are encoded by corresponding spatial relations in the representation itself, so that the time it takes people to scan across an imagined map or rotate an imagined figure increases roughly linearly with the distance or angle. Pylyshyn's side denies that the format is picture-like. On his view, imagery is carried by the same sort of structured symbolic representations that carry other thought, and the scanning and rotation data do not reveal an underlying depictive code; subjects may simply be exploiting their tacit knowledge of what it would be like to look at a picture or watch an object rotate, and their response times track the demands of that imagined task rather than the metric of a depictive buffer. Later, neuroimaging entered the argument: Kosslyn and collaborators took retinotopic activity in early visual cortex during imagery to support the depictive account, while Pylyshyn's side replied that finding activity somewhere in the visual system does not establish the format of the representation doing the work, since it could be downstream of, or incidental to, the relevant computation.

From my reading, this debate seems stalled in a way that is poorly described either as one side ignoring the data or as a merely verbal dispute. My working hypothesis is that the impasse comes from differing success conditions for a theory of imagery. Specifically, I suspect Kosslyn is mainly answering an implementational or algorithmic question, namely how imagery is carried out in the brain, whereas Pylyshyn is mainly demanding a computational account of what is represented and why, and rejects the picture view because he thinks it does not answer that question but merely postpones it to an inner observer who reads the picture. If that is right, the debate is less a factual standoff than an argument across levels of explanation, and the real unresolved question is whether a theory of representational format at Kosslyn's level counts as an explanation of imagery at all.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict for either side. Concretely: (a) Which kind of disagreement is this, at root: verbal, about standards of evidence, about whether introspective reports are admissible data, about which behavioural or neural results count as evidence, about hidden empirical predictions, or about the question being asked? (b) Is there a conceptual distinction or empirical result that could have settled or dissolved the dispute, or is one side operating with a standard no theory could meet? (c) Given that diagnosis, what would actual progress look like now: a sharper question, an experiment, a formal account of representational format, or an admission that the original framing was ill-posed? I would rather not have a literature summary; I want the structure of the impasse. Please write your answer in British English.
