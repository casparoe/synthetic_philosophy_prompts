In my reporting on constitutional design, I keep running into a tension I cannot resolve, over who may govern when the rules run out. The position I find theoretically compelling is strict legality: a legitimate state acts only through law, preferably law laid down in advance. Its virtues are the virtues of a system — ex ante generality, publicity, predictability, accountability; if officials may step outside the rules whenever they judge it necessary, the exception becomes a standing invitation to arbitrary power, and the rule of law names nothing. But there are cases where the rules run out or point the wrong way, and I cannot shake the conviction that the right act may be one no existing law authorizes — the house pulled down to stop a spreading fire, the emergency no statute anticipated. Locke seems to grant the intuition outright while trying to keep it inside a constitution. In Chapter XIV, "Of Prerogative," of John Locke's Second Treatise of Government, he writes:

This power to act according to discretion, for the public good, without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it, is that which is called prerogative: for since in some governments the lawmaking power is not always in being, and is usually too numerous, and so too slow, for the dispatch requisite to execution; and because also it is impossible to foresee, and so by laws to provide for, all accidents and necessities that may concern the public, or to make such laws as will do no harm, if they are executed with an inflexible rigour, on all occasions, and upon all persons that may come in their way; therefore there is a latitude left to the executive power, to do many things of choice which the laws do not prescribe.

I want to know whether my intuition should yield to the systematic virtues of strict legality, whether the theory should be amended or abandoned, or whether Locke's prerogative gives a reading on which both survive. Do not just pick a side: tell me what would have to be true for each move to be the right one, and how I would tell whether the compatibility reading is a real resolution rather than a face-saving way of saying that the theory sometimes loses.
