I have been reading Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, and a colleague keeps glossing its lesson in a single analogy: mathematical development works like empirical science, with conjectures as hypotheses, proofs as experiments, and counterexamples as disconfirming observations. From this he concludes that mathematics is quasi-empirical—fallible, never final, and advanced by refutation rather than by demonstration.

I would like you to audit that analogy before I accept it. First, give me an explicit correspondence scheme: what plays the role of the hypothesis, the experimental design, the observation, the background assumptions, the recalcitrant data, and the revised theory. Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion. In particular, examine whether a counterexample really behaves like an observation, whether a proof is tested the way an experimental prediction is, whether mathematical objects supply the same kind of independent constraint that a physical world supplies, and whether Lakatos's own moves of monster-barring (defining a counterexample away so the conjecture stands) and lemma-incorporation (strengthening a hidden lemma so the counterexample becomes a confirming instance rather than a refutation) are better read as familiar theory-protection known from science or as something specific to mathematical concept-formation. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that mathematics is quasi-empirical—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing substantially more work than the evidence permits; if some other analogy would capture the view better, say what it is. End by saying how confident you are in that verdict and which point in your analysis you are least sure of.
