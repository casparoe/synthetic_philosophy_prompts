I am trying to locate the source of a disagreement about whether the numbers count in rescue-style cases, and I want a number rather than a restatement of familiar positions. Consider the following case of the kind made prominent by John Taurek.

I am the only person at a remote outpost with one vial of a scarce drug. Six strangers arrive at the same time: one of them needs the entire vial to survive; each of the other five needs exactly one-fifth of the vial to survive. All six are strangers to me, none has any prior claim on the drug, each is certain to survive if given the required share and not otherwise, and no more of the drug can be obtained. I take the live options to be exactly these: give the whole vial to the one; divide it among the five; or decide between those two allocations by a fair procedure such as a coin toss. I cannot save all six.

Please give me your actual credence in the following proposition: in this case, dividing the drug among the five is what I am morally required to do, so that deciding by coin toss between saving the one and saving the five would be morally wrong. Then decompose that number into the components that do the main work. Include at least these, each with a rough probability and one sentence of justification:

(a) the claim that the claims or harms of distinct persons cannot be summed across lives the way they can within a single life, so five smaller claims do not automatically outweigh one larger claim;

(b) the claim that each of the five persons' claims is as strong as the one person's claim despite the different amounts at stake, or is instead weaker and by roughly how much;

(c) the claim that equal respect for persons requires a procedure that gives the one person's claim some real chance of winning rather than letting the many simply outvote him;

(d) the claim that even where the numbers make it permissible to favor the five, they generate only a permission, not a duty;

(e) any further component your estimate depends on.

Show how these components combine to yield your overall credence. Then identify the component to which the final number is most sensitive: if that single component moved from 0.3 to 0.7 while the others stayed fixed, how much would the overall credence change? Say which argument or case would be most likely to move that component for you.

My own credence is roughly 0.35; a colleague's is roughly 0.7. If your number lies between ours, say which of the components above is the most likely source of the gap between my colleague and me. Finally, state in two or three sentences whether your credence would differ for the standard rescue variant in which each of the six needs the whole vial — one person lies on one rock, five on another, and I can reach only one rock — and why or why not.
