I came to political philosophy late, after a career in experimental physics, and I am now reading John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* with the same care I used to give a laboratory protocol. A passage in Chapter IV seems to me to carry more weight than commentators usually allow, and I want to know whether it can bear the weight of a case Mill never saw. The passage is:

"The acts of an individual may be hurtful to others, or wanting in due consideration for their welfare, without going the length of violating any of their constituted rights. The offender may then be justly punished by opinion, though not by law."

Now take this case. An automated account run from another jurisdiction assembles, from public records, a searchable database of every researcher at a small institute: names, home addresses, party registration, salaries, and previous addresses. The account adds no threats and no incitement; it merely presents the aggregate and makes it easy to search. Several of those listed are followed and harassed; one leaves the field. No single item was illegal to publish. The operator says he has done nothing but make already-public information easier to find.

The question is not whether the operator may be censured socially—Mill's passage plainly says he may be. The question is whether the passage also tells us when the law may step in. Does automated aggregation of lawful disclosures remain the kind of hurtful-but-not-rights-violating act that Mill assigns to opinion rather than law, or does it become something else? If it becomes something else, what is the principle for drawing that line?

Please answer in the following sections, in this order: (1) **The principle restated** (roughly 150 words): set out what the passage claims about the boundary between law and opinion. (2) **Application to the database case** (roughly 250 words): apply the passage to the case and say what it yields. (3) **Where the passage strains** (roughly 200 words): identify which features of the case Mill's framework does not comfortably accommodate. (4) **Verdict** (roughly 200 words): state whether the passage yields a determinate answer and, if not, what minimal modification of the harm principle would restore determinacy.
