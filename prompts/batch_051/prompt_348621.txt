Over the past year I have been going back and forth on the gauge realism debate in philosophy of physics, and I have begun to suspect that the dispute about whether gauge potentials are real or are mere surplus structure has stalled for reasons that have less to do with physics than with how the two sides use the same texts. I keep returning to a specific paragraph in P. A. M. Dirac’s 1931 paper Quantised Singularities in the Electromagnetic Field, where he introduces the idea that the phase of an electron’s wave function need not be a single-valued function of position; Dirac says, in effect, that the phase at a point has no physical meaning, while the change in phase taken around a closed loop does. That passage now strikes me as a Rorschach test. One camp reads it as evidence that only gauge-invariant quantities such as loop phases are real, and that the potential is a redundant piece of formalism. The other camp reads it as evidence that the connection must be physically present in some sense, because the loop phase is not determined by local intensities alone and requires the potential to explain how the wave function’s phase changes along a path. The two sides have access to the same text, the same equations, and the same Aharonov-Bohm interferometry results, and yet they have not moved in decades. My question is not which side is right, but what kind of disagreement this is. Is it a substantive disagreement about ontology that could be settled by a better account of what a gauge theory says, a verbal disagreement about the word real, a difference about whether explaining a gauge-invariant effect with a gauge-variant object is legitimate, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a theorem, a distinction, a criterion, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected?
