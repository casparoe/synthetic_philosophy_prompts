I'm giving a lunch seminar next month at a field station where the audience is mostly conservation biologists and restoration ecologists, plus a couple of environmental philosophers and one or two animal welfare scientists who work on wild animals. My talk defends the claim that we should stop treating habitat destruction as automatically bad, because from the point of view of the individual animals living there, a lot of habitat loss probably reduces net suffering. My argument in a nutshell: lots of wild animals have net-negative lives full of hunger, parasites, predation, and early death; the lush, high-productivity habitats conservationists fight hardest to protect are exactly the ones that sustain enormous densities of short-lived, high-fecundity species whose offspring mostly die shortly after being born; so draining a marsh or fragmenting a forest can lower the number of suffering lives even while it lowers biodiversity. I'm not saying we should bulldoze the Amazon, just that my colleagues reflex that habitat loss is automatically bad isn't actually welfare accounting. Write me a Q&A prep sheet. For each one: what I should concede, what I should hold the line on, and a two-minute reply, plus a note on where that reply is softest.
