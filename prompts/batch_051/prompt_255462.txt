Suppose someone defends the following argument. A mind, at any instant, occupies one of a finite range of discriminable total conscious states, and the transition from one state to the next is determined by the current state together with some finite range of possible inputs. It follows that over an infinite or sufficiently long life some total state must occur twice; and because the same state plus the same input would return the same sequel, the entire stream of experience would eventually cycle. The person concludes that for a finite mind experience is in principle exhaustible: after enough time, nothing genuinely new could be experienced, and an immortal finite creature would eventually live a loop.

Now suppose we reject the analogy between a mind and a finite deck of cards. Suppose instead that the mind has a finite inventory of basic elements—elementary qualities, intensities, relations, perhaps a limited palette of affect—but is compositional, so that these elements can figure in structured contents of unbounded complexity, the way a finite alphabet generates indefinitely many distinct strings. On this picture, for any proposed catalogue of possible experiences there would be another experience more complex than any in the catalogue, though still constructed from the same finite base.

Does the exhaustibility argument survive this change? Say as precisely as you can which premise the change puts pressure on, whether the conclusion can be repaired using a different argument, and whether the repair only works by secretly reintroducing a bound on the complexity of any single experience. In your answer, give the strongest version of the original argument and of the compositional reply.
