I'm a science writer working on a profile of an evolutionary theorist who has strong opinions about what level natural selection acts on, and I want to predict how she'll rule on a new case from the calls she's already made. So far she's said: a peacock's tail is maintained by individual mate choice, not by any display benefit to the flock; an alarm-calling bird isn't looking out for the flock, since the neighbors it warns are mostly relatives, so the risky call still pays off; when one clone of aphids on the same plant multiplies faster than its neighbors, the "prudent" clone doesn't win, the fast one just replaces the others before the plant collapses; and in a slime mold chimera, the cells that end up in the stalk aren't sacrificing for the good of the aggregate, they're the lineages that got stuck while others left more spores.

Next week I'm asking her about meiotic drive: a stretch of chromosome that biases its own transmission so it gets into more then half the gametes even though it lowers the fertility of its carriers. Will she say selection favors the cheating element, favors organism-level suppressors of the cheat, or both at different levels? Is she implicitly running a gene's-eye view, an organism-level view, or a multilevel view? Which of my earlier notes is the closest analog, and what further case would cleanly settle which rule she's actually running?
