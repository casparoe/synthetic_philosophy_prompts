I'm a PhD student in moral psychology and I've been using my labmate Lena as a kind of informal experimental subject. She refuses to state a theory — she says her intuitions are for hire but her definitions aren't — but she will happily give snap verdicts on the weird cases I invent. Over the past few months she has settled into the following pattern. She insists that in every case I ask about, I make it plain that no person or animal is physically harmed, nothing is coerced or deceptive, and whatever happens is done in private. Under those conditions she still says: two adult siblings who have sex once, with contraception, and both of them glad afterwards, are doing something morally wrong, though she can't give a harm-based reason. She says a family that cooks and eats its pet dog after the dog is hit by a car and killed instantly are doing something morally wrong. She says a man who uses his own national flag to scrub out his toilet, alone, and then burns it respectfully afterwards, is doing something morally wrong. But she says eating fried crickets is not morally wrong, just disgusting; drinking your own urine in private out of curiosity is not morally wrong, just grotesque; and watching a harmless fetish video involving urine in private is not morally wrong, just not to her taste. She's secular, by the way, so this isn't coming from religious doctrine. She's obviously not running the simple rule that whatever triggers disgust is automatically wrong in her eyes.

Here is the new case I want your help with. A woman grows a patty-sized piece of human muscle tissue from her own stem cells in a lab, no sentience or nervous system involved, cooks it, and eats this 'self-steak' by herself, out of pure curiosity. It affects no one else and no one ever finds out. What would Lena say if she's trying to stay consistant with her earlier judgements? I care less about the bare yes or no than about the reasoning: what are the two or three most plausible rules that fit all the verdicts I've reported, what does each rule predict about the self-steak, which rule is your best guess for what Lena is actually doing and why, and what further case should I give her to settle the matter between any rules that remain? For each candidate rule, say what it would predict about that further case.

You are an expert in moral psychology and normative ethics. Please think it through carefully before giving me your bottom line — start by sketching the candidate rules and only then move to a prediction. Write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would in an email or letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
