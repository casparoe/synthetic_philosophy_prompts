I'm teaching Ibsen's A Doll's House and I want to run it through the respect/recognition debate — the idea that what we owe another person isn't only decent action but an attitude: taking them seriously as an equal, seeing them, letting their reasons count. Use the distinction between recognition respect (what you owe someone just because they're a person) and appraisal respect (what you earn by excelling at something), or Honneth's three registers of recognition (love, rights, social esteem), whichever does more work; arguing with them is fine too.

Here's the puzzle I actually want you to work through, not summarize. In a plain sense Torvald treats Nora well in action: he provides, he dosn't throw her out when the loan comes to light, and he even says he forgives her. So what is Nora leaving for? Is she leaving because he withholds an attitude — he never saw her as a full person, only as a doll (or his little lark and spendthrift) — and the forgiveness scene is the moment she finally sees that? Is her complaint with him, or with a society that makes her husband the only real source of recognition? Or is it something about self-respect that can't be handed to you by a forgiving husband at all?

Think about the loan and the forgery too: she did it out of love and has been paying it back in secret, expecting to be admired for it. Read her exit as either a demand for recognition, a taking of it, or a refusal to let his appraisal of her fix who she is — and defend which reading fits best. Then do the harder critical thing: where does the play resist the recognition story? Does the ending suggest recognition isn't a gift the powerful can bestow once they have the right attitudes, but something you have to achieve or take? And what does Ibsen dramatize here that the philosophical distinction alone tends to miss? I don't need quotations; work from your memory of the play and paraphrase. Don't summarize the plot for me.
