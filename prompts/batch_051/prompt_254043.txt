I am trying to work out whether, and when, I can coherently assign probabilities to my own future choices. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases. I would like a principle that captures them, or else a precise account of which verdicts have to go.

The cases.

First, the habit case. For months I have set my alarm for 6:30 and risen at the first ring on about four mornings out of five, pressing snooze on the fifth. This evening, asked whether I will rise at the first ring tomorrow, I answer 80 percent without strain, and it feels like a forecast of a natural event, not like a decision.

Second, the balanced choice. Tomorrow I must accept one of two fellowships, in Munich or in Vienna. I have deliberated for weeks and find the considerations exactly balanced. This evening, asked for the probability that I will choose Vienna, I find I cannot give one. If I forced myself to say 60 percent, it would not feel like a prediction; it would feel like choosing Vienna while pretending only to anticipate my choice.

Third, the self-effacing prediction. I am deciding whether to volunteer for a demanding project at work. I notice that as soon as I entertain the thought that I probably will not volunteer, the thought operates on me as though I had decided not to volunteer. It settles the question rather than forecasting how I will settle it.

Fourth, the settled intention. I have already decided to go to the gym tomorrow and have packed my bag; when I am in that state I have gone about 90 percent of the time. A friend offers me a bet on whether I will go, and I accept it at odds of 9 to 1 against. Giving the number 90 percent feels no different from giving a probability for rain.

Fifth, the outside observer. I also believe that a sufficiently well-informed observer could predict my choices with high confidence, and I do not think my choice in the balanced case is uncaused or indeterminate. The facts that would settle such a prediction are, I assume, already fixed; my ignorance of what I will choose is just ignorance of something already determined.

The second and fifth verdicts sit awkwardly together, and the first and third seem to point in opposite directions about whether predicting my choice is sometimes a way of making it. My provisional principle is that I may assign a probability to my own choice only from a stance that treats the choice as a piece of natural history — habit, constraint, or prior commitment — and never while I am actively deliberating about it. But that formula is too rough. In the habit case I am still in some sense deliberating, since I could resolve this evening not to press snooze, and the formula does not explain why the outside observer's stance is unavailable to me in the balanced case.

What I would like is a precise criterion, or a small set of candidate criteria, that captures these verdicts as closely as possible. For each candidate, state what it predicts about a further case I am still undecided about: I am at a restaurant where I have ordered the same dish for years, but tonight I have been seriously considering something new; asked before I open the menu to give the probability that I will order the usual dish, is there a fact of the matter about what number, if any, I should give? And tell me honestly which of my verdicts the candidate cannot accommodate, and in particular whether I must give up the balanced-choice verdict, the outside-observer verdict, or both.

Finally, do not just present a principle and stop. Tell me what would change your mind about it: which of my verdicts, if I came to hold it differently, would most force a revision of the criterion, and where in your own reasoning the failure is most likely to sit.
