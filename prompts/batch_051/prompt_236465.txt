Consider the omissive Moorean sentence: *It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining.* The sentence can be true — someone may fail to believe a truth — yet most competent speakers find it absurd to assert. Let the *normative family* of explanations be this: the absurdity is ultimately explained by a constitutive norm on assertion, such as the norm that one should assert only what one knows, or only what one believes. On this view, in asserting the first conjunct a speaker presents herself as satisfying the norm, while the second conjunct is an explicit avowal that she does not; the infelicity is a deep normative defect rather than a merely conversational impropriety. Contrast this with *semantic family* views, on which the sentence is somehow defective in its truth-conditional semantics, and *expressive family* views, on which asserting the first conjunct expresses belief that it is raining and the second conjunct directly contradicts that expressed belief, where the expression relation is treated as a non-normative descriptive fact.

What is your credence that the normative family gives the correct deepest explanation of Moorean infelicity — that is, that the correct account essentially appeals to a violated constitutive norm of assertion, rather than to the semantics of the sentence or to a non-normative expression relation? Give one overall number, then decompose the reasoning that produces it. In your decomposition, address at least the following, assigning a probability and a one-line justification to each:

1. The omissive sentence is not a semantic contradiction: it is truth-evaluable and can be true.
2. Assertion is governed by a constitutive norm of the relevant kind, as opposed to a merely defeasible heuristic or a descriptive regularity of speech.
3. A purely expressive or pragmatic account is sufficient to explain the full range of Moorean data without covertly relying on a norm of assertion.
4. The account, whatever it is, correctly handles the familiar asymmetries: third-person and past-tense analogues (*It was raining, but I did not believe it was raining; It is raining, but she does not believe it is raining*) are not absurd, and the absurdity largely disappears when the sentence is embedded where no assertion is made — for example, under *Suppose that...*.
5. The same mechanism that explains the omissive form also explains the commissive form, *It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining*, without ad hoc amendment.

Add any further terms your overall number trades on. Then show how the terms combine to yield your overall credence.
