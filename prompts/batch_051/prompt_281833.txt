I work on the game-theoretic foundations of multi-agent systems, and I have been turning over a proposal that tries to institutionalize the idea of program equilibrium. I want a disciplined mechanism analysis rather than a policy assessment.

The proposal runs as follows. Each signatory country to an international climate-and-trade registry deposits a single computer program with a neutral secretariat. At the start of a registry cycle, every deposited program is handed the full source code of every other deposited program and must output a number between 0 and 1; that number is automatically applied as the country's uniform tariff rate on goods from all other signatories for the cycle. All programs are published before any are executed, none can be withdrawn or resubmitted until the cycle ends, and the resulting tariffs are enforced mechanically. Assume the underlying static tariff game has the structure of a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: a country's dominant move is to charge the maximum tariff, while universal low tariffs are Pareto-superior to universal high tariffs.

The stated rationale is program equilibrium. In the standard formulation, players choose programs rather than actions, and because one program can condition its output on the text of another program, a program that outputs the cooperative action if and only if the rival program is syntactically identical to itself can make mutual cooperation an equilibrium of the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, even though cooperation is strictly dominated in the underlying game.

Treat the registry as a mechanism whose behavior is to be predicted. Begin by specifying the induced game and exhibiting a cooperative equilibrium: a program such that, if every signatory deposits it, low tariffs are self-stabilizing even though no individual country can enforce that outcome through its tariff choices alone. Then work through the failure modes in detail. What happens if a country appends an inert comment, renames a variable, or pads its code with unreachable instructions? Would requiring countries to provide proofs of semantic equivalence solve the matching problem, or would it merely relocate the game to the proof checker? How might the mechanism be exploited by sub-coalitions that share a program among themselves while excluding outsiders, by programs that defect only on branches the secretariat is unlikely to exercise, or by countries that deliberately keep the criterion of sameness ambiguous? What changes if a national legislature can repudiate the tariff decision after the cycle, and what enforcement powers would the registry need to prevent that? Distinguish the failure modes that can be repaired without destroying the property that made the mechanism attractive in the first place from those that cannot. Finally, assess whether the registry genuinely expands the set of stable international agreements beyond what repeated-game reciprocity already permits, and say what the exercise reveals about program equilibrium once it is made to run inside real institutions.
