I want you to take the role of a critic who knows both philosophy of information and literary theory, and to think carefully before you settle on a reading — maybe brainstorm a couple of different framings first so the final one isn't generic. Read Borges's short story 'The Library of Babel' through the lens of antimemes: ideas or pieces of information that resist being remembered or spread. As I'm using the term, an antimeme isn't just a censored idea; it's an idea whose content makes it hard to transmit, locate, or retain. The story describes a universe that is one enormous (maybe infinite) library containing every possible book of a fixed length, in apparently random order. Somewhere in it there must be a true catalog, a vindication of each book, maybe even the book that explains the whole library — but they're buried among millions of near-identical volumes, false catalogs, and pure gibberish. I'd like a reading that treats the library itself as antimemetic: not a shortage of information, but so much information that the signal cant spread because it is statistically indistinguishable from noise. Where does that illuminate something real, like why a false one-liner beats a true but complicated correction on social platforms, or why certain ideas never seem to stick? And where does the story resist the antimeme reading? The library's arrangement is itself very memorable, and Borges's little story has spread very well indeed — so what does that fact do to the idea? Be faithful in both directions; don't reduce the story to an illustration of a slogan, and don't stretch the concept until it fits everything. End by saying what the story shows about antimemes that a formal definition misses.
