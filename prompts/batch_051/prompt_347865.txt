Next month I am giving a philosophy of language colloquium defending the thesis that the name "Sherlock Holmes" is genuinely empty: it does not refer, and the apparent truth of a sentence like "Sherlock Holmes is a detective" should be explained as truth-within-the-fiction, not as truth simpliciter, and not by granting the name a referent such as a nonexistent object or an abstract object. My compressed argument is that reference is a relation and a relation needs a relatum; since there is no such person in the world, the name refers to nothing, and the felt truth of such sentences is really their being true relative to the Holmes stories. The audience includes a neo-Meinongian who treats empty names as referring to nonexistent objects precisely so that ordinary Holmes sentences come out true; a philosopher of art working in the make-believe/pretense tradition who will insist that what I call truth in fiction is not a species of truth but a move within a game of imagination; and a Millian about names who agrees the name is empty but wants a single semantics for all proper names and doubts my account can handle negative existentials ("Holmes does not exist") and modal claims ("Holmes might have existed"). Give me a question-period prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will be raised and roughly how damaging each is, phrased as those three would phrase them, each with a short reply, what I should concede, what I should hold, and where each reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks.
