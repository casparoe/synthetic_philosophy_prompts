I am a retired natural scientist who has lately begun reading political philosophy, and in discussions of Singapore and China I keep meeting the claim that a state should be judged the way one judges the management of a firm: by results. If a country is prosperous, safe, and orderly after decades under the same party, the argument continues, that government has earned its legitimacy, and objections to the absence of competitive elections are like shareholders complaining about how the board was selected while the share price sits at an all-time high. The analogy is being made to do real work here: it is used to license the conclusion that prosperity and order can justify, or at least substitute for, authoritarian rule. I would like a committed audit of it. In particular: (1) lay out the correspondences — who or what plays the role of the shareholders, the board, the management, the share price, and the market; (2) identify which disanalogies matter for that conclusion and which are merely superficial; (3) say which features of a firm the analogy quietly imports into the state; and (4) tell me whether the conclusion survives once these breaks are on the table. If it does not survive, say what a better analogy would be, if there is one. Take a definite position and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to decide.
