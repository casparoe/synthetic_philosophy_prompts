I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether one can rationally acquire aesthetic judgments from other people. My friend kept returning to Kant’s *Critique of Judgment*, especially the sections on the peculiarities of the judgment of taste. He summarized the passage roughly as follows: a judgment of taste must be rooted in the subject’s own feeling of disinterested pleasure in the object, and if I lack that pleasure, no one else’s approval can compel me to find the thing beautiful. Kant also says there is no objective rule or outside authority that could settle what is beautiful; if taste were answerable to external commands, it would become heteronomous. My friend then constructed an argument from this:

(1) A judgment that something is beautiful is a genuine judgment of taste only if it is grounded in the speaker’s own felt disinterested pleasure.
(2) Testimony from others, however reliable, cannot supply a first-person feeling; it can only supply secondhand claims about what others feel or judge.
(3) Therefore, testimony cannot ground a judgment of taste.
(4) If testimony cannot ground a judgment of taste, then anyone who forms a belief that a work is beautiful solely on the basis of testimony has not made a genuine judgment of taste.
(5) If that is so, aesthetic testimony should have no rational effect on anyone’s aesthetic judgments, and practices that rely on it—trusting reviews before seeing an artwork, assigning canonical works in a course, deferring to expert critics—are rationally suspect.

My friend thought the argument was decisive, but when I pushed him on the final step he became defensive. I am not sure whether the argument is valid, whether it equivocates on what rational effect means, or whether one of the earlier steps conflates the genesis of a judgment with its justification. Please tell me whether the argument goes through and, if not, exactly which step fails and why; if it does go through, explain what the most plausible reading of the final step is.
