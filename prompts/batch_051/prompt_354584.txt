Longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums here. I’ve come to accept a version of the Lucas–Penrose argument: for any consistent formal system rich enough to express arithmetic, its Gödel sentence is true but unprovable inside that system, yet a human can step outside the system, see that it is true, and thereby outstrip what the system proves; so no Turing machine running such a system could match human mathematical insight, and the human mind is not just a program of that kind. Comment sections keep dismissing this with one-line objections, but I almost never see an honest map of what would actually have to be true for it to fail. Act as a philosophical logician who has followed the Lucas–Penrose debate in depth. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate defeaters before you rank them. I want to know: which distinct claims the argument is really making, from the narrow claim that no machine can simulate the full mathematical competence of a human to the stronger claim that the mind is not mechanical at all; which premises could fail, including the assumption that the human mind is a single consistent system, the idealization involved in seeing the Gödel sentence’s truth, and the assumption that the machine cannot modify or extend its own system; for each escape route, what the world would have to look like and whether it is live or merely logically possible; what the position predicts that a mechanist rival would not; whether defenders have insulated it into unfalsifiability and whether that insulation is a problem; and which objections a committed Lucas–Penrose defender would themselves concede count against the argument. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me an honest bottom line about how much actually survives.
