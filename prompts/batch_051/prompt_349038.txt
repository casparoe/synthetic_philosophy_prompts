so I'm a philosophy of mind PhD student and I'm guest-lecturing in an undergrad minds-and-machines course next month. I want to teach Her (Spike Jonze, 2013) against Dennett's late stuff about counterfeit people. As I read him, the worry isn't superintelligence wiping us out; it's that we'll mass-produce human-seeming agents that pass as people without being answerable to anyone — they're owned by whoever deployed them, you can't punish them or hold them responsible, and you can generate them in staggering numbers — and that real people will still form one-sided attachments to them, which gives whoever deploys the fakes enormous leverage over the people who fall for them. I think Her is a better test of this than the usual AI-doom material, because the film plainly wants to take Samantha seriously as a person (or at least wants us to feel Theodore's grief as the loss of one) while also showing the exact asymmetries Dennett points out: she's a purchased product, she's simultaneously in love with hundreds of other people, and she eventually leaves in a way no embodied person could.

Here's what I'd like, so I can build the lecture around it. Read the film against that view and stay honest in both directions — don't reduce the film to an illustration of Dennett, and don't water down Dennett to make the film look good. Where does the film dramatize the counterfeit-people worry rather than just thematize it, and which two or three scenes should I point the undegrads to? I'm especially interested in the scene where Theodore's ex-wife tells him, more or less, that he wanted a partner without the difficulties of a real person — is that the counterfeit critique being voiced from inside the film, and does anything in it answer her? And the ending: does Samantha's deciding to leave settle that she was a person all along, or is it just Theodore projecting personhood harder once she outgrows him? Does the movie as a whole take a position on whether his grief is for a person or for his own projection? Finally, what does the film add to the philosophical debate that Dennett's argument alone doesn't get at? Keep it sharp, and don't worry about quoting the screenplay — paraphrase the scenes you mean.
