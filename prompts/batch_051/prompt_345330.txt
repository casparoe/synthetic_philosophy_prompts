Last night I had a long argument with a friend about scapegoating. He defended the practice in organizations: when an institution is in crisis, removing a designated wrongdoer—even one only marginally responsible—can discharge its accumulated guilt, because fixing blame on one person lets the community symbolically separate itself from the misconduct and then move on. I replied that this is exactly the logic of the old ritual in which a community transferred its sins onto a goat and drove the animal into the wilderness to carry them away, and that if we regard that ritual as a superstition about guilt as a transferable substance, we should say the same of the managerial version. Yet the two cases are treated very differently: the goat ritual strikes us as obviously irrational, while the institutional scapegoat is accepted as normal, even wise, practice. Are they relevantly different, or is modern scapegoating the same magical thinking in secular dress? If the modern practice is defensible, defend it against the case where the person removed is chosen not for greater culpability but precisely because the group needs someone on whom blame can be fixed.
