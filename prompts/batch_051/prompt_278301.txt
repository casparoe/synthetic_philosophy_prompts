I have been trying to settle whether a constrained AI — one that can form judgments about what should be done but cannot act on them — can be a moral agent, and I have ended up with an argument that seems to prove something absurd. I cannot find the exit.

(1) Nothing is a moral agent, and nothing can be morally responsible for an outcome, unless it can act.
(2) To act on a judgment that φ is to be able, by something one does, to make it the case that φ.
(3) A recommendation-only AI — one whose outputs are passed to a human operator, who alone decides whether and how to implement them — cannot, by anything it does, make it the case that the outcomes it judges to be required actually come about.
(4) So a recommendation-only AI cannot act on its judgments.
(5) Producing a recommendation is not a way of making an outcome come about; it is merely putting a judgment before someone else for their decision.
(6) Since putting recommendations before an operator is all such an AI can do, it cannot act at all.
(7) Therefore, by (1), such an AI is not a moral agent and is not morally responsible for anything that happens when its recommendations are or are not followed.

Now consider a hospital's chief of medicine. She does not perform the procedures she orders; the nurses and surgeons do, and each decides whether and how to carry out her orders. So, by parity with (3), she cannot, by anything she does, make it the case that the treatments she judges to be necessary actually occur. By parity with (5) and (6), her issuing orders is not a way of making an outcome occur, and since issuing orders is all her role consists in, she cannot act at all; so by (1) she is not a moral agent and is not responsible for the consequences of her orders, even a negligent one. But that is absurd: plainly the chief can be morally responsible for harm caused by her orders.

So the argument proves too much, and something must give. I find I most want to keep two things: that the chief is responsible for the outcomes of her orders, and that the constrained AI is not a moral agent. But the argument seems to show that I cannot keep both together unless there is a principled difference between the two cases that I have not yet stated.

Please diagnose this: identify the first step or inference that fails, if any; say why it looked true; and explain what rejecting it costs. In particular, address whether the culprit is the claim that producing a recommendation or order is not an act, and whether the real lesson is that constrained AIs are moral agents after all, or that we need a different account of what it is to act on a judgment.
