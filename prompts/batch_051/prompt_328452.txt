A friend has offered the following argument for a reading group on Isaiah Berlin's Two Concepts of Liberty. I am convinced the conclusion cannot be right, but I cannot find the exit. I want a committed diagnosis: name the step you reject or the inference you block, explain why it looked plausible, and say what the defender of Berlin's distinction has to pay to do so.

The argument is anchored in Berlin's discussion of the retreat to the inner citadel. The gist of the passage is this: if I cannot master the external world, I can achieve a kind of freedom by withdrawing into an inner domain, purging the desires that the world can thwart, and identifying my rational will with what is invulnerable. Berlin treats this as one of the characteristic moves within positive liberty, while also noting that it can look like a sour-grapes strategy.

Now the argument.

(1) Negative liberty is freedom from external interference: an agent is negatively free to the extent that others do not obstruct her doing what she actually wants.

(2) Actual wants are partly formed by social conditions, and they adapt to oppression: a slave may come to want only what her master already permits.

(3) So, on the negative conception, a slave with a comfortably non-interfering master can be maximally free, provided her desires have shrunk to fit the available options.

(4) Berlin's inner-citadel passage makes this worse. If a slave can also be free by purging the desires that the master can frustrate, then negative liberty, and the positive liberty of self-mastery that licences the purge, both count abject domination as perfect freedom.

(5) But the whole point of distinguishing positive from negative liberty was to explain what is wrong with such cases: the slave fails to be her own master, even if no one is interfering and even if she has adjusted her desires.

(6) The only way to avoid counting the adapted slave as free is to distinguish between the empirical self's actual desires and some rational or true self's real interests, and to judge the slave's freedom by the latter.

(7) But once a theorist or ruler is allowed to say which of my desires are truly mine, the distinction between freeing me and forcing me to be free collapses. Berlin himself documents how positive liberty has been used to justify coercion by those who claim to know my real will better than I do.

(8) Therefore either we must accept that a slave can be perfectly free, or we must accept that rulers may force us to be free. Since both are unacceptable, Berlin's two concepts of liberty cannot be made to work.

Which step do you reject? If you reject a premise, explain what rejecting it costs for the defence of negative or positive liberty. If you reject an inference, name the equivocation or shift. I would most like to keep premise (1), and I suspect premise (4) is misreading the inner-citadel passage, but I want to know what I am paying either way.
