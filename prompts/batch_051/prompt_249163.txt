I'm a science journalist and I keep running into people—researchers, PR staff, my editor—who explain some costly, over-the-top behavior as a costly signal or honest signal: the peacock's tail, the engagement ring, the company taking a public stance that loses customers, the family buying a solar array they'd keep hidden if it were only about the energy. I want a reusable procedure for deciding when that framing deserves space in a story and when it's a just-so story dressed up in evo-psych language. It has to be something I can run in about twenty minutes before filing, without a biology degree, and that I can defend to an assigning editor. Give me the tests in the order they should be run—for instance, is the cost real, is it hard to fake, is there some audience that would actually change how it treats the signaler—and mark clearly which steps are judgement calls rather than science. Then apply the procedure to two or three cases, including at least one where the checklist tells me to drop the costly-signaling angle even though it would make a better headline. At the end, say what the procedure can't do, so I don't overclaim it in copy.
