I'm on a regional biosecurity committee and we've just received an application to trial a gene drive against an invasive rodent on the island where I grew up, and I have to take a position in a few weeks. The argument I keep hearing against drives is the irreversibility one: an intervention that can't be undone ought to clear a much higher bar than one that can, and a gene drive looks like the paradigm case, because once a self-propagating construct is out in a wild population you cant recall it. The applicants have a response and I'm trying to figure out how much it actually does to the argument. They point to two developments. First, you can now build drives with a built-in copy limit (the daisy-chain idea: the drive is split into a chain of components, and because one link of the chain isnt itself copied preferentially the whole thing loses steam after some number of generations instead of spreading indefinitely). Second, you can design a follow-up drive that targets and overwrites the first one, and keep it as a kind of antidote in case the original does something unexpected. So their line is that the thing just isnt irreversible anymore, and the precautionary objection collapses back into ordinary reversible risk assessment.

I'm not asking whether we should approve the application. I'm asking what happens to the argument. Does the irreversibility objection survive these two developments, or is it dead, or does it need repair? Which premise do the new designs actually put pressure on? And if it survives only in a weaker form, what exactly is the weaker form—is it still an objection about the drive, or has it become an objection about something else?
