I'm putting together a short position statement on federalism and subsidiarity for a constitutional theory seminar, and I'd like a consistency audit before I present. The seminar is comparative, so we read US federalism cases alongside EU subsidiarity material and the occasional German or Swiss example, and I've noticed my own view has gotten a little mixed. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think subsidiarity is a genuine normative principle, not just a makeweight: authority should sit at the lower level unless the lower level can't adequately achieve the objective and the higher level can do better. (2) My favorite foundation for it is epistemic—I'm drawn to the old line that subnational units are laboratories, and that policy variety is how we actually learn what works. (3) But I'm also unapologetic about strong national constitutional rights enforced against subnational units; the fact that something's local doesn't give it any extra moral license. (4) I think when one unit finds a policy that demonstrably works, the center should adopt it and make it uniform pretty fast, rather than waiting for laggards. (5) And I think the center underreacts massively to cross-border externalities; I want it to move much more aggressively there. (6) Finally, I don't think subsidiarity needs any appeal to a pre-existing demos or shared identity at each level; it should run on purely functional, comparative-competence grounds.

I cant tell which of these are the real trouble spots and which only look like trouble. So: go through the pairs, flag the genuine tensions and the merely apparent ones, rank the genuine ones by severity, and for each real tension say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be.
