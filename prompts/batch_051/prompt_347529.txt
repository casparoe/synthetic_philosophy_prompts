I am a novelist working on a book whose protagonist, a retired actuary, has come to suspect that the phrase "chosen at random" conceals more decisions than it discloses. Once a month I host a salon of about fourteen people—mostly writers, a few designers, and one or two engineers—and I would like to devote an upcoming two-hour evening to a game that makes everyone in the room feel the problem in their hands before anyone names it. The raw material is a large circle with an equilateral triangle inscribed in it, and the guiding question is how likely a randomly chosen chord is to be longer than one side of the triangle. I want the participants to produce chords by several different physical procedures, each of which feels like a wholly natural and fair way to choose a chord at random, and to make small token wagers on the outcome as they go. My hope is that, after enough bets, they will notice that different procedures lead them to treat different odds as fair—and that this discovery will unsettle them in a way I want the novel to make its readers feel.

Please design this as a runnable evening: rules, materials, a timeline for the two hours, a scoring scheme that uses points or tokens rather than money, and explicit instructions for the facilitator about how long to withhold the names Bertrand and principle of indifference. The procedures should be set up to realize at least the three classical constructions—two endpoints chosen on the circumference; a radial direction chosen along with a point along that radius, the chord drawn perpendicular to the radius; and a point chosen somewhere inside the circle, the chord drawn perpendicular to the radius through it—without participants being told that these are the classical constructions. The debrief should map what happened to the principle of indifference: the claim that saying what is equally possible requires a prior decision about what is to be treated as equally possible, and that different symmetries of the circle privilege different answers. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for instance, someone's hand-drawn procedure will not correspond cleanly to any of the intended measures—and how to handle that on the fly. Be candid, as well, about which feature of the paradox a physical game cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must therefore supply explicitly.

Finally, I would ask you to state how confident you are in the design choices and in the description of the classical constructions, and to identify the single point about which you are least sure.
