alright, so I've been trying to get my views on causation straight, and a friend keeps telling me the package doesn't cohere but won't say exactly where. here's where I've landed:

(1) at bottom, I think causes are difference-makers. in the clean cases, c caused e just in case if c hadn't happened, e wouldn't have happened.

(2) I think the best way to make the difference-making idea precise is the interventionist story: model the situation with variables, and x causes y if there's some surgical intervention on x that would change y while you hold the other variables fixed at their actual values.

(3) I count omissions and absences as genuine causes. the gardener's failure to water the plant caused it to die. me not setting my alarm caused me to miss the meeting. I don't want to say those are just loose talk or shorthand for hidden positive events.

(4) I'm also drawn to contrastive causal claims. strictly speaking, I think its not just "the strike caused the fire" but "the strike rather than the short-circuit caused the fire," and a cause always has a contrast class, even if we leave it implicit.

(5) I'm pretty convinced causation is transitive: if a caused b and b caused c, then a caused c.

(6) I think norms and defaults do real work in causal selection. wether we say the gardener's omission or the heatwave is "the cause" depends on what we're treating as normal, and I don't think there's a context-free answer to that.

(7) Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle. Suzy's rock gets there first and smashes it; Billy's would've smashed it anyways if Suzy hadn't thrown. I'm very sure Suzy's throw caused the bottle to smash. like, not a loose way of speaking—really caused it.

so: does this hang together? where are the actual tensions, ranked by how bad they are, and what would have to give in each case? and are there any pairings that only look like a problem until you think about them a bit more?
