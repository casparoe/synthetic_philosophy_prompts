I am a student in a philosophy of probability course, and we have been working through the reference class problem—the problem of choosing which class of similar cases to use when assigning a probability to a single event. I would like you to build me a case to order. Please construct a realistic scenario in which an agent must estimate the probability of a single event, such as whether a particular person will develop a condition or default on a loan, and in which there is a natural ladder of increasingly specific reference classes. Specify two adjacent classes on that ladder, one narrower and one broader, such that each is defensible by standard statistical practice, yet they yield probabilities on opposite sides of the agent's decision threshold. The conflict must not be resolvable by moving further up or down the ladder: the next narrower class should be too sparsely populated to support a frequency judgment, and the next broader class too heterogeneous for the estimate to remain informative. You may stipulate the class sizes and frequencies. After presenting the scenario, state the event, the two classes, and the probability and decision each supports. Then explain why each class has a genuine claim to be the right one, identify the structural feature that makes the dispute ineliminable, and say what would happen if that feature were removed. End by saying how a theorist who believes in objective single-case probabilities would try to defend a unique probability here, and whether the agent is instead left with a range of defensible decisions.
