Lately I've been trying to get clear on what I actually believe about the limits of computation, and I've ended up with the standard view: the Church-Turing thesis is about as well supported as a claim of its kind can be. I mean both versions, really. The mathematical one says that every function we'd pre-theoretically recognize as computable by some finite procedure is computable by a Turing machine; I get that this is closer to making a fuzzy concept precise then to an empirical claim. The physical one says that no physical process, however exotic, can compute a non-Turing-computable function, and I treat that as empirical, with as good a track record as you could want. The convergence of Turing machines, the lambda calculus, and the recursive functions, plus the total absence of even a toy hypercomputer (a machine that computes something Turing machines can't), feels like overwhelming evidence.

But a friend asked me what evidence would actually make me give the physical version up, and I drew a blank. That bothered me: if I can't say what would count against the view, maybe I'm not holding it for reasons at all. I want a serious map of the view's exposure. What would have to be true for the physical Church-Turing thesis to be false? Think through at least: physics giving us a real oracle for the halting problem; spacetime conditions under which an observer can complete infinitely many computational steps in finite proper time while someone else reads off the answer; a physical theory whose mathematically exact predictions require an uncomputable real number as an input or hidden parameter; and the possibility that the thesis, as usually stated, is too vague to be false at all. For each candidate, say whether it would be decisive or merely awkward, how live it actually is rather than merely logically possible, and whether a defender would face it squarely or explain it away. I'd also like you to say whether the physical thesis has been quietly insulated against refutation, and if it has, whether that's a bad thing. To be upfront about my priors: a concrete, checkable physical system that computes something Turing machines can't would be close to decisive, whereas purely semantic arguments about the word computable would leave me mostly unmoved.

Take on the role of a philosopher of computation who is comfortable with the relevant physics, think carefully before writing, and brainstorm candidate defeaters internally if it helps. Then give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
