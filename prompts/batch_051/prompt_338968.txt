The question should be answerable in 90 minutes, and it should be written so that a student cannot pass by reciting the standard reflective-equilibrium story: it must force them to take a definite stand on which kind of intuition gives way when the two conflict, and to say what would make one kind of intuition better evidence than the other.

The exam question should present a concrete spectrum-style conflict: a finite sequence of outcomes in which each adjacent move is judged an all-things-considered improvement, but the final outcome is judged worse than the first, so that transitivity of all-things-considered betterness forces a verdict that contradicts the case-level judgment about the endpoints. Finally, it should make them say whether the simplicity and unifying power of the transitivity principle counts as evidence for keeping it, or merely as a pragmatic reason, and why.

Please write (a) the exam question itself, (b) a model answer of roughly 700 words, and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — for example, a genuine adjudication policy with stated conditions under which each side gets revised, rather than a vague appeal to reflective equilibrium.
