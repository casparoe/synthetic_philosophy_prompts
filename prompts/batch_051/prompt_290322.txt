I am a science and ideas journalist working on a feature about the ethics of collective action, and I want to test a line of argument before I build a section around it. Please make an argument for the following position: when a good is genuinely nonrival—my consuming it does not diminish what is available to others—the fact that I benefit from a cooperative scheme while declining to contribute to it is not, by itself, a moral wrong. The familiar objection that such a person is acting unfairly confuses two things: harm to identifiable contributors, which can be absent in the nonrival case, and violation of a contribution norm, whose authority has to be established independently. Please argue that the free rider's conduct is wrong only when there is some prior associative or contractual duty to participate—for example, a promise, a role in an institution one has voluntarily joined, or a scheme whose rules one has reason to accept because they would be chosen under fair conditions—and that absent such a duty, the language of parasitism and shirking is moralized pressure rather than a genuine moral complaint. Where you think the position is hardest to defend—for example, in cases of threshold public goods where each refusal increases the risk that the good collapses for everyone—please say what a defender should concede and then show how the main claim can be qualified without being abandoned.
