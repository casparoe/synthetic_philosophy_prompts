I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. For a feature on the history of causation, I have spent the past few months reading the occasionalist tradition in Islamic philosophy, above all al-Ghazālī's version of it. I expected a curious historical position; instead I find it far more powerful than I can easily refute. On this view, there are no necessary connections among created things: fire does not burn cotton in virtue of a power in the fire; God creates the burning on the occasion of contact, following a custom (ʿāda) that is stable but never necessary. At the level of metaphysics this is a genuine economy and unification — a single source of efficacy replaces an entire system of created causal powers, and the puzzle of how one thing transmits causal force to another disappears. Yet I cannot shake the intuition that the fire burns the cotton. I mean more than that burning reliably follows contact; I mean that fire is what does the burning, and that when a chemist or an epidemiologist identifies a mechanism, they discover something in the natural order rather than merely charting the settled habits of a will. I keep being told that this intuition is only a philosophical gloss, and that occasionalism leaves scientific practice untouched, because a stable custom is enough to support induction, prediction, and intervention. That may be exactly right, or it may be too quick, and I cannot tell. Please present both sides at their strongest, and then explain how I should adjudicate the conflict: whether the right outcome is to debunk and drop the intuition, to amend or abandon the theory, or to adopt a reading on which the two are compatible. The response should say what would justify each of those moves, not merely pick one. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
