Take Carroll's tortoise. You know the shape of it: the tortoise accepts A and B, where B is if A then Z, but it won't accept Z. Achilles adds C—if A and B then Z—as a further premise. The tortoise accepts C and still won't accept Z, so Achilles adds D, and off it goes. Standard moral: rules of inference aren't extra premises, and adding them as premises never closes the gap.

Now vary it. Replace the tortoise's all-or-nothing beliefs with ordinary Bayesian credences that obey the usual probability axioms. The tortoise has P(A)=1 and P(B)=1, and also P(Z | A and B)=1, but it refuses to assign P(Z)=1. It doesn't deny Z; it says its credence in Z just isn't settled by the other facts. Achilles points out that this isn't a gap you can sit in: P(A and B) is already 1, so P(Z) is at least P(Z | A and B) times P(A and B), which is 1. No modus ponens step needed, just arithmetic. The tortoise replies that accepting the probability axioms as formal constraints is one thing, and applying them to actually move a credence is another; if Achilles adds 'you should apply the probability calculus here' as a further constraint, the same regress reappears at that level.
