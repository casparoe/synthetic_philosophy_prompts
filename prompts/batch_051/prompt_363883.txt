I have been working through Aristotle’s On the Soul III.3, and I have come to accept a fairly strong version of his claim that every occurrent act of human thinking requires phantasia. The passage I am anchoring on is the one in which Aristotle distinguishes phantasia from perception and from belief, describes it as a movement produced by actual perception, and then asserts that there is no thinking without a phantasma. I am reading that as a claim about the vehicle or format of thought: even when we reason about universals or mathematical objects, the occurrent thinking itself has to run through some particular quasi-perceptual presentation, and there is no purely amodal, purely conceptual occurrent thinking. I want to be clear that I am not treating this merely as a claim about Aristotle’s text; I am adopting it as a live hypothesis about cognition, and I want to know what would count against it.

If that hypothesis is false, what exactly would have to be true? I want a ranked map of the considerations that should move me: findings from the study of aphantasia, introspective reports of imageless thought, results about abstract or symbolic reasoning, developmental or comparative evidence about nonhuman animals, and any a priori arguments that thought without phantasia is coherent. Which of these would be decisive against the view, and which would merely push me to broaden the notion of phantasia until it becomes hard to distinguish from any kind of particular representation? In particular, I would like to know what a committed defender of the view should themselves concede counts as real evidence against it, and whether the view has a standing tendency to protect itself by redefining phantasia. If it does, I want an honest assessment of whether that is a vice in this kind of position, or whether it is the correct response to evidence that initially looks like a refutation.
