I'm a software engineer on the trust and safety team at a smallish user-generated content site (zero philosophy background, so bear with me). Our content policy has this clause, which I'll quote exactly as its written: "Users may not publish content that is false, misleading, or reasonably likely to deceive." It was obviously drafted with fake news, scam listings, manipulated screenshots, impersonation, stuff like that in mind. Yesterday a user's entire post body was:

This sentence is false.

It got escalated to me because our automated misinformation classifier genuinely didn't know what to do with it, and now I'm supposed to write the enforcement recommendation. My gut says the clause just wasn't built for self-referential sentences, and I'm not even sure "false" applies here the way it applies to a normal factual claim. I vaguely remember that philosophers don't all agree about whether that sentence is true, false, neither, or somehow both, and it seems like which one you pick changes the policy answer. But "not a normal factual claim" probably isn't an enforcement category, and the clause also says "misleading or reasonably likely to deceive," which might catch the post even if "false" doesn't.

So what does the clause actually require in this case? Walk me through what each of these gives: the plain wording, the purpose of the rule, what the drafters would have said if someone had raised this exact post when they wrote it, and what a reaonsable user reading the policy would take it to mean. I care less about what our policy team will actually decide and more about whether the clause, on any coherent reading, reaches a post like this—and what you have to assume about truth for it to do so.
