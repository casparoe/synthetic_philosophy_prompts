I've been in a practical ethics seminar and we spent most of Tuesday arguing about whether being frugal is an individual duty or just something it's good to bring about through norms and policy. A friend of mine has this argument he's weirdly proud of, and I can't find where it breaks. He lays it out like this:

(1) Suppose frugality is a genuine individual duty. (2) If it is a duty, then on any given occasion, using more than you need when you could use less is the kind of thing you're failing at; the duty tells you not to do that. (3) But if each such occasion gives you a moral reason not to use more, those reasons add up, and they don't point at any non-arbitrary stopping point. (4) So a real duty of frugality would push you toward the most frugal life possible: rice and beans every meal, not replacing your worn-out shoes until they're unwearable, putting off the dentist, that sort of thing. (5) But that kind of life is reckless and self-punishing, and a theory that tells you to live it can't be right. (6) Therefore, there's no genuine duty to be frugal; at best we should promote frugality as a social good, but no one is obligated to be frugal themselves.

I want to reject this because the same shape of argument seems like it would also show there's no duty of charity (why give some if more always helps?) or no duty to keep promises (why be on time if earlier is even better?), and those seem wrong. But I can't actually say which premise or step he's gotten wrong. Is the bad move in (2), (3), or the jump from (5) to (6)? And once you reject whichever bit is wrong, what does a deontological reading of frugality have to give up, and what does a consequentialist reading have to give up?
