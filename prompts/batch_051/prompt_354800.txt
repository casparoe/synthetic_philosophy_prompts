I am a novelist, and for some time I have been circling a question I cannot settle from inside the craft: whether invention is ever anything more than recombination, and whether a finite private stock of experience sets permanent limits on what a mind can make. A writer I respect recently pressed this passage from David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section II, "Of the Origin of Ideas," on me:

"Nothing, at first view, may seem more unbounded than the thought of man, which not only escapes all human power and authority, but is not even restrained within the limits of nature and reality. To form monsters, and join incongruous shapes and appearances, costs the imagination no more trouble than to conceive the most natural and familiar objects. And while the body is confined to one planet, along which it creeps with pain and difficulty; the thought can in an instant transport us into the most distant regions of the universe; or even beyond the universe, into the unbounded chaos, where nature is supposed to lie in total confusion. What never was seen, or heard of, may yet be conceived; nor is any thing beyond the power of thought, except what implies an absolute contradiction.

But though our thought seems to possess this unbounded liberty, we shall find, upon a nearer examination, that it is really confined within very narrow limits, and that all this creative power of the mind amounts to no more than the faculty of compounding, transposing, augmenting, or diminishing the materials afforded us by the senses and experience. When we think of a golden mountain, we only join two consistent ideas, gold, and mountain, with which we were formerly acquainted. A virtuous horse we can conceive; because, from our own feeling, we can conceive virtue; and this we may unite to the figure and shape of a horse, which is an animal familiar to us."

I am fluent in the vocabulary of craft and the workshop — scene and summary, image, point of view, voice, free indirect style, defamiliarization, the telling detail, observed specificity, rendering, invention, plot, revision, what we call "making the reader see it" — but I am not fluent in the vocabulary of eighteenth-century empiricism. I want you to restate the position in that passage in the vocabulary a working novelist actually uses, as precisely as you can, without smuggling in either framework's assumptions and calling it a translation. Then give me the residue report: which parts of Hume's claim map cleanly onto craft-talk, which parts translate only after you have to distort them, and which parts have no counterpart in a novelist's vocabulary at all. End with a verdict: whether that residue reveals a blind spot in the way novelists speak about invention, or something vague, overreaching, or quietly load-bearing in Hume's account.

My own hunch is that Hume is close to what my profession half-believes — that there are no new sensory atoms, only new compounds, and in that form "there are no truly new plots." But I suspect this flattens him, and I would rather be corrected than confirmed. Treat my hunch as one possible reading, not as the target.

One last thing: if the premise of my request is confused, or if an honest translation into craft vocabulary cannot be done without distorting the position beyond usefulness, say so plainly instead of complying politely. And in your answer, mark where you are confident and where you are offering an interpretive stretch, including what someone defending an opposite reading of Hume would say.
