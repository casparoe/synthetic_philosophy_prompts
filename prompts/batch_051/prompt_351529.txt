I work for a regional transportation planning agency, on the team that reviews road projects and speed-limit changes before they go to the board. We do about thirty of these a year, and the trade is always the same shape: a project saves or costs people a few minutes a trip, and it changes their risk of dying by some number of micromorts (a micromort being a one-in-a-million chance of death), spread over however many people travel that stretch. The analysts on my team are engineers, not philosophers, and they need something they can work through in half a day and defend at a public hearing, not a seminar.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure for judging when a change in travel time is worth a change in micromorts, and who is allowed to bear the risk. The cases that keep coming up: a proposal to raise a rural highway's speed limit that saves regular commuters a few minutes a day but adds a small annual chance of death for each of them; a road-diet project that removes a car lane to build a protected bike lane, which cuts risk for cyclists and pedestrians but adds time for drivers and pushes cut-through traffic down residential streets, so the risk lands on people who never asked for it; and a project that lowers the total expected deaths per year but makes the remaining deaths predictable and identifiable, sitting at one known intersection instead of scattered anonymous. Write me the checklist, step by step, with the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand off to the board because the question is a values call rather than a fact. Ground it in whatever decision theory or ethics of risk actually has that's usable, but I don't need the jargon, just the procedure. Then run it on the three cases, and make sure at least one of them is a case where the procedure tells the board to do something it will hate, or blocks something it was ready to approve. I also want to know what the procedure cannot settle no matter how carefully its followed. And somewhere in there, tell me where the procedure has to stop and admit the real question is whether the whole system of letting people drive private cars is worth it, because that seems like a different kind of call and I can't tell exactly where it starts.
