You are a philosopher of psychiatry advising a clinical research unit that is drafting guidance for psychiatric trainees on how to respond to delusional speech in everyday clinical encounters. The unit works mainly with monothematic delusions, with Capgras delusion (the conviction that a loved one has been replaced by an impostor) as its running example, and it accepts a two-factor working model of delusion formation: an anomalous experience supplies the delusional content, while a second factor—a bias or deficit in how the experience is appraised—explains why the content is retained. What the unit cannot settle is what the second factor outputs: a genuine belief, or some less-than-belief state, such as a vivid imagining, an unstable in-between attitude, or a state that guides affect and behavior without being responsive to evidence in the way beliefs paradigmatically are.

The unit has limited space in its training guidance and wants it built around the practical claims that would actually change what trainees do, not the claims that are merely asserted in the literature. Your job is to produce a defended ranking of the practical implications that different answers to the belief question would have for how trainees should talk with delusional patients.

Begin by brainstorming the main implications people might draw from the claim that delusions are not beliefs. Use the following six as a starting list, but add anything significant that is missing and cut anything you regard as a red herring:

1. Whether trainees should directly challenge or correct the content of the delusion.
2. Whether patients should be judged irrational for failing to revise the delusion.
3. Whether delusional speech should be treated as assertion, as testimony, or as something closer to fiction, play, or expression.
4. Whether CBT-style interventions that evaluate evidence for and against the delusion are theoretically misfounded.
5. Whether responsibility and blame for delusion-driven actions are diminished.
6. Whether trainees may permissibly play along with the delusional content to preserve alliance and trust.

Then rank the implications by how much they should shape the guidance. Take your time, and show the reasoning rather than only the final list. For each item, say whether it genuinely follows from a well-developed anti-doxastic position, how much behavior it would change in trainees if accepted, and how robust it is across different ways of spelling out the non-belief view. State the criteria behind your ordering and any trade-offs between them. If an implication only matters under a specific version of anti-doxasticism, say so, and explain whether that version is the one the unit should adopt.
