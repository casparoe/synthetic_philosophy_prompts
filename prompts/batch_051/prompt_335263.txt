I am a novelist writing a book in which a secular ethics institute inherits the papers and endowment of a wealthy Buddhist lay patron, and I am stuck on a set-piece scene: an after-dinner exchange between two visiting fellows about whether Buddhist ethics is best understood as a form of consequentialism. I would like you to draft that dialogue. It should not be the standard caricature in which one side observes that the Buddha was concerned with suffering and therefore must have been a utilitarian, and the other dismisses outcomes as irrelevant to an inner path. I want the scene to have real argumentative tension.

The two characters should be equally competent scholars of the material. One should defend a sophisticated consequentialist reading of the Mahāyāna bodhisattva ideal: on her view, the vow to liberate all sentient beings from dukkha is an impartial, agent-neutral commitment, and the precepts, the perfections, and the doctrine of skillful means are best justified as reliable instruments for minimizing suffering across the long arc of rebirth. The other should defend a character-based, virtue-ethical reading: on his view, the path is centrally about transforming the agent's motivations by eliminating greed, hatred, and delusion, and wholesome action expresses an already-cultivated character rather than serving as a tool for producing future states of affairs. Have them genuinely respond to one another on at least these pressure points: whether a bodhisattva who is unattached to outcomes can coherently be described as maximizing; whether the classical move of saying that suffering is to be prevented regardless of whose it is, because no particular person owns it, pushes one toward impartial welfarism or fits equally well inside a virtue framework; how each view handles a case in which breaking a precept would predictably prevent greater harm; and whether the consequentialist reading imports foreign machinery such as agent-neutrality and maximization that quietly distorts the tradition's own categories of wholesome and unwholesome. Write the scene so that neither party wins by authorial fiat, each makes at least one genuine concession, and the dialogue closes at the point where the disagreement is sharpest rather than resolved.

After the dialogue, add a brief Afterword in your own voice: state which of the two readings you find better supported by the considerations the characters raised, and identify the single argument or consideration that would most likely lead you to change that assessment, or the place where your reasoning feels least secure. End the entire response with a final line of the form Summary: followed by a one-sentence statement of the crux of the disagreement.
