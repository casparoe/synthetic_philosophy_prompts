I'm a junior in high school and I somehow got picked to give the student statement at our school board meeting next month. The district is deciding whether to replace out-of-school suspensions with restorative justice circles for most nonviolent stuff—fights, vandalism, chronic disrespect, that kind of thing. I'm supposed to talk for about four minutes, and I actually want to argue for the switch, not just do the "as a student I feel heard" bit.

The problem is I went down a rabbit hole on the Dennett-versus-Caruso debate over free will and punishment. Dennett is the compatibilist: he thinks we can have the kind of competence and control that makes blame and praise legitimate even if determinism is true. Caruso is the free-will skeptic: he says nobody ever basically deserves blame or punishment in the retributive payback sense, and instead of punishing he pushes a public-health quarantine model—seperate people when they're dangerous, address the causes, but don't dress it up as desert. I think restorative justice actually looks good on both frameworks: for a Dennett-type, holding someone accountable means making them face the person they hurt and repair the damage, and for a Caruso-type, it drops the payback logic that can't be justified at all. But I can't stand up there and lecture the board on compatibilism.

The room is a mix: two board members took philosophy in college and will notice if I mangle the distinction, but most of the adults just want to know whether this will let kids get away with stuff, and one board member keeps saying "you do the crime, you do the time." Could you write the speech? I want it to land as a real argument for restorative justice that quietly does the free-will work, not as a philosophy lecture, and it shouldn't strawman the "crime and time" guy.
