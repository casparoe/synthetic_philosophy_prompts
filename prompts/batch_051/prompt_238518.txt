I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and I serve as the graduate-student representative on my university's Conduct Review Board. We are hearing a case that has left the Board deadlocked, and I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before our vote on Friday. A doctoral student in biochemistry fabricated a figure in a preliminary dataset during a lab rotation. The manipulation was caught by his supervisor before it could influence any publication, and the student admitted it only after being confronted with the manipulated file. He has since apologized privately, and his supervisor calls the episode aberrational. The Board must choose between two dispositions. The first is guilt-oriented and private: a formal finding of responsibility, a one-term suspension, a permanent letter in the student's file, and completion of an academic-integrity seminar. The second is shame-oriented and restorative: a facilitated conference in which the student explains to his lab group and supervisor exactly what he altered and why, listens while they describe how they were affected, and then accepts a public reintegration plan; its advocates argue that the audience's disappointment reattaches him to the group's norms, while opponents argue that it risks branding the student and turning a wrong act into a spoiled identity.

The deeper dispute is not about this student's character but about which moral emotion our procedures should cultivate: guilt, understood as a verdict on a bad act that leaves the agent's standing intact, or shame, understood as exposure of the agent before the community. Some of us doubt whether an institution can choose its emotional culture at all rather than merely inheriting it, and I want the memo to take that doubt seriously. Please draft the written opinion I should circulate. Approach this as an expert in moral psychology and the philosophy of punishment and institutional design. Before committing to a recommendation, take the time to brainstorm and steelman the strongest version of each side: the private, guilt-oriented disposition; the public, shame-oriented conference; and the view that the choice is not really the institution's to make. Then recommend one disposition, or a clearly bounded hybrid, with an actionable bottom line and criteria future Boards could use to decide when restorative exposure is appropriate. Throughout, separate what is reasonably settled from what is genuinely contested among moral psychologists and philosophers, especially claims about the effects and moral status of shame, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. The Board does not need a literature review; it needs a document it could vote on, one that gives the strongest consideration on the losing side its due.
