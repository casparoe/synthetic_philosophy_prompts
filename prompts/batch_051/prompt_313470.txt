I keep noticing a pattern in applied-ethics discussions, and I cannot find a way to state it that neither oversells it nor dissolves it. I run a reading group for adults — mostly engineers and social workers rather than trained philosophers — and our debates repeatedly land on the same shape of argument. Take the spare-room case: a family displaced by a fire needs somewhere to stay, I have an empty room, and the cost to me is real but modest, while the harm prevented is enormous. Considered on its own, the refusal is hard to defend. But then the same argument form returns with the second car, the annual holiday, the retirement savings, the evenings spent resting rather than volunteering. In each case, set next to the harm I could prevent with the resource, my private use of it looks hard to justify. No single local verdict seems wrong when I examine it alone, yet if I follow the standard in every case where the argument goes through, what remains is not a life I recognize as mine. The puzzle is not simply that morality is demanding; it is that the severity appears only in the aggregate, after a long run of verdicts each of which looked, in isolation, close to obvious. I can locate the mistake in none of the individual steps, only in the conjunction, and so I cannot tell whether the defect belongs to the standard, to the habit of assessing obligations one case at a time, or to my unargued assumption that an acceptable moral view should leave room for a recognizable human life.

I am looking for the established term, if one exists, for this specific configuration: a way of settling what is owed that is locally compelling case-by-case but globally unlivable, where the unlivability is not visible in any single application and seems to emerge only from summing verdicts that were separately sound. I know there is a broad family of worries about moral theories asking too much, and I can see that this belongs somewhere nearby, but most statements of that worry treat the demandingness as visible all at once, as a standing property of the view. What I am noticing is different in emphasis: at each step the demand looks modest, and the severity is a feature of the aggregate, which makes the aggregation itself feel like the thing doing the damage. Does this configuration have a name of its own? If not, I would rather you say so and coin one, with a definition and boundary cases, than hand me the nearest familiar term. Either way, I want the distinctions: how exactly this differs from the more familiar charge that a moral theory simply demands too much, from ordinary conflict between morality and self-interest, and from the thought that some helping beyond what is owed is admirable but not required; and where, in your view, the real error lies — in the standard, in the one-case-at-a-time method of assessment, or in the expectation that the standard leave room for a life.
