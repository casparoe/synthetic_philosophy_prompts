In the philosophical debate about norms of assertion, the question is what standard a speaker must meet in order for an assertion to be proper. One influential position is the knowledge norm: assert that p only if you know that p. Competing positions say the standard is truth, or justified and reasonable belief, or that the standard shifts with conversational stakes. A failed assertion does not usually end a conversation, but it licenses a distinctive kind of repair: the hearer may challenge the speaker, ask for grounds, or treat her as less reliable. Now consider a programming language's assert statement. It takes a Boolean condition and, if execution reaches it and the condition evaluates to false, raises an error and aborts. An assert that passes is a true description of the program's state at that point; an assert that fails is a bug signal. These look structurally alike to me: each is a claim governed by a standard of correctness, and each has a built-in failure mode. The philosophical argument is essentially over which standard—truth, true belief, knowledge, justification—the practice answers to, while the machine standard is simply truth, checked against the actual state with no epistemic intermediary.

The difference might be the real point. A program's assert is evaluated directly against ground truth: the machine can inspect the very values it describes, so there is no gap between its state and the world, and talk of evidence or justification is superfluous. A human asserter has no such direct access, which is presumably why knowledge or justification, rather than mere truth, seems like the right standard. So I want to know whether the assert statement is a degenerate limiting case of the assertion norm—the truth norm made trivially enforceable—or a different kind of act altogether that merely shares the name. I suspect the analogy is doing real work: if an assertion norm works like an invariant that conversational participants check at runtime, then the epistemic requirement is the human substitute for the program's ability to inspect its own state. But I also worry I am pattern-matching on the word assert, and that the failure modes—a crashed program versus a challenged speaker who can retract or offer grounds—pull the two cases apart.
