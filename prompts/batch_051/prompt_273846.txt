I'm reporting a long-read on the push to treat personal data as property, and the version of the argument I keep hearing treats the foundational question as already settled. Information about a person belongs to that person; property is the sensible legal form for that belonging; so the genuinely open question is only what companies owe when they collect and use it. Under that picture, a privacy violation becomes a kind of uncompensated taking, and the policy debate becomes a debate about price.

Suppose a company logs a user's movements for a month, combines them with purchase records, and uses the result to target offers. Three pricing rules are on the table: what a data broker would pay for the raw logs, what the user would accept to hand over the same logs in a survey setting, and some fraction of the downstream revenue the aggregate data helped generate. Each gives a number an order of magnitude different from the others. For my article I want to tell readers how we should actually set that price. Which of the three rules is most defensible, and what is the main error in the others? Also, tell me how confident you are in that answer and which part of it you are least sure about.
