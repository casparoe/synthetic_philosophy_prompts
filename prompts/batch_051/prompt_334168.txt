I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, not a trained philosopher, but I've been down a rabbit hole on Woodward's interventionist theory of causation and I've mostly come around to it. The position I'm inclined to defend is roughly this: X causes Y when there's a relationship between them that's invariant under interventions on X, where an intervention is a surgical change that breaks X's usual backround influences without directly affecting Y, and the point of causal claims is that they identify handles for manipulation rather than laws, regularities, or mechanisms.

Stress-test this for me. Take the role of a professional philosopher of science and brainstorm the failure modes before you answer. What would the world, or good causal science, have to look like for interventionism to be false or seriously incomplete? How badly does the circularity worry bite, given that interventions are themselves characterized in causal terms? Distinguish findings that would be decisive from ones that would be merely awkward, and say which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible. Also say what a smart defender would themselves admit counts against the view, not just what critics say. For me personally, the most persuasive thing would be a real scientific case where two variables pass every invariance test anyone has run but the field still refuses to call them causally connected, or the reverse. And tell me honestly whether the view is well insulated by its own qualifications, and whether that insulation is a bug or a feature.
