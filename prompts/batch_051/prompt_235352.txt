Me and a friend went around about this for hours last night and I'm still turning it over, so I'm hoping someone can tell me where the thing actually breaks. His arguement was:

(1) A huge amount of philosophy checks its theories against intuitions about cases — Gettier cases for knowledge, trolley cases in ethics, Kripke-style cases about reference, that sort of thing.
(2) The empirical evidence shows these intuitions vary systematically across cultures: give the same case to people in different places and you get different, predictable answers.
(3) If intuitions vary systematically by culture, then no intuition can be treated as evidence about mind-independent philosophical facts unless we have some culture-neutral way of saying which culture's intuitions are the reliable ones.
(4) But any such method would itself be a piece of philosophy, so it would have to be checked against intuitions, which is exactly the circle we're trying to escape.
(5) So philosophical intuitions can't give us evidence about philosophical facts.
(6) He even leaned into the fact that this argument undermines itself, saying that's only a problem if you thought intuitions could ground anything in the first place.

I want to keep (2) — I do think the cross-cultural variation is real and shouldn't just be explained away — and I'm more willing to give up (4) than (1), but I don't know if that's stable. Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I have to choose, I'd like to keep (1) and (2) and find the bad move further down, but I want to know what keeping them costs.
