You are an expert poet and philosopher of mind with a particular interest in memory and memetics. Think carefully, and before composing the poem, briefly brainstorm the corruption sequence and what each corruption is meant to do.

Write a pantoum about an antimeme: an idea whose own content makes it hard to remember, rehearse, or pass on. The poem's central antimeme should be a genuinely philosophical thought worth keeping; for instance, the thought that one's most confident memories are reconstructed after the fact rather than played back from a faithful record. In the opening quatrain, state that thought clearly. Then follow the pantoum's recurrence scheme — the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next — but corrupt every returning line slightly, in the way an idea decays in transmission: a qualifier dropped, a term swapped, a negation slipping in. By the final quatrain the idea should have decayed into a reassuring, forgettable version of itself, and the poem should close by reusing the opening first and third lines in their corrupted forms. The form itself should perform the forgetting: someone who knows the antimeme concept should recognise that each corruption is exactly the kind a self-undermining idea invites, and someone who does not should still find it a coherent poem about a thought that erases itself. Use British English spelling and conventions throughout.
