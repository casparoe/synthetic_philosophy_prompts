I write backend services for a living, so the only model of the mind I really have is processes, logs, and dashboards. When people talk about self-deception, the picture I reach for is a service that writes status: healthy to its own log and never reads the queue where the failure actually sits, so the dashboard it publishes says healthy and the process 'believes' it. I know this modular idea gets used (by me, and by people in cognitive science I've read) to argue that self-deception isn't actually paradoxical: you don't need one mind that both knows and doesnt know the same thing, you just need one module that hides a fact from another module. I want someone to audit that analogy properly instead of waving at it. Give me the correspondence table: in the self-deception case, what maps onto the process, the log entry, the queue, the dashboard, the health check, and the engineer who set the whole thing up? Then tell me where it breaks. My own worry is this: in my code, a module can only write a false log entry if some earlier process knew the true value and chose to overwrite it—so the analogy seems to sneak in a little liar/homunculus somewhere, and maybe it just moves the paradox into the architecture rather than solving it. Does the conclusion people draw from the analogy survive once the disanalogies are on the table? The conclusion I mean is that self-deception is no big mystery—that a person can genuinely keep a truth from themselves without there being one single mind that knows it and doesn't know it at once. Id rather you take a definite position and defend it than give me a menu of options.
