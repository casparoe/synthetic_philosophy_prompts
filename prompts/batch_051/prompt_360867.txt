I cover science and ideas, and next month I am running a workshop for about twenty politically engaged adults on why people vote at all. Design a seventy-five-minute session that makes them feel the force of the paradox of voting: in a large election any single vote is vanishingly unlikely to decide the outcome, so a purely instrumental, self-interested voter should abstain once casting a ballot carries any real cost—and yet large elections still draw heavy turnout. I do not want a lecture, and the name of the paradox should be withheld until the debrief. Instead, give me one or more games with real (small) stakes, written rules, explicit payoffs, and a cost of voting, arranged so that participants discover for themselves the point at which voting stops being worth it—and then have to live with the outcome their own abstention helped produce. The debrief should map moments in the play onto the expected-value calculation, the probability of being pivotal, and the collective-action problem; it should anticipate what will predictably go wrong when run (expressive voting to impress the group, attachment to a side, treating the stakes as a joke) and how to handle each; and it should be explicit about what a room of twenty cannot reproduce about a mass electorate and what the facilitator must supply.
