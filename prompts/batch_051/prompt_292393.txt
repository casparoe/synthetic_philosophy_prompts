I'm in a decision theory seminar this term and also rereading Kant on the side, and I can't shake a parallel I noticed between two things that really shouldn't go together. The seminar covered evidential decision theory and then the stuff people call evidential cooperation in large worlds: the idea that if the universe is big enough and contains lots of agents whose decisions are correlated with mine (they're similar to me, or they run the same kind of reasoning, or somebody is simulating a bunch of near-copies), then my choice isn't just a cause of local effects, it's also evidence about what all those agents will do. In setups like that, an agent who treats her own choice as representative of a policy—what if everyone like me did this—can systematically do better than an agent who only computes the causal consequences of her own act. The version that got stuck in my head is the one-shot prisoner's dilemma: defecting can be a bad deal even though it doesn't cause anyone else to defect, because your defection is evidence that you-like agents generally defect, and you'd rather live in the world where agents like you cooperate.

And now the part that bothers me: that looks almost exactly like the categorical imperative, the requirement to act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law. Both say don't just ask what happens if I alone do this; ask whether the world where this is the general policy is one you can in some sense accept or will. The large-world story even seems to give a sneaky consequentialist justification for being Kantian: if the world is large and your decision is correlated with your copies or near-copies, then universalizing is just expected-value maximization over the right reference class, and the payoff comes from acausal coordination. But I can list ways they come apart too. Kant's test is about contradictions in what a will can coherently will, not about harvesting payoffs across a multiverse. The evidential-cooperation version only gets off the ground if the world actually contains enough relevantly similar agents whose choices really are correlated with mine, which is a contingent cosmological matter, whereas Kant's demand is supposed to bind a single finite rational will whether or not its copies exist. Also, the old problem of specifying the maxim, how general everyone like me is supposed to be, shows up on both sides, but maybe for different reasons. I can't tell whether this is a deep identity of structure, a loose analogy, or just pattern-matching. Please lay out the mapping as precisely as you can, say where the two structures genuinely match and where they come apart, and tell me whether anything actually transfers from one to the other.
