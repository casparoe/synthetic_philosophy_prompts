I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. Over the last few years I have watched the word "betrayal" get used in engineering organizations to cover a range of quite different actions, and I want a working definition that is precise enough to use in real disputes but still fits ordinary usage where ordinary usage is clear.

Here are the cases I want the definition to classify:

1. A developer discovers that a colleague is faking the accessibility test results used for a company certification. After asking the colleague directly to correct it and being ignored, the developer reports the colleague to HR.
2. A developer leaves the company for a direct competitor six weeks after finishing an employer-funded training program. They take no code and no documents.
3. A security engineer learns that the company is still collecting and selling users' location data after users have opted out. They escalate internally for a year with no effect, then give the evidence to a journalist, in violation of their NDA.
4. A developer is deposed in a discrimination lawsuit against their manager. Their lawyer advises them to answer truthfully, and they testify about things the manager had told them in confidence.
5. A developer, angry after being passed over for promotion, deletes a production database on their final day.
6. A developer spends part of their work week improving a direct competitor's open-source library, knowing the competitor ships that library, and argues that the improvements help the whole ecosystem.
7. A developer finds and quietly fixes a severe security bug in the company's product. They ship the fix but never disclose the flaw to users, because publicizing it would embarrass the team.
8. A developer asks a teammate to cover for them with a code-review approval for code the teammate has not actually read.

My tentative views: 1, 4, and 7 are not betrayals; 5 and 8 are; 2, 3, and 6 I keep flip-flopping on. My working suspicion is that "betrayal" is used for at least two different things—breaking a specific obligation to an identifiable person, and harming the group by siding with outsiders or hiding the truth—and I suspect a good definition would say when an act is disloyal without being a betrayal.

Propose a definition of "betrayal," run it through all eight cases, and for each case say whether it is a betrayal of a specific person or group, and of whom. Where your definition gives a verdict that contradicts ordinary usage or my tentative views, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Then say which of the eight verdicts you are least confident about, and why.
