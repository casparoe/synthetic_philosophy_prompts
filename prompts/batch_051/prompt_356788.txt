Lately I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about originals versus copies, because I make digital art but I also sell small numbered prints, and a collector asked me why one of my prints—printed at a lab from a file I sent—is an 'original' while a scan of someone else's painting wouldn't be. I don't have a good answer, so I wrote down my gut calls on a bunch of cases and I'm hoping there's a principle hiding in them somewhere.

Here they are. If a machine produced a molecule-for-molecule replica of the Van Gogh Sunflowers, and no expert could ever tell it from the one in the museum, I'd still choose the museum one if I could have either for free, and I'd feel I got the better thing; the replica just isn't as valuable to me. But I don't feel that way about digital files: if someone copies one of my files, the duplicate is every bit as much my work as the first one, and I can't even point to which file is the original. I also don't care which numbered print from an edition of ten I get—they all seem equally 'the work' to me. Yet I do care about forgeries: if a newly painted picture in the style of a famous painter hung in a gallery for a year and everyone loved it, and then it was exposed as a fake, I'd say the picture itself got worse when the news came out, not just its resale price.

The two cases that really confuse me are these. A foundry can legally cast new bronzes from Rodin's original molds after his death, and I'd call those real Rodins, or real enough that I'd display one proudly even though Rodin never touched it. But the perfect Van Gogh replica, which is also causally connected to the real painting through whatever high-tech copying process made it, and which the artist never touched, I won't call a real Van Gogh. Both are later physical objects produced without the artist's hand but ultimately from the artist's work. Why does one count as genuine and the other not? And compare the forgery case to the digital file case: in one, I think the object's history is part of what the work is; in the other, the file's history seems completely irrelevant. Am I just being inconsistent, or is there a principle that gets all of these?

So what I'd love from you: a principle (or a small set of candidate principles) stated precisely, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, picks up each case, and tells me which of my calls it can't accommodate and why. Then tell me what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a museum replaces a fragile original with a visually identical replica but leaves the label as if it's the orginal; visitors can't tell and leave just as moved. My inclination is that this is dishonest and the visitors are missing something, but I can't say exactly what, given everything I said above about copies being fine.
