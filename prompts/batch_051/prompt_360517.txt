Here is an argument that no finite person can rationally want an endless life, and I cannot tell whether it is valid or where the fault lies. (1) A person can rationally want to continue living only if they can want, in advance, the future life they would thereby have. (2) To want a future life in advance, they must be able to represent that life to themselves determinately — as containing particular activities, relationships, or achievements they care about. (3) An endless life is an infinite series of particular activities, relationships, and achievements. (4) A finite mind cannot represent an infinite series of particulars determinately, because representing the whole series would require representing each of its members. (5) Therefore, a finite mind cannot represent an endless life determinately. (6) Therefore, no finite person can rationally want to live forever. The conclusion seems to make the very idea of a desirable eternal life — in heaven or anywhere else — incoherent, which I find hard to accept. I suspect the slip is in premise (4), but I cannot say exactly what it would be to represent an endless series determinately without enumerating it. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact inference that fails; if it is valid, identify the premise a defender of eternal life should reject and state it in the corrected form they would need.
