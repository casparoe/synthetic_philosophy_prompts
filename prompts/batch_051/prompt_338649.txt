I'm trying to make sense of something from a lecture. The lecturer summed up the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite debate as follows: 'The Mu'tazila held that good and evil are objective features of acts, visible to reason on its own, so God commands the good because it is good. That's also why they were relaxed about revelation — it mostly tells you what reason already knew, or gives the untrained a shortcut. The Ash'arites held the reverse: nothing is good or bad before God's command, so the good just is whatever God commands. For them, then, God's decrees are ultimately arbitrary; fasting in Ramadan is good only because He happened to choose it, and had He chosen the opposite, that would have been just as good.' Something feels off here, especially the 'arbitrary' part and the idea that the Mu'tazila thought revelation was mostly redundant, but I don't know enough to say exactly what. Go through this summary and tell me what a careful scholar of the tradition would accept, what they'd push back on, and where it slides between a claim about how we come to know right and wrong and a claim about what makes acts right and wrong. Does the usual Euthyphro-style way of setting up the debate survive the corrections?
