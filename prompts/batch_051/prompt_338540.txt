I am a PhD student in philosophy working on expected-value reasoning under radical uncertainty. I keep coming back to the problem usually discussed under the heading of expected value fanaticism: a sufficiently tiny probability of an astronomically large payoff can swamp any finite cost, so expected-value reasoning seems to license actions almost everyone regards as insane. Pascal's mugging is the standard toy illustration; arguments from tiny probabilities of catastrophic or transformative artificial intelligence are the more respectable-looking instances.

I think the standard diagnosis of this problem misfires. The problem is usually framed as a question about how to value enormous outcomes, or about whether our utility function should be bounded. I suspect it is really a problem about which probabilities are allowed to do expected-value work at all.

I have been defending a principle I tentatively call the Reversibility Constraint. Here is the version I am currently working with:

When a small probability p is supposed to justify a large real cost now — money, freedom, attention, years of life — the probability counts as decision-grade only if it is decisionally reversible. A probability is decisionally reversible when the agent could, in principle and at a cost small relative to the action being justified, gather some evidence such that conditioning on that evidence would flip the all-things-considered expected-value verdict of acting versus not acting. If no feasible evidence could overturn the verdict, then p is not functioning as a credence in the way expected-value theory requires; it is functioning as a fixed postulate of hope or dread, and the calculation is no longer tracking anything learnable about the world.

The rationale is this. Expected-value theory treats credences as compressed summaries of learnable structure. A probability that cannot be moved by any finite learning episode — short of actually observing the catastrophe or the reward — is not a summary of anything. It is bare possibility dressed up as a credence. So the Reversibility Constraint is not an extra risk-aversion axiom bolted onto expected utility theory; it is, I want to say, a precondition for the machinery's applying in the first place.

Two cases make the distinction intuitively vivid. Consider seatbelt-wearing: the probability of a fatal crash on any given trip is tiny, but it is decisionally reversible. Crash statistics, road conditions, time of day, and one's own driving history are exactly the kind of cheap evidence that could move the probability and, in extreme cases, flip the verdict. Now consider the Pascal's mugger: nothing I could observe before the supposed payoff deadline, at a cost small relative to the demanded payment, would distinguish the mugger's claim from the indefinitely many other baseless claims of the same logical form. The difference between the two cases is not primarily the size of the payoff; it is the reversibility of the probability.

If this principle works, it would draw a principled line between tail-risk reasoning we should take seriously — rare but statistically grounded disasters — and tail-risk reasoning we should not — one-off speculative catastrophes with no revisitable basis. But I am genuinely unsure about several things.

1. Is the principle coherent as stated, or does it beg the question by quietly presupposing an expected-value calculation about the costs and benefits of gathering evidence?

2. Is it genuinely distinct from existing ideas — value of information, option value, robust Bayesianism, imprecise probability, bounded utility, or an explicit anti-fanaticism axiom — or have I just rediscovered something familiar under a worse name?

3. What is the strongest counterexample? I worry about genuinely one-shot decisions, such as responding to a novel asteroid-detection method or a once-only medical opportunity, where the absence of a feasible reversal process seems to be a feature of the situation rather than a reason to dismiss the probability.

4. Does the principle merely temper expected-value fanaticism, or does it also threaten moderate longtermist reasoning that depends on small probabilities of enormous future value?

5. Does the constraint collapse into a requirement that agents actually gather evidence before acting? That would be absurd — we do not re-run crash statistics before every drive. But if mere availability of some hypothetical flip-evidence is enough, then isn't the test too easy for a committed fanatic to pass by asserting that some imagined evidence would change their mind?
