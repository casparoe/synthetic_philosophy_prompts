I'm a journalist working on a long piece about the strange epistemology of medical evidence, and I keep running into a tension I don't yet know how to state precisely. The evidence hierarchy behind evidence-based medicine is familiar: meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials sit at the top, followed by individual randomized trials, then cohort studies, case-control studies, case series, and expert opinion at the bottom. Regulators and journals generally will not accept the claim that a treatment works until randomized trials have shown it; observational evidence, no matter how careful, is graded as lower quality and treated as provisional. Yet the same institutions do accept causal conclusions without any randomized experiment when randomization is impossible, unethical, or plainly absurd. Smoking is accepted as a cause of lung cancer even though no one randomly assigned people to smoke; a parachute is accepted as reducing mortality from a fall even though no one randomly assigned skydivers to jump without one; the surgical removal of a brain tumor is accepted as the cause of a patient's subsequent speech deficit. The practice, then, treats randomization as nearly indispensable for causal inference in some contexts and as unnecessary in others.

I would like a reconstruction of what this practice presupposes about causation and about how causal claims are warranted. In particular: does the special epistemic authority assigned to randomization make sense only on a manipulationist or interventionist understanding of causation—the idea that a cause is the sort of thing that would change the outcome if one intervened on it while holding other relevant factors fixed? And if so, is the practice coherent? That view is often accused of circularity, since what counts as the right kind of intervention is itself defined in causal terms; moreover, in a double-blind trial the thing literally manipulated is a treatment assignment, a piece of record-keeping, rather than the biological variable whose causal role the trial is meant to exhibit. The hierarchy also seems to presuppose that the causal relations established in a trial are invariant—stable enough under changes in patients, settings, and co-treatments to license clinical decisions elsewhere—even though the trial itself is a highly artificial intervention. Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say whether its different tiers presuppose consistent things, and explain what would have to change in the hierarchy if the interventionist assumption were replaced by a mechanistic account of causation, or if the interventionist picture were pushed through more consistently than the practice currently pushes it.
