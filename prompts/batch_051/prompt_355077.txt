ok so in my stats elective we did Bayes' theorem a couple weeks ago and my teacher said this thing that I can't stop thinking about: if two rational people keep seeing the same evidence, their posteriors eventually converge no matter what priors they started with, because the data "washes out" the prior. I took that to mean that stubborn real-life disagreement is basically proof that somebody is being irrational, since if everyone just updated honestly on enough data we'd all have to end up agreeing.

But my parents have watched more or less the same news for twenty years and they have only gotten further apart. Even weirder, they don't seem to be applying different weights to the same hypotheses — they have different lists of what the possible hypotheses even are. My dad thinks every economic slowdown is about taxes and regulation, my mom thinks its about technology and trade, so the "same evidence" never even enters the same model. Does the theorem actually show that people like them are doing something wrong, or does it only apply when everyone already shares the same model and just disagrees about parameter values? And what if both of their models are wrong, because the real explanation isn't on either of their lists — does convergence to the truth require already being able to entertain the truth in the first place? If the result only works under that kind of fine print, then what does the washing-out of priors actually tell us about real arguments? I'm not doubting the math, I'm confused about how far the math is supposed to reach. If you could separate the parts that are settled from the parts where people who actually work on this still disagree, that would help — and if the honest answer is "the theorem says less about real life than the slogan suggests," don't soften it.
