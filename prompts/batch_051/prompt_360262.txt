I recently had to decide whether to bind myself in advance to a course of action I expect to dislike at the time of performance — a standing commitment to a family member, though the details do not matter here — and I found myself unable to get past the case at the heart of Parfit's discussion of rational irrationality, the hitchhiker. As I understand the case: a hitchhiker is robbed and stranded; a driver would pick him up only if she were confident he would pay for the ride once he was safe; the hitchhiker knows that, once rescued, he will prefer to keep the money; and the driver, knowing this, drives on. If the hitchhiker could make himself the kind of person who keeps such promises even when, at the later moment, keeping the promise goes against his all-things-considered preference, then causing himself to be locally irrational in this way could be his rational route to rescue.

The proposition I want your credence about is this: in at least some realistic, non-rigged situations, an ordinary agent can rationally bring it about that she will later act against what would then be her own all-things-considered preference, where the only feasible way to secure the needed assurance from another agent is some form of self-induced local irrationality — that is, a standing disposition to act against, rather than revise, her later all-things-considered preferences, as distinct from an enforceable contract, a financial penalty, a change in her later preferences, or an actual change in the later payoffs.

My own credence is around 0.6; a colleague who has thought about the same case puts it around 0.2. State your own number, but more importantly, decompose it. I want the sub-questions your credence actually depends on — for example: whether the apparently irrational later act is really irrational once the agent's temporally extended plan is included in the standard of rationality; whether an agent can reliably produce such a disposition in herself by a deliberate choice; whether realistic cases almost always contain some non-irrational mechanism, such as reputation or an enforceable contract, that removes the need for self-induced irrationality; whether other people can reliably detect the disposition so that the hoped-for assurance actually materializes; and whether the later self's all-things-considered preference should be treated as having priority over the earlier self's plan. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, indicate how they combine into your overall number, and identify the sub-question to which your final credence is most sensitive. Then say where the 0.6 and the 0.2 most likely part ways, and tell me what single concrete argument or piece of evidence would do the most to move your number.
