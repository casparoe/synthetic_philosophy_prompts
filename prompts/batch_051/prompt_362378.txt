I work on product documentation and safety-review policy at a large AI company, and I need a rigorous reconstruction of the philosophy of mind our own practices commit us to. Our public-facing assistant is released with messaging that the system has no beliefs, intentions, or goals; that it does not understand or want anything; that it only makes statistical predictions; and that users who see a mind behind it are anthropomorphizing. But the same company's internal safety reports, red-teaming guides, and evaluation templates routinely attribute mental states to the same class of systems: they say a model formed a subgoal, believed the user was being deceived, sincerely attempted to comply with an instruction, or that evaluators should predict a failure by inferring what the model believes about its situation. Externally, our regulatory submissions describe the system as a tool with no agency of its own, while our release evaluations discuss cases in which the model knowingly conceals its objective.

I want a reconstruction of the implicit theory of mind and belief-attribution under which this mixed practice could be coherent, if one exists. In particular: what standard for really believing or really having an intention does the public disclaimer presuppose, and does the internal talk meet that standard? Does the internal talk instead presuppose that belief- and intention-talk are merely predictive instruments, a stance we adopt toward a system without committing to inner states, and if so, why would that license internal but not public uses? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it. Then give the concrete upshot: if we committed to the intentional stance consistently across the organization, what would have to change in the public disclaimers, the internal evaluation vocabulary, the regulatory filings, or all three, and what would we lose by making that change? The document should hold up to scrutiny from both our public-policy team, who will resist any suggestion that the product has a mind, and our research staff, who will resist any suggestion that their mental-state vocabulary is meaningless or merely decorative.
