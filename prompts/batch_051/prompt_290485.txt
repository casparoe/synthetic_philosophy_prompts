I chair the clinical ethics advisory group at a teaching hospital. The group meets weekly for ninety minutes and reviews four or five consults, most of them from intensivists, obstetricians, and palliative care. Its recommendation goes to the attending physician and, when the group cannot agree, to the hospital legal and risk office. The other members are an intensivist, two bedside nurses, a palliative care physician, a social worker, and a chaplain. None of them has formal training in moral philosophy, and they do not need another restatement of the trolley cases; they need a working instrument.

Write a decision procedure the group can use, week after week, to decide when a proposed intervention with a serious and foreseen harmful effect may be endorsed on the strength of the doctrine of double effect, and when the consultation should refuse to offer that rationale.

The procedure should be a sequence of tests to be applied in order, in table form. The table columns are:

Step, Question to ask, Passes if, Fails if, Judgment required.

For each row, the question must be phrased in plain language a nurse or chaplain could put to a physician without a degree in philosophy. The passes if and fails if entries should name the kind of evidence that would count, with examples from clinical practice, not merely restate the four traditional conditions. The judgment required entry should be left empty where the principle really does the work, and should otherwise state exactly which point cannot be settled by the doctrine and what the group has to decide for itself.

The procedure must keep three distinctions separate from the beginning and must not assume that they track one another: intending a harm versus foreseeing it and accepting it; doing a harm versus allowing it; and causing an outcome versus failing to prevent it.

The procedure must also face what is usually called the closeness problem. Consider the familiar contrast. A surgeon proposes, for a woman with aggressive uterine cancer at twenty weeks of pregnancy, to remove the gravid uterus; the mother is expected to survive and the fetus is expected to die. Another surgeon proposes, in a case of obstructed labor, to perform a craniotomy and reduce the fetal skull to deliver the mother; the mother is expected to survive and the fetus is expected to die. The received doctrine treats the hysterectomy as compatible with double effect and the craniotomy as not, even though the death of the fetus is certain in both cases and sits somewhere on the causal route to the good effect. Write the procedure so that a non-philosopher can actually draw that distinction, or can discover when it cannot be drawn and explain why the case falls outside the doctrine's jurisdiction.

Then apply the procedure to three cases, in a second table. The table columns are:

Case, Step at which the procedure stops, Verdict, Decisive judgment call.

Use these cases: first, escalation of opioids for refractory dyspnea in a patient with end-stage chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, with respiratory depression and possible earlier death anticipated, and the prescribing physician stating that the intention is relief of air hunger; second, hysterectomy for uterine cancer at twenty weeks of pregnancy; third, a craniotomy in obstructed labor to save the mother when the fetus is expected to die. For each case, if the recorded information is insufficient, state the assumption you are adding and mark it as an assumption. Make sure that at least one worked case returns a verdict that a typical clinical ethics committee would resist, and say plainly why the procedure returns it.

After the tables, add a short epistemic status note of no more than three paragraphs. Distinguish what is settled about the doctrine of double effect from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on it, including at least the following: whether the distinction between intended and merely foreseen consequences can be drawn without the closeness problem collapsing it, whether the doctrine adds anything over a doing and allowing distinction, and whether agents are reliable enough at introspecting their own intentions for the procedure to rest on such testimony. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Where the procedure adopts one defensible position among several, say so explicitly.
