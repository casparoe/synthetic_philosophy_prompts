For a semester project in my school's philosophy of biology course, our teacher gave us a model statute and a set of hard cases that the statute's authors did not anticipate. The statute reads: "'Life' means the continuing integrated functioning of an organism as a whole — metabolism, growth, responsiveness, and homeostasis — and 'death' means either (a) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (b) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem." The case we were assigned concerns a pregnant woman who suffers a catastrophic brain injury and is declared dead under clause (b). Her body is then maintained not just by a ventilator but by a mechanical circuit that pumps and oxygenates her blood, regulates her temperature and electrolyte balance, and supplies the hormones her brain stem used to coordinate. Her cells continue to divide, wounds heal, her immune system clears an infection, and the fetus develops on schedule.

Our assignment is to decide whether the mother's body counts as "alive" under the statute's definition of life even though she is "dead" under its definition of death; whether there is now one organism or two (the fetus, the mother's body, or a mother–fetus unit); and whether the biological idea of integrated functioning comes apart from the legal definitions the drafters wrote. We are told to argue the question from the plain words, from the purpose of the definitions, and from what the drafters would probably have said if they had known about this technology, and then to say which approach should control. Does the statute give one verdict or two? Please also tell me which argument, fact, or redefinition would most likely change your mind, or where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
