I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when a rating, ranking, or forecast should be protected as opinion rather than treated as actionable fact under U.S. defamation law. I'm not looking for case citations; I'm looking for the principle that best captures my own verdicts. My instincts on cases are these:

A bond-rating agency rates a company's debt CCC and calls the bonds junk; I think that is protected. A restaurant guide gives a restaurant one star and calls it the worst fine-dining experience in the city; I think that is protected. A columnist writes that a CEO is cooking the books, without identifying any specific concealed transaction; I think that is not protected, because it implies a checkable accusation of fraud even if phrased evaluatively. A financial analyst publishes that a stock will fall at least 40 percent within six months; I count that as a protected forecast. A city health department posts a numeric hygiene score of 61/100 for a restaurant, based on a checklist inspection; I count that as a factual report, not opinion. A pundit says a candidate has a 30 percent chance of winning the election; I count that as protected.

The pair that worries me is the bond rating and the hygiene score: both are numbers produced by applying explicit criteria, yet I classify one as protected judgment and the other as checkable fact. I also find I have no stable verdict on an algorithmic service that assigns a person a 520 creditworthiness score and labels them a high credit risk.

Is there a single principle that captures my verdicts and resolves the credit-score case, or do some of my verdicts have to go? State the principle precisely, run it over each case, and tell me what it would force me to revise.
