Lately I keep seeing machine learning people talk about self-driving cars and LLMs that "know how" to drive, reason, translate, and so on, and it's struck me that Ryle never got to see any of this—he died in 1976—but it seems like exactly the kind of thing he would have had opinions about. I'd like you to work through what those opinions would most plausibly have been, case by case, seperately.

Please act as a careful historian of philosophy and a Ryle scholar, and think hard before answering. Work through these three cases: (1) a modern LLM prompted to reason step by step, generating an explicit chain of intermediate propositions before it answers; (2) an end-to-end trained system like a self-driving car or a Go engine that behaves skillfully but whose competence is stored in connection weights rather than in anything propositional; (3) the later neuroscience distinction between procedural and declarative memory. And would he be tempted to say the self-driving car has knowing how, or would he resist because know-how for him is a many-way disposition of a whole agent that can adjust, explain, learn, and correct itself, rather than a one-track mechanism?

Make your answer substantive and true to the texture of Ryle's actual position, not a generic "does AI know things?" essay.
