So I keep coming back to this argument about commitment races — you know, when two sides in a negotiation each try to tie their own hands in public, because the one who looks least able to back down wins. I half-think theres a sleight of hand in it but I cant find the exact spot. Heres my best reconstruction:

(1) A threat is credible to the other side just to the extent that the person making it would lose more by backing down after making the threat than by following through. (So public promises, sunk costs, and lost reputation are what make threats credible.)

(2) In a standoff between two sides who are otherwise in similar positions, the side with the more credible threat gets the better settlement, because the other side, assuming both are rational, sees that it will be the one to give in.

(3) Therefore, if side A raises its cost of backing down while B's cost holds fixed, A becomes the side with the more credible threat and improves its own outcome; the same holds for B if B raises its cost while A holds fixed.

(4) A rational side takes whatever step improves its own outcome.

(5) So, no matter how high the two sides' costs of backing down already are, whichever side is currently behind will raise its cost past the other's; the two sides will seesaw past each other and the costs ratchet up without limit.

(6) Eventually both have made the cost of backing down larger than the value of the thing they are fighting over. Then either both dig in and take a conflict worse than the original compromise, or one backs down and absorbs a cost worse than the original compromise. Either way, both end up worse off than if neither had raised anything.

So rational sides in a commitment race make themselves worse off. That looks like a reductio of something, but I can't tell whether it's a reductio of the claim that they're rational, or of the whole practice of public threats, or whether there's a bad step. I keep worrying about step (4): maybe "improves my outcome" has to be evaluated against what the other side will actually do, and once you read it that way the ratcheting in (5) doesn't follow. Is that right, or does the problem survive that fix?
