I've been fiddling with a variant of the Ellsberg paradox and I'm not sure the usual lesson carries over.

Set up the original two-urn version first. Urn A has 50 red balls and 50 black balls, and you know that for sure. Urn B has 100 balls, each either red or black, but you know nothing about the proportion. The standard result is that people prefer to bet on red drawn from urn A rather than red drawn from urn B, and also black drawn from urn A rather than black drawn from urn B. That pair of preferences is supposed to be incompatible with expected utility, because no single probability you could assign to red in urn B makes both of those A-bets look better, since the probabilities of red and black in urn B have to sum to one.

Now the variant. Urn B is filled as follows. A fair coin is flipped, but you don't see the outcome. If it comes up heads, urn B gets 20 red and 80 black. If it comes up tails, urn B gets 80 red and 20 black. You know all of this, including that the coin is fair; you just don't know which fill actually happened. Urn A remains a known 50/50.

Here's what I want to know. Suppose someone still produces the original Ellsberg pattern in this modified version—preferring the red bet on urn A to the red bet on urn B, and the black bet on urn A to the black bet on urn B. Does the paradox still go through in the same way? Work through what an expected-utility maximizer would say if they treat a two-stage gamble as equivalent to a one-stage gamble with the same overall probabilities (the reduction of compound lotteries), and also what someone could say if they refuse to collapse the two stages like that. Then tell me whether the standard ambiguity-aversion account—where you handle an unknown composition by treating it as a set of possible distributions and looking at the worst case—carries over to this variant unchanged, or whether I've accidentally replaced the ambiguity with ordinary risk. More pointedly: is this still a test of ambituity aversion at all, or is it now testing something else, like an aversion to compound lotteries?
