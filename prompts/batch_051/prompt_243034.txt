In a recent seminar on Hobbes’s Leviathan, a student insisted that the state of nature is best modeled as a one-shot prisoner’s dilemma. On his telling, each person has a dominant reason to strike first: the other’s cooperation cannot be relied upon, and the penalty for trusting wrongly is violent death. The social contract then works by changing the payoff structure through the introduction of an enforcer whose power makes noncompliance worse than compliance; the covenant itself, he suggested, is mostly a ceremonial label for what is at bottom a change in incentives. I want to use his analogy as the basis for a session, but I do not want either to dismiss it quickly or to let it stand unchecked.

Please audit this analogy in detail. First, produce an explicit mapping: what are the players, the available strategies, the payoffs, the information conditions, and the single-round structure in the state of nature, and what feature of the commonwealth corresponds to the arrival of enforcement? Then separate the disanalogies that are merely incidental from those that bear on the student’s conclusion. Pay particular attention to whether Hobbes’s account of the causes of quarrel—competition, diffidence, and especially glory—can be represented as preferences over outcomes, and whether the right of nature is accurately captured by a dominant strategy rather than by a permissive standard of practical reasoning. Consider, too, whether Hobbes’s covenant creates a genuine obligation or only an incentive, and whether the analogy can explain why subjects, and not just the sovereign, are parties to the transfer of right. Conclude by assessing whether the analogy supports the student’s claim that the logic of the social contract is fundamentally game-theoretic, and propose a better formal model—an assurance game, a stag hunt, an iterated game, or something else—with reasons.
