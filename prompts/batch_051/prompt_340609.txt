A colleague who works on the design of reasoning systems defends the following claim. In a proposed scheme, sometimes called HCH, deliberation is represented as a tree: one human receives a hard problem, splits it into subproblems, and passes each subproblem to another copy of the same scheme, which may split it further; at the leaves, people perform small tasks without needing to understand the overall problem. The colleague says this scheme is not just a training idealization but captures something true about deliberation itself: competent judgment can be factored into many small, independently executable cognitive operations that are then reassembled without meaningful loss of quality. My suspicion is the opposite: the decomposition step itself seems to require the very understanding it is supposed to do without.

How could one test whether factored cognition is a real property of deliberation rather than only an engineering idealization? Design the strongest concrete test you can. Choose a specific task, specify how it would be factored, specify the un-factored control, and say how you would measure loss of quality. State what pattern of results would favor each view. Then say exactly what the test would and would not settle: in particular, whether it can distinguish genuine reassembly of many small contributions from a hidden central synthesis smuggled back in through the decomposition or the assembly rules, and whether success on one task type licenses any more general conclusion about human reasoning.

Write the proposal in formal American English. The final line should be exactly of the form \answer{your one-sentence summary of the verdict}, with no text after it.
