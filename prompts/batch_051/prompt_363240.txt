Construct a thought experiment in the epistemology of coincidence: a case in which a striking anecdote is globally unsurprising but locally evidentially significant. Here "globally" means the birthday-paradox / law-of-truly-large-numbers kind of analysis: given enough occasions on which a match could occur—days, people, thoughts, meetings, and so on—an event exactly like the reported one is probable somewhere in the relevant population, so the bare fact that someone, somewhere reports such a coincidence is no evidence of anything. "Locally" means the analysis that conditions on the specific first-person details of the episode: its particular content, timing, and fit with a specific ordinary causal story. Your case must be one in which the global debunking is correct as far as it goes, and yet the local analysis of the individual episode still favors one nominated mundane hypothesis over pure chance.

The case should satisfy the following constraints:
- Fully ordinary and realistic: everyday characters, plausible numbers, no psychics, divine intervention, conspiracies, simulation, exotic physics, or omniscient observers.
- The non-chance hypothesis H1 must be mundane, fully specified, and its prior probability must be low enough that the coincidence is doing real work—the case may not simply stipulate that H1 was antecedently likely. As a starting default, treat H1's prior as no higher than 1 in 10,000 unless the scenario's details justify raising it.
- The episode must not be rigged by making the witness's observations infallible or by giving them information they could not realistically have.
- The event's evidential force must emerge from the probability structure of the case, not from stipulation: you must actually assign and compare conditional probabilities rather than merely assert the verdicts.

The purpose of the exercise is to separate population-level base-rate reasoning from episode-level evidential reasoning; a case that earns its verdict by stipulation rather than by the structure of the probabilities fails.

After presenting the scenario, work through each of the following:
1. State the event E precisely, the chance hypothesis H0, and the mundane non-chance hypothesis H1.
2. Reconstruct the global debunking: specify the reference class, estimate the number of relevant opportunities, and show why an event like E was probable somewhere in that class under H0.
3. Give the local likelihood analysis: estimate P(E | H0) and P(E | H1) for the specific episode, show that the likelihood ratio favors H1 by a wide margin even after granting H1 a low prior, and explain why the posterior for H1 remains respectable once the specific report—not merely the existence of some report somewhere—is treated as the observation.
4. Identify the structural feature that allows the global debunking and the local evidential force to both be correct, and say what the case collapses into if that feature is removed.
5. Give the strongest reply available to a sophisticated defender of the law-of-truly-large-numbers debunking—one that engages with the likelihood analysis rather than ignoring it—and state how much of the case survives that reply.
