I'm in a class on free will and determinism this semester, and a friend of mine who isn't in the class sent me an explainer article that summarizes P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment." I can't tell if it's doing the view justice or quietly mangling it. The summary in the article goes like this. Strawson says the whole debate over whether determinism rules out moral responsibility is confused, because blame, resentment, gratitude, and forgiveness aren't conclusions we reach from some theory of free will. They're what he calls the reactive attitudes: the natural emotional responses we have to other people's good or ill will toward us. We're just wired to feel them, so no discovery about determinism could show that they're unjustified; an argument can't make us give them up, which makes the responsibility skeptic's position self-defeating. Where we do hold back from blame, Strawson claims, it's always because the person couldn't help it — accidents and compulsion are like that, and cases of mental illness or childhood are the same kind of excuse applied differently. So, the article concludes, Strawson dissolved the free will problem, and the only real question left is practical: figuring out when we temporarily flip into the "objective attitude" and treat someone as a thing to be managed rather than as a participant.

I'm inclined to think the article is oversimplifying, partly because I vaguely remmeber Strawson being much more careful about why the reactive attitudes are justified than "we can't help it," and partly because I don't see how one paper could just dissolve the whole free will debate. But I don't know the text well enough to say where exactly it goes wrong. Could you go sentence by sentence through that summary and tell me what a careful reader of Strawson would accept, what they'd wince at, and whether the article's closing conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts?
