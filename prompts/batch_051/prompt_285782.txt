I cover philosophy and science for a living, and Ive been trying to figure out why the argument over Frankfurt cases is so stuck. Everyone agrees on the setup: somebody—a neuroscientist, a demon, whatever—is standing by to force the agent's decision if the agent doesn't make it on their own, but the agent goes ahead and decides on their own, so the intervener never acts. One side says the agent had no alternative and was still responsible, so the principle of alternative possibilities is finished. The other side says there's always some leftover alternative—a flicker, a hesitation—or that the contrived setup smuggles in the usual notion of responsibility. Those moves have been on the table for decades and no one moves. My hunch is the real fight isnt about the cases at all: its about what a counterexample to a principle like that has to show—must it eliminate every alternative down to the last twitch, or only the alternatives that matter for blame? Give me a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a substantive metaphysical dispute, a verbal one about the phrase 'could have done otherwise,' or two camps answering different questions under one heading?
