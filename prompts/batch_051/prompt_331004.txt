I have been developing a distinction that I want checked before I lean on it any further. By an antimeme I mean an idea that tends not to be remembered or transmitted because of something about the idea itself, rather than because it is suppressed or merely unknown. I think the failure comes in two genuinely different kinds.

The first kind I call a slippery antimeme: an idea that people fail to retain even when they understand it, because holding it properly requires keeping a structure in mind rather than a slogan, and the structure collapses into a false or misleading simplification the moment attention relaxes. My standard example is Simpson's paradox. Many people can follow the example while it is explained, and many lose the lesson days later; the accurate version is a relation among three variables, and the compact one-sentence versions are exactly the misleading ones. The second kind I call an inert antimeme: an idea that is easy to retain but almost never propagates, because it offers no conversational affordance, no status gain, no practical consequence, and no natural occasion. My example is the claim that the interests of people in the far future should weigh comparably to the interests of people alive now. It is simple, easy to believe, and almost completely without traction.

I suspect these two kinds explain different parts of the history of ideas. Philosophy is full of slippery antimemes: truths that resist compression, so they keep having to be rediscovered in each generation. Morality is full of inert antimemes: truths that are easy to hold but have no incentive structure pushing anyone to pass them on. I also think the truly invisible ideas are the ones that are both slippery and inert at once.

I want an honest assessment rather than encouragement. Is the slippery/inert distinction real, or does it collapse into existing ideas such as costly signaling, the attention economy, or merely being hard to understand? Has someone already drawn it in a more careful form? Is the philosophical-versus-moral claim defensible, or is it a false symmetry? And where does the distinction break — which cases does it classify wrongly? Please answer in American English.
