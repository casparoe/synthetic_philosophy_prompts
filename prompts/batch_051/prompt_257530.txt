I am a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I spend a lot of time in design reviews where two or three competent engineers with access to the same codebase, requirements, and performance data come away with incompatible conclusions about the right architecture. That experience led me into reading about conciliationism about peer disagreement: roughly, the view that when you learn that an epistemic peer (someone about as smart, honest, and well-informed as you are) reaches a different conclusion on a question you have both thought about carefully, you are rationally required to drop your confidence substantially, perhaps all the way to suspension. I have been collecting arguments in favor of this view, and here is what I have so far.

(1) The symmetry argument: you and your peer share the same evidence and are equally likely to have reasoned well, so there is no non-arbitrary asymmetry that would let you privilege your own conclusion over hers; treating the two judgments symmetrically drags you toward her view, not away from it.

(2) The higher-order evidence argument: your peer's reaching a different conclusion is itself evidence about how reliable your reasoning was on this occasion; it makes it more likely that you made a mistake somewhere, and a rule of rationality that told you to ignore that evidence would be a rule that ignores part of your total evidence.

(3) The accuracy argument: in the long run, people who moderate their judgment when an equally informed person disagrees end up better calibrated with respect to the truth; unless you have an independent tiebreaker, the policy of retaining full confidence in every disputed conclusion is a policy that tracks the truth less well than tempering does.

(4) The impartial-observer argument: if you were watching two strangers with equal evidence and equal ability disagree, you would have no basis for siding with one of them; since the first-person perspective adds nothing relevant, you are not in a better position in your own case than an observer would be.

(5) The anti-circularity argument: the only available reason to discount a peer's opinion is the very reasoning that the peer disputes, so dismissing her judgment in order to preserve your own view presupposes the conclusion under dispute and begs the question against her.

The thing that nags at me is that these five feel as though they share a single underlying thought, namely that my own reasoning gets no special weight from the inside, and I half-suspect that any further argument would just be another restatement of that thought. But I might be wrong. Is there an argument for conciliationism that is genuinely different from all five of these, not merely a special case, rewording, or combination of them? If there is, make the case for it and say which of the five, if any, it forces me to reconsider. If there is not, explain why this territory is exhausted and why the arguments all feel like one argument.
