I've been reading about how weather services, election forecasters, and prediction markets are evaluated, and I can't shake the feeling that the scoring system has a whole philosophy packed into it. The rule seems to be this: when someone issues a forecast of "70% chance," the forecast has to be right about the 70%, not right about the event. That is, if you collect all the days on which a forecaster says 70%, the event should occur on very close to seven out of ten of those days. A forecaster who says 90% and is right only 60% of the time gets treated as overconfident, even though 60% is still more than half. A forecaster who always says 50% gets treated as useless, even on the particular cases where the event happens. And someone who says 80% and then is wrong loses more credit than someone who says 60% and then is wrong. In short, the score measures how closely the announced probability fits what actually happens, and it penalizes confident misses more than cautious ones.

That practice seems to quietly assume a lot: that uncertainty comes in degrees that can always be reduced to a single number; that for any given situation there is one correct level of confidence; and that accuracy is the only thing that makes a forecast good, rather than honesty, usefulness, or understanding why the event will happen. But I'm not sure the scoring rule actually forces all of those assumptions, and I also notice a tension: the same broader institutions often demand a clean yes or no in practice and treat a hedged, probabilistic answer as a cop-out.
