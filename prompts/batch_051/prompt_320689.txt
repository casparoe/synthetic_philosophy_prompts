I'm on my university's academic honesty board and we've got a case that's splitting us down the middle. Our code of academic conduct says: "A student violates this section if the student knowingly asserts as true a statement the student believes to be false, or if the student asserts as true a statement whose truth the student has not made a genuine effort to verify." The clause was written in 2009 and everyone agrees it was aimed at students padding essays with invented quotations and made-up statistics, not at anything like this.

Here's the case. A senior turned in a term paper whose literature review was drafted mostly by a large language model. The model produced a paragraph of confident-sounding empirical claims plus three citations; two of the citations are real papers but with wrong page ranges, and the third is to an article that does not exist. The student pasted the paragraph in without checking anything because it read smoothly and the references looked right. She says she didn't think any of the claims were false — she genuinely had no view either way — and she never opened the cited papers. The board is split. Some say the first clause can't apply because she didn't believe they were false, so she wasn't lying. Some say the second clause is basically written for this: she asserted things without making a genuine effort to verify them. Some say she never asserted anything at all — the model did — so the clause never gets a grip.

I'm trying to work out what the clause actually requires here, and how Frankfurt's distinction between lying, bullshit, and error maps onto it. What do the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if asked now, and what a reasonable student reading the code would understand each give? Is the student a liar, a bullshitter, or someone who made an error — and can the model itself be any of those? I'd like you to actually commit to which approach should win and why, rather than just listing them. Also tell me how confident you are in the overall verdict and which point in your reasoning you're least sure of.
