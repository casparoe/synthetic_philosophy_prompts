I keep coming back to this passage in Cicero's *De Finibus* III.22, where he's laying out the Stoic view on the goal of life. The key bit, in the Loeb translation, goes:

"if a man's purpose is to hit the mark, or rather, if his actual purpose, so to speak, will be to take true aim, and if his doing everything in his power to realize his purpose is regarded as the ultimate end, he will then be right in calling all his actions, and all the actions of the virtue I am considering, morally right actions; and yet this conclusion can only be established by the very sort of distinction we have just made."

My modern Stoicism reading group is all over this passage right now. Their take is that Cicero is giving us a neat way to dissolve the tension in the dichotomy of control: the point isn't to control outcomes, it's to perfect the art of aiming. The archer can't guarantee the wind won't shift, but she can do everything in her power to aim well, and that's where the good is. So the familiar "focus on what's up to you" gets cashed out as "focus on the quality of your intentional activity, not the result."

I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy, and something about this feels off to me, but I'm having trouble getting the criticism sharp. The folks in the reading group use the archer analogy as if it's just obvious that the Stoic distinction collapses into the modern self-help version: internalise your goals, care only about effort, and you'll be serene. But I'm not sure the analogy can carry that weight, and I'm not even sure the modern reading group's conclusion is a great reading of the Stoics either.
