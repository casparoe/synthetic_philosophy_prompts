In discussions of the causal exclusion argument, a colleague of mine often defends mental causation with an analogy. The way he puts it: the mind stands to the brain roughly as software stands to hardware. A running program causes the machine's outputs, he says, even though every event in the machine has a complete physical explanation at the circuit level; the program is not an extra cause competing with the hardware but a different level of description of what the hardware is doing. By parity, he concludes, conscious mental states cause behavior even though every neural event has a complete physical explanation, and the exclusion argument misfires because mental and neural causes are not genuine rivals.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy can actually take us. Spell out the intended mapping in detail, indicating what is supposed to correspond to what: the program, the executing software state, the hardware state, the compiled code, the output, and the relevant sense in which a program is said to cause anything. Then identify the respects in which the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the causal exclusion argument from those that are merely superficial. In particular, address whether the software case genuinely solves the exclusion problem or only reproduces it at the software–hardware boundary. Finally, say what the strongest version of this analogy can establish about mental causation once the disanalogies are on the table: full nonreductive mental causation, something weaker, or nothing at all. Please answer in American English.
