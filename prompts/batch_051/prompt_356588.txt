After a long argument with a friend last night about whether Theravada Buddhism and Confucianism are religions, I came away holding the stronger view: 'religion' is not a coherent category, at least not one that does serious explanatory work. I grant that the word groups things; I deny that the grouping tracks a shared feature. It looks to me like a Christian-shaped prototype plus cases held in by family resemblance and institutional convenience, with Buddhism and Confucianism as pressure cases and so-called secular religions such as nationalism or fandom as embarrassments. My friend's retort, which I could not fully answer, was that every social category is like this and yet categories such as 'law' or 'money' still work. Tell me what would count against my position. What would have to be true for 'religion' to pick out a real feature of human social life, and which considerations—cognitive, historical, anthropological—should move me? Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward. What do careful defenders of the category themselves concede, and which of my pressure cases would they insist show robustness rather than parochialism? Finally, does my view collapse into a general skepticism about all thick social categories, and if not, what distinguishes 'religion' from 'law' or 'money' on my own account?
