For years I've assumed I believe in dirty hands—acts that are the right thing to do but still leave a moral stain on the person who does them. Then a student asked me what the underlying principle is, and I realized I've never actually tested my own case-by-case verdicts against one another. So here they are. Extract the principle, or tell me which verdicts are the casualties.

1. A governor is told a dam upstream may fail within hours. If she says so on the emergency broadcast, the police believe the resulting panic will kill many more people than a controlled lie will. She announces the dam is sound and orders everyone to evacuate calmly by three routes. Verdict: she should have told the lie, and I'd defend her in public. But I also think she wronged the people she misled—it doesn't just wash out—and I'd expect her to resign or at least make some kind of accounting afterwards, even while I'd tell her to tell the same lie again.

2. A hospital administrator during a fire tells nurses and orderlies that the east stairwell is open, when she knows it isn't, to keep them moving toward the west exit. Several people are badly trampled, though fewer than if she'd told the truth. Verdict: right thing, but I can't locate the same stain. I wouldn't object if she was back at work the next day. This pair bothers me, because the two cases look structurally alike and I still want to treat them differently.

3. A senator votes for an omnibus bill that funds rural hospitals she's spent years defending, and that also contains a provision she thinks is rotten—say, a sweetheart deal for a private prison firm. She could vote no, and the hospital funding would die. Verdict: not dirty hands. This is ordinary coalitional compromise. She shouldn't lose sleep, and I'd think it melodramatic if she resigned.

4. A president orders a strike on the leader of a militant group, based on strong intelligence that he's planning an attack expected to kill dozens. She knows he's at a family gathering and that three young children will be killed. Verdict: warranted, but not clean. She shouldn't talk about the children as mere collateral, and she should carry it as something more than a cost.

5. A governor publicly promises to keep a factory open in a struggling town, then breaks the promise six months later because supporting the factory has become politically expensive. Verdict: just wrong. Not dirty. There's no necessity in play; she should keep her word or never have given it.

6. A police chief, trying to find a child who's been abducted, has the suspect kept awake under interrogation for three days and threatens to bring charges against his wife. The man breaks and the child is found alive. Verdict: this is the one I cant settle. Somedays it feels like (1), sometimes like (5), and I don't know which way a principle should push.

So: is there a single account of when an act is right-but-stained—as opposed to merely right, merely wrong, or somehow both right and wrong—that captures these, or comes close? What does it say about case (6)? And do I have to give up the difference between (1) and (2), or one of the others, to get something that isn't gerrymandered? If it helps, run your principle on cases I haven't listed: a public-health director who downplays a rare vaccine side effect in a press conference to keep vaccination rates from collapsing during an outbreak; a parent who breaks a promise to a child for the child's own good; and a legislator who votes to authorize a military intervention she believes is morally wrong, because voting no would lose her the committee chair from which she could stop a much worse intervention later. I'd rather have a clean principle that overturns one of my gut calls than a complicated one that saves them all, but only if the clean one actually earns its keep.
