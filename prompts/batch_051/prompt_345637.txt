I'm a novelist, and I sit on the editorial board of a small regional literary quarterly — mostly fiction, but we run two or three nonfiction essays a year. Last month we accepted, pending fact-check, a personal-historical essay about a mill town that died when its one factory closed. We all liked the writing. Then the checker filed her report and it was worse than the usual: a passage the essay puts in quotation marks and attributes to a mid-century critic doesn't exist anywhere; a real poet the piece leans on is given the wrong death year and the wrong cause of death; and a long, vivid anecdote about an 1880s strike has no trace in the local historical society's records. When we asked, the author shrugged and said she'd drafted the thing "with some AI assistance" and had let the model supply what she called "period texture," adding that she didn't chase the color details because they weren't the argument. That's where the board split. Some of us say it's plain error: she believed the tool, fix the facts, print an eratta slip, move on. Others say it's Frankfurt's bullshit rather than lying — she had no intent to deceive us, but she plainly didn't care whether the details were true, which is exactly the attitude we'd reject from a human writer. A third faction says the model is the bullshitter and the author has done something closer to hiring a very fast fabulist, so our honesty policy should treat her outsourcing as the offense.

Draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before next week's meeting. It should recommend one course of action — reject, publish corrected, or publish corrected with a statement — and then set a standing policy for AI-assisted nonfiction submissions. Please have it actually settle the classification question (lying, bullshit, error, delegated bullshit, or none of the above) before it moves to consequences, and include the strongest version of the argument for whichever position we don't adopt, so the losing side doesn't feel steamrolled. One board member keeps insisting the whole debate is a category error and we should judge the text on the page; I'd like the memo to answer him to, not just ignore him.
