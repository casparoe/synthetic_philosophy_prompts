I am a philosopher teaching a graduate seminar in the philosophy of animal minds, and I have encountered a textbook treatment of Morgan's Canon that strikes me as partly right but possibly overreaching. The book summarizes the canon as follows: "Morgan's Canon holds that when an animal's behavior can be explained by a lower psychological faculty, one must never attribute it to a higher one. If a dog opens a gate, this should be explained by trial-and-error learning or associative conditioning rather than by insight or reasoning. The canon is therefore a rule of parsimony commanding us always to prefer the simplest explanation of animal behavior. Morgan proposed it to curb the anthropomorphic habit of crediting animals with reason, intention, and emotion, and its proper lesson is that any rich attribution of mind to nonhuman animals is scientifically illegitimate." I am less sure about the final sentence than about the first few. Please act as an expert on Morgan's Canon and its history, and audit this summary sentence by sentence: say which parts state the canon more or less correctly, which are common simplifications, and which read something into it that Morgan did not endorse. Then spell out what the corrected canon does and does not license about ascribing reasoning, intention, or emotion to animals, and whether the last sentence's conclusion survives the correction.
