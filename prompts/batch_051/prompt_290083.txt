Two friends of mine spent an evening arguing about a decision their professional association had just made, and I came away unsure which of them was right. They had been discussing the Habermasian idea that a decision's legitimacy depends on the quality of the deliberation behind it — specifically, on whether the discussion approximated an ideal speech situation, in which no participant is excluded or coerced and only the force of the better argument determines the outcome. I reconstructed their exchange as follows.

A: The association's decision came out of a discussion in which the chair dominated the floor, the two junior members barely spoke, and one side's proposal was set aside without anyone engaging its actual reasons. The consensus only held because the people with power wanted it to. So the decision has no legitimacy.

B: No actual discussion is free of power differences. A conversation in which only the better argument wins is an ideal that has never been realized. If legitimacy requires that ideal, then no human decision has ever been legitimate. A standard that condemns every actual case is not a standard; it is a way of rejecting all decisions.

A: I am not using it as a pass/fail threshold. It is a regulative ideal — something one approximates. We can ask whether this discussion came closer to or further from it than the available alternatives, and improve next time. Legitimacy is a matter of degree.

B: Degree on what scale? There is no measure of closeness to the ideal. The only countable things — speaking time, number of objections answered — are proxies, and choosing proxies is itself a value judgment. So "closer to the ideal" ends up meaning "closer to the outcome A prefers."

A: That is not right. We can assess how much the reasons people gave actually did the work. Did anyone change their mind because of an argument, or did the chair simply close the discussion? Did the junior members have the same opportunity to speak and be answered? Those are procedural facts, not outcome preferences.

B: But someone still has to judge whether the reasons did the work. The chair will say the outcome tracked the reasons; the junior members will say it did not. That judgment is exactly as contestable as the decision itself. So the ideal does not supply an independent test — it just moves the disagreement one level up.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Rate each of the six contributions as clearly mistaken, defensible but incomplete, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what, if anything, both A and B are missing about the role this ideal is supposed to play in assessing the legitimacy of decisions. Throughout, keep separate what is settled in the philosophical literature from what is genuinely contested among those who work on this material, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
