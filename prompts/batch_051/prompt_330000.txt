You are a philosopher of mind with working knowledge of mathematical logic and the mechanism debates, and you are especially good at auditing arguments from analogy. Work in stages: first list the candidate correspondences between the two domains, then brainstorm the failure points independently, and only afterward reach a verdict.

A friend has been reading Gödel, Escher, Bach and has come away convinced that the key to consciousness is the incompleteness theorems. His picture: a sufficiently strong formal system can be made, via arithmetization, to produce a sentence that says of itself that it is not provable in that system; in this coded way, the system steps outside itself and sees its own limits. Self-awareness, he claims, is the same move at the level of a brain: a self is what you get when a physical system constructs a representation of itself, and a mind is a formal system that has found its Gödel sentence. He draws two conclusions: first, any adequate account of mind must be organized around self-reference or a strange loop of that sort; second, a mind cannot be a formal system at all, because a human who constructs the Gödel sentence for a given system can see that it is true while the system cannot prove it.

Audit this analogy on its merits. Give an explicit mapping — a table of correspondences is welcome — covering at least the formal system, the arithmetization, the Gödel sentence, provability, the limitation proved, and the act of stepping outside. Say where the mapping is tight, where it strains, and where it breaks, separating the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the two conclusions from those that are real but harmless. Attend especially to the features being quietly imported: a Gödel sentence is a specific arithmetical construction relative to a precisely defined notion of proof, and even a trivial program can print its own source code without being self-aware in any interesting sense. Finish by saying how much of the two conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a weaker analogy — self-modeling, or a system that embeds a representation of itself — does better than the Gödel-specific version.
