A firm requires two training programs for its managers. The first is a cross-cultural management course. It presents country-level survey data on workplace tendencies — for example, the proportion of employees in each country who say they are comfortable openly challenging a superior's proposal in a team meeting — and instructs newly posted managers to treat those country means as planning inputs: expect less audible disagreement in one posting than another, read silence in meetings differently, adjust how you pitch ideas and how you solicit pushback. The course is careful to say that the figures are averages and that individuals vary widely within every country. The second program is the firm's anti-discrimination and respectful-workplace training. It says that a person's national origin must play no role in how they are evaluated, coached, assigned, or promoted, and the firm has disciplined a manager who explained a colleague's silence in performance reviews by saying, without hostile intent, that people from her country generally do not push back in public.

The company sees no contradiction: one program is evidence-based management, the other is about treating people as individuals.

I want to know what view of national character — of whether it is a coherent notion at all, and whether it is ever acceptable to generalize about peoples — this pair of practices presupposes, and whether that view is stable. Reconstruct the implicit theory under which the two programs come out consistent. Then separate what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with, state which rival views of national character they quietly rule out, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory would license it. Then press on the apparent contradiction: can the firm coherently treat country-level averages as real and action-guiding while treating any inference from those averages to an individual as forbidden? Does combining the two programs presuppose that there are group-level traits existing over and above the individuals who compose the group, or is it enough to presuppose regularities in institutions, incentives, situations, and language? Finally, say what the firm would have to change in each program if it adopted the view that national character is not a coherent property of peoples, and, briefly, what it would have to change if it adopted the opposite view that national character fixes individual workplace behavior well enough to apply the averages directly to persons. Lead with your bottom line in one sentence, and keep the whole answer under 700 words.
