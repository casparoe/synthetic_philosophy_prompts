My local EA group spent the last few meetings arguing about the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework (roughly: how big a problem is, how much an extra dollar or researcher moves it, and how little attention it already gets), and specifically about why, in practice, everyone just rates their own pet cause 9/9/9 and the framework ends up doing no real work. A friend of mine, the kind of person who builds prediction markets for everything, proposed an Open Cause Fund designed to make ITN scoring actually bite, and I cant stop thinking about where the thing falls apart first.

His mechanism, more or less:
- The fund has $5m a year, paid out quarterly.
- Any registered member can score any cause on the public list from 0 to 10 on each of I, T, and N.
- If you score a cause 7 or higher on tractability, you also have to put a probability (0 to 100) on an 18-month milestone that would make your tractability score true, like “by Q4 2026 at least three city governments will have adopted the checklist.” An external audit panel later marks milestones hit or miss.
- A cause’s score on each dimension is the weighted average of member scores, and its funding score is I × T × N.
- Each scorer’s weight starts at 1, and is updated after milestones resolve using their Brier score, floored at 0.1 and decaying back toward 1 after a year of inactivity; consistently right scorers get louder, sloppy ones get muted.
- Quarterly money goes out in proportion to the square of the funding score, but no single cause can take more than 25% of the pool in a quarter.
- New causes can be nominated with a short pitch, but they spend their first quarter on probation: scoreable, but not eligible for funding until their second quarter.

I know the obvious stuff — Goodharting the milestones, coalitions pumping up their causes scores, gaming the audit, the three dimensions not really being independent, the audit panel itself becoming a captured institution. But I want the actual walkthrough. Act as an expert on mechanism design and institutional incentives, someone who takes Goodhart’s law and Moloch-type dynamics seriously, and think carefully. Start by brainstorming the specific ways each actor would respond — cause founders, strategically minded scorers, the audit panel, the fund staff — then tell me where this breaks first over a three-year timeline, what second-order effects it sets off, and whether the obvious patches (say, weighting scorers by money they actually stake rather than by track record, or dropping the probation rule) would help or just move the failure somewhere else. I’m not asking whether ITN is the right bird’s-eye framework; I want the concrete failure story, and ideally what version of this, if any, would survive contact with the people being scored.
