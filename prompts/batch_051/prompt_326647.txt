I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy trying to make my case judgments about responsibility for autonomous-system harms cohere. A delivery robot with a faulty sensor strikes a pedestrian: I blame the company that skipped rain testing, not the robot. A surgical robot severs a nerve while executing an approved high-level plan: I blame the surgeon less than if she had held the scalpel, yet no one—surgeon, manufacturer, or machine—bears the full blame. An autonomous drone strikes a vehicle mislabeled in a database by a contractor: no human intended or foresaw this harm, and I'm unwilling to call it a mere accident. A language model prompted by a user produces a defamatory statement about a real person: I blame user and platform partly, but something remains unallocated. The pair I can't reconcile is the drone and the language model: both involve diffuse authorship, but in the drone case I'm close to accepting a genuine responsibility gap, while in the language-model case I resist that and distribute blame across humans. Is there a principle of retrospective moral responsibility—blameworthiness, not legal liability—that captures these verdicts, or near enough? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why, and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: an adaptive traffic-control system whose self-modified signal pattern contributes to a fatal crash, with engineers setting only high-level constraints.
