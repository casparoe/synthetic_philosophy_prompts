I serve on the board of a small housing cooperative, and at last month's meeting we adopted a set of policy amendments by voting on them clause by clause. The outcome was a contradiction: a majority approved each of two clauses whose conjunction entailed a third clause that a majority had just rejected. We are now revisiting the decision, and I have been asked to spend twenty minutes at the next general meeting explaining the discursive dilemma — how individually consistent members can produce an inconsistent collective judgment through majority voting on connected propositions — before we choose a new decision procedure. Don't explain the dilemma to me; plan how I should explain it to the members, who are not philosophers and who are now inclined to blame someone for rigging the votes.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. The five items should cover: (1) the one idea that must be in place first, and why the outcome will look like bad faith without it; (2) the single carrying example, built around three members and two clauses in an ordinary committee rather than a courtroom, together with what it must have and lack — in particular, why I should not use the appellate-judges case I was initially drawn to; (3) the order in which the remaining pieces should be introduced and what depends on what; (4) the misconceptions the members will predictably form (for example, that someone changed their mind, that the tally missed a vote, or that the group itself is irrational) and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and (5) the one question I can pose at the end to check whether they have grasped the dilemma as a structural possibility rather than as a mistake someone made, plus the one thing I should leave for the later procedural discussion — such as the trade-off between premise-based and conclusion-based voting — rather than settle on the spot.
