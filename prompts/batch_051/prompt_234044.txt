I'm taking a course on the politics of technology, and we're working through Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" One of the assigned companions to the course has a section on Winner that I keep coming back to because it seems to flatten his argument. The companion says something like this: Winner argues that technologies are never neutral, but are inherently political. His main proof is the low-hanging overpasses on Long Island parkways, designed by Robert Moses to block buses carrying poor and nonwhite passengers from reaching the beaches. For Winner, this shows that an artifact's politics is just the intentions of its designer written into the material world: the bridges are racist because Moses was racist, so the politics lies in human purposes, not in the steel and concrete. Winner also argues that some technologies, such as nuclear power, are inherently incompatible with democracy and will inevitably force any society that adopts them into authoritarian centralization. He concludes that because artifacts have politics in this strong sense, technological development determines political outcomes, and societies only have the illusion of choice about technology.

My unease comes from a passage in the essay itself that I went back to after reading the companion. Early on, Winner discusses the Long Island overpasses and then, as I read him, he explicitly distinguishes two different things someone might mean by calling an artifact political. The first sense covers cases where a specific technical device or arrangement settles a political issue within a particular community — the way these bridges embedded a decision about who could and could not reach the parks and beaches. The second sense covers technologies that, by their internal structure, require or strongly tend to produce a particular pattern of authority and power, with nuclear power as the central example. He emphasizes that these two senses pull in different directions and that much of the debate goes wrong by running them together. It seems to me that the companion collapses the first sense into the second and then draws a deterministic conclusion Winner himself is trying to avoid.

Is the companion's summary fair? Go through it carefully and separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what Winner would explicitly reject. In particular, does the Long Island bridge example show that a technology can be political because of the design's internal structure, or only because of the social context in which it was deployed? And does Winner's argument commit him to technological determinism? Please take a definite position on these questions and defend it, rather than just surveying possible readings.
