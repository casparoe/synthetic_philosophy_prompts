The influential thesis that human violence has declined over centuries, developed most fully in Steven Pinker's Better Angels of Our Nature, depends on a stable referent for the term violence: falling counts of homicide, battle deaths, and related harms are taken to show that the underlying phenomenon itself has shrunk. But there is now evidence from legal and scholarly practice and from experimental psychology that the category has not stayed fixed. As direct physical violence became rarer, the term expanded to cover coercive control, online abuse, and institutionally mediated structural violence; and work on prevalence-induced concept change suggests such expansion happens partly automatically when instances of a category become scarce. Does Pinker's argument survive this development? Pinpoint which premise it threatens, say whether restricting the claim to a fixed measure such as homicide per capita repairs it or merely changes the subject, and assess whether there is still a fact of the matter about whether the world has become less violent.
