I've been writing a physics engine for a side project and I keep tripping over this, and then I went down a rabbit hole reading about Newton's bucket, and now I can't let it go.

The asymmetry is this. Take a body moving in a straight line at constant speed. There is no local measurement that tells you it's "really" moving; velocity only makes sense relative to some coordinate frame I picked. My solver has no privileged zero-velocity frame, and an IMU (inertial measurement unit) on the body can't measure uniform velocity. But the second the body accelerates or spins, its no longer relative. An accelerometer or gyroscope can pick that up without any reference to the outside world. Water in a spinning bucket climbs the wall, and you can tell it's spinning without looking at anything else. Newton's point was that this can't be relative to the bucket itself, because the water and bucket are at relative rest exactly when the surface is concave.

So I'm wondering whether this asymmetry is actually justified, or whether it's just a bug we've learned to live with. Linear velocity gets the full relational treatment — there's no fact of the matter about absolute velocity. Rotation and acceleration get treated as if there's something absolute there, or at least a local difference that doesn't reduce to motion relative to other bodies. Is there a principled reason the line falls exactly there, instead of it being a historical accident or a Newtonian prejudice we should be more suspicious of?

I know this is the substantivalism-vs-relationism fight, and that Mach had something to say about the distant stars being responsible. I'm not asking for a reading list. I want you to give me the strongest case that the two kinds of motion really are different in a deep way, then the strongest case that the asymmetry is overblown and maybe we should stop treating rotation as absolute. Then give me an honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, drop it, or reframe it? And please seperate what's settled (experimentally or mathematically) from what's still genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
