I work on systems that make choices — trained models, animals in experiments, sometimes the people running a process — and I keep hitting a wall I don't have a name for. Recently a colleague and I were watching a chess engine, closed to inspection, that in thousands of games never castled queenside and in endgames repeatedly declined moves that would win material but expose its king. She thought it had learned that king safety outweighs material. I thought it carried a standing, invisible penalty for any position with the king on the c-file. A third person said queenside castling may simply never have been among the options it considered, because the search never generated those positions. Those are three genuinely different pictures of one machine — different aims, different sets of available moves — and from the outside they are indistinguishable. The same thing came up with a foraging animal that never crossed a particular ridge and with a trading algorithm that never entered certain markets: was the avoided thing unwanted, not considered, or silently unavailable?

The pattern is this. To say what such a system is really after, I have to settle both what it values and what it was in a position to choose among, yet the behavior alone seems to leave both open. Whenever the behavior doesn't fit the aim I attributed, I can always add a hidden cost for exactly the situation it avoided, or rule the unchosen action out of the space of things it ever considered. Each of those additions, taken one at a time, is perfectly reasonable — so nobody treats their accumulation as a strike against the account, even though a story that can absorb any observation with one more invisible cost or one more missing option has eventually stopped saying anything. I know this is cousins with the point that theories can be quietly adjusted to survive evidence, and with the everyday practice of reading someone's goals off what they do, but I'm after the narrower setting where what fails to be pinned down is, first, the aim the system is pursuing and, second, the set of moves that were available to it in the first place. Does this setting have an established name, and does the failure mode — the piling-up of individually reasonable hidden costs and missing options until the account is empty — have a name of its own? If the standard vocabulary fits only pieces of it, or fits it only roughly, I want to know the nearest concepts, how my case differs from each, and which is the best match.

I won't be satisfied with a bare label; I need the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are. If I've framed this poorly, or the two sides (aims and options) should not be treated as one problem, or "what the system is really after, apart from any particular model of it" is itself a confusion, tell me so plainly rather than offering a polite coinage for a pseudo-problem.
