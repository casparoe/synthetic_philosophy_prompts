I am a novelist, and much of my work consists in deciding how much blame, praise, and admiration a character deserves. My own verdicts in these matters follow a pattern I cannot articulate, and I would like you to extract the principle behind them, or to tell me plainly which of my verdicts must be given up.

Here are the cases as I actually judge them.

First, two drivers take their eyes off the road for the same three seconds, at the same spot, on the same afternoon. The first reaches the curve a moment before a child runs into the street; the second reaches it a moment after, and kills the child. I blame the second far more than the first. If I wrote the two into a novel, the second would be a person permanently marked by what he had done, while the first could be written as an ordinary, slightly comic figure. I do not believe the outcome merely reveals that the second was more careless, because I have stipulated that the carelessness was identical.

Second, two men have the same rigid, obedient, unimaginative temperament. One's temperament fits harmlessly into ordinary bureaucratic life. The other's fits just as neatly into a murderous regime, where the same dutifulness consists in signing the papers that send people to their deaths. The second signs; the first, I stipulate, would have signed just the same in his position. I call the second a villain and the first merely a difficult man. Only the time and place into which each was born differs. I cannot give a stable reason for this, yet I will not give up the verdict.

Third, a man steals money because his child is ill and he cannot pay the bill. Another man, with scruples no stronger, never steals because his circumstances have never put pressure on those scruples. I do not think the thief is worse than the lucky man; I find the lucky man no more virtuous, merely untested. So in the second case I let circumstance turn one man into a villain while the other remains ordinary, but in the third case I refuse to let circumstance demote the thief. I am aware that these two impulses sit uneasily together.

Fourth, I revise a draft. In the first version, the lapse from the first case passes without consequence; in the revised version, the same lapse kills a child, and I keep the driver's history, chosen action, and degree of attention entirely unchanged. After the revision, I find the character more blameworthy, and I would write him as a different kind of man. But this puzzles me: the only thing I changed was the timing of a child in the background, which is under my authorial control. A plot decision should not change how bad a character is, yet it changes my judgment of him, and I cannot argue myself back out of it.

What I want from you is a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, governing how much luck should count in blame and praise. It should aim to capture my verdicts in the four cases above. For each verdict it captures, show how. For any verdict it cannot capture, argue that the verdict, rather than the principle, should be revised, and say why. Then apply your preferred principle to a case I have so far been unable to settle. A man aims a rifle at his neighbor with the settled intention of killing him; the bullet is deflected by a low branch, and no one is harmed. My narrative instinct writes the scene as a bitter farce rather than as attempted murder, but his intention was exactly that of a murderer, and I do not know whether to call him one. Does your principle treat him as a murderer, as a fool, or as something else? And does it draw the line between this case and the first case in the right place, or does it force me to revise one of those two reactions?
