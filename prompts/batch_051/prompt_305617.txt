I find myself pulled in two directions by the common prior assumption and the agreement results that surround it. On one side is a genuinely compelling theory. Suppose two agents begin with the same prior probabilities over the possible states of the world, each updates by conditionalization on what they privately observe, and each then announces their resulting degrees of belief until those announcements become common knowledge. The theory says their posterior probabilities for any given proposition must then coincide. Any residual divergence would have to be traced to one of three sources: information not fully shared, a mistake in updating, or a difference in the agents' starting priors. The first two are naturally classified as epistemic defects. The third is the one that troubles me, because it seems to make the rationality of inquiry depend on arational or merely subjective starting points; if priors may differ freely, then disagreement becomes too cheap, and the theory's whole appeal lies in refusing to let disagreement rest on such freedom. The picture has the virtue of a tidy discipline: when confronted with disagreement, look for the hidden evidence or the bad inference before concluding that the evidence underdetermines belief.

But I also have a firm intuition I cannot fully disown: that two careful, honest people can share all the evidence relevant to some question — the likely effects of a policy, the probability of a contested historical claim, the trajectory of an economy — and still reasonably disagree. It does not feel like the disagreement must be pathological. The people involved seem to be reasoning correctly from the same information while simply weighting considerations differently, and I cannot identify any particular step at which one of them has gone wrong. If I accept the strong version of the common prior assumption, I seem forced to say that ordinary intellectual life is shot through with irrationality, or else that its persistent disagreements are all covertly informational. That latter diagnosis can be made to fit almost anything, which makes me suspicious of it even while I recognize its power.

I am not asking which position is the philosophical consensus. I want to know what would have to be the case for the intuition to be the thing that gets debunked and abandoned, what would have to be the case for the theory to be amended or given up, and whether there is a principled reading of 'same evidence' or 'rational' on which the agreement results and the reasonableness of deep, good-faith disagreement both survive.
