My friend is weirdly opinionated about those puzzle cases where a demon bribes or threatens you into having some attitude, and I've started treating her as a prediction problem. Your the wrong-kind-of-reasons person I know, so help me out. Think carefully, start by brainstorming candidate rules before settling, and don't just pattern-match to the obvious object-given/state-given distinction. She's not a philosopher by training, by the way—she just has strong, oddly stable intuitions about these cases.

Here are the verdicts she's been firm about.

(1) Belief. A demon says he'll break your legs unless, by dawn, you genuinely believe that a particular teapot in a locked warehouse is solid silver. You can't check, and all your evidence says it's glazed ceramic. She says you have no reason to believe it's silver. At most, you have a reason to take a pill or hire a hypnotist to make yourself believe it; the belief itself would be unjustified, and trying to form it directly wouldn't even work.

(2) Admiration. A demon offers you a fortune if you genuinely admire him—not fake it, really admire him—and by your own lights he's cruel, tacky, and boring. She says there is a reason to admire him, but it's the wrong kind: it makes it sensible to get yourself to admire him without making the admiration fitting. The admiration would still be unfitting.

(3) Amusement. A demon offers you a fortune if you find his long, derivative, badly delivered jokes genuinely funny. She says the same thing: there's a wrong-kind reason to be amused, though the jokes aren't funny and the amusement would be unfitting.

(4) Intention. A demon offers you a fortune if you now intend to drink a harmless but revolting liquid tomorrow; the money gets paid once the intention is formed, whether or not you drink it. She says you have no reason to intend to drink it. If you know you'll pocket the money and skip the drink, you can't really intend to drink; at most you have reason to bind yourself to drinking it or otherwise cause the intention.

Now the case I actually care about. Suppose the demon offers you the same fortune if you genuinely feel fear of a completely harmless rubber duck sitting on the table in front of you—not act scared, really feel the fear. The duck is, by your own lights, no more threatening than a spoon, and you can see exactly how harmless it is. Nothing else is at stake.

What would she say about that one? Specifically: (i) lay out the rule she seems to be running, (ii) find at least one other rule that fits her four verdicts just as well and that gives a different answer for the duck, (iii) give your best guess for what she'd say, with your reasons for picking one rule over the other, and (iv) name the further case you'd test her on to cleanly tell the rules apart.

I'm less interested in the exact yes/no than in the rule and the disagreement between the rules, so don't end on a non-committal note: give me a defended prediction.
