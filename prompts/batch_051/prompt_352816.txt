I teach a course on the ethics of play, and I keep coming back to a proposal someone made at a workshop a few years ago. The idea is that any game depicting armed conflict and wanting an industry "realistic war" label has to ship with a war ledger. The ledger is a permanant, un-hideable panel showing the civilian deaths, injuries, displacements, and destroyed homes or infrastructure that the player's campaign has caused, including delayed and indirect effects: the clinic you shelled in mission two shows up later as a cholera outbreak in mission five. Each entry gets a plausible real-world cost attached, maybe in dollars or person-days of recovery, and the ledger sits next to the ordinary score, achievements, and unlocks. The thinking is that war games can be fun, but the fun shouldn't float free of the cost; if you're going to trade on the aesthetics of real war, you should at least have to keep the books on it. The proposal was for a self-regulatory norm, not a law.

I'm less interested in whether the scheme is a good idea in the abstract than in how it would actually behave if studios adopted it. What would it incentivize designers to do and players to do? How would it be gamed — scripted civilian deaths you can't prevent, players turning the ledger into a high score to minimize, studios choosing settings where victims don't count as "civilians" under the accounting rules, disputes about whether arming a faction in act one makes you responsible for what they do in act three? Where does it break down, and can the failure modes be fixed without turning the ledger into just another piece of flavor text? I'd also like a guess at the second-order effects: does this make war games more serious, or does it train players to price atrocity?
