You're a philosopher of cognitive science who's good at spotting equivocations, and I'd like you to think carefully about this argument I keep coming back to. Before you evaluate it, start by writing out the different readings of the word 'module' you think are in play, and then go through the steps one by one.

Here's the argument, cleaned up as best I can:

(1) In Fodor's strict sense, a module is a cognitive subsystem that is domain-specific and informationally encapsulated: its computations can draw only on a small, proprietory store of information, not on whatever else the organism knows.

(2) Massive modularity says the mind is made up entirely of modules — perception, language, social cognition, and even central belief fixation are all modules or assemblies of them.

(3) So if massive modularity is true, belief fixation is either a module or an assembly of modules.

(4) If belief fixation is a module in the strict sense, then it is encapsulated, so it can only access a small fixed database.

(5) But belief fixation is isotropic, in Quine's sense: in principle, any belief you have can be relevant to whether you revise any other belief, so the system that fixes beliefs must be able to draw on your whole store of beliefs.

(6) An encapsulated system can't do that.

(7) So belief fixation is not a module in the strict sense.

(8) Therefore massive modularity is false.

I half-suspect the flaw is that 'module' shifts between the strict Fodor sense in (1) and a looser sense in (2)–(3), and that the conclusion only kills a strawman. But I also can't shake the feeling that the looser sense just means 'a functionally specialized system', in wich case massive modularity becomes nearly trivial. And maybe the real problem is that (4) only handles the 'module' disjunct and never rules out the 'assembly of modules' option, so the argument is invalid even before we get to the equivocation. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what's the strongest version of massive modularity that survives it?
