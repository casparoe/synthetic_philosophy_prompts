I'm taking a media law class and I keep getting stuck on the fact/opinion line after reading *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*, 497 U.S. 1 (1990). Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for the Court: “Simply couching a statement—‘Jones is a liar’—in terms of opinion—‘In my opinion Jones is a liar’—does not dispel the factual implications contained in the statement.” So the test I’ve been working with is something like: an allegedly defamatory statement gets First Amendment protection if it can’t reasonably be interpreted as stating actual facts about a person, especially if the language is loose, figurative, or hyperbolic; but if it has a provably false factual connotation, it can be actionable even if it’s dressed up as opinion.

Here’s the case I’ve been chewing on. A fintech company publishes a free public “repayment reliability score” for individual landlords, on a scale from 200 to 800, using tenants’ rent payment histories, eviction filings, and some data-scraped social media signals. It labels a particular landlord—who has never missed a mortgage payment but has been sued twice by tenants over security deposits—as “high risk: 412.” The landlord sues, saying the score falsely implies he is financially unreliable and likely to stiff creditors. The company says its just a forward-looking opinion or forecast: it doesn’t assert any specific past fact, just a proprietary probabilistic judgment, and no reasonable person would treat a three-digit score as a literal factual claim.

Apply the *Milkovich* criteria to this. Go criterion by criterion, say where the criteria underdetermine the answer, and then tell me whether the score is protected opinion or actionable fact—and whether this case shows the criteria themselves are misshapen for algorithmic ratings and forecasts. Please write in American English.
