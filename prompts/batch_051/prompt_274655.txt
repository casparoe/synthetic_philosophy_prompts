I've been turning over something weird about how we treat abandoned stuff, and I want you to help me pull out the theory of ownership that's hiding underneath it.

Here's the practice. If someone leaves a chair on the curb, or abandons a piece of land, eventually the law lets ownership lapse and someone else can take it — lost-property rules, adverse possession, escheat to the state, that kind of thing. Ownership ends when the owner stops acting like an owner, or when there's no owner left to act. But software doesn't work that way. A program whose author is dead and whose company disolved in the 1990s is still under copyright: nobody can legally copy it, patch it, or run it in an emulator without permission from someone who, for all practical purposes, no longer exists. Preservationists and abandonware sites do it anyway, but they know the law says they're in the wrong. Same with orphan books: a library can hold a crumbling out-of-print book by an author who died decades ago and left no heirs, and it still can't legally scan the text to keep it readable.

So physical property is something you can lose by not exercizing it, but intellectual property persists even when there's nobody left to exercize it. That feels like more than a legal quirk; it looks like a particular picture of what ownership is. What view of ownership does this pattern presuppose? For example, does it presuppose that IP rights are more like personal rights that survive the person, while physical property is a social arrangement that only makes sense while someone is around to use and defend it? Or is it just a contingent artifact of copyright law with no coherent theory underneath? And if we swapped in a use-it-or-lose-it picture of ownership for abandoned software and orphan works, what would actually have to change in how libraries, archives, and emulation communities operate?
