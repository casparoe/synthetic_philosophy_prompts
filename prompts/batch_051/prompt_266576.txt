I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and a coworker recently summarized something called Fitch's paradox of knowability for me. I want to know how much of his version I can trust, because I would rather not internalize a distorted account. His summary, as I remember it, went roughly like this.

Fitch's paradox refutes verificationism. Verificationism is the claim that every true proposition is knowable: for any truth p, it is possible for someone to know p. The paradox shows that this claim collapses into the obviously false conclusion that every truth is already known. The proof runs as follows. Pick some truth p that no one knows, and form the conjunction q: "p is true, and p is never known by anyone." Since p is an unknown truth, q is true. By the knowability principle, q is knowable, so in some possible situation someone knows q. But knowing a conjunction means knowing each conjunct, so this person knows that p is true and knows that p is never known. Since knowledge entails truth, if someone knows that p is never known, then it is true that p is never known — that is, no one knows p. Yet that same person also knows that p is true, which makes p known in that situation. Contradiction. So q cannot be known under any circumstances; q is unknowable. But q is true, so there is a true proposition that is unknowable, contradicting verificationism. Therefore, under verificationism, there can be no unknown truth: every truth must already be known. Since that is false, verificationism is false. The only serious ways around the paradox, he added, are to deny that knowledge entails truth or to deny that knowing a conjunction entails knowing each conjunct; both are non-starters, so the paradox is decisive.

The logical moves themselves looked straightforward to me — they resemble the sort of reductio argument I have seen in theoretical computer science — but I do not have the training to judge whether the philosophical assumptions are being characterized fairly. In particular, I suspect he is running "verificationism" and "every truth is knowable" together, and the claim that there are only two possible escape routes feels too strong. Could you audit the summary? I would like to know which parts accurately represent Fitch's argument and the view it targets, where the summary subtly misrepresents either one, and what a defender of the principle that all truths are knowable would most plausibly object to. Does the conclusion that verificationism is decisively refuted follow from the accurate parts, or does the proof rest on stronger assumptions than the summary acknowledges?
