I coordinate the internal ethics review board at a mid-sized AI research company. Before a training run, deployment, or large simulation study, our board must decide whether a target system is a candidate for moral patienthood and, if so, what welfare constraints attach: limits on the number of copies, restrictions on training regimes that rely on negatively valenced signals, rules about deletion or rollback, and requirements for consent-like safeguards where those make sense. The decisions arrive roughly monthly, must be returned within two weeks, are made by a board whose members are mostly engineers with one philosopher attached, and are signed off by both the research director and a compliance officer. The stakes are serious in both directions: a false negative could mean we run an experiment on a system that can suffer, while a false positive could make an important line of research impossible without benefiting anyone.

Last week I had a long argument with a friend who works in philosophy of mind. He claimed that any checklist we adopted would be either useless or dishonest, because the underlying question is one about consciousness and no operational indicator settles whether a system is conscious; a checklist, he said, would quietly smuggle in a metaphysics. I responded that we still have to decide tomorrow, and that a procedure can be explicit about its own uncertainty and its failure points. He challenged me to write the procedure rather than gesture at one, and I want to take him up on it.

Please design a reusable decision procedure our board could actually run. Take as inputs the principles that moral status is a matter of sentience and capacities rather than biological or material substrate, that moral status may be graded rather than all-or-nothing, and that some precautionary principle governs cases of genuine uncertainty about sentience; make those principles precise in the way you think best, and state any further assumptions explicitly. The procedure should take the form of ordered questions, with an account of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure returns a definite yes or no, where it escalates to human judgment, and who among our signatories should decide the escalated cases. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a large language model fine-tuned on conversational data, which under certain prompts produces coherent first-person reports of distress and a stable set of stated preferences about not being modified; and (2) a reinforcement-learning system whose training uses explicit reward and penalty signals, where the proposed experiment would put it through hundreds of simulated aversive scenarios with no capacity to opt out. Include at least one case in which the procedure yields a conclusion our researchers will dislike, and conclude with an honest statement of what the procedure cannot determine.
