I am a PhD student in modern history and the graduate-student member of my university library's Special Collections Acquisitions Committee. We must decide this month whether to accept a donation from the successor body of the Ashford Employment and Relief Society (AERS), a private charitable organization that, from 1910 to 1975, ran a work-relief office, a children's summer placement program, and a family-rehabilitation unit in the city where the university is located. The gift is roughly three hundred linear feet of administrative records plus about twelve thousand individual case files. The case files were compiled by salaried charity visitors and social workers; they contain psychiatric assessments, moral evaluations, family disputes, suspicions of crime or substance use, and similar material about named clients, relatives, neighbors, and informants, most of whom never consented to the creation, retention, or transfer of these records. Some of the named people are still alive, and many more have living children or grandchildren. The donor will not deposit the collection unless the archive agrees to digitize it in full and place it online, open-access, within three years; the donor says this is a matter of transparency about the charity's historical record. Some committee members argue that accepting on these terms would repeat the original wrong with faster technology, converting confidential files into searchable public dossiers. Others argue that refusing the gift, or sealing the case files indefinitely, would leave the charity's own administrative version of the neighborhood as the only record, and that the case files may be the only place where the voices and circumstances of people the official record silenced are preserved at all. We need a document the committee can act on.

Draft the decision memo. It should make one clear, actionable recommendation — accept on the donor's terms, reject the gift, or accept with specified conditions such as closure periods, redaction, name suppression, or selective digitization — and give reasons the other members could actually respond to. Treat honestly the strongest consideration on the losing side, not a straw-man version of it. Engage one complication squarely: any condition short of full open digitization may lead the donor to withdraw the entire gift, so conditions have an opportunity cost we should not hide. Finally, append a short bracketed note to the committee chair, outside the formal memo, saying how confident you are in the recommendation and which single factual assumption or normative premise you are least sure of.
