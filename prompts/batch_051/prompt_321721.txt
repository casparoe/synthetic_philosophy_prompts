For a seminar on morphology aimed at students who mostly come from natural language processing, I am preparing a sorting exercise that is meant to make them feel the inflection/derivation distinction—and the very idea of a single word—fray at the edges once they leave the textbook cases. The exercise hands them a mixed list of English forms and asks them to locate each on a cline, using the usual diagnostics: semantic transparency and drift, productivity and lexical gaps, obligatoriness, change of syntactic category, separability from the host, interaction with syntax or agreement, and so on.

The forms are: the regular plural suffix in chatbots; the third-person singular present suffix in she trains; the regular past-tense suffix in they trained; the comparative suffix in smarter; the adverbial suffix in quickly; the agentive suffix in trainer; the abstract-noun suffix in happiness; the negative prefix in unhappy; the possessive marker in the person next door's key; the particle in switch the server off; and the element cran- in cranberry.

Please sort these eleven items into five tiers, from Tier 1, 'clearly an inflected form of the same lexeme', to Tier 5, 'not usefully described as word-internal inflection or derivation at all—a clitic, a separable particle, a bound root, or a multiword unit'; Tier 3 is reserved for genuinely mixed or analyst-dependent cases, and ties within a tier are allowed. For each item, give a sentence or two explaining which criterion, or pair of criteria, did most of the work. Then pick two cases where a different criterion, weighted heavily, would shift the item by a tier, and say which placement you finally endorse and why.
