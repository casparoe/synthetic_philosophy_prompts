I cover agriculture, food systems, and the ethics of eating, and I keep running into a pattern — common among self-described freegans, but also among others — that I cannot quite make sense of. Several of the people I interview, including long-time vegans, animal-welfare advocates, and staff at food-recovery charities, refuse animal products in ordinary transactions but will eat or serve them when the products would otherwise go to waste. They keep rescued hens and eat the eggs; they cook venison from roadkill; they pull packaged meat from supermarket bins before it is compacted; they hand out donated cheese that a caterer is about to discard. They describe this not as a lapse or a pragmatic compromise, but as consistent with, and sometimes even required by, their ethics, on the grounds that no additional animal suffering and no additional demand are being created. Yet some of these same people will decline a beef stew a friend has cooked for them, and then eat the same stew from the friend's refrigerator two days later because it is about to be thrown away.

I would like to know what view of the wrongness of using animal products this practice presupposes. Does it commit them to saying the wrong lies entirely in the causal contribution — the market signal, the complicity in production — and not in the use of an animal's body as such? If so, what does the practice imply about cases where the causal link is weak but not zero, such as accepting an unrequested gift? If not, how do its practitioners avoid the conclusion that treating an animal body as a resource is itself the problem, in which case the dumpster rescue would be no cleaner than the purchase? Please reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where the practice is underdetermined between rival views, and say what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by the strongest alternative. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
