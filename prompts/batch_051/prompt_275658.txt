I've been trying to put together a little handout for an intro epistemology reading group and I keep stalling on one piece of the Meno—the bit where Socrates has to say why knowledge is worth more than mere true belief, and I'd like a gut-level explanation rather than a summary. What trips me up is the apparent swerve in the argument. First Socrates seems to say that, practically speaking, true belief does the job just as well as knowledge: someone with a true opinion about the road to Larissa will lead you there just as reliably as someone who knows it. That makes knowledge look like it has no extra value at all. Then he says knowledge is prized more because true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus, which run off if they aren't tied down, and the tie is something like reasoning out the cause—aitias logismos, if I'm remembering the transliteration right.

My confusion is this. Meno's question was about value: why is knowledge better than a true belief that gets you to the same place? The Daedalus answer seems to be about stability—how long the belief stays put—not about whether knowledge gets you anywhere better while you have it. If a true belief and knowledge are equally good guides in the moment, the tie seems to buy only durability; the belief hasn't obviously become a better guide, just a stickier one. So is stability itself supposed to carry all the added value, or is the tie supposed to change what the belief is (and not just how long it lasts) in a way that matters for action?

I'd love an intuitive reading of how the two moves fit together, maybe with a small everyday example of your own construction where the tie does or doesnt make a practical difference—and, honestly, your take on wether the stability answer answers Meno's question or quietly changes the subject.
