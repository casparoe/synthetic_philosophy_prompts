I've been going down the accuracy-first epistemology rabbit hole lately — the stuff that tries to justify probabilism by treating credences as scored for accuracy, so the rationally permissible credences are the ones that aren't accuracy-dominated by any alternative. A friend and I got stuck on something more basic than the theorems: which scoring rule are we even meant to treat as the measure of epistemic disutility? Everyone reaches for the Brier score because it's tractable, but then the log score is the one with the fancy uniqueness properties, and the spherical score keeps appearing in the technical papers while nobody can give a snappy defence of what it's actually for. There's also the naive absolute-value score and the brutal 0/1 score that only cares whether the truth got more than half.

Could you rank those five candidates into tiers by how plausible they are as measures of epistemic disutility, with a sentence apiece saying why each lands where it does? In doing so, say which features — strict propriety, truth-directedness, differentiability, finiteness — are doing the real work in your verdict. If you had to make the strongest case for the log score over the Brier score, what would it be, and is there any philosophical or practical reason the Brier should still be the default for people working through accuracy arguments?
