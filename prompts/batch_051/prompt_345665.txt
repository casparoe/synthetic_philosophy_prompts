My moral psychology professor has spent all term on one question: when someone does wrong, do they know it's wrong? She has one passage from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (book VII, ch. 3, Rackham's translation) that she reads out whenever we do a case:

"Again, it is possible for men to 'have knowledge' in yet another way besides those just discussed; for even in the state of having knowledge without exercising it we can observe a distinction: a man may in a sense both have it and not have it; for instance, when he is asleep, or mad, or drunk. ... Their using the language of knowledge is no proof that they possess it. Persons in the states mentioned repeat propositions of geometry and verses of Empedocles; students who have just begun a subject reel off its formulae, though they do not yet know their meaning, for knowledge has to become part of the tissue of the mind, and this takes time. Hence we must conceive that men who fail in self-restraint talk in the same way as actors speaking a part."

Her verdicts so far this term: a student who could recite the definitions of "wrong" and pick the right answers on exams, but couldn't apply any of it to a new case — she said he didn't know. An actor playing Iago who says "what I do is wicked" but only in character — not knowledge of his own wrongness. A woman with dementia who still says "stealing is wrong" from memory but can't connect the sentence to the thing in her pocket — not knowledge. A drunk who can still say "driving now is wrong" and roughly why, but it's not getting through — she said he has the knowledge but it's asleep. A remorseful serial cheater who can say exactly why his affairs are wrong, feels guilty each time, and keeps doing it anyway — she said he knows.

Next week's case is a man diagnosed with psychopathy who passes the moral/conventional distinction test, gives agent-neutral reasons why lying and hurting people are wrong, and will say his own cons are wrong without being prompted. But he says the judgement that his own acts are wrong has always felt flat: no guilt, no remorse, and it never tugs on what he wants to do. Predict her verdict, say which of her earlier calls are doing the most work, lay out the rule or rules that fit those calls, what each says about the psychopath, and wich one you'd bet she's running and why. Then give me the single extra case that would cleanly seperate them.
