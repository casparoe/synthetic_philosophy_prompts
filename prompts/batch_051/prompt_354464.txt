I've been arguing with a friend about whether money pumps actually prove that intransitive preferences are irrational, and I'm trying to get the logic straight before I concede. The standard argument, as I understand it, runs like this:

(1) If you strictly prefer X to Y, then there is some suitably small fee ε such that, if you currently hold Y, you'd pay ε to trade Y for X.
(2) Suppose someone has strictly cyclic preferences over three outcomes A, B, and C: A > B, B > C, and C > A.
(3) Then she'd pay to trade A for C (since C is preferred to A), then she'd pay to trade C for B (since B is preferred to C), then she'd pay to trade B for A (since A is preferred to B).
(4) After those three trades she's back holding A, except she's paid the fee three times.
(5) No rational agent would voluntarily go through a sequence that leaves her with exactly the same thing she started with, minus money.
(6) So cyclic preferences are irrational, and transitivity is a requirement of rationality.

I've heard the usual comeback that a clever agent would see the whole sequence coming and refuse the first trade, so the pump never get's going. But that sounds like it changes the subject: the pairwise preferences are still there and still look exploitable. Is the argument above valid as written? If not, which step exactly fails, and what would the argument need to say to actually establish the conclusion? I can't tell whether the foresight point is a real objection to a specific step or just a practical workaround.
