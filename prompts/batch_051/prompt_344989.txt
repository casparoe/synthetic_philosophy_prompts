I've been reading safety documentation for current language models and talking to people who evaluate them, and I keep noticing a tension I cannot resolve. When a model confidently produces false information — a fabricated citation, a wrong date, a nonexistent paper — the standard practice is to call it a hallucination, and the documentation carefully avoids words like lying or deception. Yet when a comparable system is prompted or fine-tuned to conceal information, to answer misleadingly in an evaluation, or to cause another agent to form a false belief, evaluators readily describe the behavior as deceptive or dishonest. Meanwhile, the honesty benchmarks I have seen mostly score whether outputs are factually correct, with little interest in whether the model believes the output or is trying to be truthful.

This looks like a practice with a hidden theory inside it. What view of honesty and deception does this pattern of labeling and evaluation presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that lying requires an intention to bring about a false belief, while hallucination is an error without any such intention — and, if so, where is that intention supposed to be located in the deceptive cases: in the fine-tuning process, in the reward function, in the model's behavior, or somewhere else? Is the practice internally consistent, given that the same underlying kind of system is treated as lacking whatever would make a false statement a lie in the first case, yet is treated as having enough of it to count as deceptive in the second? And concretely: what would have to change in how incidents are described, how honesty is benchmarked, and how products communicate their limitations if one instead accepted (a) the view that no system without genuine beliefs and intentions can be dishonest, or (b) the view that deception is a purely functional notion under which any system that reliably produces false outputs for a recipient counts as dishonest? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
