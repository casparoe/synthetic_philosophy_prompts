After many years of writing novels, I have settled into a set of convictions about effort and achievement, and I would like an audit of whether they actually hold together. They are as follows.

(1) The value of a work of literary art, insofar as it has value at all, resides entirely in the finished piece as encountered by a reader. If two texts were word-for-word identical, they would be the same work and of equal value, even if one was dashed off in a fortnight and the other assembled over fifteen years of misery. The cost to the author is not part of the work.

(2) Nevertheless, I think a novel that exacted more from its author — more drafts, more structural reversals, more hours of unprofitable labour before a solution appeared — is, other things equal, the greater achievement. I do not mean merely that I admire it more; I mean that the author's feat is greater. I would defend this as a fact about achievement, not a private preference.

(3) I deny that effort is valuable for its own sake. Effort is a cost. Its only legitimate claims on our regard are instrumental or evidential: it tends to build skill, reveal what one cares about, and signal commitment, but the expenditure itself adds nothing to the balance sheet.

(4) Yet I admire — indeed I revere — the writer who keeps returning to an intractable manuscript, not primarily for the book that eventually results but for something in the returning itself: the daily renewal of resolve in the face of a problem that has not yielded. I would count a life spent in such returning as well spent even if the book never appears.

(5) I hold that it is a sentimental mistake to treat suffering for one's art as though it conferred value on the art. The pain of the work buys the writer nothing in the work itself; the wounds do not enter the sentence.

(6) In my own practice, I find I cannot fully credit a chapter that arrived easily. A passage that cost me nothing feels somehow unearned, however good it may be; I am only fully persuaded by my own pages when they have been fought for.

Do these six convictions compose a single position? I can half-see places where the joints might creak, but I cannot tell which are real and which are confusions of category. I am asking for an audit rather than a lecture: please set out which of these commitments pull against which, which apparent tensions dissolve under a distinction, and what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost, with the tensions ordered by severity. When you answer, please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. British English, please.
