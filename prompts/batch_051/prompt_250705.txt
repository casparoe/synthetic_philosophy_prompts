You're a professional philosopher who works on animal ethics and knows the wild-animal-suffering literature. I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends the view that, because wild insect lives are probably on balance more bad than good and insects are astronomically numerous, reducing insect populations with well-targeted insecticides is not just permissible but morally desirable — for the insects' own sake, not for crop protection or disease control. He's good at deflecting the uncertainty objection by saying that uncertainty is a reason to intervene, not to sit on our hands. Draft eight to ten interview questions that make that deflection progressively harder to use, and make sure to press at least the following: (1) whether poisoning insects just inflicts the acute suffering he wants to eliminate; (2) the downstream effects on insect-eating animals and ecosystems; (3) the naturalness or playing-God objection; and (4) the possibility that if we don't know whether insect lives are net good or net bad, the same expected-value reasoning he uses could just as well forbid intervening. For each question, include the follow-up you would ask after the answer he is most likely to give, so he can't just restate the view. No 'what drew you to animal ethics' fluff. Start by brainstorming his two or three strongest replies, then build questions around them.
