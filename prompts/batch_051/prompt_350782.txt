I'm a novelist, working on a book set forty or fifty years out in which there's a pill that improves working memory in healthy people, and a lot of the worldbuilding is how people argue about whether it's medicine or enhancement. At a dinner the other night two friends got into it, and I'd like a referee because I dont want my novel to settle the argument by fiat. It went roughly like this:

Lena said the line is obvious in principle: therapy restores normal functioning, enhancement goes beyond it. The pill for someone with a diagnosed memory disorder is medicine; the same pill for a healthy student cramming is enhancement.

Marcus said "normal functioning" is just a range on a bell curve, so it's not clear why landing at the average carries a moral justification that going past it lacks. Nobody treats the average as sacred anywhere else.

Lena said it's not about the average being sacred, it's about what a person in a given society is expected to be able to do. Therapy repairs a shortfall; enhancement buys an edge. The first is leveling a playing field, the second is tilting it.

Marcus said but the field tilts on its own. If a third of students take the pill, the baseline for "normal" performance shifts, and the kid who abstains becomes the one with the deficit. So the line just slides around.

Lena granted that the line is context-dependent, but said so is half of medicine: hypertension thresholds are partly convention, yet the category still tracks real harms. Context-dependent isn't the same as arbitrary.

Marcus said fine, but notice what the line is doing now—it's tracking impairment and disadvantage, not "normal versus beyond normal." A vaccine pushes the immune system well past what it does on its own and we call it medicine because it prevents harm, not because it restores a baseline. So "therapy versus enhancement" looks like a proxy for "reduces harm versus adds advantage."

Lena said if that's the real question, then a memory pill available to everyone might count as therapy, because it reduces a real harm—cognitive overload, ordinary forgetting. So the distinction doesn't forbid enhancement the way people think it does.

Marcus said that's exactly my point: once you cash it out that way, "enhancement" is just a word we paste onto interventions we're queasy about, and the real argument is a cost-benefit one about who gets the pill and who's left worse off.

Please go through this move by move and say who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches or changes the subject, and who is more right overall. Also, if the question is badly posed—say, the therapy/enhancement distinction was never stable enough to be doing this much work—just say that plainly instead of pretending there's a clean verdict.
