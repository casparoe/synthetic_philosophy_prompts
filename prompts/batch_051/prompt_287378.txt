I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I’m writing about how people invoke Chesterton’s fence in debates over institutional reform. As background, here is a passage from Edmund Burke’s Reflections on the Revolution in France, in which he cautions against confusing zeal for a revolutionary cause with the spirit that produced and sustains the British constitution:

Those who cultivate the memory of our Revolution and those who are attached to the constitution of this kingdom will take good care how they are involved with persons who, under the pretext of zeal toward the Revolution and constitution, too frequently wander from their true principles and are ready on every occasion to depart from the firm but cautious and deliberate spirit which produced the one, and which presides in the other.

Many writers treat this passage as an early expression of the Chesterton’s fence principle: don’t pull down a social arrangement until you understand the good it did. But I suspect Burke is saying something narrower and more demanding. Please argue that, on the most careful reading of this passage, Burke’s caution is not primarily about preserving an institution’s history or opacity, but about preserving the particular spirit—the firm but cautious and deliberate disposition—that gave rise to an institution and still makes it workable. Then spell out how this reading separates Burke’s conservatism from a generic Chesterton’s-fence rule: what kinds of reform would Burke permit that the fence rule would block, and what kinds of reform would he resist that the fence rule would not explain?
