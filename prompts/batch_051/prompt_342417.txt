[Question]

In this course we have discussed two claims that are often run together. The first is the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument for the existence of mathematical objects. The second is the claim that mathematics plays a genuinely explanatory, and not merely computational, role in natural science. Consider two examples.

(a) Periodical cicadas of the genus Magicicada have synchronized emergence cycles of either 13 or 17 years. A widely discussed explanation of these periods appeals to the fact that 13 and 17 are prime.
(b) Honeycomb cells approximate a regular hexagonal tiling of the plane. The honeycomb theorem states that, among all subdivisions of the plane into regions of equal area, the regular hexagonal tiling has the least total perimeter per unit area.

Some philosophers cite examples such as these to show that mathematics can explain empirical phenomena, and some go further and claim that such examples support belief in mathematical objects.

Write an essay with three parts.

(i) Reconstruct the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument, making clear which premise a critic must reject if she is to avoid the conclusion.
(ii) Distinguish the thesis that our best scientific theories are committed to mathematical objects from the thesis that mathematical facts explain empirical phenomena.
(iii) Evaluate whether examples (a) and (b) support the conclusion that we ought to believe in mathematical objects. If they support it, explain exactly how; if they do not, explain exactly what further premise would be required.

[Student answer]

(i) The Quine–Putnam argument has three premises. The first, the indispensability premise, says that our best scientific theories quantify over mathematical objects — numbers, functions, sets — because mathematics is indispensable for stating those theories; by Quine's criterion of ontological commitment, a theory is committed to whatever its bound variables must range over for the theory to be true. The second, naturalism, says we should settle what exists by looking at our best scientific theories, not by a priori first philosophy. The third, confirmational holism, says theories are confirmed as wholes, so whatever a theory quantifies over shares in the confirmation of the whole theory. The conclusion is that we are justified in believing in mathematical objects. A critic must reject one of these premises: the first by showing that science can be reformulated without quantifying over mathematics, the third by showing that confirmation does not reach the mathematical parts, or the second by defending some non-scientific route to mathematical knowledge.

(ii) The first thesis is about commitment: in the canonical, first-order formulation of our best theories, mathematical entities must be among the values of the variables for the theory to come out true. The second thesis is about explanation: mathematical facts are part of why empirical regularities hold, not merely a device for deriving predictions. These are different claims. A theory could quantify over numbers merely as computational instruments without giving them explanatory work, and mathematics could also be doing explanatory work even if one's preferred formulation of the background theory treats it instrumentally.

(iii) The examples do support the conclusion, though not as a proof. In the cicada case, the explanatory work is done by the fact that 13 and 17 are prime. The reason primeness matters is that the predators and parasites of cicadas themselves tend to have prime-numbered life cycles — 2, 3, 5, and 7 years. A non-prime emergence period such as 12 or 15 years would coincide with some predator cycle every few generations, whereas 13 and 17 do not. So a purely mathematical fact explains a biological trait, and that is exactly the kind of explanatory indispensability the argument needs. In the honeycomb case, the honeycomb theorem proves that hexagonal cells minimize the amount of wax needed for a given volume among all possible cell shapes, and this explains why natural selection made bees build hexagonal combs: a lineage that built square or triangular cells would waste wax and would have been outcompeted. In both cases, the best explanation of an empirical phenomenon invokes a mathematical theorem. Since we are justified in believing in the entities posited by our best explanations, and since these theorems quantify over numbers and over geometric objects such as regular hexagons, we are justified in believing in those mathematical objects. Therefore the examples support the indispensability argument.
