I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last week I sat in on an argument at a workshop on probability and epistemology that I still can't referee. The question was how a rational person should update their beliefs when the evidence is less than certain—like when a lab assistant emails that the test line was "faint," or a colleague tells you the fabric looked blue in bad light, but you can't be sure.

Sasha argued that Jeffrey conditionalization is the right tool. The idea: instead of conditioning on a proposition you now treat as certain, you take your new probabilities over some partition—say, [test positive, test negative]—and redistribute those new probabilities across everything else while holding your old conditional probabilities fixed. That way you revise your beliefs without pretending you learned something for sure. She called it the only formal rule that takes uncertain evidence seriously.

Marcus said that's a consistency check, not a rule of evidence. The new probabilities over the partition have to come from somewhere, and Jeffrey's rule is silent about how you get them from the faint readout. If you can set them to anything, he said, the rule will happily amplify whatever you put in.

Sasha replied that this isn't a bug: the rule was never meant to extract probabilities from raw experience, only to tell you how to update the rest of your web of belief once you've settled the partition probabilities.

Marcus then said there's a worse bug: Jeffrey updating is order-sensitive. Update on [positive, negative] after seeing the faint line, then update on [machine calibrated, machine drifted] after noticing a maintenance sticker, and you get different final probabilities than if you update in the reverse order. Genuine evidence, he argued, shouldn't depend on which order you happened to notice it in. His preferred fix was to model the faintness of the line itself as a random variable and condition on your model of how such lines are produced.

Sasha answered that this just pushes the modeling back a step: now you need a likelihood for faintness, which is itself a partisan prior, and anyway order effects might be a feature rather than a bug, since later evidence can legitimately change how you should have interpreted earlier evidence.

Marcus came back with: if order effects are a feature, then you owe us a rule for which order is the right one, and at that point your not doing Jeffrey conditionalization anymore.

I honestly couldn't tell who was right. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about which point, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and tell me who is more right overall.
