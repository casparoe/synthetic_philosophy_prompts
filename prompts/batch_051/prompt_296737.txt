I read the rationalist-adjacent machine-learning and evolutionary-theory blogs, and the forums attached to them such as LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, more than is probably good for me, and I have a homemade distinction I keep using. I would like it checked properly, not flattered.

I distinguish two properties an optimiser can have. Credit fidelity is how precisely it can attribute an observed outcome — a loss, a reward, a death — to the specific components that produced it. A backprop-trained network has high credit fidelity: one scalar loss on a minibatch yields an update vector for every parameter, via the chain rule. Natural selection has low credit fidelity: each organism contributes little more than a single aggregate scalar (its reproductive output), and selection cannot decompose that scalar into contributions from particular alleles or developmental steps. It has no chain rule through the phenotype.

Credit bandwidth is how much update-relevant information the optimiser extracts and applies per unit of evaluation effort or wall-clock time. Here the positions reverse. Natural selection runs an enormous number of parallel evaluations every generation, and each evaluation is just an organism living, so it spends evaluations on a scale laboratory optimisation never does. Stochastic gradient descent, by contrast, typically gets one modest update from a minibatch whose examples were expensive to obtain and label, and the gradient exists at all only because a differentiable model was built in advance.

My claim is that the stock blogospheric line that evolution is a stupid optimiser weighs evolution on the fidelity axis alone, and that this is slightly unfair. On the bandwidth axis stochastic gradient descent looks equally stupid: it substitutes analytic structure for evaluation. So the two are not points on a single scale of intelligence; they are two corners of a fidelity-bandwidth trade-off. One spends evaluations instead of analytic structure; the other spends analytic structure instead of evaluations. A corollary I find suggestive: evolutionary algorithms inside machine learning usually fail because they inherit selection's low fidelity while running on a machine and therefore lacking its bandwidth — the worst of both. End-to-end gradient descent keeps winning despite its well-known sample inefficiency because the tasks we set are engineered to reward fidelity.

I have been calling this the fidelity-bandwidth trade-off, and I keep reaching for it to explain why natural selection produces kludges and why gradient descent cannot simply inherit selection's tricks.
