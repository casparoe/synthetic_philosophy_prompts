I read a lot of philosophy through the rationalist blog scene, and I keep noticing that the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda gets flattened there into a one-liner about every view being partly right, which is exactly the sort of claim that audience is primed to dismiss as motte-and-bailey relativism. I suspect the actual machinery — syādvāda, the saptabhaṅgī, naya as a partial standpoint — is more interesting than that one-liner, and I want to write a post for a rationalist-adjacent group blog that explains it properly without letting it collapse into ordinary perspectivalism or into a contradiction-tolerating trivialism.

Don't write the explanation; plan how I should structure it, for readers who are technically trained and broadly read in analytic philosophy but have no background in Jain thought. Give me the order of ideas and the dependencies between them: what has to be in place before the sevenfold scheme of predication can be introduced without sounding arbitrary, and what should be delayed until after readers have absorbed the right example. Give me one carrying example, not necessarily the blind men and the elephant; if you use it, say what work it would have to do and what it obscures, and if you reject it, supply something that does the work better. Then map the misconceptions a rationalist reader is likely to form at each stage and say exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off and with what move; I expect at least these three: that the view is relativism about truth, that it is just ordinary context-dependence or indexicality, and that syāt amounts to mere epistemic modesty. Close with a single check for understanding — one question I can pose at the end of the post whose answer would distinguish a reader who has grasped conditional predication from one who has come away thinking all perspectives are valid. Finally, say what to leave out at this level and where to say I am skipping it.

Keep the whole plan under 1,000 words and present it as numbered stages, with the misconception warnings placed inline at the point in the sequence where they belong.
