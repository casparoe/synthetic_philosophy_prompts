ok so im a philosophy phd student working on the ethics of blame and legal philosophy, and I keep running into the same problem: everyone has strong intuitions about whether the passage of time should excuse, soften, or erase our responses to wrongdoing, but the arguments always use one case at a time and then jump to a whole view. I want to nail down the criteria before I pick a side.

What I'm after: the desiderata for a theory of when and why the time elapsed since a wrong matters morally. By "wrong" I mean an action or omission that violated some moral requirement, and by "matters" I mean it changes what the wrongdoer, the victim, or third parties should do, feel, or be liable for — criminal punishment, civil compensation, personal blame and resentment, apology, forgiveness, maybe repair for historical wrongs. So: what does such a theory have to get right before it counts as adequate?

Please give me:
- the cases it has to handle (or at least take a forced position on), with a sentence or two on why each is a requirement — e.g. the 20-year-old murder vs the 20-year-old fraud, the wrong only discovered years later, the offender who has genuinely changed vs the one who hasn't, the ongoing wrong vs the completed one, the friend who wronged you five years ago vs the state that wronged someone's ancestors two centuries ago;
- the distinctions it must not collapse — most obviously the difference between time making the evidence worse and time making the wrong itself matter less, but also punishment vs compensation vs resentment, wrongs the victim knew about at the time vs wrongs concealed from her, and time as mere duration vs time as narrative and change;
- the facts from psychology, law, or ordinary moral life it can't just deny: how memory and personal identity actually work, the fact that resentment often doesnt fade even when we think it should, the near-universality of limitation periods, whatever else you think binds;
- which of these requirements are negotiable and which are not;
- and then the kicker: which single requirement rules out the most currently popular positions.

Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them, not on scoring every theory against the list. A table is fine. I'm most interested in structural points: can a view that says time itself doesn't matter, only the decay of evidence and the need for closure, actually explain why we'd hesitate to punish a genuinely reformed 70-year-old for something done at 20? Can a view that says time should always count against blame explain why a factory that hid its pollution for forty years shouldn't be off the hook just because the harm is old? If some desiderata end up in tension, say so rather than quietly dropping one. And to make the target concrete: I'd like the theory to be usable both for thinking about sentencing policy and for thinking about whether a friend who wronged you years ago still owes you an explanation.
