I never did any philosophy at uni, but I've ended up trying to work out a rule for when someone gets to say "this is mine". The rule I keep coming back to goes roughly: if you take something from the common stock that nobody owns, and you put your own work into it, it becomes yours, as long as you don't leave other people short of the same sort of thing. I later found out this is pretty much Locke's story about how property starts, so I've been trying to test it against cases.

The case I cant make fit is this. A mate of mine spent two years downloading out-of-copyright scans from museum and library websites: old maps, parish records, botanical prints. He didn't take the photos himself, and he didn't write the program that grabs them; he got the program free and pointed it at the sites. The program cleans the scans, straightens the pages, tags them. He did have to choose which collections to go after, and he fixed some of the tags when they came out wrong, but the real grind was the machine's, not his. The originals are all still up on the same websites, free for anyone, so in one sense he's taken nothing from anyone. What comes out the other end is a single searchable database he now wants to charge people to use.

So run the database through the two-part rule. Does he end up owning it? Is "putting your own work into it" the right description of starting a program and dozing while it runs, and does the "don't leave others short" condition even bite when the common stock is never used up? Or does the case show the rule was built for acorns and fields and goes wrong with digital things? I'm less interested in what copyright law would say than in what the rule itself has to say to stay coherent.
