I am a retired physicist, and I have recently begun working through arguments in the philosophy of emotion. I want to determine whether an ordinary human life containing grief, sadness, and the other negative emotions is better or worse than a life from which those emotions have been removed. The following argument strikes me as plausible, but its conclusion is stronger than I can accept, and I cannot locate the exact point at which it overreaches.

(1) Take any single episode of grief or sadness in a person's life. A counterfactual life exactly like the actual one except that this one episode is absent would be better than the actual life: one intrinsically unpleasant state is removed, and by hypothesis nothing else differs.
(2) So each such episode is one whose removal, taken on its own, would improve the life.
(3) It seems to follow that removing all such episodes at once would improve the life as well—indeed, more.
(4) Therefore, a life from which every episode of grief and sadness has been removed is better than the life that contains them.
(5) Hence, if a safe intervention could remove every such episode, a person should undergo it; a life entirely without negative emotions is, all else equal, better than one with them.

Does the argument go through? If not, which numbered step first fails, and what is the strongest corrected version of that step that remains true? Would the argument be repaired by deleting the episodes sequentially—one at a time, each deletion improving whichever life one then has—given that being better than is transitive?
