I'm a backend engineer, and a friend and I got into an argument about whether the routing layer in our deploy pipeline "chooses" anything, and somehow Buridan's ass (the old example of a donkey between two identical bales of hay, with no reason to pick either, starving) came up. I don't have a philosophy background, so let me lay out the criteria I've seen people use for choice-without-preference and then give you the case that's bugging me.

As I understand it, a Buridan-style choice without preference means all of: (1) there are two or more options genuinely open to the chooser; (2) the chooser is indifferent between them — it has no preference, and no reason that actually matters to it, for picking one over the others; (3) exactly one option gets selected anyway; (4) the selection is attributable to the chooser's own action or decision procedure, not to an outside shove; (5) the selection isn't explained by a preference among the options themselves. I'm genuinely unsure whether determinism should be a sixth criterion — if you rewound the world with no changes, maybe a real choice would have to come out different, maybe not — so treat that as an open question if it matters.

The borderline case is our request router. When several backend replicas are equally healthy, the router picks the one with the lowest 64-bit instance ID. The IDs were assigned essentially at random; no engineer knows which ID corresponds to which replica, and nobody cares whether 0x1a2b43c9... gets the request instead of 0x1a2b44d0... The router has no model of the alternatives, no goals, and no preferences. It just runs a total-order comparison and returns one instance, and if you replay the exact same request through the exact same system, it picks the same instance every time. The engineer who wrote the tie-break didn't pick that instance either; they picked a rule, and the rule picks the instance. So we seem to hit the surface conditions (multiple equal options, no preference, exactly one selected), but (4) is weird because the outcome traces back to the engineer's policy, and an agency criterion would be weird too, because the router isn't much like the ass but it does have a decision procedure. If you think the router just isn't the right kind of thing to be a "chooser" at all, say so, but then I want to know whether that's doing hidden work and whether treating the engineer-plus-router as the chooser changes the verdict.

Work through the criteria one by one, say where they underdetermine the answer, and then tell me whether the router's selection qualifies as a choice without preference in the Buridan sense. If the case mostly shows the criteria need sharpening, say what you'd change and whether the sharpened criteria include or exclude the router. End with a machine-readable verdict as the last line: \answer{qualifies} or \answer{does-not-qualify} or \answer{underdetermined}, and make the second-to-last line a one-sentence summary.
