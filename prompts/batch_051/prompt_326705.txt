I'm a philosophy PhD student and my dissertation has drifted into pain–pleasure tradeoffs, which means I now spend seminar time arguing about whether enough people enjoying a mild pleasant afternoon can outweigh one person's hour-long cluster headache. People in my axiology reading group keep talking past each other: one camp says the worst pains are lexically prior to all pleasures below a threshold, another says that's just violating the Archimedean condition with extra steps, and a third keeps using 'commensurable' to mean cardinally comparable, continuous, or just that people can in practice make a trade. Before I commit to a side, I want to see the full specification of what a theory of how pleasure and pain trade off has to actually get right.

So here's what I'm after: a requirements list. The cases it can't get wrong (intense vs mild pain, duration vs intensity, one person's agony vs a huge number of people's mild pleasures, intrapersonal vs interpersonal tradeoffs, past vs future suffering), the distinctions it can't collapse (commensurability vs continuity, lexical priority vs a threshold vs steep weighting, making a trade vs suppressing or ignoring pain), and the formal constraints it either respects or explicitly rejects (transitivity, completeness, the Archimedean condition, separability across persons or times). For each requirement, say what case or principle puts it on the list, and flag which ones are negotiable modeling conveniences rather than fixed points. Then briefly score the obvious contenders — standard additive welfare, lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and prioritarianism if it's even playing the same game — against the list, and say which single requirement does the most work excluding whichever contender can't meet it. I care more about what the list itself commits me too than about who wins. A table for the scoring would be great.
