I'm a TA for an early modern philosophy survey and I'm grading a short-answer question on Leibniz's moral philosophy. The question on the problem set was:

Explain how Leibniz understands the relation between morality and God's will, and how this contrasts with theological voluntarism. Also explain his account of justice as resting on charity of the wise, and what the element of wisdom is supposed to add.

One student wrote:

Leibniz is an intellectualist about the foundations of morality: against voluntarists like Pufendorf (and arguably Descartes), he holds that God commands the good because he understands it to be good, not that it is good because God commands it. That is why Leibniz puts moral truths among the truths of reason, alongside logic and mathematics, rather than among truths of fact. There is a wrinkle, though. Since God's choice of which possible world to actualize is free, and since our concrete duties concern us as inhabitants of this chosen world, the particular moral truths that bind us are contingent truths of fact; only the most abstract formal principles are necessary. Leibniz's idea of justice as charity of the wise is meant to unify love and knowledge. Charity is love of another, which he analyzes as taking pleasure in that person's happiness. The wisdom component keeps charity from being blind: it lets us see that promoting the happiness of others is the most reliable route to our own enduring happiness. So charity of the wise ultimately rests on enlightened self-interest, and Leibniz ends up close to an early utilitarian.
