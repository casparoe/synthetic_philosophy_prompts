Here's the idea for a short essay I'm drafting; I'd like comments mostly on whether the argument is defensible, where it's weakest, and whether it's saying anything new. I'm not looking for reading suggestions, just engagement with the reasoning.

The topic is disjunctivism and the argument from illusion. My working claim is that the argument from illusion gets a lot of its force from slipping between two senses of "indistinguishable." In one sense, a hallucination and a veridical perception are indistinguishable just because the subject can't tell them apart from the inside; that's a negative epistemic fact about the subject's powers of discrimination, not a shared ingredient in the two states. In the other sense, "indistinguishable" is used to establish that there is a common experiential kind present in both cases, something with the same qualitative character, which then gets treated as a sense-datum or at least a state that doesn't essentially involve the external object. The argument seems to run these together: it moves from "the subject can't tell the difference" to "there is no difference in kind to tell."

The constructive part of the essay would be a disjunctivist handling of this. I want to say a hallucination can be introspectively indistinguishable from a perception without there being any common factor that explains the indistinguishability, no "what it's like" component that is literally the same across the two. I was thinking of it by analogy with two differently shaped keys that both open the same lock: the lock can't tell them apart, but that doesn't mean they share a shape. Then I'd argue that if this deflationary reading works, the argument from illusion stalls before it ever gets to sense-data.

Specific things I'm unsure about: (1) Is this just relabeled epistemic disjunctivism, and if so is there anything original I can still say? (2) Does the negative account of indistinguishability collapse into a kind of verificationism about experience once you press on what "can't tell apart" means? (3) Am I being unfair to the argument from illusion; can the standard version run on bare subjective indistinguishability alone, without assuming a common factor? (4) Is the key analogy misleading because the two keys don't have anything like the kind of inner similarity a perception and a matching hallucination are supposed to have?

Anyway, comments on accuracy, on the most damaging objection, and on whether the central distinction is actually doing work would be appreciated. Please write in American English.
