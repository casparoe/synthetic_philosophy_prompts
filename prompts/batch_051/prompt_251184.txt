A colleague in a decision-theory reading group has been defending the following argument, and I cannot find the exit. It is supposed to show that expected utility theory is either incomplete or incoherent when it comes to how much of one's bankroll to stake on a favorable bet.

Setup. A coin lands heads with probability p > 1/2. You may stake any fraction f of your current wealth W, with 0 ≤ f ≤ 1. If heads, your wealth becomes W(1+f); if tails, it becomes W(1−f). The gamble is offered repeatedly with independent flips, and you may choose f anew each round.

The argument:

(1) Rational choice consists in maximizing expected utility: option A is at least as rational as option B just in case A's expected utility is no lower than B's for the agent's utility function u. The axioms of expected utility theory constrain the coherence of the agent's preferences but impose no particular shape on u; linear, logarithmic, and power utilities are all admissible.

(2) For a single flip, an expected-wealth maximizer, u(w) = w, stakes everything. The expected multiplier p(1+f) + (1−p)(1−f) = 1 + f(2p−1) is strictly increasing in f, so f = 1 is optimal.

(3) In the repeated version, f = 1 is ruinous: a single tails destroys the bankroll, and with independent flips a tails occurs with probability one eventually. A theory that cannot fault this strategy is missing something.

(4) The standard repair is the Kelly criterion. Among fixed-fraction strategies, the one maximizing the almost-sure long-run growth rate is the one that each round maximizes expected log wealth; it stakes f = 2p−1, which is 0.2 for p = 0.6.

(5) So rationality, at least for repeated favorable bets, seems to require logarithmic utility. The Kelly stake is the one a rational agent should choose, and it is determinate. My colleague calls this the core of geometric rationality: for repeated decisions, the geometric mean, not the arithmetic mean, is the correct currency.

(6) But log is only one concave utility among many. By (1), an agent with power utility u(w) = w^α/α (α ≠ 0) is equally rational, and their optimal stake differs: α = 1 stakes everything; 0 < α < 1 stakes more than Kelly; α < 0 stakes less.

(7) Therefore the stake a rational agent chooses depends on which arbitrary utility function we attribute to them. There is no unique rational stake.

(8) But that conclusion is absurd. A theory of rational choice ought to be able to say that, for an agent whose aim is to grow wealth over many bets and avoid ruin, staking 80% on a repeated 60/40 coin is worse than staking 20%.

(9) So either expected utility theory is defective, or the move that promotes log from a growth-rate maximizer to a norm of rationality is illicit, or the growth-rate theorem is being asked to do work it cannot do.

He concludes that expected utility theory is either incomplete, because it cannot tell you how much to bet, or incoherent, because it simultaneously requires log via the growth argument and does not require log via the permissiveness of utility functions. I find that conclusion too strong, but I cannot locate the slip. Which premise or step would you reject, why did it look acceptable, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what remains of geometric rationality. For what it is worth, I can verify (4) directly, and I would be unhappy to learn that the whole expected-utility framework has to go; but I want to know what I am actually paying if I try to hold on to both.
