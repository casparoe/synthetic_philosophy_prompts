I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether God's foreknowledge is compatable with free choice. One philosopher I interviewed kept reaching for the same analogy: God is like a hiker on a ridge who can see the whole railway line at once, while we are on the train and can only make out the next stretch of track as it comes into view. His claim was that the hiker's seeing the whole line doesn't make the train go where it goes; likewise, God's seeing your whole life at once doesn't reach into the world and force your decisions, so divine foreknowledge and free will are perfectly fine together.

I'd assumed this was just a popular version of Boethius's answer in the Consolation of Philosophy: God is outside time and has all of history present to him at once, so he doesn't foresee your choices the way a meteorologist foresees rain. But the ridge analogy is spatial, and I have a nagging sense it quietly turns time into a static block of track that already exists in both directions, which is a bigger metaphysical commitment than the free will point needs. Also, a hiker on a ridge is still in time when she looks — she just has a better vantage point — whereas Boethius's God is supposed to be eternal in a way that isn't just being around a very long time or having great foresight.

For my piece, I want to actually stress-test the analogy rather than quote it. Lay out what corresponds to what: the track, the train, the hiker, the act of seeing, maybe the direction of travel. Then seperate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion — that God's seeing doesn't coerce us — from the ones that are just decorative. Does the not-coercive point survive once those disanalogies are on the table, and does what's left look like Boethius's eternal present, or like a merely super-forecasting God? If the analogy is importing hidden commitments about time or freedom, name them. And if there is a better analogy that avoids the problems, say what it is and why it does better.
