I'm on the board of a tiny literary foundation—call it the Harrow Fellowship—and I need to send the other four directors a written opinion before our emergency call Tuesday. I'm a novelist by trade, not a lawyer, and I'm mid-draft on a book about a port city whose charter expires at midnight and nobody notices until the harbor pilot asks who can pay him, so I keep privately calling our situation a miniature interregnum: the rules have run out and it's not clear anyone is actually entitled to sit in the chair.

The mess, concretely. The bylaws say the board has six seats, and they require a majority of the entire board—four votes—both to elect a president and to authorize any disbursement over $10,000. The president resigned his seat in June during a fight over the fellowship criteria—three directors want to keep the award for debut literary fiction, two want to broaden it to graphic memoir and narrative nonfiction because a matching gift is on the table. That left five of us. The vice president now leads the three-vote camp and claims the bylaws make her president automatically whenever the office falls vacant; the other camp says the succession language only lets her preside at meetings in the president's absence, not hold the office. We voted three to two to recognize her as president, but the other side says that vote was meaningless because filling the office takes four votes, not three. Shes been acting as president anyway, and the camps have calcified: the two-vote side won't supply the fourth vote on anything until the presidency question is settled, while the three-vote side says there's nothing to settle because the VP is already president.

The reason this can't wait: the annual fellowship winner was selected in April, but their $40,000 check has to go out by the last day of next month or the money snaps back to the founder's estate, and the executor has made clear the estate will redeploy it. The bank won't release the funds without a board resolution signed by the president and one other officer. So the winner may lose the fellowship and the foundation may breach its award letter, all because we can't establish who's allowed to sign. The amendment procedure is blocked by the same four-vote rule, and we can't seat a new board until the annual meeting five months away. I don't need the nonprofit-law answer—our attorney is already arguing about that—I need the legitimacy argument underneath it: if the rules for making rules have run out, is anyone actually entitled to govern, or is every move from here just self-dealing with nicer words?

Please draft the short memo I'd circulate before the call. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a specific timeline, not a meditation on sovereignty. It should say who, if anyone, gets to act in the meantime, where their authority comes from, and how far it extends. It should engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side—especially the claim that recognizing the vice president, even on shaky grounds, is the most continuous with the founder's intent and does the least violence to the existing structure. And at the end, as a note to me rather than part of the memo, tell me which argument or consideration would be most likely to change your recommendation, or where you think your reasoning is most apt to fail.
