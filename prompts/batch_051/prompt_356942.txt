Here is an argument from a reading group that seems to prove too much, and I cannot determine which step is responsible.

Call a condition luminous just in case, necessarily, if a subject is in that condition, the subject is in a position to know that they are in it. The anti-luminosity argument concludes that no non-trivial condition is luminous; in outline, it runs as follows. Consider a condition C that admits a gradual series of cases from clear C to clear not-C, where adjacent cases are too close for the subject to discriminate reliably. If C were luminous, then in every C-case the subject would be in a position to know that they are in C. But near the boundary there must be a C-case that is indiscriminable from a nearby not-C case, and in such a case the subject is not in a position to know that they are in C. Therefore C is not luminous.

Now take C to be the condition of one's total evidence making p rational to believe.

(1) If one's total evidence makes p rational to believe, then one can rationally believe p.
(2) One can rationally believe p only if one is in a position to know that one's total evidence makes p rational to believe; a belief whose rational status was in principle inaccessible to its holder would not be one she could properly hold.
(3) The condition of one's total evidence making p rational to believe is non-trivial: there can be a gradual slide from plainly sufficient evidence to plainly insufficient evidence, with no sharp cutoff.
(4) So, by the anti-luminosity argument, there is a possible case in which one's total evidence makes p rational to believe, yet one is not in a position to know that it does.
(5) In that case, by (2), one cannot rationally believe p.
(6) But in that same case, by (1), one can rationally believe p. Contradiction.

The ways out seem to be: give up (1), and with it the claim that rational belief follows from rationally adequate evidence; give up (2), and allow rational beliefs that are in principle opaque to their holders; give up (3), and deny that evidential support shades off gradually; or reject the anti-luminosity argument itself. I would most like to retain the anti-luminosity argument and (1), but I suspect that gravely understates what giving up (2) costs.

Where does the argument go wrong? If a step is invalid, identify the transition and explain why it looked fine; if the argument is valid, say which premise is least costly to abandon and why.
