I have been reading around the philosophy of language on my own, and I keep building a version of an argument that seems to end in a place no one should be able to accept, but I cannot locate the exact step that goes wrong. Let S be a slur for some group and N the neutral term for the same group. (Think of a derogatory label for a nationality and the ordinary nationality word; nothing turns on which pair you pick.)

(1) S and N have the same extension: whoever is N is S, and whoever is S is N.
(2) The truth or falsity of a simple sentence such as "X is S" depends only on what the words in it pick out and how they are combined.
(3) So replacing S with N in a simple predication cannot change the sentence's truth value or its truth conditions.
(4) So "X is S" and "X is N" say the same thing, and to assert the one is to assert the other.
(5) But to assert "X is S" in one's own voice is insulting or wrong in a way that asserting "X is N" is not.
(6) If two sentences say the same thing, then any difference in the wrongness of asserting them must come entirely from outside what the sentences say — from social convention, taboo, the speaker's intentions, or the predictable effect on listeners.
(7) The wrongness of S does not seem to be like that. A speaker can use S in a flat, descriptive tone, meaning no harm and expecting no offense, and still say something objectionable; the word itself seems to carry the objection, and not as a merely arbitrary taboo, since its force is tied to the group it marks out.
(8) So the difference must be something the two sentences themselves say. But that contradicts (4).
(9) Since (3) and (4) flowed from (1) and (2), at least one of (1) and (2) must be false, and the natural candidate is (2): a slur must have an extra derogatory ingredient in its truth conditions.
(10) But if the derogatory ingredient belongs to truth conditions, then "X is S" asserts that X is bad or contemptible simply in virtue of belonging to the group. Applied to an ordinary member of the group, that sentence would then be false. Yet the standard reaction to a slur is not that the sentence is false; it is that the speaker should not talk that way. And if the sentence were simply false, the wrongness of uttering it would be the wrongness of asserting a falsehood, which seems like the wrong kind of wrong.
(11) So S must differ from N in what the sentences say, but it cannot differ from N in truth conditions. That looks like a contradiction, and if the argument were sound it would leave no account of slurs standing.

Where does this argument actually go wrong, and what does rejecting the relevant step commit one to? I would like to keep two commitments if they can be kept: first, that the wrongness of slurs is not an arbitrary taboo or a private intention (something like (7)); second, that switching S for N is not morally interchangeable. But I suspect those commitments are part of what is driving the contradiction.

As you answer, please mark which parts of this area are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on slurs, and do not present a contested account as though it were the consensus.
