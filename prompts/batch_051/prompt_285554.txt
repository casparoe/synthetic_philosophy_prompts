Two people in my reading group have been going in circles for months about the Church-Turing thesis, and they've finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration. One of them treats the thesis as an empirical claim about what any physical process could compute, so a reliable physical device that solved the halting problem would genuinely refute it. The other treats it as an explication: it sharpens the vague informal notion of an effective procedure into the precise notion of Turing computability, so no weird device could refute it — we'd just say the device doesn't count as an effective procedure, or maybe not as computation in the relevant sense. They've asked me to draft the document. Write the joint statement they should both be able to sign. Include: a numbered list of claims both sides accept (including how they handle the fact that Turing machines, lambda calculus, and general recursive functions all pick out the same class of functions); a table of precise disagreements, with each side's position worded the way that side would word it; the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them; and for each crux, a concrete case or argument both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Don't let it collapse into 'one says empirical, one says definitional.' Push on what would count as a falsifier, what the word 'computation' has to include, wether idealizations like unbounded time and error-free operation matter, and whether the formal equivalence results already settle anything. They both agree the math is settled; the statement should make clear what, if anything, is still open.
