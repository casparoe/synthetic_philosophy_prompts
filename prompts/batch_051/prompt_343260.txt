For the past year I have attended a reading group on animal ethics. I am a retired natural scientist — a chemist by training — who has come to philosophy rather late, and I have found that my empirical background is of only limited help in settling a dispute that two other members conducted at our last meeting and which the rest of us could not resolve.

One participant, whom I shall call M, put forward what she called the scale argument. Even if one assigns a low probability to insect sentience — she suggested, for the sake of argument, something between five and thirty percent — the sheer number of individual insects is so great that the expected number of sentient beings affected by our choices dwarfs the corresponding number of vertebrates. For that reason, she argued, insects cannot be left out of our moral accounting; the expected welfare stakes are simply too large.

Her interlocutor, D, replied that this is a version of Pascal's mugging. If vast numbers may be multiplied by small probabilities to generate moral reasons, then any sufficiently tiny probability attached to an even larger population — in a simulation, in the far future, or in some other speculative scenario — would in turn swamp the insect case. Expected-value reasoning of this form has no principled stopping point, he said, and therefore cannot guide priorities.

M answered that the analogy was unfair. The probability assigned to insect sentience is not an arbitrary or infinitesimal magnitude; it is anchored in observable capacities — nociceptors or their analogues, avoidance learning, behavioral trade-offs that look like pain. A modest credence grounded in that kind of evidence is entirely different, she said, from the bare logical possibilities on which Pascal's mugging trades.

D responded that this merely relocates the problem. The same style of reasoning, he said, gives respectable nonzero credence to the sentience of even simpler organisms and perhaps of future artificial systems, and once such credences are admitted, expected-value reasoning requires one to rank these possibilities by expected value rather than by evidence alone. Drawing the line at insects would then express a psychological comfort zone rather than a principle.

M then made a different point. She conceded that expected value is not the whole of practical reasoning: one must also consider tractability. In the case of wild insects, she noted, we may not know whether an intervention that reduces suffering in one place increases it elsewhere. The scale argument therefore does not by itself settle what to do. But she insisted that it does settle a more limited question, namely that it is a mistake to dismiss insect welfare as negligible merely because individual insects are small and short-lived.

D replied that even this limited conclusion depends on the expected-value framework he has been challenging. If one declines to let tiny probabilities of astronomically large populations dominate one's deliberations, then the scale argument shows at most that dismissing insects would be a mistake within that framework, not that the framework itself should be accepted.

At that point the discussion broke up, and I honestly could not say who had carried which points.

Please adjudicate the dispute step by step. For each claim, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and mention anything important that both participants are missing.
