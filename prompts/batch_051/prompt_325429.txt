A country facing sustained automation-driven job loss is considering a reserved-labor scheme in place of a basic income. Under it, any firm may declare one of its job categories reserved: the state then pays the workers' wages at a rate equal to the estimated cost of automating the task, the firm is barred from automating that category, and it keeps whatever the workers produce. Unemployed people may separately petition to have an occupation reserved as state-subsidized public-utility work, with the output sold at market price and the state absorbing the loss. Walk through how this scheme would actually behave: which firms and workers would seek which designations and why; whether jobs known to be redundant could still deliver the meaning, status, and daily structure that advocates say work provides; what second-order effects would follow for wages in unreserved jobs, prices, and the automation industry; where the scheme would be gamed first; and whether those failure modes can be fixed without collapsing it into make-work or back-door UBI.
