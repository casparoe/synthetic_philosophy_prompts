I am chairing a recorded on-stage conversation at a philosophy colloquium whose guest is a philosopher who has defended skeptical theism as a response to evidential arguments from evil. The guest is articulate but tends to retreat to the claim that human beings are not in a position to see God's reasons, so the moderator's task is to force him to state and defend that principle rather than use it as a conversation-stopper. Compose between eight and ten prepared questions, each paired with a follow-up aimed at the reply he is most likely to give. At least one question should address each of the following: (i) whether the move from an inability to find a God-justifying reason for a particular apparently gratuitous evil to the conclusion that probably no such reason exists is ever legitimate, and how the skeptical theist distinguishes this noseeum inference from ordinary cases in which a failure to detect something is strong evidence of its absence; (ii) the charge that skeptical theism overgeneralizes, because the same limitation on discerning God's reasons seems to undermine our ability to determine that any action is all-things-considered morally wrong, thereby pushing the view toward moral skepticism; (iii) the difference between skeptical theism and a bare refusal to let evil count as evidence against God, including what would count as evidence against the view or as a misuse of it to protect theism from falsification; (iv) whether being unable to discern particular goods behind evil is compatible with trusting God's goodness and honesty, or whether the skepticism extends to God's character in a way that leaves the theist without a basis for expecting God not to permit evil without adequate reason; and (v) whether the principle can be limited to horrendous or apparently gratuitous evils without also applying to ordinary moral reasoning about the likely consequences of actions. The follow-ups should press the standard replies rather than reward them: the distinction between seeing no reason and seeing that no reason exists, the claim that divine transcendence blocks knowledge only of particular justifying goods rather than of God's general reliability, and the claim that the view is a chastened epistemic posture rather than an unfalsifiable dogma. Keep the questions precise and adversarial but fair; do not ask for bibliographic information, textual citations, or biographical material.
