I keep noticing a structural resemblance between deterministic chaos and the problem of resultant moral luck, and I cannot tell whether it is a real identity of structure or just a shared metaphor. In deterministic chaos a system is fully deterministic — its state at one moment fixes its state at every later moment — yet it can be practically unpredictable, because arbitrarily small differences in the initial state are amplified exponentially. Two nearly identical starting points eventually lie on wildly different trajectories, so the same underlying law, plus a perturbation too small to register, settles which of two very different futures occurs. The problem of resultant moral luck seems to have the same shape. Two drivers may act with the same carelessness, but a pedestrian steps into the road in one case and not in the other; one driver becomes, in the law's eyes and in his own, someone who killed, while the other is merely careless. The conduct and its causes are held fixed, and a small, uncontrolled difference in circumstances determines the outcome that blame and punishment track.

The question is whether anything follows. One view is that chaos theory merely gives mathematical clothing to the old idea that small causes can have large effects, and moral luck never needed that idea. Another view is that chaos theory shows the amplification is not a fluke but a generic feature of the world's dynamics, which would make resultant moral luck less like a correctable quirk of our practices and more like a structural condition that any system of blame has to accommodate. Which interpretation is honest? State the mapping between the two cases as precisely as possible, say what would have to be true of both sides for a result to transfer from the dynamics to the ethics, and identify at least one thing the parallel would predict about responsibility or blame that seems false or unsupported. In particular, does the determinism/predictability distinction that earns its keep in chaos do any real work in ethics, or does the analogy fail at the load-bearing point because blame is about control rather than prediction?
