I'm revising a novel, and there's a scene I keep circling. One of my characters—an older composer, a bit full of himself—defends a position about musical sadness. His line is that music is only ever called sad because of what it does to listeners: sad-sounding music tends to make us feel sad (or something neighboring, like mournful or heavy), and we project that back onto the notes the way we call a moor gloomy. On his telling, the sounds themselves have no emotional properties at all; a piece could no more be sad than a chord could be envious. I want a second character to challenge him seriously, not with cheap shots, and I want to know where this view is actually vulnerable. Treat the composer's view as a live thesis. What would have to be true for it to be false? Give me the observations, arguments, intuitions, and perhaps thought experiments that should move someone who really holds it, sorted from the ones that merely force a tweak to the ones that would sink it. Say too what this view predicts about listeners that rival views dont predict. Then be honest: once the view is padded with all the usual hedges (its a projection, a secondary quality, a harmless façon de parler, whatever), has it been insulated so thoroughly that nothing could count against it anymore—and if it has, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just the normal shape of a claim about the emotions? I'd personally find cases where we judge music sad while feeling nothing like sadness, or feeling something entirely different, the most interesting pressure, but don't let that limit you.
