In my epistemology module, two of my peers got into a drawn-out argument about whether transcendental arguments can answer global scepticism. They asked me to referee, but I could not, and I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

First, Mina argued that the external-world sceptic is self-undermining: the very act of raising the doubt, as a piece of self-conscious thought, presupposes the capacity to represent an objective world, so the sceptic must rely on the very faculty whose deliverances she calls into question.

Theo replied that this shows at most that belief in an external world is inescapable for us, not that it is true. The fact that we cannot give up a belief does not make the belief correct; a compulsion is not a justification.

Mina answered that this distinction between how we must think and how things are is itself part of the sceptic's question-begging standpoint. A doubt that cannot be formulated without presupposing what it denies is not a genuine doubt but at best a gesture, and a transcendental argument refutes it precisely by showing that no contentful alternative can be stated.

Theo replied that Mina was now smuggling in an idealist premise. To move from "all self-conscious thought requires the category of an objective world" to "there is an objective world", one must assume that the conditions of possible thought are conditions of reality. Without that assumption, the sceptic can grant the entire story about our cognitive constitution and still ask whether it tracks anything; with that assumption, one has not refuted scepticism but redefined reality in a way the sceptic never denied.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere between, and briefly justify your rating. Then say which of the two overall positions emerges in the stronger position once the moves are separated, and identify anything important both parties are missing. In doing so, please separate what is settled among specialists from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. I would also like the answer in British English.
