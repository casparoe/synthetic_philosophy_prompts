I've been reading about futarchy—the proposal that instead of voting directly on policies, you have everyone vote on a welfare metric, and then prediction markets on the metric under each proposed policy decide which policy gets enacted. I wrote out an argument that it's basically watertight, and now I want someone to go through it properly, because the conclusion feels stronger than the premises allow for.

(1) In a well-functioning prediction market, the price is the best available estimate of whatever the contract pays out on.

(2) Futarchy creates a separate market for each policy, where the contract pays out according to the welfare metric conditional on that policy being enacted.

(3) So for any policy P, the price in P's market is the best available estimate of welfare, given that P is enacted.

(4) Therefore, if policy A's price is higher than policy B's, the best available estimate says welfare will be higher if A is enacted than if B is enacted.

(5) A policymaker should choose whichever act the best available estimate says will produce the most welfare.

(6) So futarchy's rule—enact whichever policy has the highest conditional market price—selects the policy that, by the best available evidence, is best.

I can't tell whether the dodgy step is (2) (unrealistically assuming a welfare metric that can't be gamed or manipulated), or (4) (sliding from "conditional on being enacted" to "caused by being enacted"), or whether the argument is actually valid and the conclusion is just worded too strongly. Also, does anything change if the markets are what some people call decision markets, where the price is itself part of the mechanism that determines which policy gets enacted, rather than a prediction of something that happens independently? Go through step by step: is each premise true, do the inferences go through, and if a step fails, which one exactly and on what reading?
