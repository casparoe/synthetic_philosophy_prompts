I can't stop gnawing on a question that came up after a friend told me, half-joking, that my principled objections to various egalitarian policies suspiciously line up with what would cost me money. That pushed me into reading about adaptive preferences, the idea that people in constrained circumstances adjust what they report wanting to the options they actually have, like the fox deciding the grapes were sour. The standing puzzle is how you could ever tell an adaptive preference from a genuine one, or more generally a bias from a real value, without importing your own idea of what the person should want. And then it struck me that this is structurally the same problem revealed preference theory has in economics. There you read people's values off their choices, but nothing in the choice data tells you whether a given choice expresses a real preference or a bias or a mistake, except consistency axioms, and those are silent on welfare. The mapping as I see it is roughly this: choices stand to revealed preferences as self-reports stand to real values, and the welfare criterion economists end up importing stands to the moral philosopher's theory of the good. Both fields are trying to recover a genuine self from a record of choices and reports, and both seem to need a normative standard that the data itself cant supply. I can't decide whether this is a deep point about the underdetermination of interpretation, the kind of thing that might connect to how any bunch of dispositions can be interpreted many ways, or whether I'm just pattern-matching on the shared word preference. If the parallel is deep, what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer between economics and moral psychology, and can you name a concrete case where the analogy predicts something false? I'd like your honest take, in continuous prose only please, no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables, written like an email.
