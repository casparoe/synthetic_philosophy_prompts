I'm a software engineer, so I'm used to thinking in worst-case terms: tail latency, what happens if the database goes down, that sort of thing. Over the years I've started carrying that over to my own life. When I can't put reliable numbers on the outcomes - which job offer to take, whether to move cities, whether to start a side project - I focus on the worst plausible outcome of each option and pick the option whose worst case is the least bad. A friend of mine who did philosophy says I'm basically using maximin, the rule Rawls used behind the veil of ignorance (though Rawls was talking about the basic structure of society, not my flat-hunting). He also mentioned Harsanyi, who apparently thought the right move in the same sort of setup was to assign equal probabilities and maximise expected utility instead. That surprised me, because I'd assumed my rule was just obviously the safe one.

I tried to write out my reasoning as a little pseudo-proof, and now I'm suspicious of where it lands:

(1) When I have no reliable probabilities for the outcomes, I should choose the option whose worst outcome is best, because that's the only option that protects me no matter what happens.
(2) If maximin is the right rule when probabilities are unavailable, then it should also be the right rule whenever probabilities are less than certain, since any estimate I make could be wrong and the same protect-against-the-worst logic applies.
(3) So I should reject any plan with a bad enough worst case and take the safest option available.
(4) That means never driving (worst case: fatal crash), never flying, never starting a company, probably never having children, maybe never even crossing the road.
(5) That's absurd. Nobody is irrational for doing those things, and living like that would be a disaster in its own right.
(6) So maximin can't be the right rule.

I havnt been able to tell which step breaks, or whether I'm just making a special exception for myself because the conclusion is too uncomfortable. Where does this go wrong, if it does? And separately: if Rawls and Harsanyi both start from something like a veil of ignorance, why do they end up with opposite rules - is one of them making the same mistake as my step 2, or is their disagreement about something else entirely? Please write the answer in British English.
