I'm in a comparative constitutional design class right now, and after two weeks on the UK, Denmark, and Japan I've got a thought experiment I can't shake. Take a Westminster-style monarchy and leave every formal power of the Crown exactly as it is on paper: royal assent, appointing and dismissing the prime minister, granting or refusing a dissolution, the weekly audience, the whole thing. The only change is that the monarch is no longer hereditary. Every seven years a new monarch is drawn by lottery from all adult citizens (you can opt out before the draw). The winner serves a single seven-year term, gets six months of briefings from the civil service before taking office, cannot be removed except by a two-thirds vote of the legislature for incapacity, and while in office cannot vote, hold office, or make public political statements.

What I want is not a verdict on wether this is a good idea, but an account of how it would actually behave. What does replacing heredity with a term-limited lottery do to the conventions that currently keep the reserve powers unused? Does a lottery monarch feel more or less free to refuse assent, or to pick the prime minister they prefer after a hung parliament? Do parties start treating the lottery as a campaign event, or pressure the winner, or try to route around them? Does the lack of a dynasty and a lifetime of preparation make the office weaker in practice, or does the democratic legitimacy of the lottery make it stronger? Where does this get gamed first, by whom, and how bad does it get? And which of the likely failure modes could be fixed—by changing the term, the removal rule, or the powers themselves—without gutting the point of the reform?

I'd also like you to flag the weakest load-bearing assumption in your answer: what fact about how real constitutional monarchies operate would, if it turned out false, most change your analysis?
