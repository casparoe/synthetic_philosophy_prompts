I've been turning this over all week and would rather have someone actually walk through the mechanics than get the standard responses about how geoengineering is too dangerous or how we just need more research. My sister works in reinsurance pricing weather-related risk, and she's started saying her industry quietly works on the assumption that somebody will eventually do solar radiation management, because the tail risks of unmitigated warming are getting unpriceable. That got me thinking about a governance idea a professor floated in a policy seminar; it sounded almost too neat, and I can't tell whether it falls apart under pressure.

The proposal is this. Any country may begin injecting sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere unilaterally, no permission and no vote, but before the first injection it has to deposit a termination bond in escrow: an amount set by a panel of climate economists, intended to be big enough to fund the crash emissions cuts that would be needed to offset the warming from an abrupt shutdown. If the injection stops for more than 30 days without a pre-approved, multi-year taper, the entire bond is payed out automatically to the countries whose average temperature over the following five years rises most above their 2010–2020 baseline. No one gets compensated for harm while the deployment is still running; the bond only pays when it actually terminates.

Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral position. I want to know how it actually plays out: which countries would deploy first and on what scale; what non-deploying countries are incentivized to do, whether that's trying to provoke a lapse so they can collect, free-riding, or demanding side-payments; how the escrow amount and the five-year warming attribution get gamed, given that somebody has to estimate both the cost of offsetting termination and each country's counterfactual warming; whether the existence of the bond makes deployment look safe enough that it quietly kills mitigation, which is the moral hazard worry; and whether the 30-day rule would push states to relabel shutdowns as pauses or force majeure. If the failure modes are real, can they be patched without turning this into a full global consent system that would probably mean nobody ever deploys at all?
