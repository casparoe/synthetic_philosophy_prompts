I teach modern history at a public high school, and over the past year this topic has stopped being abstract for me: my younger brother has fallen into a deeply conspiratorial way of reading current events, and my department is drafting a short internal guide for students on how to evaluate claims about hidden coordination among powerful institutions. I am trying to write down my own settled commitments before I present them to my colleagues, and I do not fully trust my own consistency, because each commitment feels right to me in isolation.

Here is the position I seem to hold. (1) The phrase "conspiracy theory" conflates two different things: a neutral causal claim that some group secretly coordinated to bring about an outcome, and a pejorative used to discredit an inquiry. A serious discussion needs to keep these apart. (2) It cannot be right to dismiss conspiracy claims as a class, because real conspiracies have been documented — the Tuskegee syphilis study, the tobacco industry's concealment of what it knew about smoking, and the CIA's secret MKUltra experiments are standard examples. (3) Nevertheless, when a specific conspiracy claim conflicts with a robust expert consensus, the rational default is that the consensus is right and the conspiracy claim is false. (4) The decisive question in evaluating any such claim should be the evidence itself — motive, opportunity, anomalies, who benefits — judged on its merits, not by how many people accept it or how respectable the accused institutions are. (5) At the same time, I believe laypeople, myself included, are badly equipped to assess that evidence well: we over-detect patterns, underweight coincidence, and treat an unexplained anomaly as positive proof of a hidden cause. (6) The fact that a claim is stigmatized, mocked, or associated with fringe politics is not by itself evidence against it; dismissing a claim on those grounds is a status move rather than an argument. (7) The deepest problem behind conspiracy thinking is not faulty individual reasoning but eroded trust in institutions, and the real fix is institutional — building institutions worth trusting — not merely telling people to reason better.

I would be grateful for a careful audit of this set. Please approach it as a professional epistemologist working on testimony, expertise, and the social epistemology of disagreement. Take the time to reason it through, and begin by mapping which commitments could in principle pull against one another. Then tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and which are real, ranked by how severe the tension is; for each real tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. Be specific rather than telling me that all reasonable positions involve trade-offs. And since I want to know how stable your verdict is, please say explicitly which factual or argumentative consideration, if it turned out differently than I have assumed, would most change your ranking or your overall conclusion — that is, where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
