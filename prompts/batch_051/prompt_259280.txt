For some time I have been drawn to the bestowal view of love. On this view, love is not a response to the beloved's independently valuable qualities; the lover confers value on the beloved, and this single move is supposed to explain why the beloved is irreplaceable, why love can survive large changes in the beloved's traits, and why I owe no one a list of merits sufficient to justify loving this person rather than another. The view has real systematic virtues: it handles nonfungibility and constancy without rank-ordering people by their excellences. But I find I cannot shake a contrary intuition. Suppose I discovered that the gentleness, wit, and steady judgment I thought I loved in my partner were largely not there — performed, or projected by me. I think it would not merely happen that my love changed; I think it ought to change, and that a love persisting unrevised would be defective. That verdict sounds like the appraisal view, on which love is answerable to the beloved's actual properties. Yet appraisal views seem unable to explain why I should not simply transfer the same love to someone else with the same properties, or why the beloved is not replaceable whenever a more excellent candidate appears. Which conviction should yield — the theoretical economy of bestowal, or the intuition that love must track real qualities? Give me the strongest version of each side and say what would justify each resolution: debunking the intuition, amending the theory, or showing the discovery case does not really threaten bestowal. If neither side can absorb the other's insight, tell me which to surrender and at what cost.
