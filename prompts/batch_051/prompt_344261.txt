There's an idea I can't stop turning over, Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the claim that the value of nearly everything we do depends on our taking for granted that human life will continue after we're gone. The doomsday case goes like this. If an asteroid were going to wipe out everyone thirty days from now, cancer researchers would lose heart — not just because their work would be unfinished, but because its whole point depends on there being future people it could help. Scheffler thinks this shows extinction is bad in a way that goes beyond the deaths it involves. I find the argument weirdly compelling. It explains why people care more about climate change than about their own mortality, and it makes the last-generation scenario feel like a categorically different loss, not just a bigger death toll. But I also have a strong intuition that if I comfort a crying child twenty minutes before the asteroid hits, that act is still fully valuable — maybe the most valuable thing I do that day — even though there is no ongoing human future for it to feed into. And I don't just mean its a nice human moment; I mean it is good in the same way comfort is always good. So which should yield? Is the intuition a failure to follow the conjecture through, or does the conjecture overstate how future-directed value is and need to be weakened? Give me the strongest version of each side, and a negotiated verdict: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the conjecture to be the thing that goes, and is there a version of Scheffler's view that keeps the diagnosis of extinction while leaving room for last-moment goods?
