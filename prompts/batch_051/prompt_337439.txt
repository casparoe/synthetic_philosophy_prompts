A friend of mine runs it like this. (1) Take any past scientific theory that was empirically successful by the standards of its own day: the caloric theory of heat, phlogiston theory, the optical ether, Ptolemaic astronomy, that sort of thing. In every case the theory later turned out to be false, in the sense that its central unobservables dont exist or the world is not arranged the way the theory said. (2) Those theories were successful in the same way our current theories are successful: they made novel predictions, unified phenomena, and guided experiment. (3) So by induction on the historical record, our current best theories are likely to turn out false too, in the same sense: their central unobservables probably dont exist. (4) But a theory whose central unobservables dont exist is not even approximately true. (5) Therefore the empirical success of a current theory gives us no reason to think that theory is approximately true. I suspect the move from the early steps to step four is where something goes wrong, as if false means one thing when we look back at old theories and another thing when we apply it to our own, but I keep losing the thread.
