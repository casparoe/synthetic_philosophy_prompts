I hold a broadly Abelardian view of wrongdoing: the entire moral quality of a deed resides in the consent of the will — in the agent's inward endorsement of what they take to be wrong — and the outward act, its consequences, and even the antecedent stirrings of appetite, fear, or anger add nothing of moral weight. I want an honest account of what would count against this view, and what would have to be true for it to be flatly false rather than merely in need of refinement. Work through the pressure points most likely to damage it: moral luck (attempted versus completed wrongs), culpable ignorance, negligence, recklessness, habitual vice, omissions, and complicity in collective or institutional harms. For each, say whether a committed defender should own it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as a misdescription, and whether it is fatal or only awkward for the position. Distinguish the defeaters I would be unreasonable to ignore from the ones that are merely apparent, and give the strongest version of each objection rather than a strawman. Conclude by assessing whether the view, once the natural qualifications are added, has stopped risking anything at all — and if it has, whether that is a reasonable feature of a normative thesis or a reason to suspect it has become empty. Leave Abelard's texts aside and argue from cases, principles, and the structure of moral assessment.
