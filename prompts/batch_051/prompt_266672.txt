I am the student representative on my school's Academic Integrity and Technology Committee. Next month we vote on whether to recommend a proposal that would replace our honor-code system with a continuous conduct-monitoring program. Hallway cameras with behavior-recognition software would flag incidents automatically; students could report one another's minor infractions through an app and earn points for doing so; and each student would receive a continuously updated, publicly visible citizenship score combining attendance, assignment completion, hallway conduct flags, and peer reports. The score would control access to privileges such as off-campus lunch, parking permits, and unstructured study periods, and the score band would be sent to colleges as part of recommendation letters. The administration argues that the program is not disciplinary in the old sense: it does not punish, it only records; it applies a single visible rubric; and it removes teacher caprice by letting the data speak.

I have been reading Foucault on disciplinary power, normalization, the examination, and the panopticon. The proposal looks to me less like a break with how the school already operates—timetables, rankings, testing, continuous assessment, files on each student—and more like an intensification of it, but I do not think that observation alone settles how the committee should vote. I need to submit a short policy memorandum with a clear recommendation before the meeting.

Please write the memo I should present. It should give a definite bottom-line recommendation; use Foucault's framework to analyze what the proposal changes about how the school observes, judges, and forms students, paying particular attention to the fact that the observer is partly automated and the score is public; give an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side; and close with the conditions, if any, under which I would change my recommendation.
