I keep running into a pattern in how people defend certain values, and I'm trying to find out whether it has an established name.

A friend insists that a reading group should always say exactly what they think of each other's drafts, on the grounds that truth-seeking is the fastest way to correct bad ideas. When the group starts falling apart because everyone gets defensive and the feedback stops improving anything, she changes tack: truth-seeking matters for its own sake, and she'd want an honest group even if it produced worse work. A few weeks later, she's back to recommending the same practice to me because of how much it improves the work.

I've noticed the same motion with tradition. A community defends a custom because of some concrete good it once did, and when that good disappears the custom becomes just theirs, or just right, not a means to anything; if the custom later acquires some unrelated benefit, the practical justification seeps back in.

The switch never seems noticed, so it isn't exactly hypocrisy, and it isn't quite a motte-and-bailey either, because the two justifications aren't a stronger and a weaker version of one claim but two different kinds of claim. Does this exact thing have a recognised name? If not, coin one and define it tightly, showing how it differs at the edges from goal displacement, Goodhart's law and value capture—and say whether the pattern is ever intellectually defensible rather than just a confused way of shielding a value from evidence. A bare label won't help much; I'm mainly after the carving.

Please write the answer in British English.
