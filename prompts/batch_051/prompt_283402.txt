Over the past several years I have become convinced that the case for nudging — sometimes called libertarian paternalism — is very hard to resist. Choice is always structured by defaults, ordering, framing, and timing; there is no neutral architecture, so declining to design the environment is not neutrality but quiet endorsement of whatever incumbent arrangement happens to exist. If we are designing anyway, it seems plainly better to design in light of what people would themselves endorse on reflection: enroll workers in retirement savings by default, put the fruit at eye level, make organ donation opt-out — while leaving every option open, so that nobody is forced. The framework has exactly the virtues I want in a social practice: it is grounded in how people actually choose rather than how they ought to choose, it preserves the freedom to choose otherwise, and it supplies a non-arbitrary, welfare-based test for when steering is permissible rather than coercive.

But after a long argument with a friend about this, I cannot shake one intuition. She is a fairly orthodox defender of the harm principle, and her core objection was not that nudges remove options but that they manage people rather than reason with them: the state or the employer deliberately uses my own documented biases — status quo bias, present bias, social conformity — against me, to move me toward a conclusion it has already selected, even when the conclusion is one I would share in a cool hour. I found I could not answer the charge that this treats me as an object of policy rather than an agent, because the whole point of a nudge is that it works best when I do not notice it. Her strongest move came when I said I wanted my choices left undisturbed: she replied that my unease is just the bias the framework exploits, and that I am dignifying status quo attachment by calling it autonomy. I do not believe that answers the worry completely, but I can no longer say exactly what remains of it.

Present both sides at their strongest and tell me which should give way. In particular, I want to know under what conditions the intuition ought to be discounted as a bias or a misfiring of our heuristics for detecting manipulative agents, and under what conditions it should instead force an amendment of the framework — a transparency or publicity requirement, a restriction to ends the chooser would endorse, a distinction between changing defaults and enlisting social comparison, or something else. And is there a reading on which the heart of the anti-paternalist objection and the heart of the nudge program are compatible, or would that be no more than relabeling the same unease as a feature of the view?
