I am preparing a case for a hospital ethics committee. Below is Section 12(b) of the hospital's policy on end-of-life care, drafted in 1983 and never amended; the clause was written to codify the doctrine of double effect for palliative medicine. Its full text:

"12(b) Life-Shortening Palliation. A physician may administer, continue, or increase a treatment that carries a foreseeable risk of hastening a patient's death only if (i) the treatment is selected to relieve the patient's severe suffering and not to cause death; (ii) the hastening of death is foreseen as a side effect but is not itself intended; (iii) the hastening of death is not the means by which that relief is achieved; and (iv) the expected relief is serious enough to justify the risk taken."

The committee has been asked to rule on the following proposal. The intensive care unit wants to deploy a closed-loop palliative infusion device. A physician writes an order specifying a target comfort score and hard upper dose limits; beyond that, the device itself, reading a real-time measure of respiratory distress and agitation, raises the opioid infusion whenever the patient's score crosses a threshold. No physician is consulted before any individual increase, and no bedside clinician exercises independent judgment at each step. Everyone involved knows the device will, in a minority of cases, escalate to doses that materially suppress respiration and hasten death; the ordering physician wants only to relieve suffering and does not seek any death. The 1983 drafters had never considered a device that doses without a human intending each step.

Your charge is to tell the committee whether deployment is permitted by, prohibited by, or simply ungoverned by Section 12(b). Work through four readings: the plain words; the purpose the clause was introduced to serve; what the drafters would most plausibly have said had this device been described to them in 1983; and what a reasonable reader in the hospital today would take the clause to require. For each reading, state a verdict and identify the precise point on which that verdict turns. Then assess whether the doctrine of double effect itself handles the case any better than the clause does: can a doctrine built around an agent's intention regulate a decision procedure distributed between a human who fixes the targets and a machine that has no intentions? I am less interested in the committee's eventual vote than in that question, and in which feature of the case is doing the main work — the closed-loop autonomy, the physician's standing intention, or the requirement that death not be the means.
