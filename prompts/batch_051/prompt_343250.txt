Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about whether compassion is an unqualified good, and we ended up stuck in the old standoff between Schopenhauer and Nietzsche. He holds that Schopenhauer is basically right: compassion — Mitleid, suffering-with — is the one genuine moral motive, the capacity to see through the illusion of separate selves and respond to another's suffering as though it were one's own. I kept pushing the Nietzschean objection: that pity is not the innocent mirror of another's pain it pretends to be, but an affect with a genealogy — part tenderness, part concealed contempt, part pleasure in one's own superiority to the sufferer, and part a contagious multiplication of suffering that weakens both the pitied and the pitier. We could not settle it abstractly, so he threw Dostoevsky at me: he says Prince Myshkin in The Idiot is the great vindication of compassion, a man whose pity for Nastasya Filippovna is the only genuinely moral force in the novel, and that the catastrophe comes from the world's cruelty, not from his compassion. I am not so sure. I suspect the novel is closer to Nietzsche's worry than to Schopenhauer's defense — that Myshkin's pity is possessive, at times almost erotic, and that it does real damage to everyone it touches, himself included.

I would like you to read The Idiot through this debate and give me a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that gives Schopenhauer's position and Nietzsche's critique their due, that does not reduce the novel to an illustration of either, and that is willing to say plainly where the text resists each reading. Please engage at least the ending — Nastasya Filippovna's body in Rogozhin's house and Myshkin's final relapse — and Myshkin's relations with Nastasya and Aglaya. I am less interested in a plot summary than in what the novel shows about the pathology of compassion that the philosophical exchange tends to flatten. Lay out your reading as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
