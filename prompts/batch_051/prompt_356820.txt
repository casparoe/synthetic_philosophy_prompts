I am a novelist, and for the past several months I have been sitting in on a university epistemology seminar to make the protagonist of my next novel, a defense attorney, reason about testimony in a way that feels earned rather than borrowed from courtroom clichés. The professor is a deliberate case-by-case thinker, and I have recorded her rulings on the following episodes. In every episode the speaker's assertion is true, and the hearer has no independent way to verify it at the moment.

1. A stranger at a bus stop tells the hearer that the next bus comes at 3:15. The hearer has no information about this stranger's reliability, but has ordinary background experience with strangers and simple practical directions. Verdict: justified.
2. A stranger in a café offers an unsolicited one-sentence tip that a particular stock will double the next day. The hearer knows nothing about the stranger or the market. The professor emphasizes that the unsolicited, unargued character of the tip is itself a reason to suspect the speaker's motives or competence. Verdict: not justified.
3. A nine-year-old believes her father's assurance that a certain museum is open on Sundays. She has no evidence beyond his word, but has years of experience with him. Verdict: justified.
4. A serious accusation against a public figure reaches the hearer through a chain of six acquaintances, each of whom was told by the previous one; none of the links can be checked. Verdict: not justified.
5. A used-car dealer tells the hearer that a car's engine is in excellent condition. The hearer cannot inspect it and is aware of the dealer's financial incentive. Verdict: not justified.
6. A forensic accountant gives expert testimony about a company's books. The juror cannot follow the analysis, but knows that the witness's credentials were examined and accepted by the court. Verdict: justified.

The scene I am drafting is this. My protagonist, in a city she has never visited, is at an airport bar. A calm, competent-sounding stranger warns her, unasked, that the bridge she is about to drive over was closed that morning for structural reasons. The stranger has no motive to deceive, but my protagonist has no evidence about this person's reliability and no background beliefs about how trustworthy offhand infrastructure warnings from strangers tend to be. The warning is true.

If I want her to reason the way the professor does, should she treat the bridge warning as justified? I can see at least three rules that might fit the six rulings: a defeasible default that entitles a hearer to trust testimony absent a concrete sign of unreliability; a stakes-sensitive rule that permits unearned trust in trivial matters but requires positive evidence when the claim is consequential; and a reductionist rule that always requires the hearer to possess some background induction about the relevant kind of source. These pull apart on the bridge scene. Which rule, or what refinement, best unifies the professor's verdicts, and what does it require for the bridge warning? If the six verdicts underdetermine her rule, what smallest addition to the scene or what separate case would most economically separate the remaining candidates?
