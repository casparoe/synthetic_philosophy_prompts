I got into a long argument with a friend who works at a patent firm, and I've been turning it over since. He told me that U.S. patent law used to care about who actually invented something first, but these days, for the most part, the patent just goes to whoever files the application first, even if someone else can prove they had the idea earlier. What struck me is how different that is from how science treats credit: in science, priority goes to whoever publishes first, but simultaneous independent discoveries—Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace—get treated as something almost mysterious, like proof that the discovery was "ripe" and would have happened anyway. If the same idea really is about to occur to several people at once, then what exactly is the first person's claim based on? The patent office seems to say that being first to file is enough for ownership, even though everyone knows someone else might have built the thing independently three months later, while science seems to want it both ways: priority to first publication, but simultaneous discovery treated as meaningful rather than as evidence the first discoverer was just lucky.

So here's what I'd like you to work out. Reconstruct the implicit theory of discovery, credit, and ownership that each practice presupposes—patent law on one side, scientific priority norms on the other. Are they incompatible, or can a single coherent picture of what a discovery is make sense of both? And if we took completely seriously the idea that inventions and discoveries are largely inevitable products of their time—that the person who gets there first is a conduit for an idea whose moment has come—what would actually have to change in patent law? Would the whole idea of a patent to an "inventor" still mean anything, or would it only survive as a prize for being an especially efficient conduit?
