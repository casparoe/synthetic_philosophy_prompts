Lately I've been mulling over whether the old arguments for a duty to remember still work now that the information environment has inverted. The writers in that tradition—Margalit arguing that we owe the victims of mass atrocity a kind of shared remembering, Rieff pushing back that collective memory often curdles into grievance and that some forgetting is necessary—were both working against a background where forgetting was the default. Memory was expensive and fragile; it required institutions, monuments, rituals, witnesses, people actually telling the story to the next generation. The live question was whether we are obliged to do that work, or whether some things should be allowed to lapse.

But the default has switched. Everything is logged, mirrored, indexed, auto-captioned, surfaced by recommendation algorithms. Keeping a trace is close to free; forgetting is now the active, expensive, usually unsuccessful project. So I'm wondering what happens to the arguments themselves, not the policies. Does the duty to remember get satisfied by the mere persistence of data, or does the Margalit-style duty actually demand something more than storage—attention, narrative, a community that does something with the record—in which case the duty doesn't disappear, it just becomes a duty to curate? And does Rieff's case for forgetting get stronger in a world where old wounds are resurfaced to drive engagement, or was his argument relying on forgetting being the default in a way that leaves it inapplicable now? I keep trying to state this as a proper crux and failing; its not obvious whether the change breaks the premises or just relocates them. What I'd like is a precise accounting: which premise in each argument was doing the real work, whether the new memory environment breaks it or just relocates it, and whether there's a repaired version that still says something worth hearing.
