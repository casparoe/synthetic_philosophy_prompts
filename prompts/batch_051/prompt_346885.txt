I’ve been going in circles about the Born-rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation, and I’d like help sorting out which of two things I should let go. On one side sits a theory with exactly the systematic virtues I trust elsewhere: it accepts only the wavefunction and unitary Schrödinger evolution, declines to posit collapse or hidden variables, and its defenders claim the Born rule can be recovered as a norm of rational choice under branching rather than as an extra postulate. The derivation is genuinely elegant — a rational agent deciding before a measurement, knowing her future selves will occupy every branch, should apportion her cares and bets by the branch amplitudes, and this yields the usual probabilities without adding a stochastic event. On the other side sits a stubborn intuition. Probability, as I ordinarily understand it, is about which of several exclusive possibilities actually happens; it presupposes that there is a fact of the matter I am ignorant of. In many-worlds there seems to be no such unknown fact: all branches are equally real, and “me after the measurement” is not one person uncertain about which branch she is in but many people, each certain of her own branch. So when the theory says an outcome with high Born weight is more probable, I cannot shake the feeling that the word “probable” has been silently redefined.

I want you to walk me through a negotiated verdict rather than just picking a side. What would it take for the intuition that probability needs an unknown fact to be the thing that yields — e.g., is it just a scope-insensitivity or self-locating confusion that should be debunked? What would it take for the theory’s decision-theoretic treatment of probability to be the thing that yields — e.g., does it really recover probability or merely rational betting behavior? And is there a reading on which both survive, perhaps by distinguishing epistemic probability from something more basic? Give me the strongest version of each position, then say which move you would make and what it costs.
