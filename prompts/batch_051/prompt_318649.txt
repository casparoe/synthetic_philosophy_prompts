I'm drafting a short position paper for a seminar on constitutional theory, and before I commit to it I want to check whether the set of views I've landed on actually hangs together. My focus is Westminster-style constitutional monarchies of the general type exemplified by the United Kingdom. Here is the package:

(a) In the abstract, I am a republican: I cannot see what could justify giving any share of public authority to someone by accident of birth in a modern democracy.
(b) Nonetheless, I support retaining the monarchy in practice, partly for continuity and partly because a hereditary head of state usefully separates the symbolic unity of the state from partisan political competition.
(c) I think the genuine constitutional value of the monarchy lies in the reserve powers — dismissing a prime minister who has lost the confidence of the lower house but refuses to resign, refusing assent to plainly unconstitutional legislation, and refusing a request for a dissolution made in bad faith. If these powers were legally abolished rather than merely dormant, the institution would be pure pageantry, and I would lose my main reason for (b).
(d) I also think the reserve powers are legitimate only because they are never in fact exercised: any actual use of them would register as a constitutional crisis rather than as the system working as designed.
(e) I think the monarch must follow ministerial advice in all ordinary matters, but I also think the monarch's regular private audience with the prime minister is valuable precisely because it gives the head of government a confidential sounding board that is not a partisan rival.
(f) I regard constitutional conventions as genuinely normative — not mere regularities — but I also think they cannot substitute for enforceable written limits, since a bad-faith government could simply breach them, and the only available remedy would be an exercise of discretion that would itself breach the conventions.
(g) Finally, I think the strongest argument for hereditary monarchy is comparative: an elected or appointed head of state would carry a democratic mandate of its own, and with it both the standing and the temptation to use that mandate against the government, whereas a hereditary monarch, having no independent mandate, lacks the standing to intervene. I take this argument seriously rather than treating it as a rationalization.

I can feel that something in here is uneasy, but I am not sure which threads are pulling against which. I would like an audit rather than a lecture: which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which tensions are only apparent, and how would you rank the genuine tensions by severity? For each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be, and then tell me which single change would do the most to restore coherence.
