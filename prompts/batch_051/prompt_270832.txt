Ashworth University's Heritage and Public Memory Committee, on which you serve, must submit a recommendation to the university president about the Founding Tableau, a 1927 fresco cycle in the entrance hall of the main library, before that building closes for renovation in the autumn. The frescoes depict the university's origin as a single heroic story: the founder surveying the site, the first classes meeting under a tree, the laying of the cornerstone, and a dignified procession of early alumni. They omit the contested breakup of the farm tenancies from which the land was assembled, the founder's later involvement in founding a eugenics society, and the more diverse early student body recorded in the archives. The 1927 gift deed requires only that the frescoes remain on public display somewhere on campus; it is silent about restoration, location, and interpretation.

Three positions have hardened on the committee. The alumni association and the student traditions board take the monumental view: the frescoes should be cleaned and restored to their original brightness and left in the entrance hall, because their role is to hold up a worthy image of the past for present students and alumni to emulate, and sacrificing that inspirational force would be a real loss. The university archivist and a local preservation society take the antiquarian view: the frescoes should be conserved exactly as they are, with no restoration and no interpretive signage, because their value lies in being a complete, unedited survival of how this institution has remembered itself, and every later layer—the 1960s overpainting, the smoke stains, the original varnish—is part of that record. A faculty-student coalition takes the critical view: the frescoes should not remain the library's public face; either they should be moved into a museum of the university's history, where they can be broken up and framed as contested evidence, or the entrance hall should be redesigned so counter-images and the omitted histories occupy the same space. The coalition argues that an institution that walks daily through a false origin story cannot honestly reckon with its past or become a different kind of institution.

Beneath these positions is a disagreement about what an institution's history is for: whether the past should mainly inspire, be preserved for its own sake, or be judged so that a different future becomes possible.

Write the policy memo the committee will submit to the president. The memo should make one clear, actionable recommendation—choosing among, combining, or refining these three options—give the reasoning for it, and include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration supporting the option the committee ultimately does not choose.
