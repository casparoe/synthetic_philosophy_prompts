I write about the ethics of public criticism for a general-interest magazine. For several years I have been tracking the published rulings of a widely read advice columnist who answers a narrow question in each column: does this person have standing to make this criticism? Three of her rulings seem directly relevant to a controversy I am now covering, but I am genuinely unsure what they imply when taken together.

In the first, a city comptroller whose office had spent a decade steering no-bid consulting contracts to a firm owned by a political ally's brother then wrote an op-ed attacking another city for awarding a no-bid water-management contract. The columnist ruled that he lacked standing. His factual assertions were accurate, but she held that his ongoing participation in the same kind of non-competitive contracting undercut his standing to make the criticism.

In the second, a former corporate accountant had, early in his career, helped a client smooth quarterly earnings in ways he now regards as fraudulent. He eventually left the profession, publicly disavowed what he had done, and became a writer on accounting fraud. When he blamed a company for similar earnings management, the columnist ruled that he had standing: the misconduct was behind him and explicitly repudiated, not part of his present conduct.

In the third, a financial commentator who had never managed other people's money criticized a fund manager for charging fees that steadily eroded clients' returns. The columnist ruled that she had standing, on the ground that inexperience is not a moral blemish and there was no inconsistency in her record to undercut the criticism.

The new case is this. A food-policy editor ran a small magazine in the 1990s that depended on unpaid interns, a practice he has never disavowed and sometimes still defends as ordinary for the period. Today he writes a scathing column condemning a restaurant group's current reliance on unpaid kitchen interns. If we want the columnist to remain consistent with her prior rulings, how should she rule on him? Which prior ruling is the closest analog? If those rulings underdetermine the answer, spell out the distinct principles she could be following, say what each would require in the new case, identify which reading of her record is better and why, and describe the further case that would cleanly separate the competing principles.
