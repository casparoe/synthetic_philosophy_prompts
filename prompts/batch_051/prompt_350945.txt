After forty-odd years in neurobiology I've gotten into philosophy lately, and the view I keep comming back to is the hedonistic imperative—the idea that when biotechnology is mature enough we shouldn't just treat suffering when it shows up but should phase out the capacity for suffering itself, replacing the old pain/pleasure system with something like information-sensitive gradients of well-being that still tell you something's wrong (tissue damage, a bad decision, someone you love in danger) without ever bottoming out in the raw awfulness. I suspect I find it compelling for a deflationary reason: I spent a career seeing how crude and conserved the neural machinery of pain actually is, so preserving the ancient wiring doesn't strike me as obviously wise. But I want to know what I'm risking in accepting it. Give me exactly five numbered items: the five considerations that would most count against setting the abolition of suffering as the long-run target for biotechnology, ranked from the one that should most shake my confidence to the one that's merely awkward. Each item should be a sentence or two, no introduction, no conclusion, and for each say whether a defender of the view should feel it as a real cost or can reasonably shrug it off.
