Just got back from a long argument with a friend about Benjamin Franklin's virtue chart — the thing where he picks thirteen virtues, keeps a little notebook, and puts a mark in the row for every offense each day, concentrating on one virtue a week so he cycles through the whole list a few times a year. My friend is convinced the whole exercise is basicly self-deception: you either get really good at noticing your own lapses only after the fact and forgiving yourself for them, or you quietly loosen what counts as a lapse until the page comes out clean. I think that's too cynical — it seems genuinely possible that keeping the chart could change a person's character, not just their record-keeping or the story they tell themselves about it. But by the end of the night we both admitted that niether of us could say what observation would actually separate those two readings.

So treat this as the claim in need of a test: Franklin-style daily self-monitoring of moral lapses improves moral character, where moral character means something in the neighborhood of a standing disposition to do the right thing unprompted — and not merely improved self-monitoring, improved self-presentation, or a drift in what the person counts as a lapse. I want you to design the best test you can, with these sections, in this order:

1) The claim, operationalized (about 2 paragraphs): what exactly you would measure, in whom, over what timescale, and how you'd define improvement so the test doesn't build in the conclusion from the start.

2) The design (2–3 paragraphs): conditions, a control group, what the control group does instead of the chart, what you'd randomize or match on, and how you'd handle the obvious problem that people know whether they're doing the intervention.

3) What result would convince you each way (1–2 paragraphs): be concrete — what pattern or size of difference would count in favor of the claim, and what pattern would count against it.

4) What the test could never settle (1–2 paragraphs): the residue that no design, however clean, can reach, because the heart of the claim is partly about something private or about what character even means.
