I am a novelist. In the novel I am currently drafting, two characters—a father and his adult daughter—are quarrelling on a ferry quay after an overnight sailing has been cancelled. The daughter claims she knows the last ferry on the alternate route will still run, because she checked the operator's timetable that morning and it shows a Saturday service. The father replies that she does not know this, because if she is wrong they will miss the custody hearing and lose access to her son; his line, as I have drafted it, is: what a person knows shrinks when the cost of being wrong goes up. I want the scene to be psychologically true, but I also want the father's claim not to be philosophically naive; I have been told it is close to a live position in recent epistemology, and I would like to know exactly how much weight his sentence can bear.

While researching, I came across the following passage in a writers' handbook (a guide to philosophical ideas for novelists):

"Epistemic contextualists argue that whether you know something depends on how much is practically at stake for you. In Keith DeRose's famous bank case, Hannah knows the bank will be open on Saturday when the stakes are low; in the high-stakes version, where a large check will bounce if she is wrong, she no longer knows, even though her evidence has not changed. Contextualism is the thesis that the word 'knows' works like an indexical such as 'here' or 'now': its content shifts with the speaker's practical situation, which explains why we can truthfully say 'she knows' in one conversation and 'she does not know' in another. Many contextualists also endorse pragmatic encroachment, the view that whether you are justified in believing something depends on the practical costs of being wrong. Taken together, these ideas show that knowledge is not a fixed relation between a person and a fact, and that traditional invariantism, on which knowledge is insensitive to stakes, has been largely abandoned."

The passage feels too tidy to me, and I suspect it may be running together positions that professional epistemologists keep apart; but I cannot locate the joints with confidence, and I do not want to put a sentence in the father's mouth that a careful philosopher would immediately flag.
