I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I keep going back and forth about whether saying DNA contains a "code" is literal or just a catchy metaphor. I've collected my own verdicts on a bunch of cases and I want to know if there's a single principle behind them, or which of my verdicts has to go.

Here are the cases as I see them. Morse code is definately a code: an arbitrary pairing of letters and dot/dash patterns, kept in place by convention. A Caesar shift used by two spies is a code, obviously. ASCII encoding for text is a code. A player piano roll is a code too, even though the piano understands nothing; a human designed the mapping from hole positions to notes and the mechanism just executes it. By contrast, smoke rising from a fire is not a code—it carries information, but nothing is doing any encoding. Tree rings aren't a code either, just a physical trace. The bee waggle dance isn't a code: it transmits information about distance and direction, but it feels more like a measuring instrument than a convention. And yet the genetic code—the codon-to-amino-acid mapping—I want to call literally a code. The mapping looks arbitrary in the right way, since a different codon table for the same twenty amino acids seems possible, and that arbitrariness does real explanatory work for why a point mutation has the effects it does. But here's the rub: there's no sender, no receiver with intentions, no convention. A ribosome isn't interpreting anything; it's just chemistry.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like: a thing is a code iff there's a stable mapping between two families of items, a physically implemented reader that takes one side as input and yields the other as output, and the mapping isn't a direct causal or iconic relation but could have been otherwise. That saves Morse, the ciphers, ASCII, the piano roll, and maybe DNA, while excluding smoke and tree rings. But I can't see why the waggle dance fails on that story—it has a stable mapping from movement parameters to distance and direction, plus a reader (another bee) that responds appropriately. So either the principle is too permissive or I'm wrong that the dance isn't a code.

Is there a way to sharpen that principle so it keeps my verdicts, or does it force me to drop one—probably either the dance or DNA? And what does the surviving principle say about cases I haven't listed: an mRNA transcript, a QR code, a stop sign, a paramecium following a chemical gradient, and a thermostat's bimetallic strip? Finally, should I say the ribosome "reads" a code in the same sense a spy reads a cipher, or should I stop thinking of the genetic code as literal?
