I'm taking a course on the philosophy of logic this term, and we spent two weeks on epistemic logic. In a popular introduction I was reading alongside the class, the author gives what struck me as an overly brisk dismissal of the possible-worlds framework. The passage, as I remember it, runs more or less like this: "On the possible-worlds account, you know a proposition just in case it is true in every world you consider genuinely possible. Now suppose something you know logically entails another proposition. Any world where the first proposition is true is a world where the second is true, so the second is true in every world you consider possible, and therefore you know it. It follows that an agent who knows the axioms of Peano arithmetic knows every theorem of arithmetic, including theorems no human has ever proved. Since that is plainly false of human knowers, the possible-worlds semantics for knowledge is refuted, and no amount of tinkering can save it."

I realize the author may be compressing a subtle technical result into a slogan and then treating the slogan as a refutation, but I can't locate exactly where the slide happens. Which parts of this summary would a careful defender of the possible-worlds framework accept? Which parts are better read as consequences of an idealizing model than as claims about flesh-and-blood reasoners? Is the final sentence warranted by anything accurate in the paragraph? I would also like a sense of whether the standard responses — treating the closure as implicit or dispositional knowledge, adding explicit knowledge or awareness operators, weakening the accessibility relation, or adding impossible worlds — are genuine repairs to the framework or just relabelings that leave the underlying idealization untouched.
