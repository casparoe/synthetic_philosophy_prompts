My sister had a terrifying reaction to anesthesia last winter: the paralytic kicked in but the sedation didn't, so she was awake on the operating table, unable to move or signal, and she later repeated back things the surgeons said. I've been a bit obsessed since then with what it means to say someone was conscious during a stretch when they couldn't report anything. A friend of mine in a cog-sci program gave me the following argument, which he says shows my question is confused:

(1) The only evidence we can ever have that another person is conscious of some content is that they report it — where reporting includes speaking, typing, pressing a button, or any deliberate signal.
(2) What gets reported is exactly what has been globally broadcast in the cognitive workspace: the content that wins the competition and becomes available for speech and deliberate action.
(3) So every conscious content we can point to, study, or verify is a globally broadcast content; the two always show up together in practice.
(4) If two things always show up together and no possible observation could ever seperate them, then they are not really two things; positing an extra 'phenomenal' residue beyond the broadcast would be unscientific.
(5) Therefore phenomenal consciousness just is global broadcast/access. Once you have explained the workspace competition, you have explained the 'what it's like', and the access/phenomenal distinction collapses.

I have a gut feeling it moves too fast, especially the jump from (3) to (5), but I can't articulate the mistake — my sister's episode seems like a counterexample and yet, as he points out, she did end up reporting it afterwards from memory, which is itself a kind of report. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and what would a corrected version of that step say? Please give me exactly five numbered items in response, one or two sentences each, with no intro or conclusion: (i) whether the argument is valid, (ii) which step first goes wrong, (iii) precisely what the error in that step is, (iv) whether the conclusion is false or merely unsupported, and (v) what the argument would need to add before its conclusion could follow.
