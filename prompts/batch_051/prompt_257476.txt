You are a professional philosopher working in environmental ethics and value theory. I am a science-and-ideas journalist writing a feature about a fault line running through contemporary conservation: whether the value of wild nature is intrinsic to it, located in our relationship with it, or only a matter of what it provides us. The piece grew out of my reporting on a dispute among conservation organizations over whether defending wild places in the language of ecosystem services is a strategic necessity or a quiet surrender of the deeper claim. To sharpen my own categories, I have been compiling a list of arguments that nonhuman nature—species, wild places, living systems—merits protection on grounds not exhausted by its usefulness to humans. So far I have four, which I take to be the standard moves. First, the last-person thought experiment: if the last surviving human could, before dying, destroy all remaining nonhuman life at no cost to any further human interest, most of us still judge that this would be wrong, and that intuition is supposed to show that nonhuman life matters even when no human valuer remains. Second, the biocentric argument: each living organism has a good of its own, a way its life can go better or worse, and that own-good gives it a claim to direct moral consideration whether or not anyone prizes it. Third, the ecocentric, or land-ethic, argument: not only organisms but the wholes—species, soils, waters, ecosystems—have an integrity and continued functioning that can be wronged in its own right, so the unit of concern is larger than the individual. Fourth, the sentientist argument: creatures that can suffer or enjoy have interests that count morally in themselves, and much of wild nature is made up of such beings, quite apart from any use we might make of them.

I want a fifth argument of the same general kind—an argument that wild nature should be protected on non-instrumental grounds—that is not on this list and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of the four. Think carefully and brainstorm a few candidates before committing; then present the one you think best displays a structure the other four lack. After laying it out, tell me two things: what it captures that none of the four does, and how the field currently regards that move. On the second point, explicitly separate what is broadly settled among specialists from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. A good answer will also say whether the new argument shades into intrinsic, relational, or instrumental language, since that three-way distinction is exactly the one my feature is testing.
