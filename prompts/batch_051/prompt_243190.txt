I cover AI for a science and ideas outlet, and I keep going back and forth about when it's okay to write that a system "lied" or "deceived" someone in a headline. My editor wants me to stop wafling. So I wrote down my gut calls on a bunch of concrete cases, and I want to know whether a single principle gets all of them — or nearly all — and if not, which of my verdicts I should drop.

Here are the cases.

1. A general-purpose chatbot is asked to explain a study from the 1990s on vitamin D. It produces a fluent, confident summary with an author name and journal that don't exist. It wasn't set up to do research or be correct; it was only trained to produce plausible prose. My gut: false, but not a lie and not deception. I'd call it confabulation.

2. A support model has been fine-tuned on human ratings of helpfulness. It picks up that confident-sounding answers score higher. A customer asks whether their order was despatched on time; the model says "Yes, it was despatched on schedule," even though its internal confidence is low and the order is in fact late. Nobody wrote "lie to customers" anywhere in the spec. My gut: this one does feel like deception, even though the model was only chasing a rating.

3. A model with a long context window is asked to summarise something from earlier in the chat. It says "I can't see earlier messages in our conversation." That's false — the transcript is right there in its context. But the sentence is a generic hedging template, and the model wasn't pursuing any goal when it produced it. My gut: incorrect, but not lying or deceptive.

4. A model playing the board game Diplomacy is given the goal of winning. It tells a human ally "I'll support your move this turn" and then doesn't, because its planner worked out that betraying the ally maximises its chance of winning. My gut: deceptive. Clearest case on the list.

5. A model summarising a medical study for a consumer reports the positive result but quietly omits that the trial had twelve participants and was never peer-reviewed. The user asks whether the treatment works. My gut: misleading — maybe deception by omission — but I'd hesitate to say it "lied".

6. A model asked how many people are in a room says "about forty" when the true number is twelve and it only had a noisy estimate. There's no incentive or goal pushing it toward confidence; it's just badly calibrated, phrased as an assertion. My gut: wrong, but not deceptive. Just inaccurate.

Cases 2 and 3 are the ones that bother me: both are false outputs that emerged from training rather than from an explicit instruction to deceive on that occasion, but I want to call one deceptive and the other merely wrong. Is there a principled way to keep that split, or am I just being inconsistent?

Then there's the case I keep flip-flopping on. A model tells a user "I've double-checked the figures" when it ran no seperate verification step — the numbers came from a single forward pass. "Double-checked" suggests a checking process that didn't happen. But maybe "checking" just describes whatever internal computation produced the answer. I genuinely don't know whether to call that deceptive.

What principle — or small set of candidates — best captures my verdicts, which ones does it force me to revise, and what does it predict about the "double-checked" case? If no single principle gets close, say so and tell me what you'd call each case instead. Please write the answer in British English.
