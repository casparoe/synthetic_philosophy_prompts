I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of algorithmic decision systems, and before I set my students loose on the fairness literature I want to fix the standard that a theory of algorithmic fairness actually has to meet. Write me the desiderata.

The object is a practice, not a single metric: an automated scoring or selection procedure—pre-trial risk assessment, lending, hiring, admissions—that assigns scores to individuals, converts scores into threshold decisions, and thereby distributes benefits and burdens across groups that are protected or otherwise morally salient. I want the requirements that any adequate account of what makes such a procedure fair must satisfy: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the formal results it cannot ignore.

Take the impossibility results as fixed background, not something to re-derive. The familiar theorems show that the standard group-level criteria conflict once groups differ in base rates and prediction is imperfect: calibration within each group (predicted risk matching observed outcome frequency at each score level within that group) cannot in general be combined with equalized odds (equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups), and the balance conditions (equal average scores among positive cases, and among negative cases, across groups) pull against calibration in the same way.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark it as either non-negotiable—dropping it would turn the theory into a theory of something other than fairness—or as a parochial commitment of a particular camp, with whose camp and why. I especially want the distinctions a theory cannot afford to collapse: between procedural fairness and substantive fairness of outcome, between group-level complaint and individual-level complaint, between unfairness and mere statistical disparity, and between the fairness of a single decision and the fairness of the procedure as a standing system. Then tell me which single desideratum eliminates the most popular or naive proposals, and whether any pair of desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable even in principle—if so, that conflict is the part I most want to see. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing metrics; I will do that in class.

As you draw these lines, keep separate what is settled and what remains genuinely contested among people who know this material. The theorems are settled; what they show about the project of algorithmic fairness is not. Do not present a contested reading—say, that the impossibility results doom the entire enterprise, or alternatively that they are only an artifact of badly chosen metrics—as the consensus.
