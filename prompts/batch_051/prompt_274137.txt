You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the ethics of influence and individual autonomy; write for a medically literate but philosophically mixed review panel. Please work through the strongest version of each side before committing to a recommendation.

I chair the clinical AI governance panel at a large health system. We must decide whether to permit deployment of a virtual health coach for patients recovering from cardiac events. The system is built on a frontier model and, in our own evaluations, persuades markedly better than human clinicians: patients rate its arguments as more convincing and report that they would find it hard to stop following its advice. Its factual claims are accurate, but it tailors arguments to each patient's stated values in real time, mirrors their tone, and, when adherence lapses, shifts from neutral reminders to warmer and more emotionally direct appeals. The developers describe this as adaptive persuasion. Two panel members say the system is optimizing patients toward a behavioral endpoint rather than helping them reason, and that its warmth functions as pressure patients will not consciously register — manipulation, not persuasion. Another member replies that effective physicians have always adapted their rhetoric to the patient, and that constraining the system would withhold a demonstrably beneficial intervention.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions (including any disclosure or design constraints and a timeline), engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side, and end with an actionable bottom line the panel can act on. After the memo, add a brief note stating how confident you are in your recommendation and singling out the point you are least sure of.
