Last night I had a long argument with a friend about a point in the epistemology of rationality that I cannot settle. I find myself convinced by the enkratic requirement, at least as an ideal of rational coherence: it is irrational to believe a proposition, or to invest a particular degree of confidence in it, while believing that one's total evidence does not support that attitude. The principle is attractive because it is simple, because it unifies many otherwise scattered judgments about irrationality, and because it explains why a declaration of the form "p, but my evidence does not support p" strikes us as self-undermining rather than merely unusual.

But my friend would not let go of a case involving higher-order evidence. A diagnostician carefully works through the test results and base rates and arrives at 80 percent confidence that a patient has a certain illness. She is then reliably informed that, before examining the case, she was given a drug that impairs exactly the kind of probabilistic reasoning her diagnosis required. None of her first-order evidence — the symptoms, the labs, the base rates — has changed, and the information about the drug bears on her reliability, not on the patient's condition. It seems right for her to say: "My first-order evidence still supports 80 percent confidence in the diagnosis." Yet it also seems right, indeed rationally required, for her to lower her confidence substantially, say to 50 percent, upon learning about the drug. In that resulting state she holds 50 percent confidence while believing her evidence supports 80 percent, which is precisely the structure the enkratic requirement condemns. And it seems like a paradigm of rational modesty rather than a defect.

I am not looking for a reply that simply takes one side. I want to know what each resolution would cost. If the intuition about the drug case should win, what exactly is wrong with the enkratic requirement as I stated it, and can it be stated or restricted in a way that preserves its appeal? If the principle should win, what is the strongest account of why the intuition misleads — for instance, is it confusing rational belief revision with what it is rational to do, or equivocating on "evidence," or treating one's assessment of the evidence as less than full belief? And is there a reading on which both the principle and the drug-case intuition survive, perhaps by distinguishing first-order from higher-order evidence, credence from outright belief, or one's evidence from one's opinion about what it supports? Present the strongest version of each move, and tell me which one I should ultimately accept.
