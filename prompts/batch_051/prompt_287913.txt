Over the last few weeks I've become sort of obsessed with the doomsday argument, and I can't settle whether it's a genuine piece of evidence or a probabilistic parlor trick. On the one hand, the reasoning feels hard to escape: conditional on the self-sampling assumption, my birth rank among all humans who will ever live should be treated as a random draw, so finding myself somewhere around the hundred-billionth human seems to strongly favor a future where the total number of humans is not astronomically larger than the number who have already lived. If humanity were going to last for millions more years with trillions and trillions of people, my observed rank would be an absurd coincidence, so the tidy Bayesian thing to do is shift my credence toward a relatively soon end. That has real virtues: it's simple, it's principled, and it gives a definite answer where gut-level thinking gives none. On the other hand, I have a firm intuition that my position in the birth order is just an indexical fact about me — the kind of thing that can't bear on how many people will come after me. It feels like saying "I'm the 137th customer at this restaurant, so it will probably close soon," which is only good reasoning if I already know something about how the restaurant decides when to close. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would actually take for the intuition to be the thing that gets debunked, what it would take for the argument to be the thing that gets amended or abandoned, and whether there's some reading of "evidence" or "favor" on which both can survive — and whether that reading guts the argument.
