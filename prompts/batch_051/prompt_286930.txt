Suppose a U.S. state with an incarceration rate and correctional culture typical of the country as a whole decides to import, in a limited and indirect way, the principle underlying the Norwegian prison at Halden: that the deprivation of liberty is itself the punishment, and that the conditions of confinement should in most other respects resemble ordinary life outside. Rather than rebuilding its facilities or changing its sentencing laws, the state proposes to change the way prisons are paid.

Under the rule, each facility's annual budget would be divided into a guaranteed custody grant, covering security, shelter, food, and base salaries, and a variable normalcy supplement worth roughly one-quarter of the total. The supplement has two independently earned halves. The normalcy half is paid in any year in which an independent inspectorate, using unannounced visits and administrative records, certifies that the facility as a whole met all of the following: residents averaged at least ten waking hours per day out of their cells; every resident was offered at least one contact visit per month without glass or mesh barriers; every resident who wanted it had access to paid work or credible vocational training; and each custody staff member ate at least one meal per shift in the dayroom alongside residents. The outcome half is paid as the facility's five-year new-conviction rate for released residents falls below a risk-matched baseline drawn from comparable facilities statewide, with nothing paid above the baseline; but a facility may collect the outcome half only in a year in which it also collected the normalcy half in full. Any supplement money a facility fails to earn reverts to a statewide fund for victims' compensation and reentry housing, and the custody grant may not be reduced as a penalty.

Assume the rule is adopted in good faith but without new construction money, additional staffing mandates, or changes to sentencing and release law. Analyze how it would actually behave: which actors inside the system would change their conduct and how; what the wardens, line officers, residents, and the state corrections department would each be incentivized to do, including the less obvious forms of gaming; what second-order effects would appear in security classification, disciplinary practice, and release decisions; and which failure modes could be repaired by tightening the rule without abandoning its purpose, as opposed to being inherent to the design.
