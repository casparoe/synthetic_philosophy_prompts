Construct a thought experiment for a course on social epistemology. We are often told that the Condorcet jury theorem shows that if each member of a group is individually more likely than chance to be right about a yes/no question, and members reach their views independently of one another given the facts, then as the group grows its majority decision becomes virtually certain to be right. I want a case that tests the force of the independence condition, not by making the members stupid or corrupt, but by giving them all the same imperfect shared input.

Requirements for the scenario:

- It should be a realistic binary decision in an institution: a hospital panel, an audit team, a parole board, a hiring committee, or something similar.
- Every member of the group is individually competent: using the information available to them, each member has the same probability p strictly greater than one half of reaching the correct decision.
- The failure should come from a shared signal or common cause that every member sees, such as a preliminary report, a shared database summary, a training module, or a letter placed in every file. It should not come from collusion, deference to a leader, vote-trading, or pressure.
- The shared signal should itself be better than chance but less reliable than each member's private information, and it should be plausible that members weight it as they do without being obviously irrational or negligent.
- The case should include explicit numerical values for the reliability of the private information, the reliability of the shared signal, and the weight members give to each, so the result can be checked. Show that as the group grows, the majority's probability of being correct approaches some value strictly below p, while each member remains individually competent in the sense above.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. State what each member's probability of being correct is and how it is produced by combining private and shared information. State the large-group majority reliability and show the calculation. Explain exactly which condition of the Condorcet jury theorem is violated and why the majority no longer converges to certainty.

Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work: why does removing or altering that feature restore the conclusion of the theorem? Finally, suppose someone defends the naive lesson that sufficiently large groups of individually competent people should be trusted for this reason. Say how that person's reasoning goes wrong in this case, and state the correct fallback claim about majority rule that the case supports.
