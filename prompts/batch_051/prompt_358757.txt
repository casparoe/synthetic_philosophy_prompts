Someone proposes scrapping backprop entirely and training a large model the way a gene pool gets trained: start with, say, ten thousand randomly initialized networks, score them all on a fixed benchmark each generation, keep the top one percent, and repopulate by making mutated copies of the survivors, with no gradients and no crossover, and each child allowed to differ from its parent in at most a handful of weights. The pitch is that this is exactly the sort of blind, foresightless hill-climbing people have in mind when they call evolution a stupid optimizer, and since that process produced us, it should be able to produce a competent model. Walk me through how this would actually play out. What is really being optimized? Where does the compute go? What would the population actually look like after a few thousand generations, and how would the search path differ from the path gradient descent takes through weight space? Which problems would it handle surprisingly well, and where should we expect it to stall or collapse? Then give me an honest verdict on whether any of its failure modes are fixable without quietly smuggling gradients back in. At the end, say how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the point you're least sure about.
