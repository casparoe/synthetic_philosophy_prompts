I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so I keep thinking about this like a test case for a system: the feature people think is missing from free will turns out to make the agent worse when you actually build it in. I'm writing a plain-English argument — probably just a blog post — that the ability to do otherwise in the strong sense (same past, same laws, different choice) is not what anyone actually wants out of free will. What they want is the other stuff: self-control, reasons actually mattering, being able to change your mind, not being coerced, being the ordinary author of what you do. I want a clean pair of thought experiments to make that vivid, so build me two cases to spec.

Case 1: a person who genuinely has the strong ability to do otherwise. I don't mean an observer couldn't predict them — I mean given the entire actual past and the actual laws of nature, their choice on some particular occasion could have gone the other way. Deliberation and reasons can be part of the story, but the final outcome can't be fully fixed by past plus laws. And this ability should come out as a defect: if you offered the person a chance to make that part of their decision process deterministic, they'd take it. The defect has to flow from the kind of ability it is — the way the indeterminacy is placed in the process — not from someone else punishing them, and not merely from the person not knowing they have it.

Case 2: a person in a fully deterministic world, so they never have the strong ability to do otherwise even once. But this person has everything on the worth-wanting list: they think things through, their reasons make a difference, they can override impulses, they can revise what they care about over time, nobody's coercing them, and in the ordinary sense their actions are theirs. The point of Case 2 is that you'd choose to be this person over Case 1 even after being told the determinism is airtight.

Constraints: ordinary realistic settings (home, work, an everyday decision), one paragraph per case, no sci-fi — no implants, no evil neuroscientist, no Laplace's demon, no perfect predictors, no time travel. The two people should start from the same baseline in competence, background, and temperament, so the big difference is the decision mechanism (indeterministic versus deterministic) rather than overall sanity or intelligence; if Case 1's indeterminism ends up reducing their reasons-responsiveness, say so, since that's part of what you built it to do. Don't stipulate that Case 1 "shows" anything; describe the mechanism and let the verdict fall out.

Then run the checks. For each case: state the verdict each camp would give — the hard-liners who say free will requires the strong ability to do otherwise, and the worth-wanting camp I'm arguing for — and why. Name the single feature of each case doing the work, and what each camp would say if you removed just that feature. Then the honest part: give me the strongest attack on each case from the side it embarrasses. A hard-liner will say Case 1's person has randomness, not free will, because the person isn't the source of the outcome; a hard-liner will also say Case 2's inability to do otherwise makes the whole package hollow. Tell me where those attacks actually land, wich one is the objection I should be terrified of, and whether I can patch the case without cheating or should just concede the point.
