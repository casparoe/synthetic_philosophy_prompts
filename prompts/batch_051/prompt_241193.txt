I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about whether a given atrocity counts as genocide, and I do not have a precise name for it. When the accused party is one they are inclined to defend, they insist on the narrow legal criterion from the 1948 Genocide Convention — roughly, the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, as such — and they dismiss broader uses as rhetorical inflation. Moments later, when condemning an adversary, the same speaker applies genocide in an expansive, moralized sense meaning little more than a campaign of mass killing or forcible displacement, without establishing the required intent. The same pattern shows up across documents from one organization, which invoke the narrow definition in their formal conclusions and the broad sense in their policy summaries. The choice of sense tracks the speaker's or institution's prior verdict; it is not a stable commitment to either the legal or the moralized use. I do not think this is ordinary equivocation, since the speaker is not confused about the two meanings, and it seems different from motte-and-bailey because the narrow legal sense is not obviously the more defensible ground. Is there an established concept for this pattern, and what exactly distinguishes it from a persuasive definition, special pleading, and plain inconsistency? I would not be satisfied with a bare label; I want the boundaries of the phenomenon — what it is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
