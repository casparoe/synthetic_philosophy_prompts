I keep running into arguments about whether some vote counts as a "lesser evil vote," and I've started to suspect the phrase is doing more work then it can support. Different people seem to mean different things by it. My partner uses it for any vote cast while holding your nose, even if you actually like half the candidate's platform. A friend thinks it only applies in a two-party general election where both candidates are bad and one is worse, so you pull the lever for the less bad one. Another friend says even voting for a candidate you broadly like can be a lesser-evil vote because the party as a whole is still bad. I need a working definition, not just a vibe: what makes a vote a lesser-evil vote as opposed to an ordinary strategic vote, a sincere vote with reservations, or a protest vote?

Here are the cases I want the definition to get right. (1) In a general election with two viable candidates, you vote for A mainly to stop B, though you think A is also pretty bad. (2) Same election, but you vote for A because you like most of her platform, though you dislike her foreign policy. (3) In a safely blue district, you vote for a third party you genuinely prefer, knowing A will win anyway. (4) In a toss-up district, you vote for that same third party even though it might hand the seat to B. (5) You stay home because both viable candidates are unacceptable. (6) In a primary, you vote for a bland moderate you don't like to block a polarizing candidate who would probably lose the general. I'm fairly sure (1) is the paradigm case and (2) is not; I have no idea what to say about (4) and (5); and I worry a good definition would force me to call (6) a lesser-evil vote even though the word "evil" feels too strong there. Propose the definition, run all six cases through it, and tell me which verdict is least secure and whether you'd revise the definition or just defend the surprising answer. And say whether abstention in (5) can count as a lesser-evil position even though it's not a vote.
