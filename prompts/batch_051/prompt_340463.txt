I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and last week two of our regular contributors got into an argument at lunch that I genuinely couldn't referee. I'll lay out the moves because I want a clean second opinion.

The trigger: our audio intern had loaded a MIDI file of the slow movement of Mahler's Ninth into a cheap orchestral sample library, listened on headphones, and said he'd "heard the piece." (1) Marisol, who covers classical music, said that was absurd: the work includes the specified instrumentation, so a bunch of synth approximations is no more a performance of the symphony than a plot summary is a novel. (2) Dev, who covers electronic music and production, replied that this can't be generally right. The work is the abstract pattern of pitches and durations, and the MIDI version instantiates that pattern (badly); if all the notes are there, its a performance of the same work — a terrible one, but not a different thing.

(3) Marisol came back with the rock case. Plenty of music has no score; the song is built in the studio as a recording, and a cover is usually a cover of that recording, not of some abstract pattern lying behind it. So Dev's one-size-fits-all pattern story can't explain how a recording itself can be the work. (4) Dev answered that this just shows works are individuated by the practices around them: some are performance-works anchored to a score, some are playback-works anchored to a canonical recording. But in the classical practice that anchors the Mahler, instrumentation is part of the identity of the work, so the MIDI rendering is an arrangement, not a performance. They went back and forth a bit more, but I think those four moves are the core.

Adjudicate this in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion. For points 1-4, rate each of the four labeled moves as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with a sentence saying why; for point 5, say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
