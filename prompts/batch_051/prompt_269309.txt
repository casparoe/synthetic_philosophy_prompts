Heres my working criteria for a category mistake, Ryle-style. A sentence commits one when (1) it treats something as if it belonged to a different logical type than it does — like asking where the university is after you've been walked through every college, library, and admin building, as if the university were one more building on the tour; (2) the problem isnt that the sentence is false, its that it only looks as if it says something about a thing at all; (3) its not metaphor, because a metaphor knows its borrowing from another domain, and a category mistake doesnt; (4) a useful test is to try to paraphrase it away — if the paraphrase does no real work, the original was leaning on confusion. The same machinery is what Ryle used against the Cartesian idea that a mind is a ghost in the machine, an extra non-bodily thing alongside the body and its doings. Now take: "the government believes inflation will fall, and its worried about it." Completely ordinary language. But a government is not a mind — its officials, offices, documents, procedures — so by my criteria you could argue we're treating a collective as a ghostly extra thing. Is this sentence a category mistake? Go through my four criteria one by one, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and tell me which criterion, if any, you'd revise. Then do the same for "the market panicked." If you answer differently for the two cases, say what does the work.
