You're a philosopher who works on blame, moral criticism, and the norms of holding people responsible. I'd like you to think carefully about this one, because I keep going in circles.

The term I want to get clear on is standing to blame. Here's the phenomenon: sometimes a criticism is true — the person really did the wrong thing — but we still think the person making the criticism had no business saying it. Not because its tactless or bad strategy, but because of something about them. I want a working definition of what it takes to have standing to blame, and what makes someone lose it.

I have a list of cases. The definition should get these right, and where I'm unsure I want you to tell me what your definition says and whether I should bite that bullet.

1. A woman who was assaulted blames the man who assaulted her. I'm confident she has standing.
2. A total stranger, with no stake in the matter and no complicity, denounces a company for illegally dumping waste into a river. I lean toward him having standing, but I can't fully defend it — maybe standing requires being a victim or having some relationship?
3. A competing company that has been dumping the same waste into the same river denounces the first company. I'm confident this one lacks standing, or at least that the criticism is undermined, even though the criticism is just as true.
4. A woman who did burglaries in her twenties and hasn't stolen since condemns a man caught burgling last week. My intuition is that she has standing, maybe even more than someone who's never stolen, but I can't articulate why.
5. A man who smokes a pack a day tells his teenage son not to smoke, and later reproaches him for smoking. Here I genuinely don't know whether he's lost standing, kept it, or has some of it but less.
6. A government with a poor human rights record condemns another country's human rights abuses. Assume the criticism is accurate and likely to help the victims. I feel strongly that something is off, but I'm not sure I want to say people in the condemned country should dismiss it.
7. A close friend of a wrongdoer, who wasn't involved at all, blames him in private. Does friendship give someone more standing or less? I can see both ways.
8. A businessman who paid a bribe later publicly blames the official who took it. I'm confident he's lost standing, even though the official is also wrong.

So first: brainstorm at least three candidate definitions of standing to blame — for example a clean-hands view, a non-complicity view, something about being entitled to uphold the norm, or something about the right relationship to the wrongdoing. Then settle on the best version, run it through all eight cases, and tell me where it forces me to change a verdict I gave. After that, revise the definition if its worth saving, or defend the surprising verdicts if you think my instincts were wrong.

Keep me honest too about how standing relates to neighboring things: whether the blame is deserved, whether it's hypocrisy in a broad sense, and whether the target has any right to object. If the cleanest conclusion is that standing isn't one thing but a couple of different things people run together, say that.
