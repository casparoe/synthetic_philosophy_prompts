I sit on a three-judge panel of a state court that, for its sins, keeps drawing the defamation line between fact and opinion in cases about ratings, rankings, and forecasts. We've built up four decisions I can cite to myself, and now there's a new case that I'm fairly sure falls right on a fault line between two of them.

Our track record:

- In the bistro case we held that a review calling a dish "gluey" and "a war crime on a plate" was non-actionable: the context was a review, the language was subjective, and no reasonable reader would take it as a statement of fact.
- In the county-bond case we held that a credit-rating note saying the county was "likely to run out of cash by the end of the fiscal year" was non-actionable: it was forward-looking, hedged, and pointed to the public financials it was built from.
- In the doctor-rating case we held a site liable for publishing "complication rate: 31%" as a flat fact with no context: the number is measurable and verifiable, so it isn't opinion.
- In the landlord-score case we held a platform's "maintenance score: 3.1 out of 10" actionable when the platform couldn't or wouldn't show its work: the number invited readers to infer undisclosed bad facts, and a defamatory implication is just defamation done politely.

The new case is the landlord-score case's cousin. A different platform publishes a "maintenance score: 3.1 out of 10" for the same landlord, but this one publishes the whole formula and links every input: response times to repair requests, city inspection records, user-submitted photos. The inputs are verifable; the final score is just a weighted conclusion from them, and nobody claims the arithmetic is wrong.

What should we decide if we're being consistent with our own four cases? Which of our cases is the closest analog? Be honest about the principle we'd be adopting either way — if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the result, say so and give me the further case that would tell the two principles apart. Put the bottom line on its own final line as \answer{protected}, \answer{actionable}, or \answer{underdetermined}.
