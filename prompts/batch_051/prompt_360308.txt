I want you to answer for a reader who is a database systems engineer with professional fluency in transactions, reads and writes, commits and rollbacks, ACID semantics, access control, and the distinction between schema-level and data-level operations, and who has never studied philosophy of language. For this reader, restate the Austinian contrast between constative and performative utterances, together with Austin's later conclusion that this contrast cannot bear the weight originally placed on it, using only the reader's technical vocabulary. A first approximation would model a constative such as "the account was debited at 09:41" as a read: a query that reports existing state and is assessed for accuracy. A performative such as "I hereby delete the record" would be a write: an operation that changes state and is assessed for something more like success or validity than truth. Develop this first approximation into a systematic translation. Give technical counterparts for felicity conditions, the roles of authority, procedure, and uptake, the distinction between illocutionary and perlocutionary dimensions, and the specific failure mode of infelicity. Then supply the residue report: identify which parts of the speech-act picture carry over cleanly, which carry over only if the engineer's vocabulary is stretched in ways a careful practitioner would resist, and which have no counterpart at all. Close by assessing the following issue honestly. Austin came to hold that statements are themselves acts, which suggests that every alleged read is also a write; and it is equally natural to think that some performatives do not merely update data within a fixed schema but define or alter the schema itself. Does the read/write translation survive these two points, or do they expose that the database vocabulary presupposes a fixed transactional framework whose very existence is among the things speech acts can institute? Finally, give your verdict: is what fails to survive a defect of the database vocabulary, a sign that some Austinian claims were less precise than they appeared, or some of each?
