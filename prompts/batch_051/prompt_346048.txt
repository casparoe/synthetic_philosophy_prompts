I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether the Dutch book theorem actually shows what people usually say it shows. My friend thinks it's straightforward: if your credences violate the probability axioms, there's a set of bets you'd take at those odds that guarantees you lose money no matter what happens, and that's irrational, so obey the axioms. But I've got a nagging feeling the argument moves faster than the theorem allows. Here is the argument as I reconstruct it:

(1) If an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then there exists a set of bets, each fair according to those degrees of belief, whose joint outcome is a sure loss.
(2) A rational agent would not accept a set of bets whose joint outcome is a sure loss.
(3) Therefore, if an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, that agent is irrational.
(4) Therefore, rationality requires degrees of belief to satisfy the probability axioms.

I'm most suspicious of (1) and (2), because they seem to slide between what an agent's credences make fair and what the agent would actually accept as part of a package. A clever Bayesian could say she evaluates bets one at a time, not as a bundle, or that seeing the whole bundle changes her information. Also I remember something about diachronic Dutch books being different from synchronic ones, where the target isn't static incoherence but an updating rule, and those arguments need extra assumptions. So tell me: is this argument valid, and if not, which step exactly fails? Where would a defender of the Dutch book argument need to add a premise, and what should that premise say in its strongest defensible form?
