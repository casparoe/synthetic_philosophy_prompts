I help run the game design club at my high school, and every spring we hold a showcase where students submit short digital and tabletop games. Each submission receives a written design note from a student judge explaining what the game is trying to do artistically, not just whether it is fun. We keep defaulting to story, art style, and music when we write these notes, even for games where the interesting decisions are in the mechanics. I have been reading about what some people call the aesthetics of agency: the idea that games are a distinctive art form because they work in the medium of agency, so what a game expresses is carried by the kinds of choices, abilities, goals, and constraints it gives the player — much as what a dance expresses is carried by movement rather than by a written description of movement. I want to turn that idea into a procedure our judges can actually follow.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure — an ordered checklist or set of questions — that a student judge with no philosophy background could run on any submission to determine whether and how the game's mechanics are doing aesthetic work, as opposed to being merely functional, conventional, or decorative. The constraints are these: each judge has about twenty minutes with a game and a one-page statement from its designer; the resulting note has to be specific enough to be useful to the designer; and our faculty advisor wants a shared rubric, so the procedure must be transparent enough that a second judge can see why a note says what it says. The procedure should also flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and a judge has to decide. Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions: a story-driven visual novel whose choices change dialogue but leave the underlying mechanics unchanged; a minimalist abstract game about rationing a limited number of moves across a grid; and a polished action game that is genuinely fun but uses a completely standard control scheme and goal structure. I would especially like to see the procedure force a judge to say something the judge will not want to say — for example, that a game the judge personally enjoyed is not doing much artistically — and also that it catches something a designer might not have intended. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot detect, such as artistry in pacing, the feel of a control scheme, or the overall arc of a session.
