I'm a PhD student working on algorithmic fairness, and a policy colleague forwarded me the following paragraph from a widely shared explainer on risk scores in criminal justice:

"There is a formal result, often called the impossibility theorem of algorithmic fairness, which shows that no risk score can be fair to two groups at once. The theorem says three conditions are mutually incompatible unless the groups have identical base rates or the predictions are perfect: calibration (a score of 7 must mean the same probability of reoffending for each group), equal false positive rates across groups, and equal false negative rates across groups. So a fair algorithm is mathematically impossible. The practical lesson is that every risk score is either calibrated but has unequal error rates, which means it is biased against one group, or it has equal error rates but is no longer calibrated, so scores stop meaning the same thing for different groups. There is no third option, and any regulator who claims otherwise is denying the math."

I am fairly confident the theorem itself is being reported more or less correctly, but I suspect the last two sentences slide from a result about the incompatibility of three specific criteria to the much stronger conclusion that algorithmic fairness as such is impossible and that policy faces a forced binary choice. Go through the paragraph claim by claim. What do the impossibility results actually establish, what do they leave open, and does the policy conclusion follow from the mathematical part? In particular, I would like to know whether accepting the theorem forces one to accept the "no third option" claim, and what a careful version of the explainer's point would say instead.
