In my philosophy of language course we covered Tarski's undefinability theorem: a formal language meeting fairly modest conditions cannot contain a predicate true of exactly its own true sentences, on pain of the liar paradox, and truth for that language only becomes expressible from a metalanguage — whose truth in turn requires a meta-metalanguage, and so on. In a seminar on mystical theology we have been discussing the apophatic tradition: the view that no affirmative predicate applies literally to God, that God is beyond being and non-being, and that the tradition nevertheless keeps speaking through negations and then through negations of those negations, so that its discourse forms a ladder pointing beyond itself. I cannot decide whether the resemblance between these two ladders is deep or superficial. Both seem to make ineffability relative to a level of discourse: what is unsayable at one level is sayable one level up. But apophatic theology also seems to claim an absolute limit — that no possible language at any level literally captures God — whereas the Tarskian hierarchy seems to guarantee that nothing is ever absolutely unsayable, because ascent can always continue. Is the apophatic move a genuine analogue of semantic ascent, or does the analogy break exactly there? What would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one to the other, and does the parallel predict something false — for instance, that every apophatic unsayable should become literally expressible at a higher level? Finally, does the parallel illuminate the claim that what cannot be said can nonetheless be known, or does it misdescribe the kind of ineffability the mystics are after?
