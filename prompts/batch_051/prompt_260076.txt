A friend has argued me onto a ledge about Boethius, and I cannot find the step that gives way. I take it as a datum that at least some human acts are free in the robust sense: at the moment of choice, the agent could have done otherwise. But the following argument seems to show that Boethius's solution to the foreknowledge problem destroys exactly that datum, and I cannot see where it goes wrong.

(1) Infallibility: it is impossible that God knows a proposition and that proposition is false.

(2) In Book V of the Consolation of Philosophy, Boethius holds that God is not everlasting but eternal: God does not know events from a standpoint earlier in time, but knows the whole course of the world in one timeless and immutable act of knowing.

(3) Let C be some apparently free act, say my raising my hand at noon tomorrow. God knows C in that single timeless act.

(4) So C cannot fail to occur: infallibility leaves no possible world in which God knows C and C is false.

(5) And God's knowing C is itself fixed across all possible worlds. A timeless and immutable act of knowing does not have one content in some possible worlds and a different content in others; the act is what it is.

(6) Therefore C occurs in every possible world: there is no possible world in which I fail to raise my hand at noon tomorrow.

(7) But freedom requires exactly such a world, since I could have refrained only if there is a possible world in which I do not raise my hand.

(8) Thus the act is not free, and the argument applies to every act and every event in the timeline. So Boethius's eternity, introduced precisely to leave contingency and freedom intact, entails that nothing is contingent or free. The cure is worse than the disease.

Which step or inference do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why the step looked secure, and say whether the remaining position can still deliver both of Boethius's aims: infallible divine knowledge and genuine human freedom. Please write your answer in American English.
