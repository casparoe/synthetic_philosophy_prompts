The standard conversation just goes in circles because both sides can point at the mug experiments or at field studies, so I want a different angle.

Here's the counterfactual I want you to reason through. Suppose that tomorrow a cheap, safe, one-time biomedical intervention becomes available that makes a person's valuation of gains and losses symmetric around their current reference point: losing $100 hurts exactly as much as gaining $100 pleases, and the reference point updates almost immediately after any transaction or ownership change—so the willingness-to-accept/willingness-to-pay gap collapses. A mid-sized rich country randomly assigns the intervention to a representative 5% of its adult population permanently, and the other 95% stay exactly as humans are now. There's no selection effect and no placebo weirdness; everyone knows the intervention exists but is not sure who has it.

Walk me through what happens over the next thirty years, treating this as an exercise in mechanism, not story. I want a chain of reasoning, laid out visibly. Concretely: what happens to trade in goods and assets, labor mobility and wage bargaining, sunk-cost behavior in firms and households, insurance uptake and risk-taking, negotiation and litigation, political incumbency and status-quo policies? Where does removing the asymmetry make things more efficient, where does it backfire, and where does it mostly redistribute rather than create or destroy value? And how much does the answer bear on the underlying question of whether loss aversion is a bias or a legitimate preference: which observed outcomes would convince you that the untreated 95% are leaving gains on the table, and which would convince you the asymmetry is doing real work that a symmetric agent would be exploited or worse off without? Please flag each link in the causal chain as robust or speculative, and tell me what would have to be true for your main predictions to be wrong.
