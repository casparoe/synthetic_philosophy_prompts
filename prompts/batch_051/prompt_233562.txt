I'm drawn to a view about prosperity that seems almost forced once two steps are granted: the only states good for their own sake are welfare, freedom, security, and things of that kind; and material abundance is not among them, but is at best a powerful means to them. On this view the value of wealth is purely instrumental — surplus, productive capacity, and reserves are good exactly insofar as they secure or buy the things that are good for persons, and once those goods are fixed, further abundance adds nothing. The view is parsimonious, gives the right answer about money on an uninhabited island, and yields a crisp criterion for how much is enough.

But I keep returning to a comparison I cannot make come out neutral. Consider two societies equal in population and equal in the distribution of welfare, freedom, and security, with uncertainty and future risk stipulated away. In the first, these equal conditions are achieved amid great abundance: enormous capacity, deep reserves, infrastructure in surplus, slack everywhere. In the second, the same conditions are achieved under austerity: everyone is adequately housed, fed, and free, but the society deliberately forgoes building or keeping more than it needs and runs on the thinnest margins. I find it hard to accept that nothing makes the first society better, and the abundance itself seems to be part of what does.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the instrumental theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of 'value' or 'good for its own sake' on which both survive. Is the intuition tracking a genuine final value, or is it recycling risk, status, or an aesthetic preference for surplus — and how would I tell?
