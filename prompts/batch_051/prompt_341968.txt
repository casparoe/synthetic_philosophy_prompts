I am a PhD student in philosophy of science with a background in the foundations of machine learning, and I have been trying to think about the problem of induction through the lens of the no-free-lunch theorems rather than treating them as a side issue. The way I see it, Wolpert–Macready-style results tell us something awkward: averaged over all possible target functions, with each function weighted equally, any inductive method is exactly as good as any other, and no method beats random guessing without assumptions. That looks like a formal sharpening of Hume's point that induction cannot be justified by its past success alone, and also a direct challenge to any claim that a particular method—Bayesian updating, simplicity-based learning, whatever—is the one right way to generalize. But I suspect the no-free-lunch results are not just a skeptical dead end; they are constraints that a serious positive theory of inductive inference has to satisfy, because any method that does well somewhere must be paying for that success with assumptions that fail somewhere else.

What I want is a specification of the desiderata: what must an adequate theory of inductive inference accomplish once one takes the no-free-lunch theorems and the classical problem of induction seriously? For each desideratum, give the case, result, or consideration that forces it, and mark clearly which desiderata are non-negotiable for any view and which are preferences of particular camps. I already tentatively think the list must include at least these: (a) it must locate exactly where a method's advantage comes from, since the theorems rule out free advantages; (b) it must give a non-circular account of why that advantage should persist in the actual world rather than only in the problems the method was built for; (c) it must handle Goodman-style relabelings like grue and bleen, explaining which predicates or hypotheses are projectible without falling back on a blanket uniformity-of-nature assumption that merely restates the problem; and (d) it must account for the apparent practical success of science and ordinary learning, including finite-sample success, not just asymptotic idealizations. I want the full list, not only my four guesses, with the reasoning behind each item.

Please act as a professional philosopher of science who is comfortable with both formal learning theory and the Humean and Goodmanian literature. Begin by brainstorming the constraints a theory would have to respect, and then give me the final desiderata list. In addition to the items themselves, I want three further judgments from you. First, flag any subset of desiderata you suspect may be jointly unsatisfiable: my own suspicion is that the demand for non-circular justification and the no-free-lunch constraint that no method can dominate across all possible worlds are in tension, and I want to know whether that tension is real or merely apparent. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the standard historical positions—enumerative inductivism, a purely falsificationist account that disavows positive confirmation, and purely subjective Bayesianism are the ones I have in mind. Third, briefly assess whether Solomonoff-style universal priors or meta-induction over a pool of candidate methods satisfy your list, or whether they merely relocate the problem. A structured list or compact table is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
