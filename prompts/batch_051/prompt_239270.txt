Act as a professional philosopher of science who works on machine learning interpretability. Start by steelmanning my proposal, then evaluate it candidly.

I have been defending a principle I call opacity conservation. The claim is that when one AI system interprets another — for example, when a large language model is asked to describe what a hidden unit or attention head in a vision model is "really" detecting — the interpretation does not destroy opacity; it relocates it. The target becomes legible only because the interpreting system supplies judgments it cannot itself account for, so whatever opacity is removed from the target reappears in the interpreter, with a little extra opacity added by the interpretation itself. If you then use a third model to interpret the interpreter, the same thing happens one level up. Automated and recursive interpretability therefore cannot reduce opacity in the combined system: they can move it around, make it finer-grained, and dress it in fluent prose, but total opacity is conserved or grows. The only genuine reductions, I think, come from either making the target system simpler or getting a human to actually absorb the explanation well enough to re-derive it. I am aware this sounds like a thermodynamics metaphor, and that may be all it is. Is there a real principle underneath the analogy, or am I merely saying that an explanation issued by an opaque system is opaque? Has this position already been worked out under another name, and if so, what is it actually called? Then give me the strongest counterexample you can construct: a case where an interpreter that is itself highly opaque still produces a net reduction in opacity in the target. Would that refute the conservation claim, or only show that "opacity" is not the right additive quantity?
