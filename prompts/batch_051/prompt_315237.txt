I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'd like help thinking through a disagreement at work rather than generic management advice. A few months ago, my team owned a service that needed a large data migration. In the design review, my colleague Marco noticed that one of the paths lacked a data-validity check. He mentioned his worry to me privately before the review but didn't raise it in the meeting, partly because he hadn't written that module and didn't want to expand the scope of the review. I raised a smaller issue, but I stayed quiet about the missing check too. The migration ran as planned, corrupted several hundred thousand records, and cost the team about a week of recovery work. In the postmortem, Marco said he didn't cause the failure: he wrote none of the migration code, he approved nothing, and he left the code exactly as he found it. When I pressed him, he said a cause has to be something you do, not something you don't do.

I have a strong intuition that Marco's position is at least partly wrong, but I can't articulate why, and his argument strikes me as more coherent than I'd like. Is he wrong? If he is, what exactly makes a silence or a failure to act count as a cause, and how do we draw that line without making every uninvolved employee a cause of everything bad they could have prevented? If he's right, I'd like to understand that too, and why the intuition that he shares responsibility is misleading.

Please answer as a professional philosopher specializing in causation and responsibility. Before settling on an answer, work through the strongest version of Marco's position and the strongest reply to it, step by step. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
