Sentience is usually treated as a matter of degree rather than an all-or-nothing property: there is no sharp line between the barely sentient and the clearly sentient, only a gradual ramp upward from very simple organisms. I have been trying to think through the consequences of this view for moral status, and I have ended up somewhere uncomfortable. A thermostat continually senses its environment, holds to a target state, and corrects deviations from it, which looks from the outside like a very simple version of what more complex nervous systems do. If sentience is graded and has no lower cutoff, then a thermostat must occupy some position on that same scale, with a very small but nonzero share of sentience, and therefore a very small but nonzero share of moral considerability. But once a thermostat and a bee both sit on a single scale, the remaining questions look purely quantitative: how many thermostats' interests add up to one bee's, and how many bees' add up to one human's? I want to keep the graded view of sentience, but I can no longer see how to avoid being committed to exact exchange rates of that kind, even if we could never discover the numbers. Could you show me the formula, or at least the principles that would constrain one?
