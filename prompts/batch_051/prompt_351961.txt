I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and if I've reinvented something obvious or am misusing a label, I want that said plainly. I've been using a framework for thinking about how the past gets explained, and before I keep using it I want it stress-tested.

I call it the Commit-Log Model. The idea starts from how you understand a codebase you've inherited: you read the git log backward from the current checkout, find the commit that introduced each surviving feature, and treat that commit as the reason the feature exists. The model says historical explanation often does the same thing with institutions, technologies, and ideas. The present is the current checkout of the repo. An explanation is a reconstructed commit log; every major feature of the present gets traced to an introducing commit, and a past event's importance is measured by the size of its diff against the present — how much of the current build it explains.

So far that's a description of how explanation works. The stronger claim is that this procedure creates a systematic bias. Because we walk backward from the current checkout, we narrate the commits that happened to merge as if they were always going to merge. We treat the surviving branch as the line of development and the abandoned branches as not really part of the story, even though at the time there were many live branches and none of the earlier commits was aimed at our current release. The maintainers were working against their own roadmap, not ours. Carried over to history, this means we underweight possibilities that were real for the people at the time but didn't end up in the present, and we make causal ancestors of the present look more inevitable and more purposeful than they were.

I see myself doing this with the history of computing: I tell the story of the personal computer as a patch series leading to today's phone, and I mention the mainframe mainly as a dead branch, even though to the engineers working on it the mainframe was the live project. That's the move I'm trying to name.

I've heard this resembles something called Whig history, which I know only from secondhand blog posts. I want to know whether the Commit-Log Model is just Whig history translated into version-control language, whether it says anything Whig history doesn't, and where it goes wrong or stops being useful. Two worries in particular: first, the analogy may be weakest exactly where it's most tempting — historical agents sometimes do commit patches against a target they can state, and the present does constrain explanation because it's the one state we actually have; second, the model may confuse explanation with evaluation, since showing that a past event matters for the present is not the same as endorsing it.

Don't lay out options and leave me to choose. Take a definite position: is the Commit-Log Model a useful diagnostic, a misleading metaphor, or something in between? Give the strongest objection to it, and identify at least one kind of historical case it handles badly and what amendment would let it handle that case. Please write in American English.
