In a debate about the situationist critique of virtue ethics, someone I know keeps deflecting the empirical evidence by insisting that virtues are like skills. A pianist, he says, is not equally good in every hall, before every audience, and on every instrument, but we do not conclude from that variability that pianistic skill is an illusion; we conclude that skills are context-sensitive dispositions that can nonetheless be real, cultivated, and explanatory. So, he argues, when situationists show that helping, honesty, or compassion vary with ambient factors—finding a coin, being in a hurry, the presence or absence of onlookers—they have not shown that character traits are unreal or that virtue ethics is in trouble. They have merely shown that virtue, like skill, is exercised reliably only in favorable conditions.

I am supposed to audit this analogy rather than simply reject the conclusion it is used to support. Please give me a detailed audit of the claim that virtues are like skills in the way this argument needs them to be. First, build an explicit correspondence table between the skill case and the virtue case: what plays the role of the musician, the instrument, the audience, the hall, the practice regimen, the performance, and the standards of success? Second, identify the respects in which the mapping is faithful. Third, identify the respects in which it breaks down. In particular, interrogate what the skill picture quietly imports into the moral case—for instance, the role of practice and corrective feedback, the value-neutrality of many skills, the difference between a domain-relative ability and a global trait, and the difference between performing a task well and acting from the right reasons or with the right motive. Finally, state whether the analogy can carry the conclusion my colleague draws from it, whether the situationist challenge survives the analogy largely intact, and what a better analogy would look like if this one cannot do the work assigned to it.
