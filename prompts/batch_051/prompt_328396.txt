I run the review desk at a mid-size foundation that funds community-level anti-violence and anti-poverty programs. Our proposal template requires every project to include a theory of change, and we've held an unofficial house rule that no mechanism counts unless it can be restated in terms of individual people: beliefs, choices, opportunities, behaviors, relationships. The rule came out of a course I took on methodological individualism, and I mostly like it because it stops grantees from hand-waving with phrases like structural racism, social norms, or community trust as if those were causes floating above actual people. But I keep hitting cases where the individual-level translation seems to lose the actual intervention. A grantee wants to reduce retaliatory shootings by changing how a whole neighborhood reads public acts of disrespect. Another wants to lower evictions by shifting the political equilibrium between tenant councils and landlords. My reviewers want to know: is this a legitimate mechanism the proposal can rest on, or is it a red flag that the applicant hasn't done the micro-level work?

Write me a reusable screening procedure my reviewers can follow, in about 30 minutes per proposal, to decide when a social-structural explanation is acceptable in a theory of change and when it has to be sent back for individual-level reconstruction. I want it to keep the useful core of the individualism rule without becoming a dumb ban on any sentence containing the word norm. Give me a checklist or decision tree, then two or three worked applications to the kinds of cases above, including at least one where the procedure forces us to reject a proposal we would otherwise like (or approve one we would otherwise hate). Then tell me honestly which cases the procedure can't settle, where the reviewer has to make a call and I have to sign off on it. Assume my reviewers have no philosophy background and won't read anything longer then two pages.
