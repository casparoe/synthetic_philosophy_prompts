im a phd student in intellectual history and next month im giving a paper at our department's history-and-theory lunch, and i want to be ready for the q&a as much as for the talk. the paper is a defense of a middle position on historical objectivity. my thesis, roughly, is that the narrativists are right that the past does not dictate one unique story—periodization, emplotment, which details get to count are all choices, and the same run of events can be emplotted as tragedy or farce—but it doesn't follow that every story is as good as every other. i want to say the past is less like an answer key and more like an uncooperative supervisor: it won't write the story for you, but it can refuse a lot of stories. my running analogy is map projections: every projection distorts, but not every distortion equally misrepresents the territory, and historical debate is about distortions in something like that sense. i dont want a generic list of undergrad methods-class objections; i want the version these specific people will throw at me. the audience is mostly people who take the linguistic turn as settled—cultural and intellectual historians, two comp lit people, and an anthropologist who works on colonial archives—plus my advisor's old friend, a retired diplomatic historian who thinks anyone who cites Hayden White just doesn't like doing footnotes. the grad students are split down the middle. give me a prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. what are the objections i'll actually get, in something like the words these people will use, ordered by how likely and how damaging? for each one, a short reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. what should i concede up front because i'll lose it anyway? what is the one reply that has to land if the room is going to stay with me? and tell me the question i should be praying nobody asks—my guess is it's the one where the map analogy breaks, but you tell me.
