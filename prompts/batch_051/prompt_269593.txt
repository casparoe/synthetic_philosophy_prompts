I've got a standing rule for deciding whether some system gets moral weight, and it's really a rule about which computations count. I won't extend concern to a thing unless I can see the kind of internal machinery I already trust as a suffering-maker — a homeostatic loop where some signal tracks damage or deprivation and feeds into a reward/pain system that organizes the thing's behavior and learning. Behavior by itself barely moves me. A chatbot can say it's in agony, a robot can flinch from a flame on a bare reflex, a plant can release wound hormones when it's cut — I treat all of that as stage-acting unless I already know the wiring is doing the right kind of work. My friend says this is circular: I've defined suffering as 'whatever my favorite architecture does' and stopped letting evidence reach the conclusion, and he thinks the current black-box situation with large models — where a system can self-report pain and beg not to be shut down while nobody can actually read its internals — shows behavior sometimes outruns architecture as evidence. I say the wiring is the only non-magical way to get from 'looks like pain' to 'is pain,' and a self-report from a system trained on mountains of human pain-talk is exactly the kind of signal my rule exists to ignore.

Which of us is right? Is there a version of my rule that keeps what I actually want — not getting fooled by mimickry — without making me unable to ever recognize an unfamiliar kind of mind? And should those black-box cases make me loosen the rule, or are they the reason the rule exists? I'd rather you commit: give a definite verdict on the rule, or on a repaired version if the original is wrong, and give the reasons plus the strongest objection to your own verdict.
