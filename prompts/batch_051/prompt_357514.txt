i've got a policy about disagreements and I want to know if it's actually defensible or just a way of protecting my ego. The rule is this. When someone disagrees with me who I genuinely think is about as likely to be right as I am—same subject matter, comparable care, all that—I do the conciliatory thing about the specific question. I lower my confidence, sometimes to 50/50, and I really do take their view as seriously as mine. But I don't let the same disagreement lower my confidence in my own general reliability at that kind of question. Last week this came up with a friend over what a state ballot measure was actually going to do. We both read up on it, we're about equally careful about this stuff, and we came out opposite. I dropped to roughly 50/50 about the measure. But my estimate of how good I am at getting ballot measures right didn't move at all, and I didn't think that was a bug. My reasoning was that the disagreement had already moved my belief, so also lowering my general self-confidence would be paying for the same evidence twice—double-counting. My friend says that's convenient: if I really treated him as my equal, I should lower both, because a disagreement is exactly the sort of thing that tells you your machinery misfired. But I worry that if I lowered my general self-trust every time I disagreed, one disagreement would leak into everything else I believe on the basis of that same competence. So: is this policy defensible? Under what assumptions would the evidence update the specific belief but leave the general self-estimate unchanged? What would have to be true for the double-counting argument to work, and how would I tell from the inside whether I'm reasoning well or just quarantining each mistake as a one-off? At the very end, put your verdict as the last line in \answer{...} using one of: defensible, not defensible, or defensible only with changes.
