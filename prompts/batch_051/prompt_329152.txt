I am a retired chemist who has come to philosophy late, and I have been asked to lead a two-hour discussion for a reading group of other retired scientists on the question Taurek raises about whether, when we can rescue either one stranger or five strangers, the greater number should count in our decision. I do not want an explanation of the argument; I want a teaching plan. What is the order in which the ideas must be introduced—and what depends on what in that sequence—so that the position that the numbers should not count is understood as a claim about reasons for action rather than as a denial of arithmetic or an indifference to lives? Which single rescue case should carry the session, and what features must it have, and lack, to keep the discussion from drifting into questions of cost, risk, or professional duty? Which misconceptions should I expect from scientifically trained people who are new to ethics—for instance, that Taurek denies that five deaths are a worse outcome, or that he thinks it would be wrong to save the five—and at what point in the sequence should each be headed off? What should I leave out at this level, and what final question or brief example would tell me whether someone has genuinely grasped that the issue is whether distinct claims may be added together rather than whether lives have value?
