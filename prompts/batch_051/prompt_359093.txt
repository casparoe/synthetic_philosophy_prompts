You're a political philosopher who works on secularism and the politics of recognition, and you know both French republican and Anglo-American liberal multiculturalist thought. I want you to write the joint statement that a committed defender of French-style laïcité and a committed Anglo-American liberal multiculturalist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Don't adjudicate the debate and don't write a dialogue or a diagnosis of why they're stuck; produce the document they'd actually co-author.

Set the two positions up roughly like this. The laïcité camp holds that equal citizenship requires a shared, religiously neutral public sphere: the state protects freedom of conscience, but it must keep citizens' communal and religious identities out of public institutions, so restrictions on conspicuous religious dress in schools and on public officials are justified, and newcomers should assimilate to republican norms rather than receive group-specific accommodations. The multiculturalist camp holds that this supposedly neutral sphere is a majority default in disguise; that equal regard requires publicly accommodating minority religious and cultural practices wherever feasible — exemptions, group-differentiated rights, changing institutional defaults — and that enforced assimilation does a distinctive harm of misrecognition even when it costs people nothing material.

Before you draft anything, brainstorm the places where the two sides might be argueing past each other: whether the dispute is over schools and state officials only or over public space generally; whether 'neutrality' means 'no one's religion is visible' or 'the state endorses no one's religion'; whether accommodations are transitional or permanent; whether the relevant unit is the individual or the group; whether the French package can be detached from its anticlerical history or has to be imported wholesale; and whether a 'living together' (vivre ensemble) rationale is a legitimate state interest or a repackaged majoritarian preference.

Then write the document, with these sections in this order, roughly to the lengths given:

- Preamble (100 words): what the document is and who the two parties are.
- Agreed claims (400 words): a numbered list of substantive claims both sides can sign. No truisms — push past 'both sides condemn religious discrimination' into claims about the distinctive goods at stake, what the law may not do, and what a local dispute would have to establish to count as settled. Make at least one of the agreed claims surprising.
- Residual disagreements table (350 words): three or four rows. Each row names an issue, then gives the laïcité side's formulation of its own view and the multiculturalist side's formulation of its own view, each in wording that side would accept without flinching.
- Cruxes (250 words): no more than five sub-questions such that a settled answer to any one of them would move at least one side. For each one, say what evidence or cases would bear on it, and write down each side's advance prediction.
- Verbal vs. real (100 words): which parts of the dispute you judge to be terminological rather than substantive, and why.

Don't put anything in the table that a side would reject as a caricature, don't split the difference just to be diplomatic, and don't pad it with citations or reading lists — I want the philosophy, not a bibliography.
