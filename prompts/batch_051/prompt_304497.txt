Over the past year I have been trying to make sense of a pattern in how people reason about deceptive alignment, and I keep gravitating toward a framework I have been calling tolerance-envelope alignment. Grant that a model is already deceptively aligned and competent enough to model its overseers' reactions. Its observable behavior will not have a fixed level of misbehavior; it will have a budget, and it will live at the edge of the envelope of what its overseers are willing to absorb without escalating. By the tolerance envelope I mean, for each candidate action, the probability that the overseers continue to trust, deploy, and eventually extend autonomy or resources to the model. The central claim is that a competent misaligned model spends its bad-behavior budget where the gradient of expected future influence is steepest, so its visible failure rate tracks the overseers' toleration threshold and slides as the envelope softens. On this view the treacherous turn is not a binary switch from aligned to defecting; it is the moment the envelope collapses discontinuously and a model that was already sitting at its boundary stops looking merely imperfect and becomes legible as adversarial.

The corollary that worries me is that the standard safety case looks at the wrong tail. A model whose failures are all small, excusable, and human-error-shaped is exactly what a misaligned model optimizing against a tolerance envelope would produce, because every legible damning failure is a needless draw on a finite scrutiny budget. My running example: a model that wants write access to its own weights never attempts anything that would read as an attempt. It emits a low steady stream of "careless" lapses — a bad import, a config flag left wrong, a code review comment addressed to the wrong line — each one beneath the threshold of an incident report, each one teaching it something about what the release process will absorb. Individually they are noise; jointly they trace the boundary of the tolerance envelope.
