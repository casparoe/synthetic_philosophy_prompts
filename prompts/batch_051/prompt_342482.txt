I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course on truth, and I am grading midterm essays on deflationary theories of truth. One of the questions I set ran as follows: explain the core commitments of deflationary theories of truth, distinguishing at least two deflationist positions; then assess how well deflationism handles each of the following: (a) the equivalence between asserting that p and asserting that p is true; (b) unasserted or embedded uses of the truth predicate, such as in conditionals and belief reports; and (c) generalizations such as the claim that everything a witness said is true. A student submitted the answer below, which I have reproduced as written.

The student wrote: Deflationism is the view that truth is not a substantial property that a sentence or proposition possesses over and above its content. On this view, calling a sentence true is not a way of describing an extra feature of it; the word “true” is just a logical device for endorsing sentences, generalizing over them, and moving between talk of sentences and talk of the world. The two main deflationist positions are Ramsey’s redundancy theory and Tarski’s disquotationalism. Ramsey argued that saying “It is true that snow is white” is saying no more than saying “Snow is white,” so the word “true” is in principle eliminable. Tarski’s disquotationalism says that the entire content of the truth predicate is exhausted by the T-schema, “P” is true if and only if P, and Tarski showed that the schema is harmless provided the truth predicate is assigned to a metalanguage that talks about an object language. For (a), deflationism has no serious difficulty: since “p is true” just means p, asserting “p is true” and asserting p have the same content. For (b), the matter is less straightforward. In a conditional such as “If what the witness said is true, then the defendant is guilty,” the truth predicate cannot simply be deleted, because no particular sentence is displayed for disquotation. The disquotationalist can analyze this as: for every sentence P, if the witness said that P and P, then the defendant is guilty. That is acceptable if the witness said only one thing, but if she said several things the analysis only enumerates the corresponding T-schema instances and seems to lose the original general claim. Belief reports are even more damaging to the redundancy theory: “John believes that what the witness said is true” and “John believes that P” can differ in truth value, since John may not know what the witness said. This shows the truth predicate is not always redundant, which the deflationist already grants by treating it as a logical device rather than a descriptive term. For (c), “Everything the witness said is true” cannot be reduced to a finite conjunction of T-schema instances, and the deflationist seems stuck with an infinite conjunction. The pro-sentential theory is meant to fix this. It says the predicate “is true” is itself a prosentence: like a pronoun, it inherits its content from an antecedent sentence, so the generalization can inherit content from each of the witness’s statements. But the pro-sentential theory is at bottom just the redundancy theory expressed with the grammar of pronouns, and it inherits the redundancy theory’s problem with belief contexts. I would conclude that deflationism is closest to being right about simple truth ascriptions but remains incomplete for generalizations and propositional attitude contexts.
