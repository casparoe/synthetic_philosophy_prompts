In my philosophy of law seminar we have been discussing standards of proof, and an asymmetry in the law unsettles me. In the blue bus hypothetical, a bus hits a parked car and drives off, and the only evidence against the Blue Bus Company is that it operates eighty percent of the buses on that route. A jury would not find the company liable by a preponderance of the evidence on that statistic alone, even though the probability of liability is 0.8. Yet the same jury would find liability on an eyewitness identification of the bus as belonging to the Blue Bus Company even where the circumstances of observation make such identifications only seventy percent reliable. The gatecrasher hypothetical suggests the reluctance runs deep: a randomly selected attendee would not be held liable for entering without paying even when only forty-nine percent of attendees paid, so the odds of nonpayment just barely cross the preponderance threshold. Is there a defensible principle that justifies treating weaker individualized evidence as proof while rejecting stronger purely statistical evidence, or does consistency require the law to revise one of these verdicts? If you defend the asymmetry, address the objection that the eyewitness's testimony is itself a statistical base rate wearing the clothes of a particular witness. Please answer in American English.
