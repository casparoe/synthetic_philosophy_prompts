I have been trying to pinpoint where a recurring disagreement between two colleagues actually lives, and I would like your help in the form of a decomposed probability estimate rather than a verdict. The dispute concerns vote selling, which is usually treated as a paradigm repugnant market: it is possible to buy and sell the thing, but many people hold that putting it up for sale is itself corrupting. Both of my colleagues accept three shared assumptions: that an individual vote has almost no chance of deciding an election, that the law already permits large asymmetries in the conversion of money into political influence through donations, lobbying, and volunteer labor, and that any prohibition on vote selling would be imperfectly enforced. Yet one gives about 85 percent credence to the proposition that the standing prohibition on vote selling is justified all things considered — that is, that a legal, regulated market in votes would be morally impermissible to institute — while the other gives about 25 percent. The first insists that a vote is not an ordinary commodity and that pricing it would corrupt the franchise itself; the second replies that this is an empty gesture at a mysterious harm and that the real work is being done by worries about coercion and downstream electoral consequences.

Please give me your own credence in the same proposition, where the candidate market is specified as follows: any eligible voter may contract, for an openly disclosed price and with both parties to the contract identified, to cast their ballot in a particular election as the buyer directs. Decompose the estimate into at least these terms: (1) the probability that there is a distinctive commodification or corruption harm that cannot be reduced to coercion or to bad electoral outcomes; (2) the probability that, conditional on there being no such distinctive harm, consistency with the already permitted forms of money in politics would make the prohibition difficult to defend; (3) the probability that a real-world vote market would in fact be coercive toward poorer sellers or would systematically degrade governance; and (4) the probability that the prohibition is enforceable enough to amount to more than a symbolic restriction. For each term, give a number and a sentence or two of justification, note any important dependencies among the terms, and then show how you combine them into your overall credence. Finally, tell me which single term your overall credence is most sensitive to and what evidence would move that term by more than 0.2 in either direction, and give your best guess as to which term most likely explains the gap between my two colleagues' numbers.
