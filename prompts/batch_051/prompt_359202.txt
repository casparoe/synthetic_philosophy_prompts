So my uncle's been on the liver transplant list and I fell down the internet rabbit hole about how they decide who gets an organ. From what I can tell, the system mostly ranks people by how sick they are — like, closest to dying wins — not by how many years they'd actually get out of it. A doctor we talked to even said they'd catch heat for giving a liver to a healthier person who'd do great, because the rule is basically sickest first full stop.

I keep trying to pin down what that rule assumes about what's fair. Does it assume that being worse off gives your claim extra weight, over and above the total good the organ would do? Or does it assume that equality of health is just good in itself?

Because here's what bugs me. If equality itself were the point, then making everyone equally sick would count as an improvement, and that's obviously nuts — no one at the hospital would sign off on that. So maybe the policy isnt really egalitarian at the level of outcomes, and its actually trying to express something narrower about giving priority to the worst off. But I can't tell where the priority stops.

What theory of value or fairness does the sickest-first rule presuppose? Does it strictly need that theory, or would the same ranking be consistent with a bunch of different views? And what would the allocation rule have to look like if we replaced it with the view that all that matters is total health gained? Also, is it weird that the same hospital, in a mass casualty situation, tries to save the most lives it can — as if its using two different theories of fairness depending on the day?
