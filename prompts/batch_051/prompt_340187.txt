I keep coming back to a question and I want to see it worked out as fiction rather than argued out. The question is whether a finite mind could ever run out of new experiences. Suppose the grain of your experience is finite — only finitely many distinguishable shades, sounds, textures, combinations of feeling and thought, because your sensory and conceptual resolution has limits. Then the catalogue of experiences you are capable of having is enormous but maybe finite. A creature with perfect, involuntary memory who lives long enough would eventually have had every experience it could have, and from then on every moment would be a second pass through a finished list. But if experience doesn't work like that — if any two moments differ in ways that matter no matter how fine your grain of experience is — then even a finite mind could go on being genuinely surprised forever. That's the fork I want the story to sit inside.

Seed: someone — a person, or something close enough to a person — who has been alive, or at least awake, long enough that they begin to suspect they are nearing the end of the catalogue. They notice moments that are near-perfect duplicates of moments they've already had, and it unsettles them. Write the story from their point of view. Make the story actually think about the idea — what 'running out' would feel like, whether a last experience could even be a coherent idea, what distinguishes repitition from novelty — rather than using it as set dressing. It's fine if it leans one way; I'd just rather it not settle the question too neatly.
