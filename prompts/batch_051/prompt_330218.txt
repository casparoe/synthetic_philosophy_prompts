I want you to run a real historical case through a philosophical lens, and then run the lens through the case. The case is the Placiti cassinesi: a set of notarial records from 960 to 963 in southern Italy, around Capua, Sessa, and Teano, in which a Latin legal frame gives way to witnesses' sworn statements written in a Campanian Romance vernacular. That texture is why the Placiti are routinely treated as the earliest securely dated texts in Italian, the moment the vernacular surfaces as a distinct written language inside institutions that still operate in Latin. The lens is the problem of language individuation: when does a speech form become a language of its own, and in particular, when does Latin become Italian? Bring two positions to bear. The first is the continuity view: linguistic change is gradual, neighbouring generations mostly understand one another, and no point in the chain marks a transition, so talk of the birth of Italian runs into a sorites problem. The second is the pragmatic or metalinguistic view: a language becomes distinct when speakers and institutions treat it as distinct, for instance when it is written as a separate object, deliberately contrasted with Latin, or made to carry a name. I am not asking for a survey of Romance philology; I know the standard birth-certificate narrative and I distrust it. What I want is the disciplined mapping. For the Placiti, say exactly what the object is whose identity is at stake: the witnesses' speech, the scribal representation of that speech, or the later classification by philologists. Then work through the plausible individuating criteria one at a time: mutual intelligibility, structural divergence, diachronic continuity, the conscious contrast between a Latin frame and a vernacular testimony, the legal function of recording sworn statements, and the difference between a spoken continuum and a written record. For each criterion, say what it would license calling the Placiti language: Latin, Italian, neither, or both, and where the criteria pull apart. Then identify which features of the episode each criterion explains and which it misses or distorts. Finally, reverse the direction: does this episode support the pragmatic view of linguistic individuation, the continuity view, or does it show that both are asking the wrong question? I want a definite position on whether the question of when Latin became Italian is a question about the world or about boundary drawing, and whether the Placiti are evidence for the birth of Italian or evidence that birth is the wrong metaphor.
