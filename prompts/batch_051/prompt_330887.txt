I spent 35 years in a physical chemistry lab before I retired, so I'm used to categories doing real work: an element either has a certain electronic configuration or it doesn't. Lately I've been reading philosophy of social science, and it's made me suspicious of alot of the generational talk I hear around town.

Here's my situation. I volunteer as the data-and-research person (by default, because I'm the only one who know's a regression from a hashtag) for a mid-sized nonprofit that runs job training and mentoring for people roughly 16 to 30. Every couple of weeks the board or a funder brings in a claim like "Gen Z won't do rigid schedules" or "Millennials are the first generation that wants purpose over pay," and wants us to redesign programs around it. If we're wrong in one direction we waste six figures and a year of programming; wrong in the other direction and we look out of touch and stop getting referrals. Nobody on staff can tell me where these claims come from.

I want a reusable procedure — a checklist, really — for deciding when a difference attributed to a generation or cohort ("Boomers," "Millennials," "Gen Z") deserves to be treated as real for how we run programs, and when it should be re-described as age or life stage, as the times we happen to be living through, or as noise from a small sample. It has to run in about 15 minutes per claim, using only the data we already have (participant ages, outcomes, intake notes) plus whatever article someone brings to the meeting. And it has to be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Two things I half-understand, and I'd like built in rather than gestured at. First, Ian Hacking's looping kinds: the idea that labeling a group of people can change the group and the label, so a "generation" may be a moving target partly made by the people who study and market to it. Second, the demographers' age-period-cohort problem: age, calendar year, and birth year are locked together, so you can't cleanly separate whether a difference is because people are 20, because it's 2024, or because they were born in 2004. I'd like the procedure to genuinely use that fact, not just repeat it.

Work the procedure through two or three concrete cases. At least one should be: "Gen Z won't do rigid 9-to-5 schedules, so our job placement program should de-emphasize clock-in work." Include one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — e.g., my own pet theory that Generation Alpha is plainly different comes out unsupported. Also say plainly what the procedure cannot do.

One formatting request: after the prose procedure, give me a table with columns: Case (one sentence), Claim under test, Step that catches it, Verdict (treat as real / treat as age / treat as period / treat as noise / can't decide), and Where judgment can't be removed. If a case's verdict doesn't fit any of those, say why in the Verdict cell. No reading list — if you invoke Hacking or the age-period-cohort problem, put the idea into a step, not a footnote.

Thanks.
