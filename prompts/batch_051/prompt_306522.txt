I've been turning this over for a while and would love a second take. I've been reading some Nietzsche lately — not the commentary, just the passages themselves — and the eternal return keeps bugging me because I can't tell which of two things he actually meant by it. On one hand, he seems to use it as a kind of test: imagine a demon tells you that you'll live this exact life again and again, every humiliation, every bit of boredom, forever, and the question is whether you'd bless that or curse it. On the other hand, he sometimes writes as if he literally thought the universe does repeat itself, on the grounds that time is infinite while the possible arrangements of matter are finite, so eventually everything has to loop back.

The problem is that the cosmology that made the second version at least plausible to him has changed a lot since his death. We now have the Big Bang, so the universe looks like it had a beginning rather than an infinite past. We have accelerating expansion, which seems to point toward heat death rather than a collapse and a bounce. And then there is the Poincaré recurrence theorem: in a finite, closed system, after an unimaginably long time the system gets arbitrarily close to an earlier state again — but on those timescales you also get Boltzmann-brain-type problems, where an isolated brain with fake memories of your whole life is vastly more likely to fluctuate into existence than a full repeated universe. So my question is what would Nietzsche have done with all this? Would he take it as killing the literal loop but keep the test, ditch the test, or rework the recurrence into something mythic? And would he think the physical facts even matter for what the demon is asking? I keep landing on the thought that for him the whole point is whether you can affirm your life enough to want it back, and the physics was mostly scaffolding — but Im not sure he'd let himself keep the test once the cosmology is gone.
