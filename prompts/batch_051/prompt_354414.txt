For work I am doing on the nature of collective action and shared intention, I would be grateful for help in extracting the principle that seems to be governing my own intuitive judgements. Below are six cases, each followed by the verdict I find myself giving. I give the verdicts not because I am sure of them, but because they are the data: I want to know whether a single, tolerably simple principle captures them all, and, if not, which verdict should be abandoned.

Case A: the railway walkers. Two strangers leave the same railway platform and follow the same route towards the same housing estate for ten minutes. The pavement is narrow, so they end up alongside one another; they keep identical pace, stop at the same crossing, and turn at the same corner. Neither speaks. Each would be mildly put out if the other suddenly halted, because each would then have to step around the other. Verdict: nothing joint is going on; they are merely walking in parallel.

Case B: the agreed walk. Two colleagues decide over lunch to walk back to the office together. One says that a letter must be posted first; the other replies that they can cut through the park. As they walk, each notices when the other is held up and slows or waits; one watches the crossing signal and tells the other when they can cross. Verdict: a paradigm case of acting together.

Case C: the petition. Twenty thousand strangers sign an online petition against the closure of the only library in the borough. Each reads the same text, adds their name, and intends their signature as a contribution towards the number at which the question must be put onto the council's agenda. No signatory communicates with any other; each knows only that the petition exists and that others are adding to it. Verdict: I do call this acting together, but in a markedly thinner sense than Case B. The jointness lies almost entirely in the shared aim and in the design of the aggregation; there is almost no mutual responsiveness.

Case D: the kitchen brigade. The cooks and waiters of a small restaurant work through a Friday evening for which no one has drawn up a plan and about which no one has given instructions. Nobody speaks during the rush. Each watches the others: a cook sets a plate on the pass a moment before it is collected; a waiter steps aside as a cook carries hot oil through; a request that belongs to another section is passed to the right colleague rather than answered badly. Each treats the completion of the service as something to be brought about together. Verdict: I call this full-blooded joint action, as genuine as Case B, though there is no explicit prior plan or agreement. The jointness seems to be achieved in the doing.

Case E: the scripted developers. Six software engineers, now living in different countries, sign up in January to build a new application described by a specification detailed enough to list every function signature. Each then writes code alone for eleven months, strictly to the specification. The code is merged mechanically by a shared repository; the engineers never message, call, or see one another's screens. Each checks the repository once a week only to confirm that the build still compiles. The application is delivered and works. Verdict: I do count them as acting together, but only in an attenuated, quasi-mechanical way. The jointness is inherited from a plan to which each is now responsive only by conforming to the specification. Something present in Case D is missing here, and I would like to be able to say what it is.

Case F: the assembly-line worker. A worker on an automobile assembly line tightens four bolts on each chassis that passes, once every seventy seconds, for nine hours. The line's pace is set by a machine. The worker has never met the people upstream or downstream, cannot see the finished car, and has never been asked to think of the product as a whole; the job description mentions only the four bolts. They adjust their rhythm to the line in the way one adjusts to a conveyor belt, not in the way one adjusts to a partner. Verdict: I do not count this as joint action at all. The worker is part of a process that is collectively arranged, but they are not, in my judgement, a participant in a joint action with the downstream worker. It resembles Case E in its low degree of interaction, and yet the two cases feel different; I cannot yet say exactly why.

An obvious first pass says that agents act together just when, for each of them, (i) their plan includes the parts of the others as parts of one combined activity, and (ii) they are responsive to the others in executing that plan. But the principle is embarrassed at once. Case C shows almost none of (ii), yet I call it joint; Case D has no such plan before the service begins, yet I call it joint in the fullest sense; Case E has (i) but very little of (ii), and I count it joint only in an attenuated form; while in Case F both conditions are at most barely present and I count it not joint at all. I suspect that one of the two conditions is derivative from something I have not named, or that the conditions are alternatives rather than additive, or that one of my verdicts must go.

Please help me find the simplest principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, framed at the intentional level: in terms of what the participants intend, know, and attend to, not merely in terms of the causal or functional structure of the group. I am not interested in a principle that simply enumerates the six cases.

Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order:

1. The principle (approximately 250 words). State the principle I should adopt, precisely and clause by clause, with each clause paired with the case or cases that motivate it.

2. The competitors (approximately 550 words). State the one or two serious alternative principles that nearly capture my verdicts, and show exactly which cases each handles well and which it mishandles.

3. Further cases (approximately 300 words). Construct two cases I did not list, and for each say what your principle predicts, whether the competitor principles would agree, and which prediction you would defend.

4. The costs (approximately 250 words). Say honestly which of my six verdicts the recommended principle cannot accommodate, or which it overturns, and why that is the least costly concession. If a more complex alternative that saves all six is worse than that concession, say why.
