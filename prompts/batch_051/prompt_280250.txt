I'm putting together a handout for a small rationalist-adjacent reading group, and I want it to be a sorting quiz about antimemes — ideas that are bad at being remembered or spread. The group knows the usual touchstones (qntm's Antimemetics Division stories, LessWrong infohazard threads, the SCP entries) so I don't need the output to explain what an antimeme is from scratch. What I want is an instrument that sorts a concrete case into a type of antimeme, but sorts it by the causal structure of why the idea doesn't replicate, not by whatever label the person happens to know. The split I think matters most: is the idea failing because of something in the idea itself, or something in the environment around it? And then within each arm, distinguish passive decay from actively maintained suppression, and distinguish self-erasing content from plain memetic unviability. That's the line I keep loosing the thread on, so I'd like the tree to force it.

The quiz should land around six or seven leaves. At minimum these five have to appear somewhere as leaves: memetically inert ideas (true, low-arousal, hard to compress — they just don't hook); socially costly ideas (accurate but expensive to hold or repeat); self-erasing ideas (the content itself gives you a reason not to retain or transmit it); context-dependent knowledge (the idea is fine but only survives inside a system, practice, or community that is rotting away); and deliberately unrecorded knowledge (people know it but won't write it down, so it dies with them). Add a sixth or seventh leaf only if the logical space actually forces it. I especially want the unoccupied combinations called out and diagnosed — is a cell empty because it's incoherent, because nobody would want to hold that position, or because nobody has really explored it? The diagnostic questions should be answerable by someone who doesn't know the taxonomy, and they should be concrete: e.g., a question about whether the idea would spread fine if everyone suddenly had an incentive to spread it, versus a question about whether writing it down changes anything. Test it against cases like: a safety rule everyone keeps forgetting; a clinical finding that a popular intervention doesn't actually work and gets rediscovered then re-forgotten every decade; an unwritten quirk of an old machine known only to two retiring technicians; and a mathematical construction you can verify step by step but can't hold in your head as one thought. Walk those cases through the tree in the output so I can see it actually sorts them, and flag any two leaves the tree can't cleanly separate instead of forcing a fake question.

Format the response as a JSON object with these fields: `tree` (nested nodes, each with `question` and an `answers` array of `{option, next}`, where `next` is either a nested node or a leaf id string, and terminal nodes carry a `leaf` id), `leaves` (array of `{id, label, definition, example, awkward_commitment}`), `unoccupied_combinations` (array of `{combination, diagnosis, note}` where diagnosis is one of "incoherent", "unattractive", or "unexplored"), `cases` (array of `{case, sorted_to, question_path, confidence}`), and `separation_note` (a string noting which leaves couldn't be separated and why).
