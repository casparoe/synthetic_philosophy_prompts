I am an undergraduate mathematics major currently taking a year-long sequence in topology and abstract algebra. My instructors repeatedly introduce a definition by calling it the "right" one, and they clearly mean more than that it is equivalent to an earlier definition in the familiar cases: continuity via open sets is said to be the right generalization of the epsilon–delta definition, and the group axioms are said to be the right abstraction of symmetry. I would like a working criterion for what makes a mathematical definition the right one — a criterion I could actually apply to a new proposed definition and get a definite verdict, not a slogan such as natural or having the right morphisms unless you spell out exactly what that amounts to.

Here are the cases I want the criterion to handle.

1. Continuity. Compare (a) epsilon–delta continuity for maps between metric spaces with (b) continuity defined by inverse images of open sets for maps between topological spaces. I am fairly confident that (b) is the right generalization, because it captures the idea that a small change in the input forces a small change in the output without depending on a distance function. But (b) also has consequences that feel like side effects: any map from a discrete space is continuous, and any map into an indiscrete space is continuous. Does this show that (b) overgeneralizes, or is it a harmless consequence that any right definition would have?

2. Symmetry and groups. Compare (a) a set with an associative binary operation (a semigroup), (b) a semigroup with an identity element (a monoid), and (c) a monoid in which every element has an inverse (a group). I am fairly confident that (c) is the right abstraction of symmetry, because a symmetry is something that can be undone. But one-way processes, such as the natural numbers under addition or the evolution of an irreversible system, form only a monoid or a semigroup. Does that mean the group axioms are right only relative to the target of symmetry, and not privileged among algebraic structures in general? If so, what does that reveal about what "right" means?

3. Compactness. On a metric space, sequential compactness (every sequence has a convergent subsequence) and open-cover compactness (every open cover has a finite subcover) coincide. In arbitrary topological spaces they diverge: a sequentially compact space need not be open-cover compact, and an open-cover compact space need not be sequentially compact. I lean toward the open-cover definition as the right one, but mainly because it is the choice that makes the product theorems work; I cannot articulate a deeper reason. If "right" means generalizes without changing the original metric-space meaning, then neither candidate manages that. What should the criterion say about a case in which the right generalization necessarily changes the intuitive content?

4. Measurable sets. In measure theory the measurable sets must form a sigma-algebra: closed under complements and countable unions. The main alternatives are closure under only finite unions (finitely additive measures) or closure under arbitrary unions. The standard choice is usually defended by saying that countable additivity is exactly what the convergence theorems of integration need. Is the sigma-algebra definition the right notion of measurability, or is it a pragmatic choice made relative to a fixed agenda, which might come out differently for a finitely additive theory? I genuinely do not know.

5. Topologies on ordered and computational spaces. In domain theory and the semantics of programming languages, one builds a topology on a partially ordered set — often called the Scott topology — by declaring a subset open iff it is upward closed and inaccessible by directed suprema. The payoff is a tight fit: with this definition, the continuous maps are exactly the monotone functions that preserve directed suprema. But the definition looks engineered backward from a desired class of continuous maps, so any criterion that ties a definition's rightness directly to its making the intended maps come out right threatens to become circular. Does this case force a morphism-based criterion, and if so, how does such a criterion avoid circularity? I have no settled view here.
