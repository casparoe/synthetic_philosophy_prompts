For the last month I have been unable to decide between two postdoctoral positions, and a few nights ago a friend and I had a long argument about what my indecision actually reveals. The positions look, by any accounting I trust, evenly matched, though not identical: one is at a more prestigious institution with better equipment, and the other offers a warmer, more supportive lab and keeps me near family. Every time I come to rest on one, the other's advantages seem to expand. My friend's position, pressed over the course of the evening, is that there is no such thing as two options that are both equally good and genuinely distinct once I stop deceiving myself. Either I have a real preference I have not owned up to, in which case the job is to excavate it, or I do not prefer either, in which case there is nothing left to decide and I should simply pick, perhaps by coin, and stop treating the matter as open. He claims my weeks of hesitation are themselves decisive evidence: someone who was truly indifferent would not hesitate, since no choice would be lost. I resisted this, but I had trouble saying exactly what was wrong with it. I want to say that a choice can be rationally unresolvable without being unimportant, and that being unable to choose is not automatically a confession of hidden preference.

Which of us is closer to right? Is my prolonged indecision best understood as a failure of self-knowledge, as a case of two options whose values cannot be placed on a single scale, or as a case in which preference simply runs out and no further deliberation can help? And if preference does run out, what follows about what I should do: is flipping a coin rationally any different from continuing to wait for a consideration that may never arrive? I am not asking which fellowship to take; I am asking what a state like mine shows about the relationship between preferring and choosing, and whether a rational person can be genuinely caught between two equally good options.
