I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether inverse reinforcement learning and its preference-learning descendants actually recover human preferences or merely rationalize whatever behavior was observed, and I came away with a half-formed idea I would like written up as a proposal. My suspicion is that the familiar identifiability problem — that many reward functions make a given policy optimal — is not the interesting obstacle, because even after the standard regularizers are added, the method still assumes the observed behavior is optimal under the target reward; once you allow that much real behavior comes from habit, social compliance, or error rather than from preference, a "successful" run may not recover a preference in any sense that licenses using the reward for alignment or for predicting what the person will endorse. Please write a one-page workshop-paper proposal (roughly a six-to-eight-week project) centered on the question: what exactly does a successful run of inverse reinforcement learning recover, and which assumptions about the agent are doing the work when we call the output "preferences"? State the question so it has a determinate answer, explain why the existing positions leave it open — roughly, the view that ambiguity plus regularization is the whole problem, and the view that what is recovered is only a revealed preference, a redescription of the behavior — and then set out an approach that could make progress, saying why that approach rather than either another ambiguity-regularizing algorithm or a purely armchair analysis. Also say what a useful deliverable and an honest failure condition would be. I will add references myself, so do not include citations or a bibliography. Start directly with the proposal itself, with no preface or commentary.
