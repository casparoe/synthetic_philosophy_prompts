I'm on the committee revising the preamble to our university's code of conduct, and a colleague has proposed opening it with the sentence "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you." The discussion made clear that the six of us meant at least four different things by it: a strict reciprocity test, an invitation to imagine oneself in the other person's circumstances, an opaque way of saying "respect everyone," and a piece of moral decoration that commits us to nothing. It now falls to me to draft a short memo on what we would actually be adopting, and I don't want to paper over the disagreement with a platitude.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that this one-liner could be making? For each candidate reading, state it as a principle precise enough to be applied; say whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible moral claim, or something false or incoherent; give an example of what it would require or forbid on a campus; and identify which common use of the sentence it corresponds to. Then give your own assessment: is there a reading that is both faithful to the ordinary sentence and worth having in a code of conduct, or would we be better off replacing it with something more explicit—and if so, what? Because I'll be defending the memo in front of people with different prior commitments, I'd also like to know which of the readings you are least confident about, and what evidence or argument would change your mind about it. If the deeper problem is that the sentence has no determinate content outside a specific context of application—so that asking what it "really" means is itself confused—please say that plainly rather than producing a tidy menu.
