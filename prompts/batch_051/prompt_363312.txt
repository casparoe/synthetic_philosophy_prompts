A friend who reads widely in political theory recently gave me the following summary of Francis Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis:

"Fukuyama's argument is that the collapse of Soviet communism showed that liberal democracy had won the battle of ideas. The great ideological struggles that had driven modern history—between liberalism, fascism, and communism—were essentially over, because liberalism's rivals had been discredited. History, in the meaningful sense of the long-term evolution of societies toward better forms of government, had reached its endpoint: liberal democratic capitalism is the final form of human government. Fukuyama was therefore making an empirical prediction about the actual course of world politics: the post-Cold War world would become more peaceful, with liberal democracy spreading gradually and facing no serious ideological challenger. The Yugoslav wars, the rise of China, and the later revival of religious and authoritarian politics show that this prediction failed."

I have also heard Fukuyama's actual position described as more qualified—that he denied he was predicting an end to all conflict, and that he had serious worries about the long-term health of liberal democracies—but my friend says those qualifications don't undermine the central claim, and that later events have simply proven the thesis wrong. I don't know the material well enough to tell whether this summary is fair. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts are accurate, which parts are common simplifications or overstatements, which parts Fukuyama himself would reject, and whether the conclusion that later events refute the thesis follows from the accurate parts of the summary?
