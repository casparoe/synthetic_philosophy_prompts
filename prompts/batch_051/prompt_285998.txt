I am a high school student, and a puzzle has come up in two different parts of my week that I cannot yet resolve. In my statistics class we assign probabilities to almost everything: a spinner has a one-in-four chance of landing on red, a poll carries a margin of error, and a weather forecast gives a 70 percent chance of rain tomorrow; nobody objects to those numbers. But in math club, when I asked what odds the members would accept on a bet that the twin prime conjecture will be proved within twenty years, or what probability they would assign to the claim that P differs from NP, the same people who comfortably gave probabilities to tomorrow's weather insisted that you simply cannot assign a number to an unproven mathematical statement. One friend defended this by saying that a probability is only a measure of ignorance, and since we are plainly ignorant of whether the conjecture is true, we should be able to give a number. Another replied that probabilities make sense only where there is a repeatable chance setup or at least a fact we will eventually observe; a mathematical theorem is already true or false, no experiment ever settles it, and attaching a credence to it is a category mistake rather than a useful estimate. The disagreement has become a running argument, and I cannot tell whether there is a real principle behind this asymmetry or only a social convention about where numbers are permitted to appear.

I want an essay of roughly 800 to 1,000 words that does three things. First, it should state the asymmetry sharply: what exactly is supposed to differ between my uncertainty about a coin toss and my uncertainty about an open conjecture, such as the twin prime conjecture or the question whether the trillionth digit of π is odd before anyone has computed it? Second, it should find the strongest candidate principle that could justify treating empirical uncertainty and logical uncertainty differently, and test that principle honestly against nearby cases, for example a lottery ticket that pays out only if a named conjecture is proved before a fixed deadline, or a market in which people trade contracts on the eventual resolution of the Riemann hypothesis. Third, it should reach a definite verdict: should the asymmetry be kept because it tracks something real, revised because it applies only sometimes, or abandoned because it is an inconsistent habit? The essay should not settle for saying that mathematicians are simply more certain than statisticians; it should examine what a number attached to a theorem would even mean, and under what conditions such numbers would be coherent, including how they ought to change as proofs and refutations arrive.
