I have been reading C. S. Peirce's 1878 essay "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" on my own, and I keep getting stuck on the following passage: "It appears, then, that the rule for attaining the third grade of clearness of apprehension is as follows: Consider what effects, which might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object."

The general idea — that a concept's content is somehow tied to the practical difference it would make — makes sense to me as an engineer. But I can't tell how literally Peirce means it. In particular: does "conceivably" mean he is including effects that never actually occur but could be imagined, so that the meaning of a concept is fixed by a kind of possible-worlds test rather than by anything that ever happens? And is the last sentence a claim about what a concept is, or only about what we can legitimately distinguish — that is, if two beliefs share all their conceivable practical consequences, are they literally the same belief, or just beliefs we have no warrant to treat as different? I keep reaching for an operational-definition reading, but I suspect that flattens the passage.

Could you walk me through what the maxim commits Peirce to, and where a non-philosopher is most likely to misread it? As part of your answer, please say which alternative reading of "conceivably" or "practical bearings" would most change your interpretation, and where you think your own reading is least secure.
