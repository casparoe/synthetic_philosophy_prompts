I'm taking a graduate seminar in Bayesian epistemology, and we've spent the past three weeks on the problem of old evidence: how should we understand the fact that a known datum like the anomaly in Mercury's orbit seems to confirm a later theory like general relativity, even though a strict Bayesian would already assign the datum probability one. Three candidate rules have surfaced in the seminar. The temporal-novelty rule says only observations made after a theory is proposed can confirm it. The use-novelty rule says that already-known facts can confirm just as well as new predictions, provided the theory was not constructed to fit those facts. The surprise-reduction rule says a known fact confirms a theory only when the theory removes a prior anomaly or explains a regularity that was otherwise puzzling; an already-explained or unsurprising fact confers no confirmation even if the theory entails it.

Over the term, our instructor has given the following verdicts. She counts a novel, surprising observation that a theory predicted as strongly confirming. She counts the precession of Mercury's perihelion, known and unexplained for decades before general relativity, as confirming general relativity. She says a regression curve fitted to a scatterplot receives no confirmation from the very points that fixed its coefficients. And she says that a model built with an extra free parameter adjusted to reproduce an already well-explained regularity receives no confirmation from reproducing that regularity.

Next week she will rule on this case. A purely formal model of plant branching, developed without consulting any data and without free parameters fitted to observations, is later noticed to entail a documented relation between branch angle and leaf count that botanists have long known and already explain through an established mechanistic account. The relation is familiar, unsurprising, and not the subject of any controversy. Given her track record, should she say this relation confirms the model, or not? I want your prediction of her verdict, the strongest statement of the rule she seems to be running, which candidate rule the case would eliminate, and the further case that would most efficiently distinguish the rules that remain.
