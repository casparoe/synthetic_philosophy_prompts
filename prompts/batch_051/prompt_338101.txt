I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about Ibn Khaldun's theory of dynastic cycles. On my friend's telling, the pattern is this: a cohesive group from the periphery conquers a sedentary state through what Ibn Khaldun called asabiyya, a rough group solidarity; within three or four generations, urban luxury, court intrigue, and the ruler's need to break his own kinsmen erode that solidarity; and the dynasty decays until a fresher, more cohesive group from the periphery replaces it. My friend thinks this is close to a law of premodern politics. I kept objecting that the cycle, if real, runs through particular institutions and incentives, and that a clear-eyed founder might have built different ones. We could not settle it in the abstract, so I want you to reason through a counterfactual.

Take a large agrarian sultanate in the Islamic world around 1500, of the kind Ibn Khaldun described: a settled peasant base, an urban court, a semi-pastoral tribal periphery that supplies the best soldiers, and a ruling group of tribal origin that has already begun to settle in the capital and live off its revenues. Suppose its founders are convinced the cycle is real and enact one constitutional rule meant to break it: succession may never be hereditary. When a ruler dies, a council of provincial commanders must choose the next ruler from a different clan than the deceased, and the new ruler's own clan is barred from the top military and fiscal offices for one generation. Assume the rule is actually enforced for at least the first few successions; everything else about the state remains as it would otherwise have been.

Predict, with your reasoning explicit, how this polity looks after two hundred years. I want a chain of mechanisms and incentives rather than a story: what the rule does to the interests of clans, provincial commanders, and the ruler himself; where the old cycle is genuinely interrupted and where it reappears in a new form; which effects you are confident about and which are guesses; and what would have to hold for your prediction to be wrong. I am less interested in a yes-or-no answer than in whether the trajectory is best described after two hundred years as the same cycle, a transformed cycle, or a genuine escape from cyclical dynamics.

If I have posed this badly — for example, if the rule would collapse before it could test the cycle, or if asabiyya is too vague to carry the explanatory weight I am putting on it — say so plainly rather than complying with what I asked.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Make each item one predicted mechanism or outcome, stated in a sentence or two, and include how confident you are in that link.
