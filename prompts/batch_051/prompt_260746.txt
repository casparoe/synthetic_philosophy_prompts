Last Friday at a department happy hour two of my colleagues got into it over whether LLM hallucination is lying, bullshit, or just error, and I ended up listening long enough that I'd like to use the exchange in my philosophy of technology seminar. Problem is, I'm not sure the moves actualy land the way the speakers think they do, and I don't want to present it as obviously going one way. Here's the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Maria said it's bullshit in Frankfurt's sense, because for Frankfurt the bullshitter isn't someone who hides the truth so much as someone who is indifferent to it, concerned only with whether the utterance is useful or impressive. An LLM producing a confident-sounding false answer is almost a laboratory case of that: no hidden intent to deceive, but also no concern with how things are.

(2) David said that move is too quick. Bullshitting, on any account worth taking seriously, is something a person does — a purposive activity. An LLM doesn't care about anything and has no purposes at all. So its output can't be bullshit any more than a faulty thermometer can be said to lie. Whatever's wrong with it is error.

(3) Maria answered that he was locating the intention at the wrong level. If the people who design and deploy the system optimize for fluent, confident, plausible output rather than verified output — and know they're doing so — then the bullshitter is the design team or the company, and the model is more like their script. The talking points a press secretary reads can be bullshit even though the paper has no mind.

(4) David said that proves too much, since it would make any under-tested product a bullshitter: the calculator manufacturer who cares more about shipping than testing isn't making the calculator bullshit when a bug returns 2+2=5. And anyway, an LLM is literally trained to predict the next token of human text, and human text is mostly true, so the system does aim at truth in a statistical sense; when it misses, that's error, not bullshit.

(5) Maria said 'aims at truth in a statistical sense' is doing more work than it can bear. Predicting what a human would type next is not the same as representing the world. If people are systematically confused about something, the best predictor of what they'll say is also confused. And the trainer can't inherit the truth-conduciveness of the average training token.

I had to leave right after that, before anyone tried to settle it. Could you go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move? For each of the five moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify the rating; then say who has the stronger overall position; and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
