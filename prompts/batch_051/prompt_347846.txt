One of my moral psych profs is weirdly into the old virtue ethics vs situationism fight, and by now she's got a pretty stable personal system for ruling on whether a candidate trait gets to be a real virtue. I've kept a list of her verdicts over the term:

- Conscientiousness: real virtue.
- Fairness about dividing shared resources: real virtue.
- Being the life of the party: not a virtue — she says it just doesn't hold together well across settings.
- Physical thrill-seeking: not a virtue, same reason: skydivers aren't especially likely to take other kinds of risks.

She keeps saying she rejects traits on two different grounds, but I haven't seen her make the two collide yet. One ground is something like: if a trait doesn't show decent cross-situational consistency, it can't be a virtue. The other is more like: even a highly consistent disposition has to be a thick, whole-life excellence of character to count, not just a narrow habit or preference. I'm trying to figure out which one she'd let win when they point in different directions.

The next case I have to predict is orderliness (keeping one's space and schedule in order). In her own data, orderliness is surprisingly stable across home, work, and money contexts — more stable than fairness, maybe. But it also looks, on its face, like exactly the kind of narrow habit her second ground is suppose to rule out.

What would she say, and why? I'm less interested in the yes/no then in the rule or rules she's actually running: I can see at least two that fit her track record so far, and they diverge on orderliness. Give me the candidate rules, what each predicts for orderliness, your best guess about her verdict with the reasons for it, and then one more case I could bring up that would best tell the rules apart.
