I'm on the rules commitee for a co-op with about 400 members. We elect a seven-person board every year and vote on bylaws and spending proposals four or five times a year; the elections decide who sets dues and hires the management company, so people care a lot. We use ranked-choice ballots for the board, but every couple of years someone shows that a voting block gamed the last election, and then somebody else proposes we switch to Borda, approval, instant-runoff-with-a-runoff, something like that. The committee is all volunteers, nobody on it has a math background, and we get maybe 40 minutes per proposal before the room gets restless. I want something we can actually run, not a lecture.

I half-remember a theorem, Gibbard–Satterthwaite I think, that says roughly: with three or more options on a ranked ballot, any rule that always picks a single winner, doesn't just rule out some candidates from ever winning, and isn't a dictatorship can be gamed by someone misreporting their preferences (the jargon for "can't be gamed" is "strategy-proof," but nobody in the room will say that). But I don't know exactly what counts as being gamed or as a dictatorship, and I'm not sure whether it covers the methods people keep proposing.

Please write a checklist-style decision procedure my committee can use when a voting rule is proposed or when someone complains the current one is being gamed. Ask the questions in order; say what counts as a good-enough answer at each step; say where the procedure stops with a no and where it hands the decision to the full membership because it's a values call rather than a math one. Keep the real distinctions from the theory even if we never name the theorem in the room. For example: does the rule always produce a single winner? is there a voter whose reported ranking decides the result no matter what everyone else reports? can someone get a better outcome by turning in a ranking that isn't their honest one? Which of these have to be checked, in what order, which shortcuts are safe, and which simplifications would ruin the procedure? Flag the places where the procedure will misfire because the theorem doesn't apply—approval voting, hybrids—and mark the steps where judgement can't be eliminated.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) adopt Borda count for board elections, (b) keep instant-runoff but let the board chair break ties by naming the winner, and (c) use approval voting to pass a bylaws amendment. Make sure at least one of the worked examples comes out with an answer we won't like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot decide for us.
