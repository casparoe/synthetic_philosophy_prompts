I keep returning to a parallel between a short passage in Turing's paper on the imitation game and the way contemporary evaluation of large language models handles benchmark contamination. Turing, as I read him, did not treat extrasensory perception as a joke or as a possibility that had to be settled before the game could be taken seriously; he treated it as a live threat to the validity of the game's verdict, and he thought the appropriate response was not to abandon the test but to run it under a condition that closes off the telepathic channel — a room built to block such communication — so that the game's outcome could only be produced through the conversational capacities the game is meant to probe. Contemporary evaluation hygiene seems to me to make the same move. Evaluators increasingly treat contamination as a standing risk rather than a fantasy, and they respond by redesigning the test situation so that the verdict no longer depends on a prior confirmation, for every model and every test item, that nothing leaked: test sets assembled after the training cutoff or otherwise kept private, dynamically regenerated benchmarks, canary strings that make inclusion detectable, and reporting norms that discount results on known-to-have-leaked corpora.

In both cases the structure appears to be as follows: we want to measure a capacity; we notice a route by which the measurement could succeed without that capacity; and rather than resting the measurement on a prior resolution of whether that route is real or was actually used, we alter the test situation so the route cannot carry the result, then treat performance inside the altered situation as evidence of the capacity. Is this a deep identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? State the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true of each side for the other side's conclusions to transfer, and name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false.
