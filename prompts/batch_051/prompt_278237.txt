In debates about whether historical atrocities can be ranked, my own position is that ranking the magnitude of historical evils is not only possible but sometimes morally required, and that a principled refusal to make any such comparisons out of reverence for the victims amounts to a kind of moral evasion. It is not the crude thesis that every atrocity can be assigned a precise numerical score or that all atrocities admit of a complete ordering; it is the thesis that some comparisons — between a genocide and an isolated massacre, say — are determinate and important enough to assert and act on.

What are the genuine points of vulnerability in this position, and which considerations would refute it outright rather than merely complicate it? I want a map of the position's exposure, not a list of objections, and I would like the relevant considerations sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Address the strongest versions of the case against commensuration — against there being any common scale on which different atrocities can be measured — for instance, that different atrocities differ in kind rather than degree, that any such scale abstracts away from what made each event the distinctive evil it was, and that the act of ranking itself wrongs the victims. For each, say whether a defender of the position would concede it as a real limitation and how much it should move me. Finally, say honestly whether the position, as qualified above, has been insulated from refutation by retreating from a general claim about ranking to the claim that only some comparisons are possible or warranted whenever it is pressed. If it has been so insulated, is that a defect in a view of this kind, or an appropriate modesty about what comparisons can show?
