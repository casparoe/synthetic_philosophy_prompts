I keep seeing Abelard's Scito te ipsum recommended as this underrated early source on moral psychology, so I finally read a modern translation of the relevant chunks, and a philosophy vlogger I follow summarized his view like this: Abelard denies that the external act adds anything to sin; sin is nothing but consent to an evil intention, so if you want to kill someone but refuse to go along with the desire, there is no sin, while if you consent but the killing never happens, you are exactly as guilty as if you had actually done it; and therefore, since only the inner consent matters, the law has no real business treating people differently based on what they actually did. The first part seems defensible as a reading, but the jump to the law seems off to me, and I dont want to just trust my vibes.

The passage I keep coming back to is the one where Abelard says the performance of an act adds nothing to the sin, because the guilt is already complete at consent, and then he spends some time distinguishing how God assesses things from how human courts assess them, since human judges can only see what is public and punish the deed proportionately, whereas God punishes the mind. As I understand it, that distinction is supposed to do real work: the act does add something to the civil penalty, even if not to the sin. But maybe I am importing that because it makes the view less weird.
