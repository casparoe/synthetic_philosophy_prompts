In my formal epistemology seminar we've spent the last three weeks on Bayesian convergence results—the theorems that say, given enough shared evidence, agents starting from different priors will come to agree on the truth. Everyone in the room agrees the math is right; what we're stuck on is what the theorems actually buy us. One camp says they substantially deflate the problem of the priors: if any remotely reasonable prior eventually washes out, the intial arbitrariness just doesn't matter much in practice. The other camp says the limit results are doing no real work, because convergence can be arbitrarily slow, the conditions (like agreeing in advance on which hypotheses get prior probability zero) smuggle in exactly the part that was contested, and real agents never see infinite evidence anyway, so the prior is never actually gone at any finite time. The same examples have now been trotted out three weeks running and no one has moved.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about the math, or even about how fast is fast enough. It's a disagreement about what a solution to the problem of the priors would have to look like—whether the standard is finite-time norms, asymptotic ones, or something about decisions and predictions rather than beliefs. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, and given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a sharper theorem, a new distinction, a change of question, or a reason to think nobody should move.
