Okay here's a rough idea for a short essay I'm kicking around. Working title: "Preregistration was a frequentist fix; what would the Bayesian version even be?"

The basic thought is that the replication crisis got told mostly as a story about null hypothesis significance testing. P-hacking, optional stopping, HARKing, running a dozen analyses and reporting the one that hits p < .05. The remedy people converged on was preregistration: fix your pipeline in advance so theres really only one test, or at least you know how many tests there are and can correct honestly.

But if you take the Bayesian line seriously, the problem doesn't vanish, it just relocates. A Bayesian analysis has its own researcher degrees of freedom: choice of likelihood, family of priors, hyperpriors, stopping rule (which matters for the likelihood even if you're not doing error control), what posterior summary to report, what counts as a robust effect. You can run ten priors and report the one where the effect looks solid, and that's p-hacking in spirit, but it isn't p-hacking. So my question is: why hasn't a "preregister your prior and likelihood" norm caught on the way preregistration did?

Then I want to make three points. First, the Bayesian analogue is under-discussed partly because Bayesian methods are still sold as the fix for the crisis rather than another venue where the same thing can happen. Second, its actually harder to preregister a prior than a test, because a prior is supposed to encode background knowledge that you often cant fully specify in advance, and any one formal prior understates your actual uncertainty. So there's a real tension between honest prior elicitation and the fix-it-in-advance ideal. Third, maybe the right unit isnt the prior at all, but something like a preregistered decision-relevant posterior summary, plus a stated range of robustness checks over priors. That way the thing you commit to in advance is the claim you're willing to stand behind after seeing how it moves under prior variation.

Obvious objections I can already see: (a) if the prior and likelihood are reported, readers can just redo it, so it's less severe than p-hacking; though in practice almost nobody redoes it, and sensitivity analyses can themselves be cherry-picked. (b) priors are supposed to be subjective, so there's no correct prior to fix; but I think that's exactly what makes naive preregistration weird, and I havent fully handled it. (c) Maybe the whole comparison is a catagory mistake because preregistration was about error control, and Bayesians don't claim error control.

Could you give me comments on the idea, especially on whether point two is actually right, and whether point three is a real solution or just moving the goalposts? Also: where does the analogy between researcher degrees of freedom and p-hacking seriously break down? And at the end, please say how confident you are in the comments and single out the one point you're least sure about.
