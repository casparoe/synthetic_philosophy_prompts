An AI lab is attempting to build a lightweight institutional stand-in for coherent extrapolated volition — roughly, the preferences people would converge on if they were more informed, more deliberative, and less at the mercy of transient impulse — without having to endorse any single philosophical account of what idealization consists in. Their proposal is a standing mechanism for setting the system's value defaults. For each high-stakes tradeoff the model must resolve by default (how heavily to weight a user's current stated wishes against their inferred longer-term interests, which categories of content to treat as harmful, when to nudge rather than comply), a stratified random panel of a few hundred affected users convenes for three days. Panelists read a balanced docket of steelmanned arguments for each candidate default, produced by paid advocates whose compensation depends only on how accurately and forcefully they present the position assigned to them, not on which position wins. At the end of the three days, each panelist records a first-choice default and a brief statement of the consideration that would most change their mind. The distinctive feature is the payment rule: panelists are paid their full honorarium only if, when contacted without warning eight to twelve weeks later and asked the same question cold, they give the same first choice they gave after deliberation; panelists who report that they have changed their mind forfeit the honorarium. The aggregate of the surviving first choices becomes the model's default until the next scheduled review.

I do not want a verdict on whether this is a legitimate way to run a lab or a sound reading of coherent extrapolated volition. Treat it as a mechanism and work out how it would actually behave. In particular: what does the payment rule incentivize among panelists, what does the advocate-compensation rule incentivize among the people who write the docket, and what does the existence of the mechanism incentivize among the lab that convenes it and among users who know their preferences are being elicited on this schedule? Where can the mechanism be gamed, where is it likely to systematically misrepresent exactly the thing it is meant to recover — post-reflection, post-information preferences — and which of the failure modes are repairable by tweaking the protocol rather than being inherent to the goal of eliciting stable reflective preferences?
