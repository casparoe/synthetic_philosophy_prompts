Dont judge me for this, but I'm building a list of arguments that moral particularists can press against generalism — the view that moral judgment essentially works by applying principles to cases. As far as I can tell these are the canonical four:

1. The holism of reasons argument: a feature that counts in favor of an action in one context can count against it, or not count at all, in another, so no principle that treats it as an atomistic reason is going to be right.
2. The uncodifiability argument: any principle simple enough to actually guide action has exceptions, and once you add all the exceptions its either false as a general claim or so hedged that it's useless — so generalism is stuck between false rules and empty ones.
3. The perception/capacity argument: competent moral judgment looks like a trained capacity to see what a case calls for, not like subsuming the case under a rule, and principles look like post hoc rationalizations.
4. The shapelessness argument: the class of right actions has no common non-moral shape for a principle to latch onto, so there is nothing there to codify in the first place.

Is there another argument for particularism I'm missing? I want something genuinely different from these four — not a restatement, combination, or special case of them. Ideally something that doesn't lean on the metaphysics of reasons at all, maybe grounded in moral psychology or in how moral discourse actually functions socially, though if you have a fifth metaphysics-of-reasons argument that's genuinely distinct I'll take it.
