I keep turning over the 2000 US presidential election — Bush, Gore, Nader, Florida, hanging chads, the Supreme Court stopping the recount — and I want it analyzed as a social-choice case rather than as legal history. I know the basic facts, so don't bother looking anything up: give me the lens. If voters have full preference orderings over Bush, Gore and Nader, what did the relevant orderings plausibly look like for the blocs that mattered (regular partisans, would-be Nader voters in Florida, etc.)? Treat the actual system — state plurality contests plus the electoral college plus the recount fight — as if it were a social choice function, and lay out exactly where it fails Arrow's conditions (IIA seems the obvious one with Nader as a spoiler, but also whatever else you see). Is it right, on this reading, that Bush was the winner only because the agenda and the counting were shaped a particular way, and that a Condorcet winner might well have been Gore? Then run it in the other direction: what does this one messy case do to the theory? Does it show Arrow was describing a real crisis of democratic legitimacy, or does it show the theorem just doesn't bite on actual elections because they were never aggregating full preference orderings into a transitive "will of the people" in the first place? And be explicit about the difference between settled stuff in social choice and seriously contested readings — I don't want "Arrow proves democracy is impossible" or "the Court just picked Bush" smuggled in as if everyone in the field agrees with them. Seperate what the models actually show from what people merely hope they show.
