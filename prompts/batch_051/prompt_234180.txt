A position I keep pushing in my seminar is that an archive is not a neutral repository of whatever happens to have survived; it is an apparatus of selection with consequences for who can later make claims, prove an identity, or be remembered. Decisions about what is collected, described, preserved, restricted, or destroyed shape in advance what it is possible to say on the basis of the record. On this view a silence in the sources is not a mere absence but a produced condition, and it tends to be distributed unevenly: institutions and elites are thickly documented, while the poor, the defeated, and the ordinary leave faint traces, so that when the record fails they are often the ones least able to reconstruct themselves through other channels.

I want to test this picture against a concrete case, the 1973 fire at the National Personnel Records Center outside St. Louis, which destroyed millions of individual military personnel files, chiefly for Army and Air Force veterans discharged between roughly the First World War and the early 1960s. The causes were mundane — overcrowded shelving, combustible storage, no sprinklers — and no one has argued the fire was deliberate. Veterans seeking pensions, medical benefits, burial honors, or proof of service have since had to rebuild the record from unit rosters, pay stubs, letters, and sworn affidavits. A colleague objects that this is exactly the wrong case for the theory: an accident is not an exercise of power, and the lens fits only willful destruction, such as a regime burning its own files. Analyze the fire through the lens of the archival-politics position and tell me whether the objection succeeds. Specifically: who is silenced here, and in what sense of "silence"? Should we say the silence was produced by the Records Center's custodial decisions even though no one intended it? What does the reconstruction effort reveal about the official archive's supposed centrality? And does the case ultimately support the theory, complicate it, or show that it needs a sharper account of the difference between deliberate suppression and institutional neglect?
