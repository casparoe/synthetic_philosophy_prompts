Two people in my longtermism reading group—Ilana and Felix—got into an argument about the hinge-of-history hypothesis, and I genuinely couldn't decide who was right. The version they were debating goes roughly like this: our time is unusually high-stakes because decisions made now could shape or close off civilization's long-run trajectory, so the expected value per decade is higher now than in most of history. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Ilana: The hinge claim isn't generational vanity; it falls out of straightforward expected-value reasoning. If there is a non-negligible probability of existential catastrophe this century, and the future is astronomically large, then the expected value of decisions made in this century dwarfs the expected value of decisions made in, say, the 1400s. That makes the present special in exactly the sense that matters for deciding where to put attention and effort.

Felix: That calculation quietly assumes the probabilities themselves are sharply concentrated in the present. But every era's inside view makes its own uncertainties feel pivotal. From inside the 1400s, the printing press and contact with the Americas must have looked like enormous branch points, and with hindsight they were. The feeling that our own risks are uniquely load-bearing is exactly what availability bias would produce.

Ilana: The difference isn't psychological detail; it's technological scale. No fifteenth-century technology could have ended the species. Nuclear war, engineered pandemics, and misaligned AI could. That's a newly acquired capacity to close off the entire future, not just to shift which civilization wins. The hinge claim is about that capacity, not about how uncertain things feel.

Felix: Scale matters only together with a lock-in mechanism. Existential risk per year during the Cuban Missile Crisis was plausibly higher than it is now, yet nothing got locked in; we walked the risk back. For "hinge" to mean more than "these risks are serious," you need an account of how present choices either foreclose future choices or make some trajectory permanent. Without specifying that mechanism for AI or bio, the hinge label adds nothing to the ordinary claim that current risks are large.

Ilana: A hinge doesn't have to be a single event or year. It can be a bounded period—the coming decade or century—during which the trajectory becomes path-dependent. And it's false that past episodes produced no lock-in: the nuclear era locked in a security regime, and the Allied victory locked in a set of international institutions. The claim is just that the current period is where the largest expected branch of possible futures sits nearest the present.

Felix: But once you allow century-wide hinges and count everything from the agricultural revolution to the Industrial Revolution as a lock-in event, a great many periods count, and the claim that we live in the hinge loses its edge. Worse, the claim that expected value per decade is unusually high now is unfalsifiable without agreed probabilities, and those probabilities are precisely what different camps disagree about. The word "hinge" compresses a stack of disputed forecasts into a single intuition pump. What predictive work is it doing?

Ilana: The work is upstream resource allocation. Nobody defending the hinge claims certainty; the claim is that the question of whether now is pivotal should dominate resource allocation because the asymmetry is so lopsided. If there's even a modest chance we are at a hinge, and the future is astronomically large, then the expected value of taking the hinge seriously is enormous. Unfalsifiability, if it is a problem at all, is a problem for all long-run forecasting, not an objection to the hinge specifically.

Felix: That gets the decision theory backwards. The asymmetry cuts in the other direction too: if there is also some chance that the real hinge lies elsewhere in time, then uncertainty alone doesn't privilege the present. You need the present to have higher expected value per decade than competing candidate periods, not merely to be one uncertain candidate among others. The fallback to "even a small chance" is a Pascalian argument for acting as if the hinge is true; it is not an argument that it is true, and the two are doing different work.

Then say who is more right overall, if either—or whether the dispute is genuinely unresolved. Also tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing that would move the discussion forward. Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and don't present a contested position as the consensus—including, if relevant, the hinge-of-history hypothesis itself.
