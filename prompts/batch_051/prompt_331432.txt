I have come to think that the mainstream Buddhist doctrine of karma is best reconstructed not as a cosmic law of retribution, a theodicy, or a kind of fatalism, but as a theory of moral desert: the view that an agent's happiness and suffering across the cycle of rebirth are fitting or merited responses to their own past intentional actions, in approximately the sense of desert discussed in analytic moral philosophy. On this reconstruction, karma concerns proportionality, fittingness, and the agent's own conduct, with two distinctive commitments: the agent need not remember the action, and the settling of the account may be postponed to a later life.

Tell me what would count against this reconstruction. I want the defeaters considered at two levels: objections to karma as a substantive theory of desert, and objections to reading the tradition as a desert theory in the first place. Among the first: the claim that not all suffering is karmic in origin, since some traditions explicitly attribute illness or injury to other causes such as the elements, accident, or other people's agency; the apparent disproportionality between a trivial act and a vast result; the existence of collective or group karma, if any; the practice of transferring merit, since desert is standardly non-transferable and transferability may collapse the idea of an agent getting what they, in particular, merit; the status of the awakened person who has ended the causes of future birth yet continues to live out a residual body and may still experience pain; and the deeper difficulty that desert seems to require a persisting subject of the kind the no-self teaching appears to deny. Among the second: the worry that "desert" imports a Western, agent-centered, moralized framework into what is traditionally presented as a depersonalized causal process; that fittingness is something the interpreter reads into the doctrine rather than something the doctrine asserts; and that the tradition's emphasis on intention and the defilements makes karma more naturally a theory of character-formation or habit than a creditor's ledger of merited pain and pleasure.

I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a clear separation between what a defender of the desert reconstruction would themselves concede as counting against the view and what they would dismiss as a misplaced objection brought in from the wrong framework. I also want an honest assessment of whether the reconstruction has been qualified into unfalsifiability by moves such as "not all suffering is karmic," "results may ripen only after many lives," and "the agent is not a self but a causal continuum" — and whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind or exactly what a multi-life theory of moral desert should look like. I personally find the transfer-of-merit objection and the no-self objection the most threatening; tell me whether they genuinely are, or whether I am overrating them.
