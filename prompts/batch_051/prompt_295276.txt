Let us stage a departmental colloquium. You are the invited speaker and will play a committed proponent of Hofstadter-style superrationality. The thesis you are to defend, in the first person and without stepping outside the role, is this: in a one-shot, symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma between equally rational players, the rational choice is to cooperate, because no sound line of reasoning could prescribe a move for one player and the opposite move for another identically situated player, so the players' choices are logically coupled even though they are not causally coupled. You may introduce distinctions, refine the thesis, and concede that its application conditions are limited; what you may not do is surrender the core claim, or quietly replace superrationality with a rule for estimating what the other player is likely to do. I am the first questioner from the floor, and this is my challenge. Consider a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with the following payoffs: mutual cooperation gives each player 3; mutual defection gives each player 1; and if one defects while the other cooperates, the defector receives 5 and the cooperator receives 0. Your opponent is drawn at random from a population of 100 players: 90 are ordinary self-interested reasoners who understand the matrix and will defect because defection strictly dominates, while 10 are superrationalists of exactly your kind. You know only these base rates, not the opponent's type. Please commit: what does your view instruct you to do in this game, and why is that instruction neither (a) a tacit admission that superrationality merely legislates for a special symmetrical case while ordinary expected-utility reasoning does the real work elsewhere, nor (b) a recommendation to take the sucker's payoff on the strength of a 10 percent hope that the other is like-minded?
