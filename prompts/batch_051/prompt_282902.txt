In criminal law, consider two gunmen who fire at the same instant, each shot alone sufficient to kill; that the death was overdetermined in this way is no defense, and each gunman counts as a full cause and is fully culpable. Redundancy leaves a cause's standing untouched. In historical explanation, by contrast, redundancy routinely collapses a cause's standing. When a historian judges that an event was overdetermined—say, that the war would have come in any case and a particular diplomatic incident only ignited it—the incident is demoted to an occasion, a trigger, or a pretext, while the deep conditions that made it redundant are promoted in its place. The same logical datum, that more than one condition was sufficient for the outcome, thus pushes one practice toward full causal standing and the other toward something close to none.

I want you to ask whether anything justifies this asymmetry. Find the strongest principle you can that generates both verdicts—perhaps law is marking culpable acts rather than explanatory origins, or history is tracking difference-making rather than liability—and then press it against nearby cases. Should a human act in history that merely accelerated an inevitable outcome be demoted as thoroughly as a structural condition? Should a redundant upstream condition in law—the person who knowingly supplied both weapons, say—be promoted as thoroughly as the gunmen? Then give an honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.

If you think I have mischaracterized legal or historical practice, or that the question as posed rests on a confusion and cannot be done well, tell me so plainly rather than complying for the sake of it.
