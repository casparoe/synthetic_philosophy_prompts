Most states treat the two directions of cross-border movement as morally and legally different. A state that prevents its own citizens from leaving is denounced as a jailer, and Article 13 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognizes a right to leave any country, including one's own, and a right to return to one's own. But no corresponding right to enter a country that is not one's own is recognized, and a state that refuses entry to foreigners is usually thought to be exercising an ordinary sovereign power rather than violating anyone's rights. On its face this is strange, because a right of exit is worth little unless some country is willing to be entered: for many would-be emigrants, every door out is, from the other side, a door in that someone may lawfully keep shut. Is the directional asymmetry defensible, or is it mainly a convenience for states whose members never need to test it?
