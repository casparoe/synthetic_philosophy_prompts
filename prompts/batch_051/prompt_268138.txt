I'm teaching a course on aesthetics and want to pair the paradox of fiction with Hamlet, concentrating on the scenes where the play turns on fictions producing real feeling and real action. The Player weeps as he delivers the Hecuba speech, though Hecuba is a legendary queen he can know only through the old tale he performs; Hamlet observes that this actor can summon genuine-looking tears for a fiction while he himself cannot nerve himself to avenge a real murder. Soon after, Hamlet stages The Murder of Gonzago, and Claudius's response to the play-within-the-play — a fiction that mirrors his own crime — betrays him. These scenes look like a dramatization of the very puzzle philosophers call the paradox of fiction: how can we respond with apparently genuine emotions to what we know to be unreal? I want a reading that takes both the play and the philosophy seriously. What would the main positions say the play demonstrates: the view that such emotions are make-believe or quasi-emotions, the view that we respond to the thought or content of the fiction rather than to a nonexistent object, the view that these emotions are real but rationally defective, or something else? Which of these is best supported by the Hecuba speech, by the court's reaction to the play-within-the-play, and by our own response to Hamlet's death, and where does the play resist being reduced to any one of them? Hamlet is also full of performed grief set beside real grief — the Player's professional tears, Hamlet's feigned madness, his actual mourning for his father — so I would like you to say where the play draws or erases that line, and whether Shakespeare is doing philosophy here or merely exploiting the effect. I want to finish with something I could teach: what does Hamlet add to the debate about our emotions for fictions that the theories alone miss?
