I cover the history of science for a general-interest magazine, and I keep a standing editorial policy about simultaneous discoveries. When two or more investigators arrive at the same result independently within a few years — Newton and Leibniz on the calculus, Darwin and Wallace on natural selection, and lesser-known cases from my own beat — I take the coincidence as strong evidence that the discovery was "in the air," and I write it that way. I downplay individual priority, sometimes refuse to name a single discoverer at all, and frame the result as something the field was about to reach. Colleagues have told me this is unsound: simultaneity is only weak evidence of inevitability, and my policy withholds credit from people who actually did the work.

I was comforted in this habit by something Charles Darwin wrote in a letter to the geologist Charles Lyell dated 18 June 1858, after seeing Alfred Russel Wallace's manuscript on natural selection:

So all my originality, whatever it may amount to, will be smashed. Though my Book, if it will ever have any value, will not be deteriorated; as all the labour consists in the application of the theory.

I read this as support for my policy: the party who had most to lose seems to say that the scientific value lies in the working-out and application, not in who had the idea first. But lately I worry that I am using a private, emotionally raw letter as a credentialed excuse for a habit I already held, and that Darwin may simply have been consoling himself. So my question is this. Is my policy a defensible way to describe how discovery actually works, or is it a deterministic bias dressed up as an editorial rule? What exactly does independent simultaneity show about whether a discovery would have happened anyway, and at what rate, if anything, should it reduce the credit we give an individual? And am I entitled to Darwin's letter as evidence here, given what it is and when it was written?
