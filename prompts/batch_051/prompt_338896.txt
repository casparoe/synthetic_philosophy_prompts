I keep running into a particular move in discussions of whether life can be meaningful without any cosmic or built-in purpose. The move is: a life is like a story, and what makes a story good — a shaped arc, struggle that pays off, growth through adversity, a satisfying resolution — is exactly what makes a life meaningful. On this view, a life that is just a series of pleasant but disconnected episodes is a bad story and therefore an impoverished or meaningless life, and the absurdist worry that life lacks meaning can be answered by saying that we make our lives meaningful the way authors make texts meaningful. How far does this analogy actually hold? Spell out the mapping first: what in a life corresponds to the author, the protagonist, the reader, the editing process, the climax, and the ending? Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for the intended conclusion and the ones that don't. For example, I have heard it argued that many people do not experience their own lives as narratives at all, and that lived time is not organized like plot time; are these fatal to the argument, or just loose ends? Finally, say what survives: is narrative coherence a genuine source of meaning in a life, or was the analogy doing all the justificatory work? If it fails at the load-bearing points, what better comparison, if any, would carry the point more honestly?
