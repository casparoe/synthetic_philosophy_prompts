Hey — I'm a backend engineer on a small team that builds autonomous agents for on-chain games and lotteries, and I have no philosophy background, so if there's jargon for what I'm about to describe, please keep it plain. We keep getting burned by a class of game where the move that looks right for one agent turns into everyone losing when a bunch of agents do the same thing, and we don't have a reusable policy for deciding whether to enter. These decisions happen hundreds of times a day, and the stakes range from a few dollars in gas to six figures.

The two toy cases that got me thinking (from an old Scientific American column someone showed me, Hofstadter I think) are:

- Platonia: a guy tells 20 people that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon tomorrow, that one person gets a billion dollars. If zero or more than one send, nobody gets anything, and nobody is allowed to communicate. The superrational move I've seen described is to send with probability 1/20 rather than just always sending.

- The Luring Lottery: Scientific American actually ran this. Readers could send a postcard with a number; writing 1 got you one entry, but you were free to put any number and get that many entries. The prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, awarded to one randomly drawn entry. Bigger number = better odds but a more diluted pot, and the per-player EV-maximizing number is to match everyones else's total, which runs away if everyone does it. The magazine's suggested superrational play was roughly: roll a die with as many sides as the number of likely players, and send a single entry only if you roll a 1.

Our agents keep finding the smart-contract version of these things: a fixed-jackpot claim that only pays if exactly one address claims, airdrops where your claim count dilutes the payout, etc. Decisions are one-shot, no communication, mostly anonymous, and the agent has maybe a second or two to decide; we can't put a human in the loop per decision, but we can code a policy ahead of time. Whatever we ship also has to be explainable to our head of product, who will not accept 'the agent did the philosophical thing' as an explanation.

So here's what I'd like: a reusable decision procedure — questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it outputs ENTER or DON'T ENTER, and when ENTER, how many entries or what randomization probability. Ground it in whatever the superrationality idea actually says, but translated into something a backend team can implement without reading the philosophy, and be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as math.

Then run it on three cases:

(a) A Platonia-style contract: exactly one adress can claim 100 ETH during a 24-hour window; if two or more claim, nobody gets paid; if zero claim, nobody gets paid. The agent can't coordinate with anyone.

(b) A Luring-Lottery-style airdrop: a fixed pool of 1,000,000 tokens; each address can claim a ticket count of its choosing; the winner is one randomly chosen ticket; and the payout is the pool divided by the total number of tickets, so every extra ticket dilutes the payout.

(c) A boring fixed-jackpot lottery with a small entry fee and a huge number of players, where one agent's entry doesn't change the pot at all. I want this one included so we can see where the procedure stops being special and just collapses to ordinary EV math.

At least one of these should produce an answer my team is going to hate — I suspect it'll be (a), where the procedure will tell the agent to hold back sometimes even though always entering looks like a strictly better shot at the money. Make that explicit.

Finally, end with a section on what the procedure can't handle and where a human has to override it, because I need to know the failure modes before I pitch this to the product lead.
