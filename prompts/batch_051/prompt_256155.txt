I got really into sourdough during the pandemic, and it's made me weirdly obsessed with a question I thought I'd settled in high school biology. My starter sits in the fridge doing nothing for weeks, then I feed it and it bubbles and doubles, and I caught myself saying it was alive. That started an argument with a friend who does biology research about whether viruses are alive, and now I've been turning over cases for days. I tried to write down my gut verdicts, and they're messier than I expected.

A dog is obviously alive. A mule is alive even though it can't reproduce. A worker honeybee is alive even though it's sterile. A fern is alive. A forest fire is not alive, even though it consumes oxygen, grows, spreads, and in a loose way reproduces. A bacterial endospore, sitting there dormant and not metabolizing, is alive to me. A virus sitting just as dormant on a doorknob is not alive, even though it springs into action inside a cell. A prion that makes other proteins fold wrong is not alive. A crystal growing in a supersaturated solution is not alive. A lichen is alive, though I'm honestly not sure whether it's one living thing or two. A mitochondrion inside a cell, which has its own DNA and divides on its own schedule, is not alive on its own. And my sourdough starter is alive, but I suspect that's just becuase of the yeast and bacteria in it.

I used to think the familiar checklist of metabolism, growth, reproduction, and response to stimuli would settle all of this, but it clearly doesn't. Is there one principle that gets all these verdicts, or at least most of them, and which of my calls should I give up? Please also tell me what your principle says about two cases I can't decide on: a self-repairing machine on another planet that mines materials and builds copies of itself, and a seed that sits in a jar for a hundred years and then germinates.
