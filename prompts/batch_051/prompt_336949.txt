I work on coordinate reference systems and time standards for mapping software. My working vocabulary is datums, origin shifts, affine transformations between frames, epoch dates, units and scale, proleptic extension, and backward compatibility of coordinate data. I am not a historian or a philosopher. I want you to restate the main positions in the debate over the civil calendar's epoch and year numbering — the traditional Gregorian calendar with the AD/BC era and no year zero; the astronomical convention that inserts a year zero and numbers earlier years negatively; proposals for a non-religious epoch such as a Holocene-era origin; and the deflationary view that any epoch is equally arbitrary — as faithfully as possible in my vocabulary. Treat the choice of epoch as a choice of origin, a calendar as a convention for assigning numeric labels to days, and calendar reform as a coordinate transformation with conversion costs and legacy-data problems.

Then provide the residue report. For each position, say what translates cleanly, what you had to distort, and what has no counterpart in coordinate-system vocabulary at all. In particular: does the dispute over whether there should be a year zero amount to more than an off-by-one indexing convention plus a disagreement about whether years are intervals or labels? Does the case for a non-religious epoch survive the translation as a substantive normative claim, or does it reduce to a preference for which coordinate frame should be the default in new systems? And is the incumbent's appeal to continuity and avoidable conversion costs merely a compatibility argument, or does it contain something my vocabulary cannot say? The point is not to legislate the calendar but to find out which parts of this debate are substantive and which are only coordinate conventions.
