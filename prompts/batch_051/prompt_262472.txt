I keep encountering Chesterton's fence as a move in arguments about institutional reform, and I want to steelman it before I use it again, because the literal version seems to prove too much. The argument, written out the way I understand the stronger deployments of it, goes like this:

(1) For any existing institution, rule, or settled practice — call it a fence — you may not tear it down or substantially rework it unless you know why it was put up: what function it serves, or served for the people who built and maintained it.

(2) But for almost every real fence — the filibuster, zoning, tenure, the jury, particular legal doctrines, professional licensing, the two-party primary — no individual reformer actually possesses that knowledge in anything like its full form. The reasons are distributed across many minds, partly tacit, partly unrecorded, and sometimes simply lost; what any reformer has is at best a partial and contestable story.

(3) So for almost every real fence, the epistemic condition in (1) is unmet: the reformer is to some degree ignorant of why the fence is there.

(4) Therefore, by (1), almost no proposal to remove or substantially rework an existing fence is justified.

(5) But that conclusion would condemn reforms I cannot bring myself to condemn: the abolition of chattel slavery, the end of debtors' prisons, the repeal of the laws that barred married women from owning property. Each was, at the time it was attacked, an existing fence; and the reformers who attacked it could not have supplied a complete account of the functions those arrangements served for their defenders.

(6) Since I will not accept (5), at least one of (1)–(4) has to go. But each departure seems to cost me something real: (1) is the fence itself; (2) is just honest epistemic modesty; (3) follows; and (4) is simply the fence applied to the cases (2) and (3) describe.

I would most like to keep something close to (1), read as a strong default or burden-shifting principle rather than a veto. And I would also like to keep the verdicts in (5). But right now I do not see any principled way to hold both, only ad hoc exceptions. So I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me which step or transition in the argument I should reject, and what exactly rejecting it costs. If the right answer is to reinterpret (1), give me the reinterpretation with enough precision that it delivers the cases in (5) without quietly turning back into the principle that caused the trouble in the first place. And if some step after (1) is where the overreach happens, say where and why it looked like a harmless inference. Please write in American English.
