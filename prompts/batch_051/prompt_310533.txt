Act as a philosopher of probability who actually knows the anthropics literature, and please think through this carefully instead of giving me the standard LessWrong tribal takes. I've been reading rationalist blogs for years, and one thing I can't wrap my head around is why the SSA vs SIA debate is still completely stuck. Both sides agree on all the math conditional on their setup; the fight is supposed to be over how to even set up self-locating probabilities. SSA says, roughly: reason as if you're randomly sampled from your reference class, so "there are more observers like me" does not make my observations more likely. SIA says, roughly: my own existence is evidence for hypotheses on which more observers exist, so between otherwise empirically identical theories I should favor the one with the bigger population. That leads to the Presumptuous Philosopher scenario, where SIA apparently lets you be nearly certain that a theory with 10^20 observers is true rather than one with 10^10, purely from noticing that you exist. That feels like getting something for nothing, but I also can't shake the feeling that SSA ignores real information, because under a hypothesis with zero observers I couldn't be around to wonder about this. The practical stakes show up in the Doomsday argument: SSA does the ominous shift, SIA cancels it out.

My question isn't who wins; it's what kind of disagreement this even is. Is it a substantive disagreement about what counts as evidence, a verbal disagreement about the word "probability," a hidden fight over reference classes, or are SSA and SIA really answers to two different questions that keep getting conflated under one label? And what would progress look like: a decision problem where they diverge, a Dutch book, a formal result, a sharper distinction, or something else? If your diagnosis is that no progress should be expected, say why. Maybe start by brainstorming all the places the two sides could be talking past one another before you commit.
