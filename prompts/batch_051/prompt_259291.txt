I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether arriving late after promising a fixed time is importantly different from arriving late after merely saying one would try. We kept returning to a pair of cases. In the first, someone says, "I'll be there at three," and arrives at twenty past; the person waiting feels mildly betrayed and lowers her estimate of the speaker's reliability. In the second, someone says only, "I'll try to be there around three," and arrives at the same twenty past; the delay, the inconvenience, and the disappointed expectation are otherwise identical, yet the person waiting feels nothing more than mild annoyance and does not revise her estimate of the speaker's reliability at all. Ordinary practice therefore seems to treat the words themselves as creating an obligation over and above whatever consideration and mutual convenience already require. My friend insisted that this extra obligation is real even for small promises about arrival times; I could not say what it consists in.

Hume presses a version of this difficulty in A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, Section V, "Of the obligation of promises":

"The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions."

In the same section Hume concludes that the obligation of promises has no force antecedent to human conventions, because no natural act of the mind corresponds to the words and the will cannot by itself bring a new obligation into existence; promising is, on his account, an artificial contrivance serving the interests of society. What I would like to know is whether this picture vindicates or quietly dissolves the asymmetry my friend was defending. If promising is a convention rather than an act of mind that binds a future self, then the three-o'clock promiser seems blameworthy not for a distinct act of betrayal, but for failing to perform within, and so degrading, a useful social device; the hedger has simply never invoked the device, although the hedger's conduct may be indistinguishable in intention and inconvenience caused. Is there a principled difference between the two friends that survives this deflation—perhaps a difference in the expectations each knowingly created—or is ordinary resentment at the broken small promise an unearned moral reaction that we keep only because it maintains the convention? If you would keep the asymmetry, say precisely what the promise adds and why it should count even between friends at low stakes; if you would not, say what ordinary practice should look like instead and what we lose by abandoning the distinction.
