Ok, I'm trying to see how robust Russell's analysis in "On Denoting" is when the description isnt merely empty but contested. He states the principle he's running as: "This is the principle of the theory of denoting I wish to advocate: that denoting phrases never have any meaning in themselves, but that every proposition in whose verbal expression they occur has a meaning." Standard textbook case: in 1905 France is a republic, so "The present King of France is bald" comes out false, because the existential-plus-uniqueness unpacking has nothing that satisfies "present King of France." Fine. Now change the setup: suppose France in 1905 still has no officially recognized king, but there are two rival claimants, each of whom is sincerely addressed by his own supporters as "the present King of France." A supporter of claimant A says to a supporter of claimant B, "The present King of France is bald." On Russell's analysis, is this still simply false the way the standard case is? Or does the principle in the passage force something else—since the phrase only has meaning in the whole proposition, and nothing in the verbal expression alone fixes which claimant is meant, the same words might express different propositions in different mouths? Work out what Russell's theory should say here, and say whether "the present King of France" still counts as having no meaning in isolation once two candidate denotations are in play. And tell me whether Russell needs an extra social or conventional fact to pick out a denotation, and whether that is a problem for the principle. Bottom line first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble.
