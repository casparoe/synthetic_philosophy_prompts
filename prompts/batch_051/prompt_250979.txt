In my reporting on global supply chains, I keep returning to a case that splits me down the middle. A garment factory in a low-income country pays two dollars a day, and its workers, whose next-best option is casual farm labor at a dollar a day, compete for the jobs and describe them as the best chance they have. The factory's buyers could absorb a six-dollar wage without cutting a single job. On one side, a theory I find genuinely compelling says that a voluntary exchange which leaves each party better off than their next-best alternative cannot be wrongful exploitation; on the contrary, refusing the offer or raising the wage by fiat would, on this view, harm the very people one means to protect. On the other side, I cannot shake the intuition that there is something morally wrong about a buyer capturing so large a share of the gains from trade when the worker's fallback is so desperate, even if the deal leaves the worker better off. I want to know which of these should give way, and at what cost. Give me the strongest version of each side, then assess the main resolutions: that the intuition rests on a comparison to an irrelevant baseline and should be discounted; that the test of mutual benefit needs an added constraint on how the gains are divided; and that both can be preserved by distinguishing benefiting someone within an unjust background from wronging them. For each resolution, say what would justify it and what it would force us to give up. Do not just pick a side; show me what it would take for the theory or the intuition to be the thing that yields.
