I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I've noticed a pattern in how the people around me talk about automation that I cannot justify. Suppose a plant automates a production line and four hundred workers are laid off. The event is treated as a human cost and a policy problem: a way of life has been destroyed, and someone—the company or the state—is expected to provide retraining, transition assistance, or compensation. But suppose a company never opens a plant that would have employed those same four hundred people, because a mostly automated line can produce the same output with a handful of operators. That decision is treated as good business, not as four hundred people being denied a way of life. The same four hundred positions are absent from the world either way, and if work matters because it supplies income, purpose, and a place in a community, then the same amount of whatever work supplies is foregone in both cases. The only apparent difference is that in the first case the jobs existed before they disappeared, while in the second they existed only as a counterfactual. Is there a principle that genuinely justifies treating the destruction of existing jobs as a serious loss while treating the non-creation of equivalent jobs as no loss at all? Give me the strongest candidate justification—something about reliance and expectation, identifiable versus statistical victims, loss aversion, or a difference between destroying and failing to create—and test it against nearby cases: a company that cancels a planned manual plant because automation became cheap, a region that never builds labor-intensive infrastructure, a young person who cannot enter a field because its entry-level work has been automated away. If your principle rests on tangible losses like seniority and mortgage payments, say whether the asymmetry survives when the thing we care about is primarily meaningful work rather than income. Then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or admitted as a bias.
