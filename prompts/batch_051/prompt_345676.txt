Two friends of mine, Ruth and Liam, got into it at dinner last week and I genuinely couldnt sort out who was right. Ruth had been describing a case from her hospital: a doctor told an elderly patient her recovery would be slower and harder than expected, because the doctor thought the patient, who was depressed and stubborn, would refuse physio and go downhill if she heard the real odds. The patient did the physio and recovered well. Ruth said the doctor did the right thing. Liam asked whether the lie itself was a point against the action. I wrote down their four moves afterwards.

(1) Ruth: of course it was. Lying always counts against an action. Here it was outweighed by the patient's recovery, but a version of the same action without the lie would have been better, other things equal.

(2) Liam: you're not describing what happened, you're just insisting on a price list for features. The lie wasn't a cost that got outweighed; it was the thing that made the hope possible, so in this case it counted in favour, or at least didn't count against. Whether something is a reason for or against depends on the situation.

(3) Ruth: if features can switch valence like that, you can't learn from cases at all. The fact that a lie made things go well here tells us nothing if 'it was a lie' might count against next time and in favour the time after. I'm not saying lying always makes an action wrong overall. I'm saying it's always a wrong-making feature, even when the action is right on balance.

(4) Liam: that's a distinction without a difference. If a wrong-making feature can be so thoroughly outweighed that it leaves no trace in what you should do, you've made the claim unfalsifiable. And you're already smuggling in the verdict by calling it 'a lie' rather than 'a way of giving her hope.' The description does the work.

I gather this is the moral generalism versus particularism fight, but I'd rather you adjudicate what these two actually said than give me a survey of the literature. And don't just side with whoever sounds more careful. Please give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction or conclusion: points 1 to 4 each rate and justify one of the moves above, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and point 5 gives your overall verdict on who is more right about what. Write in British English.
