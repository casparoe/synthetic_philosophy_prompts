I am a retired physical scientist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy of law, and I keep encountering a configuration in the logic of rules that I do not have a name for. The configuration concerns a body governed by rules, one of which states how the other rules may themselves be amended. In the mild form, the body uses the old procedure to change the amendment rule just for the future — say, changing a voting threshold by a vote that satisfies the old threshold but would not satisfy the new one — and one can simply say the old rule governed the change. The sharp form is different: the body follows the old procedure to adopt a new amendment rule that is made to apply to its own adoption, for instance by declaring that the new procedure is the only one that is, or ever was, the valid way to amend. The new rule then seems to be valid only if the act that adopted it was invalid, and invalid only if that act was valid; the act looks lawful exactly when it fails and unlawful exactly when it succeeds. I know this is somewhere near the jurisprudential idea of an ultimate rule of recognition, and near circularity or bootstrap problems in logic, but neither captures the temporal, validity-shifting character of the thing. Does this configuration have an established name, and how exactly does it differ from those neighboring ideas? If there is no established name, I would welcome a proposed one, together with a definition and a rule for deciding the borderline cases. I am not satisfied with a bare label; I want the conceptual carving. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
