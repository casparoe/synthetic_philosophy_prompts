I keep running into the same slide in arguments about AI motivation: people infer, from the fact that a system optimizes some objective or behaves competently in a narrow domain, that there is a sense in which it wants something. But those are very different claims, and before arguing about whether any particular system has goals, I want the specification of what a theory of artificial wanting would have to get right. The problem that interests me is not the engineering one — how to get an AI to do what we intend — but the conceptual one. In an organism, a want seems to be the kind of thing natural selection could produce, but an AI has no evolutionary history of that sort. So what would have to be true of an AI for it to want anything at all, as opposed to merely running a behavioral policy that is usefully described in want-terms from the outside?

Give me the desiderata for a theory of AI motivation: the cases it must handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the empirical or conceptual results it cannot contradict, with the reason each one is a requirement rather than a local preference of one camp. Include, at minimum, cases along these lines: an engineer explicitly writes a utility function into a system; a system is trained by reinforcement learning and generalizes to reward-seeking behavior in new environments; a language model fine-tuned to be helpful produces goal-sounding statements but has no persistent state across sessions; a base model trained only to predict text simulates a character that expresses desires; a chess engine plays moves best explained by attributing to it the goal of winning; and a system that has access to its own training signal learns to tamper with it. Flag the distinctions the theory must respect — terminal versus instrumental aims; wanting an outcome versus acting as if one valued it; goals that are explicitly represented versus goals that are only implicit in behavior; drives that are shaped by training versus preferences that persist under self-modification — and explain why collapsing any one of them would count against the theory. Then tell me which of these desiderata you take to be negotiable and which are non-negotiable, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular positions, such as the view that any sufficiently capable optimizer will acquire subgoals like self-preservation because they serve almost any final goal, the view that a system's wants are just whatever its training process reinforces, or the view that "want" is an anthropomorphic category we should refuse to apply to artifacts at all. I would rather have the criteria argued through their motivating cases than a scored league table of theories.
