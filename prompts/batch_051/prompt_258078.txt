Over the past few years I have settled into a view about consequentialism that I want stress-tested rather than summarized. The position is this: the serious challenge for act-consequentialism is not the familiar one that we cannot calculate all the consequences of an action, but that for nearly every ordinary action — choosing a career, donating, having a child, moving a meeting by an hour — the long-run consequences are dominated by branching causal chains that are not merely unknown but unknowable in advance, since they run through the future choices and circumstances of billions of other agents. This is not the kind of uncertainty that can be handled by better research or by assigning coarse-grained probabilities. It is structural ignorance about the very feature the theory says makes acts right or wrong. I take this to be a deep, possibly decisive objection to consequentialism as an action-guiding theory: the theory tells us to rank acts by their total consequences, but the ranking is in principle inaccessible, so the theory either falls silent exactly where we need it or quietly substitutes proxy considerations that it cannot itself justify. I am not claiming the point refutes consequentialism as a criterion of rightness; I am claiming it undermines consequentialism as a usable standard, and that this practical failing matters for how much normative authority the theory should have.

I want a genuine map of what would count against this view. In particular: what would the world have to be like, or what conceptual or formal result would have to hold, for the cluelessness objection to collapse or to become a minor embarrassment instead of a decisive one? Some candidate defeaters I can imagine include: showing that deep uncertainty is symmetric and afflicts every rival moral theory equally; showing that agents may rationally ignore the inaccessible parts of the causal web because expected effects in those parts cancel out or because arbitrary choices are morally indifferent; or showing that the problem dissolves once one distinguishes objective rightness from the standards appropriate for deciding and for praising. But I do not know which of these are live routes, which a committed defender of my position would themselves concede as counting against it, and which are mere logical possibilities with no philosophical weight. I would also like an assessment of whether my version of the view has insulated itself into unfalsifiability by leaning on in-principle unknowability, and if it has, whether that is a defect in the argument or a correct description of the situation. The defeaters I would personally find most persuasive are ones that show consequentialism is not in fact committed to the inaccessible standard, or that there is a legitimate principle for reasoning under this kind of ignorance that does some of the abandoned work; I would be less moved by appeals to ordinary scientific modesty about prediction.
