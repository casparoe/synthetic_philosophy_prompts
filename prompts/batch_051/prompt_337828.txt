I've been going back and forth with a friend who got deep into the "time is just entropy" view after too many pop-physics videos and blog posts, and now he's fully convinced the arrow of time is basically a counting trick dressed up as metaphysics. His favorite analogy is a fresh deck of cards. The deck starts sorted by suits in rank order — a special, low-entropy macrostate — and after a few riffle shuffles it's in one of the astronomically many "messy" arrangements, so of course it looks like there's a direction: order to disorder. He says the universe is the same trick at cosmic scale. It began in a ridiculously low-entropy state and has been drifting toward the overwhelmingly larger region of high-entropy states, and that statistical gradient is the only reason we can tell past from future. Since the underlying microphysics is time-symmetric, he concludes that time's arrow isn't fundamental; it's just a consequence of initial conditions plus counting states. People who insist time is "real" are, he says, reifying a property of our coarse-grained descriptions.

I buy a lot of this, which is exactly why I can't land the counterargument. Can you act as a philosopher of physics who's willing to be genuinely charitable to his view, and before you settle on a verdict, brainstorm the strongest possible version of the deck analogy and the strongest objections to it? Then audit how far the analogy actually carries. I want the explicit mapping first: in the universe case, what plays the role of the deck, the shuffling, the sorted initial state, the space of messy arrangements, and the claim that disorder is overwhelmingly likely? Then the disanalogies: the deck is shuffled by hands from outside; the sorted deck was prepared by a person who already lives in a world that has an arrow of time; entropy depends on how you choose to coarse-grain; the universe isn't being stirred by an external shuffler. Which of those are just cosmetic and which ones actually threaten his conclusion? Please separate the disanalogies that matter for his intended use — the claim that time's arrow is "just statistics" and not fundamental — from the ones that don't. If the analogy is quietly importing features of decks that the universe lacks, I want to know exactly what got smuggled in. Finally, does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and is there a better analogy here, or is the deck picture a dead end for this debate? A table of correspondences is fine if it helps.
