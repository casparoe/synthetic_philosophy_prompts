I'm giving a talk at my hospital's monthly clinical ethics conference next month. My thesis is a version of the Epicurean view: death itself is not bad for the person who dies. I'm not saying dying can't be horrible, and I'm not saying it doesn't matter to the people left behind — only that once you're dead there's no longer a subject for the harm to attach to, so the state of being dead cant be a harm to you. The audience is about forty people: mostly palliative care doctors and ICU nurses, three or four clinical ethicists, a hospital chaplain, and one health economist who thinks in QALYs. They're smart but they wont care about the intrinsic/extrinsic badness distinction unless I can make it pay rent. My argument sketch is roughly: (1) a harm has to happen to a subject, (2) death removes the subject, (3) the standard deprivation account smuggles in the picture of you still existing after death as a deprived person, and (4) the symmetry point: we don't treat the eternity before we existed as a loss, so treating future nonexistence as one needs a special justification. What I want is a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them, each ordered by how likely it is and how much damage it could do, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it's the dangerous one.
