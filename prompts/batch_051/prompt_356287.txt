I've been trying to hold together two things for years, and a friend in my reading group finally forced them into an argument I can't shake. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis. (1) The doctrine of a good creation says the world as God originally made it was good. (2) But if God created life through evolution by natural selection, then death, predation, disease, and a staggering amount of animal suffering were built into the process from the beginning — hundreds of millions of years before any human being existed to sin. (3) So that animal suffering can't be blamed on human sin or on the Fall. (4) A perfectly good God would permit suffering on that scale only if it was necessary for some good that couldn't be had without it. (5) The usual goods offered for human suffering — free will, soul-making, the value of moral struggle — don't apply to non-moral animals; a gazelle being eaten alive isn't exercising free choice or growing a soul. (6) The only candidate left is that evolution is just a good way for God to create, but that's restating the point under dispute, and an omnipotent God shouldn't need predation and agonizing death as an instrument for making life. (7) So the doctrine of a good creation and the science of evolution are incompatible as they stand.

I refuse to accept the conclusion, so something has to give, but each step seems to do real work. I'd most like to hold onto (1) and (2); if that's not possible I want to know exactly what I'm paying. Please don't give me a menu of options — commit to a diagnosis: which step you'd reject or reinterpret, why it looked so solid, and what someone who accepts evolution and affirms a good creation has to revise in order to accept that diagnosis. End with \answer{...} containing a short label for the step you reject, like \answer{premise 5}.
