I am a PhD student in epistemology, and I am teaching a 16-student upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar. We are two sessions into a unit on Nozick's tracking theory; the first session covered the sensitivity condition, the adherence condition, the grandmother case, and the role of methods. I want the next session—one 90-minute class—to be a structured activity rather than a discussion of the reading, in which students feel the force of the thought that a belief-forming method can be excellent at tracking ordinary truths while being structurally incapable of tracking the negations of skeptical hypotheses. In the skeptical world, the method produces exactly the same output on exactly the same signal, and Nozick's denial of closure is supposed to fall out of that fact about methods rather than from a refusal to take an obvious inferential step. I also want the activity to expose a problem students will not get from the standard brain-in-a-vat discussion alone: whether a belief tracks depends on how the method is individuated, and it is not clear that any principled individuation yields both the ordinary verdicts and the skeptical verdicts.

I have a rough sketch. Students design belief-forming methods as lookup tables from a small set of observable signals to beliefs; the class then runs these methods against a deck of possible worlds and scores them. The crucial feature is a similarity ordering in which a world where an ordinary proposition is false (the hands are gone and the signals differ) is closer than a world where the same proposition is false but the signals are identical (the envatted world). Working in small groups, students try to design methods that track target propositions, then subject their methods to counterfactual audits; the class then tests whether a method that tracks an ordinary proposition thereby tracks the skeptical negation inferred from it. Please turn this sketch into a runnable activity.

Write the response in five titled sections, in this order, with the following approximate lengths:

1. Learning aim (150 words). State what students should come away seeing, and which standard way of teaching the material this activity is meant to displace.

2. Materials, setup, and the possible-world deck (350 words). Give me the concrete materials: the worlds and their hidden and observable features, the similarity ordering, the groups and roles, and how the room should be arranged.

3. Rules, scoring, and timeline (600 words). Walk through the session in phases with exact timing inside the 90 minutes, the rules for designing and auditing methods, and a payoff scheme that uses no real money and does not reward luck over design. Include a phase in which two groups type the same underlying process as different methods and discover that the tracking verdict flips.

4. Debrief (500 words). Map specific moments in the activity—including the method-typing collision—to the sensitivity condition, the method parameter, and the failure of single-premise closure in the brain-in-a-vat case, and say at what point each concept gets named.

5. Failure modes and honest limitations (400 words). Predict what will go wrong the first time I run it and how I should recover in the room, and be explicit about which features of Nozick's view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

No citations or reading lists: the session should stand on its own. Aim for about 2,000 words total.
