I recently joined the staff of an institute that advises governments on long-horizon public investment, and I have been reading Keynes on probability and uncertainty because our group must decide how much of its uncertainty analysis should be numerical. Last week two senior colleagues argued about exactly this, and I have been unable to sort out who is right.

The disagreement, as I recorded it that afternoon:

(1) Hartwell said that for something like the net cost of a coastal adaptation project in forty years, no meaningful numerical probability is available. This is Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty: risk is like a roulette wheel, where the odds are known, while uncertainty concerns one-off structural futures whose odds are themselves unknown. He concluded that any number we attach in such cases is false precision, and the honest output is qualitative scenarios and expert judgment, not an expected-cost figure.

(2) Okafor replied that Keynes's point was never that uncertainty rules out all probability judgment, only that it rules out a calculable numerical probability. She said Keynes still allowed a rational degree of confidence and ordinal comparison between alternatives, so Hartwell's move from rejecting a precise number to rejecting any comparison is too strong.

(3) Hartwell answered that Okafor was leaning on Keynes's early Treatise on Probability. In his later reply to critics of the General Theory, Keynes described cases such as the price of copper twenty years out or the obsolescence of a new invention, where there is no scientific basis for any calculable probability; we simply do not know. What fills the gap, Keynes thought, is convention, which holds until it suddenly shifts. So a ranked comparison of one-off projects is just convention dressed up as evidence.

(4) Okafor said the later passage is a diagnosis of how investors actually behave, not the claim that convention exhausts rationality. She added that Keynes's early work already separated probability from what is called the weight of argument, so two non-numerical judgments can differ in evidential support even though neither warrants a number, and nothing in the later passage withdraws that distinction. In any case, refusing to rank options is itself a choice under uncertainty, so if Hartwell's reading were right, Keynes would have left us with no rational basis even for preferring one qualitative scenario over another, which cannot be the correct reading of a thinker who spent years constructing a logic of partial entailment.

(5) Hartwell replied that market conventions are at least repeatedly tested by outcomes, whereas a forty-year coastal decision is a one-off with no such test, and the evidential support Okafor invokes is imported from a past that, on Keynes's own account, is not a reliable guide to those futures. So the ordinal comparisons she wants are, for this class of decisions, as much a pretense as point estimates.

(6) Okafor answered that if that were right, the residual rational obligation would be to state the limits of any comparison rather than abandon comparison; Keynes's view, she said, reduces to a demand for honesty about the kind and amount of support a judgment has, not to silence about relative likelihood.

The practical issue is real, but I am not asking for project advice. I am asking for a point-by-point adjudication of this argument about Keynes: where each speaker is right, where each overreaches, what both are missing, and what, if anything, follows from that for our uncertainty-analysis decision. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
