I am a retired geochemist who now hosts a small colloquium on the norms of reasoning, and having come to philosophy late I often find myself surprised by how much argumentative practice is adversarial while the value of that adversariality is simply taken for granted. Our next guest is a philosopher who has defended, in several essays, the claim that argumentation should be developed as a competitive sport—structured matches with scoring rubrics, rankings, side-switching requirements, and public stakes—on the grounds that competition is what forces reasoning to become rigorous rather than merely comfortable. I would like the Q&A to go beyond having the guest restate this proposal.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press the view where it actually strains? I want at least one question, with a follow-up keyed to the reply a competent defender is most likely to give, on each of the following pressure points. First, whether competition selects for motivated reasoning, overconfidence, and rhetorical polish rather than accuracy, and whether scoring rubrics can realistically track soundness rather than persuasiveness. Second, the Goodhart-style worry that once argumentative skill is scored, competitors will optimize the rubric rather than the underlying epistemic aim, and whether repeatedly revising the rubric merely relocates the problem. Third, whether making competitors argue both sides produces genuine belief revision or only strategic empathy and a kind of two-sides cynicism. Fourth, whether any improvement in group reasoning should be credited to the competitive incentives themselves or instead to the mere presence of attentive judges and an audience. Fifth, whether the dispositions trained under competitive conditions transfer to ordinary deliberation or remain quarantined inside the game. For each of these, the follow-up should not simply re-ask the first question; it should be aimed precisely at the response a sophisticated defender would already have prepared.
