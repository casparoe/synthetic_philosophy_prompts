I've been going in circles on a Boltzmann brain argument, and I can't tell were the mistake is. The worry is usually stated as a one-liner, but I tried laying it out as a numbered argument and now I'm not sure whether it's valid and merely self-undermining, or whether one of the moves is actually bad. Here it is:

(1) If our best current cosmology is roughly on track, the universe is either spatially infinite or will persist long enough to churn through a mind-boggling number of thermal fluctuations.
(2) Across that history, the number of "Boltzmann brains" — briefly self-aware configurations that fluctuate out of near-thermal chaos and then dissolve — dwarfs the number of ordinary brains produced by biological evolution on planets.
(3) When you don't know which kind of observer you are, you should apportion your credence according to the relative frequencies of observer-moments; roughly, reason as if your current observer-moment were sampled from that distribution.
(4) Therefore, right now, you should be almost certain that you are a Boltzmann brain rather than an evolved ape.
(5) But if you are a Boltzmann brain, your apparent memories — including your apparent knowledge of cosmology and your apparent ability to follow steps (1) through (3) — are just features of a random fluctuation and can't be trusted.
(6) So if (4) is right, you shouldn't trust the very cosmology cited in (1) or the counting in (2), which undercuts the argument's own premises.

Is this argument valid, and if not, which step actually fails? I half-suspect (3) is smuggling in a measure over observer-moments that nobody has actually defined, but then I worry that just means (2) isn't well-defined rather than that the inference is invalid. If the argument is valid up to (4), is the right response that (5)–(6) gives us reason to reject one of the early premises instead of accepting the conclusion? Be specific about the step and the strongest reading on which it would go through.
