In an argument the other day about whether some politician's line about "urban crime" was racist, the other person said "it's a dog whistle," and that was treated as the end of the discussion. I've seen this move enough times that I want to actually understand it. The analogy, as I understand it: a dog whistle is pitched too high for a normal human ear, so the public hears something unremarkable, but the intended audience perks up and gets the real content. The point being made is that a statement can be racist even if most listeners detect nothing, and that the speaker can deny it precisely because the message is hidden in plain sight.

But I've never seen anyone audit the analogy, and I suspect its doing a lot of quiet work. Can you lay out what maps onto what — the whistle, the high frequency, the dog, the human, the automatic way dogs respond to sound rather than interpret a meaning — and then say where the mapping breaks when you're talking about people? I'm thinking of things like: a real dog whistle requires a clean physical cutoff between who can and can't hear it, the dog isn't in on any history of usage, and the person blowing it has total control over the signal; in the political case the audience is half-hearing, half-guessing, consciously knowing a code, or pretending not to get it, and the speaker can half-intend it or pick a phrase that just happens to resonate. Does the conclusion the analogy is usually used to support — that a statement is racist because its target audience receives it as a racial message, whatever the speaker claims — survive those disanalogies, or is "dog whistle" smuggling in an intent claim it was supposed to get around? And is there another analogy that does the job better?
