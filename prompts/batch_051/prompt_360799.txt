I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've spent to long in the replies whenever a piece touches gendered language. There's a move that shows up over and over, and I've never had a clean name for it. Someone defends a default word — 'mankind,' 'chairman' — by saying it's just the neutral human form and everyone knows women are included. Then someone else rewrites the sentence with a different default, one the audience already hears as politically loaded: 'mankind' becomes 'whitekind,' 'chairman' becomes 'chairwhite,' the Declaration's line about all men being created equal comes back as a line about all whites. The rewrites aren't a real proposal. The point is to make the invisible default visible by trading it for a default nobody would call neutral, and to let the reader's flinch do the work.

I don't think the move is just an analogy, because the two phrasings aren't independent cases being compared; the substituted sentence exists entirely to borrow your visceral reaction. It's also not quite a reductio, because the conclusion isn't that the defender's position collapses into nonsense but that it already leans on a presumption the defender would reject in the switched case. And it's not exactly parody, because the rewriting is doing a specific argumentative job rather than just mocking.

So what is this thing called? Is there an established term for it in rhetoric, argumentation theory, or philosophy of language, and which nearby concept fits best — parody, analogical reasoning, intuition pump, or something else? If it's nameless, how would you name and define it, and where are its edges: when does the substitution genuinely expose a hidden default, and when does it just smear a word by borrowed feeling? I don't just want a label; I want to know what kind of work the move is actually doing.
