People have always defended courts striking down laws by saying judges are the one branch that doesn't have to face voters, so they're the only ones who can protect a minority when the majority wants to crush it. That defense assumed the choice was basically binary: either courts get a final veto or the legislature is unchecked. But in the last few decades a bunch of countries have built a third option — weak-form review — where the court can rule that a law violates rights, but the elected legislature can still push the law through anyway if its willing to say so out loud (with a special majority, a re-vote, or just publicly accepting the court's warning and doing it anyway). My question is whether the old counter-majoritarian defense of strong judicial review survives this. Which premise is actually doing the work once legislative override is a realistic option, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still worth having? If you wanted to defend strong review anyway, what exactly would you have to believe about what legislators will do after a court has flagged a rights violation?
