Okay so I've been turning over a parallel and I can't decide whether it's actually deep or just me pattern matching. On the theology side: the satisfaction theory of the atonement says sin is basically an unpaid debt — you've robbed God of the honor he's owed by sinning, and Christ pays it back on your behalf, with interest, so the scales are balanced and you can be let off. Penal substitution says no, sin isn't a debt, it's a crime; God's justice demands punishment, and Christ takes the punishment the sinner deserved. On the legal side there's an obvious-looking split: a tort creates a liability that in principle anyone can discharge — the point is making the injured party whole, and whether the check comes from the wrongdoer, his insurance company, or a rich friend, the debt is gone. A crime is different: it's a wrong against the public that has to be answered by the offender being punished, and we don't let someone else do your jail time for you. So the parallel would be: satisfaction theory is to penal substitution as tort is to crime, and the fulcrum in both pairs is whether the 'bad thing' a wrong creates is the kind of thing wich can be transferred to somebody else. But here's where I start to sweat it. The law has all kinds of pragmatic reasons for the split — insurance markets, deterrence, the state's interest in monopolizing violence, not wanting rich people to buy their way out of prison — and none of that seems to be what's going on in the atonement. So: is there a real identity of structure here? If I grant the parallel for a second, what would have to be true on both sides for a result to carry over from one domain to the other? And can you name something the parallel predicts that's actually false — something the legal analogy would lead me to expect about penal substitution or satisfaction theory that theology just flatly denies?
