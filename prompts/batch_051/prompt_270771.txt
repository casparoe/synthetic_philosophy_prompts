I have been working through Joseph Butler’s two sermons on resentment and forgiveness of injuries, Sermons VIII and IX of his Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel. The passage I keep returning to is the one in which Butler argues, as I read it, that the immediate passion of resentment upon being harmed is a kind of natural defense, not itself vicious, while the deliberate cultivation of resentment into malice and revenge is what forgiveness must put away. I have built the following set of convictions on that reading, and I want an audit before I present them in a seminar.

Here is where I have landed:

1. Involuntary anger at being wronged is not itself a moral failing; indeed a person who never felt it at all would be missing something morally important.
2. Forgiveness is the voluntary forswearing of resentment itself, not merely the refusal to act on it; after forgiving, one no longer harbours the attitude of resentment against the wrongdoer.
3. Forgiveness is owed unconditionally: it does not depend on the wrongdoer’s repentance, apology, or reparation, and making it depend on those things is a moral mistake.
4. Even when one has forgiven, it can be right to seek punishment through impartial institutions, and to take protective measures, provided the aim is not revenge.
5. A person who has forgiven may still feel a fresh surge of anger when confronted with a new reminder of the wrong; this is not a sign that the forgiveness was incomplete.
6. Forgiveness does not require restoring the relationship to its previous level of trust and intimacy; it may be right to forgive while permanently refusing to be vulnerable to the person again.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which are only apparently in tension? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision is. Do not just treat every pair as equally problematic; I want a discriminating assessment. Also tell me whether there is any reading of Butler’s distinction between sudden anger and settled malice that makes all six commitments jointly tenable, or whether one of them must be abandoned or sharpened.
