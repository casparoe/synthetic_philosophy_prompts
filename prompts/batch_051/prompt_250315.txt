I'm in a seminar on the Presocratics, and a fellow student has reconstructed the Eleatic line of thought in a way that appears to force a conclusion I find unbearable, but I cannot locate the step to reject. His reconstruction runs as follows. (1) Every thought and every utterance is of something: there is no thinking or speaking of nothing. (2) Therefore whatever can be thought or said has being, and what is not cannot be thought or said at all. (3) A false assertion is an assertion that things are a way they are not; so false speech would have to be speech of what is not, which (2) already rules out. (4) Hence no assertion can be false. (5) For anything to change, it must come to be what it previously was not; but what it previously was not is a case of what is not, so change would require admitting not-being. Therefore change is impossible. (6) Similarly, for the world to contain many things, each would have to differ from the others; but to differ is to be what another is not. Difference, too, is a relation to not-being. So plurality is impossible. (7) Reality is therefore one, undifferentiated, and unchanging, and the world of many changing things that we seem to inhabit is an illusion. (8) But this very argument appears to depend on what it denies: anyone who works through it passes from confusion to conviction, and the inference moves from premises to a distinct conclusion, so it presupposes change and difference in order to establish their unreality.

He insists this just is Parmenides' result and that the appearances should be abandoned. I want to keep the appearances: I would far rather give up one of the premises than accept that change, plurality, and error are illusions. The argument seems to go wrong somewhere, but each premise considered on its own looks forced. Which step should I reject, why did that step look so plausible to the Eleatics, and what does rejecting it cost someone who still wants to explain how false belief, change, and plurality are possible?
