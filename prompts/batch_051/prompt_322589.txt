Question:
Explain the relationship between bounded rationality and satisficing. Is satisficing best understood as a form of maximizing under constraints (for example, with search costs included in the utility function), or as a different standard of choice altogether? Illustrate with an example.

Student answer:
Bounded rationality is Simon's point that real decision-makers have limited time, information, and computational capacity. The classic expected-utility model — list all the options, evaluate each, pick the best — doesn't describe how real choice works. Satisficing is his alternative: the agent sets an aspiration level (a threshold for what counts as good enough) and takes the first option that meets it. The two ideas fit together because satisficing is the procedure Simon thinks bounded agents actually use: since a bounded agent can't find the maximum, it uses a threshold instead.

When you look closely, satisficing is still a kind of maximizing. Once you count the cost of searching for more options as part of the payoffs, the satisficer is maximizing after all: she stops searching exactly when the expected gain from looking further falls below the cost of looking. The aspiration level is really just a rough way of marking where that cost-benefit tradeoff comes out. So satisficing isn't a genuinely different standard of choice — it's expected utility maximization with search costs folded in.

For example, choosing a restaurant: I don't rank every restaurant in town. I take the first place that looks decent and not too expensive. That looks like satisficing, but under the surface I'm balancing the trouble of walking further against the chance of a better meal. So satisficing is best understood as a descriptive claim about how finite agents actually choose, not as a rival normative standard.
