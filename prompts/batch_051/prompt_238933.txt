I've been going back and forth with my housemate about Malthus, becuase he thinks the guy has been unfairly turned into a punchline. His line is that Malthus was not wrong, just early: the arithmetic really does run against endless exponential growth on a finite planet, and the twentieth century didn't refute the principle of population so much as buy time, what with the Green Revolution, fossil fuels, and the demographic transition. But when I sat down with Malthus's actual argument, it seemed to me he was making a much more specific claim about how the positive checks—famine, disease, war—and the preventive checks—delayed marriage, moral restraint, and everything else he filed under vice—operate in real time, not just the vague idea that eventually resources run out.

So now I'm stuck on a more exact question. Suppose Malthus could be shown everything from, say, 1850 onward: synthetic fertilizer and the Green Revolution, birth rates falling as countries got richer, and twentieth-century famines that didn't straightforwardly track population outrunning the food supply. What would he himself make of the modern rescue that says he was right but just early? Would he accept it as a friendly amendment to his theory, or would he think people have turned what was a falsifiable prediction into something unfalsifiable by endlessly re-dating the catastrophe? Is there a version of his theory that survives the demographic transition while still being recognizably his, and where exactly would he draw the line between his theory still standing and a different theory wearing his name? I'd rather have an answer that reasons from what he actually argued than another generic verdict on whether he was right or wrong.
