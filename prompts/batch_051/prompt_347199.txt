Two regulars at my local rat-adjacent meetup, Amira and Ben, got into it the other week about whether computational irreducibility puts a hard cap on explanation. I mostly listened; now they've both asked me to referee, and I honestly can't tell where the move is.

Amira started with the standard version: if a system is computationally irreducible, there is no shortcut that produces the state at step t faster than running the system for t steps. So for those systems the full explanation just is the simulation, and any shorter story leaves out something causally load-bearing. Doesn't that make some systems in principle unexplainable, except by the useless gesture of pointing at the run itself?

Ben said she was conflating a simulation with an explanation. Weather is computationally irreducible at the molecular level, but we don't explain the weather by running molecular dynamics on the atmosphere. We explain it with pressure gradients and fronts and coarse-grained objects. Explanation is about finding the right compression, and compression is relative to the question being asked. Saying the microstate is irreducible tells you nothing about whether macro regularities can be explained.

Amira shot back that this is the problem: coarse-graining changes the topic. If the question is why this particular cellular automaton configuration at step 10,000 has it's exact pattern, a macro summary that says it's one of a vast family of configurations misses the very property in question. Irreducibility says no compression preserves that property, so some questions have no answer except to run the thing. And running the thing isn't explaining, it's duplicating.

Ben conceded that for the exact micro configuration there's no shorter description, but he said that's a strange thing to ask for an explanation of. It just is the deterministic consequence of the rule and the initial condition iterated. That's an explanation, even if an expensive one, because explanations don't have to be shorter than the process they describe.

Amira replied that he'd now made explanation so cheap it covers anything that makes the outcome look non-magical. On that standard, I explain a proof by writing every line of the derivation with no generalization and no insight, and we've lost what made explanations worth caring about in the first place.

Can you adjudicate the argument as it actually went? I want to know who's right about which specific claim, where they're talking past one another, and whether there's a third position that keeps the good parts of both.
