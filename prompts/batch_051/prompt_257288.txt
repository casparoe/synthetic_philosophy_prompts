I have been turning over a claim that seems either obviously true or nearly empty depending on the day: that if you watch a person choose for long enough, across enough different situations, their choices reveal what they actually want — their real preferences, as opposed to what they say they want, what they think they want, or what they would want under some idealized conditions.

The thing that bothers me is that the same behavior always seems to be consistent with more than one underlying want. If a man never eats the dessert in front of him, he might care about his health, or his appearance, or he might simply not like those particular desserts. If a woman keeps choosing the harder project, she might value challenge, or fear being seen as idle, or be bad at estimating effort. Watching more choices helps only if the new situations are chosen well enough to break these ties.

Suppose I had the resources to run any study I liked with willing participants. What would be the strongest test of the claim that choices reveal real preferences? I want a concrete design: what I would vary, what I would measure, what pattern of results would count for the claim, and what pattern would count against it. Then I want the honest remainder — what even a successful test would leave unsettled, such as whether the inferred preference is a genuine feature of the person or merely a compact way of summarizing their choices, whether it would survive situations unlike any they have faced, and whether it is what they would endorse after reflection. Where exactly does the testable portion of the claim end and the stipulation begin?
