I have come to hold a broadly atomist-cum-corpuscularian view of matter: to be material is to be impenetrable and solid — to occupy a region of space to the exclusion of other matter and to resist intrusion into that region — while the void is simply whatever lacks these features, and therefore is not matter at all. I mean this as a claim about what matter is, not as a claim about what any historical atomist believed in detail. Tell me what would count against this identification. Among the things I already suspect of causing trouble are fields, which superpose rather than excluding one another; bosons, which can share a quantum state; and the physical vacuum, which is not mere emptiness. But I cannot tell which of these are genuine defeaters and which are changes of subject. Lay out the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those a defender of the view would concede as counting against it from those they would dismiss, and say what the identification commits me to that rival accounts of matter do not. I would find it most persuasive to be told which single consideration most threatens the view, and whether the view's ability to insulate itself is a strength or a vice.
