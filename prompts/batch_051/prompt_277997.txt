I am a software engineer rather than a philosopher, so I will state the motivating distinction in the way I understand it, then describe a concrete rule built on it.

There is a distinction between cranes and skyhooks. A skyhook is an explanatory device that is invoked as if it descends from above: it does real work in a system, but nothing in the system constructs it or explains how it got there. A crane is a piece of machinery that is itself built from lower, simpler pieces and that can in turn be used to build higher pieces. A related pathology is greedy reductionism: insisting that, because something can in principle be assembled from lower pieces, only the lowest-level description is true, and higher-level descriptions should be discarded even when they are doing real work.

Now consider this proposed rule for a medium-sized software organization. Every abstraction in the codebase—every public interface, service, domain concept, schema, and architectural layer—must be submitted together with a documented crane: a construction that realizes the abstraction from strictly lower-level abstractions. Each of those lower-level abstractions must come with its own crane, recursively, until the chain bottoms out in primitives the organization explicitly declares to be ground: the language runtime, the kernel, the database, fixed external APIs, and so on. Code review may reject any abstraction whose crane is circular, merely relabels the abstraction, or silently imports the very capacity it is supposed to build.

I am not asking whether this rule is a good idea in the abstract. I want an analysis of how it would actually behave if adopted and enforced. In particular: What would it incentivize in engineers and architects, and how would people game it? Once skyhooks are banned, where would they go—into the choice of ground primitives, into third-party models and vendor APIs, into tests, documentation, the runtime, or the team's own shared vocabulary? What second-order effects would the rule have on architecture, maintenance, onboarding, delivery speed, and the way the organization reasons about its system? Would it select for genuine cranes or merely for convincing crane-shaped justifications? Would it push the organization toward greedy reductionism, and what would that look like in an actual codebase rather than in principle? Are the failure modes fixable by tightening what counts as a crane or as ground, or would tightening merely relocate the problem?
