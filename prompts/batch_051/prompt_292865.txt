I am a first-year mathematics graduate student, and this topic stopped being abstract for me two months ago. I was assigned to grade for our department's transition-to-proofs course, and a student asked me directly why proof by contradiction should carry any weight: why should deriving a contradiction from the assumption that a proposition is false count as a proof that it is true, rather than merely as evidence that something is wrong with our assumptions or with the logic itself? I could reproduce the standard formal answer, but I could not tell whether I believed it. Since then I have been reading about intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism, and I have been surprised to discover that a debate now roughly a century old has, by all appearances, not moved.

The two sides, as I understand them, run as follows. Classical mathematicians treat well-formed mathematical claims as bivalent: a claim is determinately true or false independently of whether anyone proves it, and the law of excluded middle expresses that mind-independent determinacy. Constructivists instead explain the meaning of the logical constants in terms of what counts as a proof — the proof-theoretic reading often called the BHK interpretation: a proof of 'P or Q' must deliver one of the disjuncts together with its proof; a proof of 'there exists an x such that P(x)' must deliver a witness x; and 'not P' is a construction that would turn any proof of P into a proof of an absurdity. On that reading, excluded middle amounts to the claim that we possess a method that would decide every mathematical statement, and constructivists deny that we have any such thing.

What puzzles me is that neither side seems able to lose on the other's terms. A standard double-negation translation maps every theorem of classical arithmetic to a theorem of intuitionistic arithmetic, so the consistency of classical arithmetic reduces to that of intuitionistic arithmetic; from inside the constructivist framework, classical mathematics is not refuted, only reinterpreted. Conversely, intuitionistic arithmetic has the disjunction property and the existence property: when it proves a closed disjunction or a closed existence statement, it actually furnishes one disjunct or a witness, whereas classical arithmetic does not. Constructivists take these properties as evidence that their logic better captures the ordinary meanings of 'or' and 'exists.' Each side, in short, can translate the other's theorems without conceding the other's meanings.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about which theorems are true, since each logic can largely recover the other's theorems; it is a disagreement about what it takes for a mathematical claim to have been established, and ultimately about whether mathematics is an account of a mind-independent realm or of our constructive capacities. That hypothesis may be doing too much work, which is why I am asking.
