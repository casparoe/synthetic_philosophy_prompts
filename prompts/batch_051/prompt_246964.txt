After a career in experimental science—where one learns to ask whether a conclusion survives when the supports of the experimental setup are removed—I have recently turned to philosophy and to the literature on aligning artificial systems. I find myself stuck on a conceptual question about an old argument often called Goodhart's law, and I would like to know whether it survives a specific change in assumptions.

The classical argument, as I understand it, runs as follows. We cannot directly optimize the outcome we truly value, so we choose a proxy that is assumed to correlate with that outcome. Once the proxy is made the target, the correlation is no longer an external fact; optimization exploits every discrepancy between proxy and value, and the system is driven into precisely those regions where the proxy reads well while the value does not. The argument implicitly assumes a fixed, externally specified proxy and an optimizer free to move in whatever direction maximizes it.

The change I have in mind is the one introduced by aligning current language models with human feedback. The proxy is now a learned reward model—a neural network trained to predict human comparative judgments, and frequently retrained on outputs produced by the very system it scores. The optimizer is not an unbounded expected-utility maximizer but a policy pulled a limited distance from a pretrained distribution, with an explicit KL penalty anchoring it to that distribution. The underlying value is not a single measurable quantity such as output per worker but a diffuse notion of helpfulness and harmlessness, partly constituted by the raters' judgments, which may themselves shift as the raters see different model outputs. My question is whether Goodhart's argument survives this change. Does the optimization pressure now bite on the reward model rather than on the policy? Does the KL penalty restore a premise of the classical argument, or merely slow the decoupling without removing it? Or does the argument lose its footing because its premise of a fixed external criterion no longer holds—and if it does lose its footing, does something like the Goodhart effect re-emerge in another form, perhaps as a claim about learned proxies drifting under iterative optimization? I am interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in a policy recommendation.
