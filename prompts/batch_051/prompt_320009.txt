At a reading group I attended, two people got into an extended argument about a hypothetical. Imagine a procedure in which each of a person’s neurons is replaced, one at a time, by a functionally identical artificial unit that preserves all the original neuron’s input-output behavior and its connections to the others. The person remains awake and responsive throughout and shows no change in memory, personality, or ability. Elena argued that after the final replacement the person obviously survives: at no point did the person die, and identity here is just a matter of psychological continuity, which was preserved at every step. Marcus replied that unbroken outward functioning cannot settle the question, because a sufficiently good replacement would speak, recall, and behave exactly the same even if the original subject had been silently eliminated and a duplicate left in its place; preserving the pattern is not the same as preserving the person. Elena answered that if “I survived” and “I was destroyed and replaced by an exact duplicate” are indistinguishable by every possible observation from the inside and from the outside, then the distinction is one without a difference; there is no hidden Cartesian fact deciding the matter. Marcus shot back that there is a fact of the matter from the first-person point of view: either the original subject’s stream of consciousness continues through the replacements or it is interrupted, and the fact that outside observers cannot tell only shows the limits of their evidence, not that the question is empty. Otherwise, he said, a destructive scan-and-copy upload would be just as survivable, which he claimed Elena cannot accept. I could not tell who was scoring points. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: identify which claims in each of the four moves are right, which are wrong, and what both parties are missing, and then say who has the better case overall. Please respond in American English.
