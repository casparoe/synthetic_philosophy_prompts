So I've been going back and forth with a friend about what "corruption" actually tracks, and I keep finding that the line people draw—including me—dissolves when I try to cash it out. If a city councilor votes down a housing project because a developer slipped them an envelope of cash, that's corruption and they should be removed. But if the same councilor votes the same way because the developer spent two years taking them to dinners, introducing them to real estate friends, making them feel like an important person, and quietly promising to help with a job after office, most people don't call it corruption. They call it access, or networking, or just politics being politics—even though the vote has the same effect and the councilor is being moved by personal interest either way. Same with a purchasing manager who takes a kickback versus one who keeps choosing a vendor because the vendor's rep is their golf buddy. The obvious difference is cash: a legible, one-shot, explicit exchange. But I'm not sure thats a moral difference rather than just a cognitive readability difference.

What I'd like is the strongest principle you can find that actually generates both judgments—cash kickback bad, sustained social capture fine—and then an honest verdict on whether it survives. Test it against a case where the cash is fully disclosed and legal but still obviously corrupting, and a case where the ingratiation is just as calculating as any bribe (the rep literally has a system for becoming the manager's friend). If no principle works, say whether we should expand the word "corruption" to cover a lot of ordinary relationship-based influence, or narrow it to explicit quid pro quo and admit there's no deep concept underneath.

And then push it to incorruptibility, because that's the part that's bothering me. The folk ideal is someone who can't be bought. But if the real failure mode is captured judgment—being moved by a need to be liked, to belong, to keep access, to feel like the sort of person who matters—then a person with no price but a strong need for approval is corruptible in all the ways that matter, and someone immune to every form of influence would be either paranoid and alienated or stripped of all their cares. I want a verdict on whether the ideal of the incorruptible person survives this, or whether it should be revised down to something like "reliably resists the influences most likely to distort judgment in this particular role." And because I'm asking for a verdict, tell me what would change your mind: which case or argument would most likely flip the conclusion, and where is your reasoning shakiest. Oh and keep it as plain continuous prose like an email—no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
