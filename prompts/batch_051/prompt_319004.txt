I've been tinkering with a distinction for a luck-egalitarian framework and I want it stress-tested before I get too attached. Everyone works with Dworkin's split between brute luck and option luck: brute luck is the stuff that just happens to you no matter what gambles you took, option luck is how a gamble you knowingly entered turns out. But I think there's a third kind I want to call ambient luck, and I want to know if it's real or if I've just relabled brute luck. Ambient luck is the luck of the menu itself. Before you ever get to decide whether to take a gamble, the set of gambles on offer, and the odds they come with, are already fixed by a background you didn't choose. Two people can be exactly equally prudent, make exactly the same choices, and still one ends up worse because her menu was worse: same bets available but at worse odds, or the safer bets missing entirely. The bad result isn't brute luck in any narrow sense, because it arrived through her choice, but it isn't option luck either, because the choice she made was the prudent one and what did the damage was a property of the menu rather than the turn of the gamble. My worry is that if you just push this into brute luck, brute luck ends up swallowing everything, since even option luck is only possible because some menu was supplied to you by luck, and then the brute/option distinction stops doing its job. So: is ambient luck a genuine third category, or does it collapse into one of the other two (or into a mixture of them)? If it's genuine, what does a luck egalitarian owe the person who drew the bad menu — does this just get us back to the familiar point that luck egalitarianism has to care about background inequality, or does it change the standard harshness objection where the theory seems to abandon people who chose badly? And if someone has already made this move under another name, I'd rather learn that now than keep pretending I invented it.
