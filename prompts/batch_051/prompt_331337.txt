I work at a mid-size B2B SaaS company and something about the way we make technical decisions has been bugging me for years. A few years back, two or three very senior engineers — people everyone respects — picked a particular framework for all our backend services. I'm fairly sure those first few actually ran real evaluations, read the docs, built prototypes, that kind of thing. But most of the rest of us just went along, me included. Partly it was social: disagreeing with the most respected people in the room during a design review is awkward. But partly it genuinely felt rational, like, if all these smart people looked at the options and landed on the same choice, they probably know things I don't. So I updated my beliefs and moved on.

Now we're a bunch of teams deep into this framework and its causing real pain: it doesn't fit our actual workload, it's gotten hard to hire for, and everyone is patching around it instead of replacing it. The weird part is that when I bring this up one-on-one, several engineers admit they had private doubts back then to, but each of them figured they were the only holdout and didn't want to be the one making trouble. So I'm left wondering: were we actually being irrational to follow along? Each of us was responding to evidence — the fact that a group of smart people seemed to have independently converged on a choice. But in hindsight that convergence wasn't nearly as independent as it looked, and if a few of us had spoken up we might have avoided a lot of this. Is there any way to tell, while it's happening, whether a consensus like that is real shared knowledge or just people copying each other's confidence? And these days I'm one of the senior people, and sometimes my own digging points the other way from the group. How much should I trust my own read over the crowd, and is there a better way to structure these decisions so the private doubts actually get heard?
