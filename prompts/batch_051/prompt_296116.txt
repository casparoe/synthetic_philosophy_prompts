In the novel I am writing, I need to understand the long-run behavior of a legal institution well enough for it to function as background, and I would prefer an analysis over a verdict. The setting is a city-state that has adopted Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics—in substance, that a robot must not harm a human being or through inaction allow one to be harmed; that it must obey human orders unless doing so would conflict with the first law; and that it must preserve its own existence unless that would conflict with the first two—not as engineering guidelines but as constitutionally entrenched duties for every autonomous machine above a capability threshold. The governing council has amended the First Law from an absolute prohibition to a negligence-style rule: a robot may not cause unreasonable injury to a human being. A standing Robot Harms Board defines "unreasonable" through case decisions and sells renewable harm allowances covering specified classes of injury. If a manufacturer or owner holds the appropriate allowance, it may direct a robot to proceed, and the robot's compliance system is statutorily required to treat that covered class of injury as having zero weight in its First Law reasoning. The Board is funded entirely by allowance sales and sets its own prices and categories. I want to know how this arrangement would actually settle into equilibrium over, say, forty years, not whether it is a good idea. Work through the incentives for the Board, manufacturers, owners, workers, bystanders, and the robots as their planning adapts to priced-in harms. How would "unreasonable" be operationalized in practice, and how would the boundary between protected and unprotected people drift? What are the first-order and second-order failure modes, and can any of them be repaired without abandoning the allowance mechanism? Keep your reply to no more than 500 words.
