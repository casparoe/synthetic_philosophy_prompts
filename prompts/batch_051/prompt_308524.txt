I'm a novelist, mostly writing historical fiction set in the ancient world, so I'm in and out of biblical texts all the time. Over the years I've settled into a reading habit I can't tell is honest. At my desk I read the exodus story the way a historian would: what can be corroborated, what looks like later editorial work, whether a flight from Egypt of anything like that size is plausible. On Sundays I read the same chapters as scripture, as something addressed to me, and I dont ask whether it happened. My rule is strict quarantine. The historical reading and the devotional reading are not allowed to correct each other; if the historian in me suspects the exodus narrative is mostly not reportage, the believer in me still receives it as revelation on Sunday morning, and I refuse to call that a problem. I know that sounds like I'm protecting the devotional reading from evidence, and maybe I am. But the quarantine also protects my work: I get a colder eye and a warmer one, and mixing them would ruin both. So: is this policy defensible, or is it just compartmentalisation with pretensions? Is there a principled version of reading the same text as history and as revelation at once, without one reading dissolving the other, and where does the quarantine break down? I want the shape of the policy, not a yes or no. Could you answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, one or two sentences each, no introduction or conclusion, and use British English.
