I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how we talk about things that *didn't* happen. The other day I sat in on an arguement between two people I'd been interviewing for the piece — call them the philosopher and the lawyer — and I want the thing adjudicated properly, not just reported. The fight was about a scenario that had come up in one of the interviews:

Jim goes on holiday for a fortnight and asks his neighbour, Priya, to water the tomato plants while he's away. She says yes. There's a heatwave; Priya forgets entirely; the tomatoes die. Question: did Priya's omission cause the tomatoes' death?

Here's roughly how it went, in order.

(1) The lawyer said: obviously yes — if Priya had watered them, even once, the plants would have survived. That's just the counterfactual test, the standard starting point for causation in both law and philosophy, and her omission passes it as cleanly as a shove would.

(2) The philosopher said: no. An omission isn't an event; it's the absence of one. You can't point to a time or a place where the not-watering happened. And if you let omissions into causation, then every single person on earth who also didn't water those tomatoes caused their death too. That's absurd, so omissions can't be causes.

(3) The lawyer came back: the "everyone else" problem is a problem for the counterfactual test generally, not for omissions specifically — there are always thousands of but-for conditions, and we still manage to pick out *the* cause. The selection here is obvious: Priya was asked and she agreed. That norm, that expectation, is what makes her omission the cause and everyone else's omission irrelevant.

(4) The philosopher replied: and now norms are doing all the work. You're no longer tracing what happened in the world; you're deciding who you think should have done something. If no one had promised anything, would the cause be the lack of rain, or Jim's failure to install drip irrigation? The answer just tracks what we care about or consider blameworthy. That's fine for assigning blame, maybe, but it's not causation any more — it's ethics in a lab coat.

Please adjudicate this move by move. You are a professional philosopher working on causation and the metaphysics of omissions, so think carefully: start by brainstorming the main positions one could take on omissions, their strengths and weaknesses, and only then deliver your verdict. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating; then say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. If the truth is somewhere in between, I want to know exactly where the line is and what principle draws it. Write your answer in British English.
