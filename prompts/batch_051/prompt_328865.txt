I have been turning this over since my family fell into a slow argument about my uncle, who has advanced dementia and whose earlier signed papers now conflict with what he sometimes says and does. Defenders of the papers keep reaching for the story of Ulysses: knowing he would not be trustworthy once he heard the sirens, he had himself lashed to the mast and ordered his crew to ignore his later pleas, and the comparison is supposed to show that a person's earlier, clearer decision may bind the later, altered person even against that later person's explicit wishes. I can see that the fit is not exact: Ulysses's ordeal lasted a matter of hours, whereas my uncle's condition lasts years; Ulysses came back to being the same man, while my uncle likely will not; and Ulysses's crew were carrying out a fixed instruction, whereas a care team is responding in real time to a person asking for things now. But I cannot tell which of these differences are fatal to the argument and which are only surface.

I would like a careful audit of the analogy. Please set out the correspondence first: what plays the role of the earlier self, the later self, the signed document, the ropes, the crew, the sirens, and the pleas to be untied. Then work through the main disanalogies and say which ones actually matter for the conclusion people are trying to draw—that the earlier directive should be honored over what the person in the bed is now expressing—and which ones do not. Finally, does that conclusion survive once these differences are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if some other comparison would do the work better, what would it be?
