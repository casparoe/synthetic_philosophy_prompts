Been reading Locke's *Second Treatise of Government*, and there's a passage in it that keeps bumping up against something I notice in modern constitutional crises too:

"Many things there are, which the law can by no means provide for; and those must necessarily be left to the discretion of him that has the executive power in his hands, to be ordered by him as the public good and advantage shall require: nay, it is fit that the laws themselves should in some cases give way to the executive power, or rather to this fundamental law of nature and government, viz. That as much as may be, all the members of the society are to be preserved: for since many accidents may happen, wherein a strict and rigid observation of the laws may do harm; (as not to pull down an innocent man's house to stop the fire, when the next to it is burning)..."

Now set aside his king-and-parliament framing. The pattern I keep seeing is this: every set of rules runs out somewhere, and somone fills the silence by appeal to necessity or the obvious good, and then afterwards the off-book act gets folded back in—by retroactive approval, by a new rule, or just by everyone deciding not to make a fuss. I'm not after the usual name for the narrow royal power Locke himself is describing; I already know that one. What I want to know is whether the broader configuration has a name in political or legal theory: a legal order's built-in silence, plus discretionary action that occupies the silence and then gets normalized, plus the standing question of whether the gap was really there or was partly created by the person claiming it. If it doesn't have a name, what would you call it, and where does gap-filling stop being gap-filling? A bare label won't do much for me—I need someone to say what this is not, and which neighbouring ideas it keeps getting confused with.
