A colleague in my department put the following chain of reasoning to me and I cannot find a step I am willing to call false, even though the conclusion seems far too strong. It is a version of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, but he has tightened it so that it is supposed to show more than that nobody can be morally responsible: it is supposed to show that even the effort to become a better person cannot be to one's credit.

Here is the argument as he set it out.

(1) You are truly morally responsible for what you do only if you are truly responsible for the character, motives, preferences, and values that issue in the doing.

(2) You are truly responsible for those conditions only if you chose them.

(3) You could have chosen them only if you already had a prior character, set of motives, preferences, and values in the light of which you chose.

(4) You are truly responsible for that prior character only if you chose it, which again requires a still earlier character, and so on.

(5) The regress can be stopped only if you are causa sui — a cause of yourself — in a way that would make you responsible for being the sort of cause you are.

(6) You are not causa sui in that sense, because to cause yourself you would already have to exist as the cause that does the causing.

(7) Therefore you are not truly responsible for the character and motives from which your action issues.

(8) Therefore you are not truly morally responsible for the action.

Then he added the extension that disturbs me most:

(9) The same reasoning applies to every act of self-examination, resolution, therapy, or discipline: each is issued from a character you did not ultimately choose.

(10) Therefore no one can deserve credit or blame for any effort at self-improvement, or for any belief, preference, or commitment derived from such effort.

(11) But it is absurd to say that someone who works hard to overcome a vicious disposition, or who undertakes years of treatment to control an addiction, does not deserve any credit for the resulting character.

(12) Since (1)–(10) entail (8) and (10), and (10) is absurd, at least one premise or step must be wrong.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2), because they capture what I mean by 'truly' moral responsibility; I would rather give up (9), because I suspect the extension trades on a different sense of 'responsible'. But I cannot name the step, and my colleague says that if I reject (9) I also have to reject (1). Where does this go wrong, if it does? What does rejecting each live option cost — for example, rejecting (1) in order to keep everyday responsibility, or rejecting (2) and stopping the regress at the first character one finds oneself with, or rejecting the inference from (1)–(6) to (8) by distinguishing different senses of 'choose' or 'responsible'?
