I've been reading Andersen to my daughter at bedtime and The Emperor's New Clothes has been nagging at me ever since. Everyone knows the obvious moral about vanity or about being too scared to speak up, but I keep getting stuck on something else. Lately I've been going down a rabbit hole about whether you can take someone else's word that a work of art is good — someone whose taste you trust tells you a film is devastating, or a restaurant is superb, and you repeat it as if you knew. Some people think your aesthetic judgment has to come from your own experience to count for anything; other people think it's perfectly normal to borrow it. I think the fancy term I stumbled on is aesthetic testimony.

In the tale, the ministers and then the whole town don't see the cloth but they take the weavers' description of it — the colors, the pattern — and then they take each other's word for it at the parade, until a kid says the emperor hasn't got anything on. I want to read the story through that lens. Is it taking a side on whether aesthetic judgment can come from other people's say-so, or is it only about lying and the fear of looking stupid? Does the child prove first-hand experience wins, or is his point not even about beauty — just about whether the clothes exist — so the story is a worse fit for the question then it first appears? And is there anything in the tale that a defender of either side of the debate would find useful, something the standard arguments leave out? I keep flip-flopping on whether the con only works because the weavers are frauds and everyone else is insincere — if an honest, knowledgeable critic had told the emperor the clothes were beautiful, would the story still have its warning in it? I'm basically trying to write a little essay for my own sanity, and I'd like the reading to be honest in both directions: not forcing the story to be a philosophy lecture, and not ignoring the places where the story won't cooperate.
