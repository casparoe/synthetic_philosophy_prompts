I have noticed what looks like a structural parallel between the value of a statistical life as used in health economics and the concept of temperature in statistical mechanics, and I would like to know whether it is deep or merely a loose analogy. On the economic side, one arrives at the value of a statistical life by taking the small amounts people are willing to pay for small reductions in their mortality risk and scaling up until those reductions add up to one expected death, so there is no particular person who is the statistical life. On the physical side, temperature is likewise a property of an ensemble: it is defined for large collections of molecules near equilibrium, while no single molecule has a temperature. In both cases a quantity that appears to carry real information and to guide decisions belongs to the aggregate rather than to any member of it, and in both cases its well-definedness depends on conditions that can fail. Is this a genuine identity of structure? If it is, does the statistical-mechanical fact that temperature becomes ill-defined for small systems or systems far from equilibrium transfer to the value of a statistical life, and what would the corresponding breakdown conditions on the economic side be?
