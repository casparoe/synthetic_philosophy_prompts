Consider the following argument that rational people with a common prior cannot publicly announce different posterior probabilities.

(1) Aumann's agreement theorem states: when two agents share the same prior over a common space of possibilities, and their posterior probabilities for a given proposition are common knowledge, then the two posterior probabilities must be equal.
(2) Alice and Bob share a prior about whether a new drug is effective. They then privately examine different bodies of evidence, after which Alice's posterior is 0.7 and Bob's posterior is 0.3.
(3) Each then truthfully announces their posterior to the other, and both announcements are public and mutually heard.
(4) After these announcements, it is common knowledge that Alice's posterior is 0.7 and Bob's posterior is 0.3: each knows both values, each knows the other knows both values, and so on.
(5) The theorem therefore applies and yields 0.7 = 0.3, which is impossible.
(6) Hence the situation in (2) and (3) cannot involve two rational agents with a common prior; any two rational people who publicly state different credences must not genuinely share a prior, and the familiar position of agreeing on fundamentals while disagreeing about the evidence is simply confused.

I find (6) extremely implausible, but each step looks individually plausible to me. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the first step that fails, explain exactly what the theorem does and does not imply once agents can hear each other's announcements and update on them, and state what actually follows about rational disagreement under a common prior.
