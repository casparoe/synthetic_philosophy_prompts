I am a novelist. For the research phase of any book set in a living community, I have a fixed rule: I take the community's own self-description as my primary evidence about its internal life, and when an ethnographer, journalist, or former member offers an interpretation that conflicts with that self-description, I discount it almost entirely, however credentialed or widely credited the account may be. My reasoning is that outsiders and ex-members writing for an outside audience have incentives to flatten the community into a legible and marketable story, while the members themselves preserve the granularity I need. At the same time, I know such communities often curate their self-description, police dissent, and train members to disregard defectors—not merely an epistemic bubble in which outside information is absent, but something closer to an echo chamber in which outside voices are actively discredited. There is a real possibility that my rule merely imports the community's own filtering mechanism and calls it research method. Is my rule defensible as a way of protecting novelistic granularity, or is it a motivated self-deception dressed up as discipline? If it is not defensible as stated, what is the closest version that keeps the benefit without the cost?
