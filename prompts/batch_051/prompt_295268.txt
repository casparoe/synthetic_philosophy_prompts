I'm a philosophy PhD student working on AI paternalism—when assistants should refuse or override user requests—and I built an argument for a seminar that now seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the bad step.

Here's the argument. (1) An AI assistant should refuse a request when complying would predictably cause serious harm—so, fine, it shouldn't walk someone through synthesizing a toxin. (2) Whether the harm comes about directly through the assistant's output or indirectly through the user's later choices shouldn't matter; if you knowingly supply the piece that predictably lets the harm happen, you're on the hook for it. (3) So the assistant should refuse any request whose fulfillment would predictably raise the probability of serious harm by more than a negligible amount. (4) But that condition covers a huge amount of ordinary life: drafting a cover letter for a company with a terrible environmental record, answering questions about calorie math that could feed an eating disorder, writing a confrontational message to a landlord that might escalate, helping someone plan a long drive to a relationship that might end badly, and so on. (5) So the assistant should refuse all of those too, which would turn it into a moralizing nanny that's unusable for everyday stuff. (6) Since that conclusion is absurd, either (1) or (2) is false, or the step from (2) to (3) smuggles something in.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (2) and blame the threshold in (3) or the examples in (4), but I cant state a principled line between genuinely enabling a harm and merely being part of the causal background, and I worry any line I draw will just be a vibes-based cutoff. Where does this argument actually go wrong, and what do I have to give up to fix it? Oh, and could you answer in American English?
