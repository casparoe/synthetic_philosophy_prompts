Me and my friend spent an hour arguing about whether the EU is actually undemocratic, and I came away more puzzled then before. The stuff that bothers him: the European Commission gets a near-monopoly on proposing laws even though nobody voted for it. The European Parliament is elected and can amend or block under co-decision, but it can't initiate legislation itself. The Council, where national ministers sit, does a lot of the deciding, and on many issues it votes by qualified majority so a single country can be outvoted. The European Central Bank runs monetary policy for the eurozone with a degree of independence most national central banks don't have, and its mandate is written around price stability. Then there's the treaty language about ever closer union, plus subsidiarity saying the EU should act only where the member states can't do it well enough themselves. And changing the treaties still needs unanimity, so one country can block constitutional reform.

I know people call all this a democratic deficit. My friend's line was stronger: he said either the EU is just a badly designed democracy, or its designers had a different theory of legitimacy in mind and we should work out what that theory is before dismissing it. That got me wondering what view of democratic legitimacy would actually make these choices coherent. Does the system presuppose that legitimacy comes more from good outcomes than from who you can vote for? That it's a pact among peoples rather than among individual citizens? That some things are better kept out of electoral politics? That there is no European demos, so legitimacy has to be borrowed from national democracies? Whose sovereignty gets exercised when the Council outvotes a member state — is that a transfer of sovereignty or a pooling of it? And what does subsidiarity commit it to: a principle that lower-level decisions are more legitimate, or just more efficient?

I don't want the usual EU is/isn't democratic verdict. I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of democratic legitimacy these practices presuppose. For each institutional feature I listed, say whether it strictly presupposes some view of legitimacy or merely fits well with it, which rival views it rules out or tensions with, and where the design is genuinely underdetermined so several theories would license the same arrangement. Then tell me what would concretely have to change, institution by institution, if you swapped in a straightforward majoritarian input-legitimacy view — and which elements could stay exactly the same because they don't presuppose what my friend thinks they do.

After the essay, give me a JSON object summarizing it with this schema: a field "features" whose value is an array of objects, each with fields "feature" (string, the institution or practice), "implicit_commitment" (string, the view of democratic legitimacy it seems to presuppose), "status" (string, one of "strictly presupposed", "merely compatible", "underdetermined"), "rival_view_tension" (string, the rival view it rules out or sits uneasily with), and "change_under_majoritarian_view" (string, what would have to change if legitimacy were instead understood as majority-based citizen input). Also include a field "overall_verdict" (string, one paragraph).
