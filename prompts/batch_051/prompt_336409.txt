Hey, I'm a PhD student in epistemology and I've been writing down my gut reactions to a bunch of made-up assertion cases. I want to know whether there's one norm underneath them or whether I'm just inconsistent. Here are my verdicts.

1. Lottery. A friend announces flat-out "my ticket won't win," purely on the basis of one-in-ten-million odds. Verdict: not okay. I want to say "you don't know that," even though he's almost certainly right. But I'd be totally fine with him saying "it's overwhelmingly likely my ticket won't win."

2. Stopped clock. Someone looks at a clock that stopped at 3:15 and says "it's 3:15." By coincidence it really is 3:15. Verdict: not okay, even though what he said is true and looking at a clock is normally excellent evidence. Something about how he got there bothers me.

3. Lucky guesser. A guy at trivia night with no idea shouts "it's Napoleon!" and happens to be right. Verdict: not okay to assert, even though true.

4. Careful doctor. A doctor reads a correctly labelled lab report and tells a patient "your test came back negative." The lab actually mixed up the samples and the patient is positive. Verdict: fine. I don't blame her at all; given her evidence, she said exactly what she should of said. It would be strange to say she shouldn't have told him that.

5. Moore-ish. Someone tells you "the store is closed," and then, a second later, adds "but I don't believe the store is closed." Verdict: not okay, even though the first part might be true and he might have plenty of evidence for it.

6. The one I keep flip-flopping on. Someone tells a friend "the restaurant is open until 10" based on a reliable app that hasn't been updated today, and the restaurant actually closed early for staffing. I go back and forth between "fine, same as the doctor" and "no, he shouldn't have said it flat out."

My starting thought was that the norm is just: assert only what you know. That handles 1, 2, 3, and 5 cleanly. But the doctor case pushes me toward something like: assert only what you're justified in believing. And then the lottery and stopped-clock verdicts stop making sense, because those people have great statistical or normal evidence. So I'm stuck.

Is there a single norm of assertion that gets all of these, or at least a principled way to sort them into permissible assertions versus blameless but improper ones? Which of my verdicts do I have to give up, and what's the least ad hoc repair? And what should I settle on for case 6—or is there another case I should test the candidate rule against that would break the tie? I'd rather have a simple principle than a pile of patches, but I also don't want to drop a verdict I actually feel strongly about just because it's inconvenient.
