I have been trying to work out what actually makes an oath bind, and my verdicts on particular cases seem to pull in different directions. The case that started it is the Greek one: the gods themselves swear by the river Styx, and a god who breaks such an oath is punished in ways even gods fear, even though no external power could have coerced the god into keeping it. That makes me suspect that an oath's force cannot simply be the expected cost of breaking it, but other cases make me unsure. I want to know what principle, if any, captures my pattern of verdicts, or which of them I should give up.

Here are my cases. (1) Styx: I take the oath to be genuinely binding and the punishment deserved, even though the god could not be forced. (2) A person at gunpoint who swears to hand over their savings has not bound themselves; the oath is void. (3) Someone swears to a dying parent that they will look after a younger sibling, and decades later this costs them dearly; I think they are bound even though the parent is gone and no court or third party could hold them to it. (4) A child who swears a blood oath of everlasting friendship in a treehouse is not thereby bound as an adult; I treat that as not a real oath. (5) Crossing one's fingers while swearing empties the oath: it does not bind the swearer, and I do not think the witness may treat it as binding. (6) A soldier who swore an oath to obey orders is not, in my view, bound to obey an order to commit a war crime; I am inclined to say the oath binds them to refuse. (7) A person who swears on a holy book they do not believe in, with full intention to keep the promise, seems to me to bind themselves by the promise but not, exactly, by the oath.

The tentative principle I have been testing is this: an oath binds just in case it is a free and sincere act by a competent person whereby one deliberately places oneself under a standard one cannot later disown. That handles the duress and finger-crossing cases as failures of freedom or sincerity, and it can explain the Styx case if the relevant standard is something objective in the order of things rather than the swearer's own psychology. But it does not capture what the oath adds in the deathbed case, where a mere promise would already bind on most accounts; and I cannot settle whether the holy-book case should count as an oath at all. If it should, the principle may be too subjective; if it should not, I may be smuggling in theism.

Can you offer a principle, or a small set of candidates, that gets these verdicts or most of them, with each clause of the account earning its place by one of the cases? Which of my verdicts does the account force me to revise, and what does it say about the case I keep returning to: a person who swears an oath of secrecy to a friend and then learns that the secret is a serious crime the friend committed? Is the oath binding, void, or something in between?
