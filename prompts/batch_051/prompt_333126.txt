Here's the exam question (20 points):

Explain what a QALY is and how cost-per-QALY analysis is used to set priorities in health care. Then give two distinct objections to using QALY maximization as the decision rule for allocating scarce health resources in a pandemic. Finally, consider a triage protocol that gives absolute priority to treating identified patients already in the hospital over preventive measures that would reduce the risk of death for a larger number of unidentified people. Here's the student's answer:

A QALY (quality-adjusted life year) combines length of life and quality of life into one number. A year in full health is worth 1, a year in a health state rated 0.4 is worth 0.4, and two extra years at 0.5 equal one extra year in full health. In priority-setting you compute the cost per QALY gained for each intervention and fund them in order from the lowest cost per QALY upward, because that buys the most health for a fixed budget. Since QALYs are a measure of utility, QALY maximization is the health-sector version of utilitarianism, which is why it is used as the efficiency criterion.

The first objection is that QALY maximization ignores distribution. It counts a QALY the same no matter whose it is, so it will fund a small gain for a mildly ill person if the numbers beat a more meaningful gain for someone who is much worse off. That is why people bring in severity and fair-innings considerations. The second objection is that health-state valuations are method-dependent: time trade-off and standard gamble give different values, so the resulting QALY scores are not as neutral as they appear.

Would a QALY-maximizer endorse the triage protocol? Yes. The identified patient is a real, named person whose QALYs can be counted, while the people whom prevention would save are only probable and unidentified. Certain QALYs for an identifiable person outweigh merely expected QALYs for unidentified people, so the maximizer would give absolute priority to the identified patient. That is the same conclusion as the rule of rescue.

This also fits the value of a statistical life. The standard VSL figure (roughly $10 million) is the amount people are willing to pay to save an identified individual, so building VSL into policy commits you to favoring identified patients over statistical ones. So the rule of rescue and the VSL literature both line up with QALY maximization: a life you can put a name to is worth more in the calculation.
