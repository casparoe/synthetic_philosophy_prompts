I've been thinking about evidential decision theory, and I've built an argument that now strikes me as proving too much. I'd like you to tell me where it goes wrong.

(1) Evidential decision theory says to choose the act that would be the best news — the act whose performance gives you the strongest evidence that good outcomes obtain — even when the act does not cause those outcomes. That is why, in Newcomb's problem, where a predictor has filled an opaque box with $1,000,000 if it predicted you would take only that box and left it empty otherwise, while a transparent box plainly contains $1,000, you should take only the opaque box: your taking only that box is evidence that the predictor placed the million dollars inside it, even though your choice does not cause the predictor's earlier action.

(2) Now consider a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a stranger you will never meet again. Suppose you have good reason to think the stranger faces the same payoffs and reasons in the same way you do. Your cooperating does not cause the stranger to cooperate. But your cooperating is evidence that the stranger cooperates, and your defecting is evidence that the stranger defects.

(3) By (1), if the evidential correlation in (2) is strong enough, you should cooperate — not because your cooperation causes anything good, but because it is evidence that the stranger's cooperation, and therefore the good mutual-cooperation payoff, is more likely.

(4) Now take a large world: a universe containing an enormous number of agents in situations relevantly like yours, running the same kind of reasoning. For any act you consider, your performing it is evidence that very many of those similar agents perform it too.

(5) By the same reasoning as (3), in a large world you should evaluate acts by what they would mean if chosen by the whole correlated group. You should cooperate in every one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a similar reasoner, keep promises to strangers, give to any cause that similar reasoners would give to, and more generally act as though you were choosing on behalf of everyone who reasons as you do — even where your act has no causal path to their behavior.

(6) But that conclusion is absurd. It makes me rationally required to pay real costs for outcomes in corners of the universe I cannot affect, it seems to double-count consequences my act does not bring about, and it would leave nearly every decision hostage to unverifiable correlations with unseen agents.

(7) So evidential decision theory, applied to large worlds, must be rejected somewhere — or the conclusion in (5) must be accepted.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would most like to keep (1) and the one-boxing verdict in Newcomb's problem, and I am most suspicious of the move from (3) to (5), but I cannot yet say what is wrong with it. Is there a subtle slide in the argument, or is it valid with the real choice being between evidential decision theory and the conclusion in (5)? I am not asking for a literature review; I want your own reconstruction of the options.
