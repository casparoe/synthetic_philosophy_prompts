In my philosophy of language seminar I keep returning to an argument that seems to force a contradiction from premises each of which I find difficult to deny, and I would like a diagnosis. Here is the argument as I have it:

(1) 'Sherlock Holmes' is an empty name: there is no such person, and the name has no referent.
(2) The semantic contribution of a proper name is exhausted by its referent; so, where a name has no referent, an atomic sentence combining it with an ordinary predicate expresses no complete proposition.
(3) Therefore 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' expresses no proposition and is neither true nor false.
(4) But 'In the Conan Doyle stories, Sherlock Holmes is a detective' is simply true.
(5) A sentence of the form 'In the fiction, S' can be true only if the embedded sentence S supplies a proposition for the fiction operator to evaluate.
(6) By (4) and (5), 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' must supply a proposition after all.
(7) By (2) and (6), 'Sherlock Holmes' must have a referent after all.
(8) But (7) contradicts (1).

Thus at least one of these commitments must be abandoned, and yet I would most like to keep (1) and (4). Where does the argument go wrong? If it contains a subtly invalid step, identify it; if it is valid, say which premise is least costly to reject. In either case, explain what the main positions in this area—Millian, Fregean or descriptivist, artifactualist, and pretense-theoretic accounts—each have to give up in order to escape the contradiction.
