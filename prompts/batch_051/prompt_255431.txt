I've been trying to sort out a running dispute with a colleague about what a scale of civilizational advancement actually measures, and I've built an argument that ends somewhere I don't accept. Here is the setup. One familiar family of measures, the Kardashev-type, ranks civilizations by the rate at which they can command useful energy: planetary endowment, then stellar output, then galactic output, and so on. A second family, the Barrow-type, ranks civilizations by the smallest structure they can deliberately manipulate: bulk engineering at one end, then molecular machines, then atomically precise fabrication, and smaller still. Both styles feel as though they capture something real. One can point to a civilization that disassembles a moon for raw material but cannot do fine fabrication, and another that builds nanoscale machines but never leaves its home planet, and neither obviously looks like a lesser achievement merely because of that contrast. Yet the following chain suggests the whole project is projective.

(1) A scale of advancement assigns each civilization a value along a single dimension, so that comparisons can be read directly off the ordering.
(2) If two civilizations differ on irreducibly different dimensions of capability, a single-dimension scale must either ignore one dimension or adopt a rate of exchange between them.
(3) Energy reach and engineering grain are irreducibly different dimensions: commanding more power and arranging matter at a finer scale are not amounts of the same quantity, and neither can be defined in terms of the other.
(4) These dimensions can come apart. Nothing rules out a civilization that commands stellar energies but achieves only coarse, bulk fabrication, or one that commands a modest energy budget but builds at the molecular or atomic scale.
(5) When reach and grain come apart in this way, there is no fact about which civilization is more advanced unless some exchange rate is forced on us by the phenomena.
(6) But any exchange rate between reach and grain is imposed, not discovered: it records what the rater happens to value, not any feature of the civilizations being compared.
(7) Therefore, for pairs in which reach and grain pull in opposite directions, no objective comparison of advancement is available.
(8) If such pairs have no objective comparison, then advancement is not a complete ordering of civilizations; it is at best a partial order, defined only where one civilization dominates on every relevant axis.
(9) Yet we make comparisons the partial order forbids. We would unhesitatingly rank a stellar-output civilization above a planetary-output one, or an industrial society above a pre-agricultural one, even when they are not simply tied on every axis.
(10) So either those familiar rankings are systematically false, or the very idea of advancement is a projection dressed up as a measurement.
(11) That conclusion seems unacceptable, so one of the early steps has to go.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I had to choose, I would most like to keep (3) and (9), because I think the two dimensions really are distinct and I also think some cross-century comparisons are not merely conventional. But I do not see how to hold both without smuggling in an exchange rate from somewhere. Is there a subtle equivocation in the middle of the chain, or is the argument valid and the cost of keeping (3) and (9) simply higher than I think?
