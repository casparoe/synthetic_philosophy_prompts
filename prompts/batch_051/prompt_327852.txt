I am a novelist working on a book whose first half follows a democracy unraveling through small, legal, administrative steps, and people I trust keep telling me the category for what I am describing is democratic backsliding rather than a coup or a revolution. Before I adopt that framing, I want to pressure-test the concept against a work of fiction. Read Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale — the novel, the early episodes of the television series, or both — through the lens of the democratic-backsliding literature. Which features of Gilead's rise fit the standard criteria, such as being incremental and legalistic, formally constitutional, led by elected insiders, and aimed first at courts, the press, and electoral rules, and which features are closer to a revolutionary break? More fundamentally, does the novel treat backsliding as something a society can recognize while it is happening, or as a retrospective, politically contested label? Does the story show that label doing real explanatory work rather than merely partisan work, and who inside the story gets to decide how the change is described? Finally, where does the fiction resist the political-science reading, and what does it add to the comparative debate around Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States that the academic argument alone misses? As the very last line, give a one-sentence summary of your overall reading, with nothing after it.
