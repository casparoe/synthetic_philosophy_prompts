I am a philosophy professor preparing a presentation for an interdisciplinary workshop on concepts and mental representation six weeks from now. The audience will be mostly philosophers of language and mind with broadly Fregean or externalist commitments: they hold that whatever concepts are, there must be something about a concept that remains stable across its varying applications and fixes what a thought is about. There will also be a smaller group of cognitive scientists who work on categorization and who consider prototypes and exemplar models adequate to whatever instability concepts actually display.

My paper defends the following thesis. In the letter-string analogy programs of the fluid-concepts tradition — the family associated with the Copycat program, which solves problems of the form abc → abd, ijk → ? by gradually warping representations along dimensions such as alphabetic position, group length, and direction — the graded, context-sensitive slippage of representations is not a performance defect that runs on top of stable contents. It is the basic operation by which contents get applied, and any attempt to reconstruct the concept as a stable entity, whether a definition, a prototype, or an externally determined kind, mistakes a frozen trace of that operation for the mechanism that produced it. My linchpin is that letter strings are the least favorable domain for this claim: if the instability appears even where the domain can be formalized exactly, then it should be treated as central rather than as a special pathology of messy real-world concepts.
