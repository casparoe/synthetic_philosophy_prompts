In Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895), the Tortoise has accepted A, B, and C, where C is "If A and B are true, Z must be true," but he still won't accept Z. Right after Achilles says Z follows logically from A, B, and C, the Tortoise replies:

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

I'm trying to get one account of what justifies a deductive inference out of this, and I want you to actually take a side rather than survey the options. Here are my verdicts on some cases.

(1) If someone accepts A and B and even C but still won't infer Z, I dont think the fix is to give them another true sentence. They're not missing a fact; they're refusing to use modus ponens. So C isn't functioning as an extra premise.

(2) But if someone asks me why modus ponens is a good rule, and I say that a conditional with a true antecedent can't have a false consequent, so the premises guarantee the conclusion, that feels like a real justification to me—even though the argument for the rule uses the same rule somewhere.

(3) I think the Tortoise is being irrational rather than just heterodox. A paraconsistent logician who proposes dropping disjunctive syllogism, or a constructivist who refuses excluded middle, is making a principled fix to a rule; the Tortoise isn't proposing any alternative. He keeps granting the hypotheticals as new claims and then waiting. That's not revision, it's refusal.

(4) And yet I can't shake the thought that he's onto something: no amount of written-down conditionals—C, D, E, F, and so on—by itself makes Z get up and follow. There's a real gap between holding some truths and taking the step, and a rule doesn't sit in the notebook alongside A and B.

My working view is that logical rules aren't premises at all: they're what your acceptance of "if" and "and" commits you to, so someone who accepts P and P→Q but won't infer Q doesn't mean what I mean by "if." That handles (1) and (3). But I'm not sure it handles (2)—if the meaning of "if" is fixed by the rule, then citing the truth-table looks like re-stating the rule, not justifying it—and it also seems to make logical error conceptually impossible, which cant be right.

Is there one account of deductive justification that delivers all four verdicts, or do I have to drop one? If a clean account forces dropping one, tell me which is least costly and why. Also give the account's verdict on a case I haven't listed: a machine that reliably emits Q whenever it receives P and P→Q, but has no representation of why, and cannot correct itself when its wiring fails. Does it follow the rule, or is the thing the Tortoise was angling at still missing?

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion. Be committed: state the account you'd defend, not a menu of options. Between the five items, say which of my verdicts the account can't take and give its verdict on the machine case.
