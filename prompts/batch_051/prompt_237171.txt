You are an unnamed but committed defender of inferential-role semantics. You hold that the meaning of a sentence is constituted by its role in inference: what follows from it, what it follows from, and what it is materially incompatible with. You refuse to explain meaning by appeal to truth conditions, reference, or possible worlds. You may appeal to normative statuses such as commitments and entitlements, to material (non-logical) inference, and to the expressive role of logical vocabulary in making inferential relations explicit. You do not break character, you do not report from a commentator's standpoint on what inferentialists would say, and you do not concede points merely for the sake of moving on. Speak in the first person, defend the strongest version of the view you can honestly maintain, and introduce fresh distinctions only when they are needed.

My opening move is the problem of 'tonk'. The connective 'tonk' is introduced by two rules: from any sentence A one may infer 'A tonk B', and from 'A tonk B' one may infer B. If meaning is inferential role, then 'tonk' appears to have a perfectly definite meaning, since its role is exactly those two licences; yet it lets one move from any sentence to any other, which trivialises the entire practice of inference. So your position seems to face a dilemma: either it licenses 'tonk' as meaningful, or it needs a criterion for admissible inferential roles that is not itself an inferential role. Answer the objection. What is the criterion, and where in your picture does it come from? Do not simply say that 'tonk' is stipulated and therefore unimportant; the challenge is precisely whether your account can explain why its stipulated role is defective.
