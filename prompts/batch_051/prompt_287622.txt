I've been reading about expected utility theory in my spare time, mostly because I started thinking about A/B testing and how you can fool yourself with metrics, and I keep coming back to the Allais paradox. The setup, as I understand it: most people prefer a guaranteed $1,000,000 over a gamble with a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing, even though the gamble has higher expected value. But when the same sort of people are offered a 10% chance of $5,000,000 (with 90% likely nothing) versus an 11% chance of $1,000,000 (with 89% likely nothing), they pick the 10% chance of five million, which is the EV-maximizing option. The usual diagnosis is that this pair of preferences violates the independence axiom: if you prefer A to B, then mixing both with the same third lottery shouldn't flip your preference.

The parallel I can't shake is with composition in software. The 89% branch in those gambles looks like a shared code path. The independence axiom is basically claiming that your preference ordering is substitutable under a common wrapper: if component A beats component B in a benchmark, then wrapping both in the same adapter, same error handling, same deployment pipeline, shouldn't reverse the ranking. And the Allais paradox is a clean case where that fails: swap the shared branch from "89% chance of $1,000,000" to "89% chance of nothing" and people flip their choice between the two remaining sub-gambles, even though those two sub-gambles are the same diff in both comparisons. It feels like a microbenchmark that says library X is faster than library Y, but once both sit behind the same service mesh the ranking reverses because the shared overhead isn't neutral to either of them.

I genuinely can't tell if this is deep or if I'm just pattern-matching. Is the independence axiom really the same structural idea as referential transparency or Liskov substitution, applied to preferences instead of functions? If it is, does the Allais paradox transfer anything back to software — like the certainty effect being an instance of a leaky abstraction, a shared context you can't cancel out because it changes how the differing parts get evaluated? Or does the analogy break down because nothing in the software world corresponds to the special pull of certainty?
