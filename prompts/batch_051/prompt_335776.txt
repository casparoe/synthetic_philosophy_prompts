I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been invited to give a lunch talk to an anesthesiology department, and I keep going in circles on one thing. Act as a professional philosopher of mind: think carefully, and before you commit to a reconstruction, brainstorm two or three different things the practice might be committed to, then settle on the most defensible one.

Here's the puzzle. In a modern OR, a lot of anesthesia is managed with a processed-EEG depth-of-anesthesia monitor: it reduces the raw EEG to a single index, and the anesthesiologist keeps that index in a target band and then acts as if the patient is genuinely unconcious — they cut, they tell the family the patient felt nothing. (I know awareness-with-recall sometimes happens and the monitors aren't perfect; set those failure cases aside. I'm asking about the success cases, where the index really does track what the clinician thinks it tracks.)

Now suppose someone is moved by the philosophical zombie argument: zombies are creatures physically and behaviorally identical to us — same EEG, same index number — but with no phenomenal consciousness. If such a being is possible, two patients could have the same monitor reading while one has experience and the other has none, so the move from "index is 42" to "no experience is happening" isn't innocent observation. It has to rely on some picture of the mind-brain relation: maybe reductive physicalism, maybe the weaker claim that consciousness supervenes on the physical with lawful regularity in our world, maybe just an inference to the best explanation from the index tracking arousal.

So: what exactly does this practice of using such monitors to declare patients unconscious presuppose about the relation between physical states and experience? Is it strictly commited to a particular metaphysics, or compatible with several? And if the hospital took the conceivability argument seriously — not as a reason to abandon anesthesia, but as a genuine epistemic constraint — what would have to change in the protocols, the consent forms, and what clinicians are allowed to tell families about whether the patient felt anything?
