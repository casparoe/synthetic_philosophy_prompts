In a seminar on the metaphysics of time, we have been stuck for three sessions on Aristotle’s De Interpretatione chapter 9, specifically the paragraph about the sea-battle tomorrow. As I understand the passage, Aristotle grants that either there will be a sea-battle tomorrow or there won’t, and that the disjunction is necessary; but he denies that it is already settled which branch is true, and he seems to resist the inference from the sentence’s already being true to the event’s being necessary. The class has split roughly in two. One side says that bivalence is nonnegotiable: every declarative sentence is either true or false, so if the sentence “there will be a sea-battle tomorrow” is true now, then the battle is already inevitable, and that is the very outcome Aristotle wants to avoid. This side therefore concludes that Aristotle is best read as rejecting bivalence for future contingents, leaving a truth-value gap until the event happens. The other side says bivalence is harmless; truth is not the same as settledness or necessity, and the necessity belongs only to the disjunction or to the conditional, not to either branch. They think Aristotle himself says exactly this, and that the first side has confused the law of excluded middle with the law of bivalence. We all agree on the words of the chapter and we all understand the formal options; no one moves. My working hypothesis is that the two sides mean different things by “true”: one has in mind correspondence with a fully determinate realm of facts, the other has in mind a weaker notion of correct assertion or semantic value. But I am not sure that diagnosis is right, or whether it would even help. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, substantive, exegetical, formal, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress?
