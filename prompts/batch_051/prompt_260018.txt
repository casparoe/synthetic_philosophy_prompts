I have been thinking about the Pasadena game, which is usually presented as follows. A fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it comes up heads for the first time; if the first heads appears on the nth toss, the player receives (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n dollars, where negative values are losses. The naive expected value is the sum over n of (1/2^n)[(-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n], and once the powers of two cancel this becomes the alternating harmonic series. That series is only conditionally convergent: its positive and negative parts each diverge, so the expectation does not exist in the absolute sense, and its limit depends on the order of summation. A friend tells me this is an artifact of making the coin exactly fair, which is both an idealization and a measure-zero assumption among possible biases.

So let the same payoff scheme stay fixed while the coin becomes biased: heads occurs with probability p on each toss, independently, and q = 1 - p. Work out the formal expression for the expectation as a function of p, and then compare p = 0.6, p = 0.5, and p = 0.4. For each case, say whether the expectation is absolutely summable, merely conditionally summable, or divergent, and what the value is when it exists. Then address the conceptual question: does the original Pasadena lesson survive this variation, or does the game become a knife-edge curiosity whose bite disappears as soon as the coin is not exactly fair? In particular, if p is slightly greater than one half, should a decision theorist regard the game as unproblematic merely because its expectation is now finite, even though the payoffs remain unbounded in both directions? I am less interested in the arithmetic than in what this tells us about how seriously undefined-expectation puzzles should constrain expected utility theory in real contexts.
