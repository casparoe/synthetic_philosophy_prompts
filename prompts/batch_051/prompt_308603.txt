Consider the following variation on the "should the numbers count?" rescue case associated with Taurek. You have one dose of a life-saving drug, and six strangers need it; all will die without it. You can give the dose to a single person, A, or to a group of five, B1 through B5. In the familiar deterministic version, whoever receives the dose is saved with certainty, and the question is whether the fact that five would be saved rather than one settles what you should do.

Now change one parameter. The drug is a guaranteed match for A's condition, but for reasons none of you control it would save each member of the group only with probability p, independently, where p is known in advance and between 0 and 1. Work first with p = 1/2, then generalize.

Take the two familiar camps in the original dispute. The aggregator holds that five equal claims outweigh one, so you should save the group; expected-value reasoning about lives saved is the natural extension of this view under uncertainty. The anti-aggregationist, following Taurek, holds that the mere number of people on each side does not make one side's claim weightier; one strand of this view also holds that fairness is best respected by choosing a procedure that gives each individual the same ex ante chance of being saved—which, in the deterministic case, is what a fair coin between the two sides accomplishes, since each of the six then faces the same 1/2 chance of survival.

Work through what each position says in the probabilistic version. For the aggregator, compute the expected number of lives saved under each choice and state how the prescription depends on p: at what value of p does the recommended action switch, and what should the aggregator say at that boundary? For the anti-aggregationist, keep the negative and the positive strands separate. First, is there a version of the negative view on which numbers never settle the matter even here, so that saving A is permissible for every p? Second, take the positive fairness strand seriously: what mixed strategy equalizes each individual's ex ante survival probability as a function of p? Give the randomization weights at p = 1/2 and the general formula, and say when the procedure ends up selecting the one-person side more often than the group side, and when the reverse.

Then evaluate the disagreement. Does the usual pattern—aggregator saves the five, anti-aggregationist flips a coin—survive this variation, or does it invert at some values of p? Does the probabilistic case make either view more or less intuitively costly than it was in the deterministic original? In particular, say whether the variation tells us that the real issue is whether the numbers count, or instead something about how individual claims should be respected when the efficacy of the options differs.
