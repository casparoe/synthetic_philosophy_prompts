I want you to act as a decision theorist who has worked on collective action and the theory of voting; think through this slowly, and maybe brainstorm the candidate escape routes and what each costs before you commit.

A colleague walked me through this argument and I can't find the exit. It seems to prove that the most rational citizens are the ones who know the least and stay home, which can't be right.

(1) In a large election, my ballot changes the outcome only if it breaks or creates a tie among the other voters — i.e., only if I turn out to be pivotal.
(2) For any election big enough to matter, the probability I'm pivotal is absurdly small; in a close House race it's maybe one in tens of thousands, and in a presidential race it's smaller by orders of magnitute.
(3) So the expected instrumental payoff of voting is (pivot probability) × (how much more I'd value the better candidate winning). Even if I'd pay, say, a million dollars to see my candidate elected, the expected value comes out to a few cents.
(4) It costs more than a few cents in time and hassle to register, get to the polling place, and cast a ballot. So by my own lights I should abstain.
(5) The same arithmetic applies to political information. Whether I spend ten hours studying the candidates or none at all, my chance of being pivotal is basically unchanged, so those ten hours are pure loss. The argument therefore says the citizens who take politics most seriously are behaving even less rationally than the ones who vote without thinking.
(6) Which lands at the perverse conclusion that democracy only meets its own standards of legitimacy if enough voters are irrational in exactly this way. Run the argument on everyone and no one votes, at which point one ballot would decide everything — and premise (2) would be false. So the theory's central premise is true only as long as many people refuse to accept the theory.

I have a hunch something shifts between the single-voter calculation and the 'everyone does it' scenario in (6), but I can't state it cleanly, and it might equally be buried in (2) or (3). I'd rather keep the expected-utility framework in (1)–(4) than fix this by flatly declaring that voting is a duty, but I don't see how the framework escapes (6). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the standard rational-choice account of voting?
