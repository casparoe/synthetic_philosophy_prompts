I am a philosophy professor working in philosophy of mathematics, and I defend the following position. In the complex numbers, the choice of one square root of −1 as i is a pure convention: conjugation is a field automorphism that fixes the real numbers and swaps the two square roots, so no purely algebraic property distinguishes them. I treat this as a paradigm case of a more general thesis: mathematical singular reference is only well-defined up to isomorphism. A structure of the order type of the natural numbers has no distinguished first element or successor function except in a particular presentation, and its elements are merely positions in a structure. Where a non-identity automorphism exists, canonical labelling is impossible; the question which object is which is not a question about the mathematical object but about a chosen representation.

What would count against this view? I can already think of a few standard candidates—for example, a categorical characterization of the complex numbers that privileges one root by adding further structure, or a theory of content on which the convention that i denotes this root is made true by a familiar non-structural relation. But I want a sharper map of the position's exposure. Please provide exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Each of the first four should identify a different kind of consideration that genuinely counts against the view—an observation, formal result, possible case, argument, or consequence to which it is committed—ranked from most damaging to merely awkward, and should say whether a committed structuralist would concede it counts against the view or would dismiss it. Let the fifth item be your meta-assessment: how confident you are in the overall ranking, and the single point you are least sure of.
