I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the difficulty that the things we call pleasures — the taste of sugar, the relief when a headache finally lifts, pride in finishing a long project, the satisfaction of understanding a proof, schadenfreude, the pleasure of a musical resolution — do not seem to share any single introspectible quality. I've been collecting candidate answers to the question of what, if anything, all pleasures have in common, and so far I have four:

1. The felt-quality view: all pleasures share a distinctive hedonic tone or feeling, even if it is difficult to isolate in some cases.
2. The attitudinal view: what unifies pleasures is not a sensation but a subject's attitude of liking, wanting, or being pleased about something.
3. The functional-role view: pleasures are unified causally — they are states that play the role of a positive valuation signal, reinforcing whatever produced them and biasing the organism toward repetition.
4. The representational view: pleasures have a common intentional content — each one represents its object or situation as good, as goal-attainment, or as an improvement in one's condition.

I am reasonably confident these four capture the standard philosophical moves I have encountered. I would like you, as a professional philosopher of mind working on pleasure and well-being, to give me a fifth candidate commonality that is genuinely different from these four — not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of them, and not merely an objection to the project of finding a commonality. Before committing to one, take some time to brainstorm several possibilities and explain briefly why most of them collapse into one of the four. Then present the strongest genuinely new candidate, show in detail how it differs from each of the four, and give an honest assessment of whether it actually solves the heterogeneity problem or only relocates it. Please answer in American English.
