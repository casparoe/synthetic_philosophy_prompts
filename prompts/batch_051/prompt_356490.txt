I follow a standing policy about what I may believe about my own motives. Whenever an explanation of my behavior flatters me, I do not allow the fact that it feels introspectively obvious to count as evidence for it at all. I require external corroboration before I will endorse the explanation: a written record of my past choices that it also fits, a prediction from someone who knows me and gains nothing from agreeing with me, or a cost I paid that the less flattering story would not predict. I adopted the policy after catching myself attributing a donation to generosity when the wider pattern of my choices suggested I was mainly managing my reputation. Last month the policy made me set aside, as likely self-deception, my conviction that I had criticized a colleague's manuscript on its merits rather than out of envy. I returned to that conviction only after another colleague, who had not seen my comments and had no reason to spare my feelings, raised nearly the same objections independently. I now cannot tell whether the policy is a calibrated response to the actual structure of self-deception or a standing prejudice against introspective evidence. Is the policy defensible? Under which accounts of self-deception—a divided mind, biased evidence-gathering, or a diachronic process of controlling what one notices—would flattering introspective reports deserve that kind of blanket distrust, and under which accounts would the policy be discarding evidence that only introspection can supply? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps its protection while giving uncorroborated introspection some evidential weight when the circumstances are genuinely diagnostic?
