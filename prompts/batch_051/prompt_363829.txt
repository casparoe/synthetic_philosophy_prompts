Criminal trial procedure in many jurisdictions has a familiar but striking asymmetry. A video recording of the incident is routinely treated as the decisive check on conflicting testimony: when witnesses disagree about what happened, the footage is used to settle the dispute, and a witness can authenticate the recording simply by testifying that it accurately depicts what they saw. The same system insists on an exact chain of custody before a bag of drugs, a blood sample, or a fired bullet can be admitted — not because anyone thinks the bullet is lying, but because its route from seizure to courtroom is part of what makes it evidence. For a digital video file, which can be edited, recontextualized, or now synthesized outright, no comparable demand of technical provenance is imposed. What theory of recorded evidence does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that a camera is a transparent channel from world to courtroom while human witnesses are fallible interpreters, or that physical custody matters to reliability in a way that a file's causal history does not? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology that makes the recording-as-tiebreaker practice intelligible, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and explain what authentication and jury-instruction practice would have to change on a view that treats every recording as a constructed, fallible representation whose credibility depends on demonstrated provenance. The practice, after all, subjects its most decisive evidence to its least demanding test; say whether that tension is real and what it shows about which view of testimony and recordings the courts are quietly committed to.
