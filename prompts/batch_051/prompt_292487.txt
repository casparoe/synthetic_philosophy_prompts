I fell down a rabbit hole with my cousin about Hilbert's hotel the other night, and now I can't get it out of my head. The setup is a hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on forever, and it's completely full. To make room for one more guest, you just tell each person to move one room up, and now room 1 is open. What I find attractive about this is the rule it runs on: two collections are the same size if you can pair each member of one with exactly one member of the other. By that rule, the even-numbered rooms are the same size as all the rooms, and nobody has to count anything. It's clean, and it gives a definite answer in every case.

But my cousin has gone a bit finitist on me. He says the whole thing cheats, because you never actually finish moving an infinite line of people. There is no last guest, so the shift is never completed, and so there was never a moment where the hotel was full and then a room became open. He says 'full' means every room has a body in it, and no amount of announcing a reassignment changes that unless the moves actually happen. And I have to admit, when he puts it that way, I have a stuborn feeling that a part should be smaller than the whole, and that a process with no last step can't be treated as something that's already done.

So I'm stuck between a rule I find elegent and a gut feeling I can't shake. Should I drop the gut feeling and just say it's my finite brain tripping over something it wasn't built to picture? Or should I give up on actual infinities being anything more than a way of speaking? Or is there a reading where the rule and the intuition are both fine — like the math is allowed to pretend the move is finished, but the hotel story doesn't mean anything in the real world? I want to know what each option costs and what would actually justify taking it, not just a speech about how Cantor was right.
