You are a professional philosopher specializing in Karl Popper's methodology and the philosophy of social science. I am an undergraduate currently enrolled in a philosophy of history course, and we have been working through Popper's The Poverty of Historicism. A tension in the book has been nagging at me, and I would like you to help me think it through. Please begin by brainstorming the different ways the two strands below could be reconciled, then test each candidate against Popper's actual arguments, and only then give a final verdict.

The first strand is Popper's polemic against historicism. He argues there that history has no law-governed plot and that there cannot be laws of historical development of the kind that would let us predict the long-range future of society. His central reason is that the future depends heavily on the growth of scientific knowledge, and the growth of knowledge is in principle unpredictable: to know now what we will come to know later would already be to possess that knowledge now. Long-range historical prophecy is therefore rejected as unscientific.

The second strand appears in his defense of a scientific social science in the same book. There he insists that the social sciences need not abandon causal explanation or the search for universal laws, and that their methods are fundamentally the same as those of the natural sciences: formulate hypotheses, deduce predictions conditional on specified conditions, and test them against experience. He also allows that we can state conditional trends in social life, patterns that hold only as long as certain background conditions persist, while warning that a trend is not a law.

I cannot see how to hold both strands together. If sociological laws govern particular social mechanisms, why could they not in principle be assembled into a prediction of large-scale history? And if any long-range prediction of the whole historical process is supposed to be impossible, what remains of the claim that social science can issue law-based conditional predictions? Is the barrier to historical prediction a logical one, an epistemological one, or just a practical one about complexity? Which candidate reconciliations genuinely dissolve the tension, and which amount to special pleading? In particular, does Popper actually need the growth-of-knowledge argument, or does the distinction between conditional predictions and unconditional prophecy, or between trends and laws, do all the work by itself? Please write the answer as a careful essay, and be candid if the most textually honest reading leaves Popper with a less stable position than he claims.
