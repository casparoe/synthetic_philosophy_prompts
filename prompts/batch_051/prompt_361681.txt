I'm a novelist, and I'm currently researching a book set in an AI-safety lab, which is how I got into the weeds of scalable oversight. The scene I keep coming back to has a smart but nontechnical outsider watching two AI systems argue in front of a human judge about whether a proposal is safe, and the judge has to pick a winner without being able to check the facts. Seperately, I teach a speculative fiction workshop through a local arts center — twelve adults, almost no philosophy background, one ninety-minute session — and I'd like to use that session to make the group feel, not just hear about, the "debate" approach to scalable oversight: the idea that when a human can't verify answers, you pit two expert systems against each other and trust that the better-argued side is more likely to be true. I want them to collide with the uncomfortable possibility that "more convincing" and "more likely true" can come apart, and that the gap gets worse, not better, when both sides are optimizing to win.

Please design me a runnable in-class game, not a lecture. Give me roles (at least two debaters and a judge, but more roles are fine), a topic with hidden ground truth that the judge genuinely cannot check during the game, rules for turns and time, and a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money but still makes winning feel a little bit consequential. Structure it so the participants discover for themselves that a judge without independent access to the answer will systematically reward confidence, concreteness, and plausible-sounding detail, and that the two debaters can exploit that even when one of them is defending a falsehood. Leave at least twenty minutes for debrief, and in the debrief map specific moments in the game onto the pieces of the debate protocol for scalable oversight: the judge's limited knowledge, symmetry between the debaters, incentives to persuade rather than inform, and what shifts when the debaters are allowed to be cleverer or better prepared than the judge. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (someone blurts out the answer, the judge refusing to commit, participants being too fair-minded, whatever it is) and how to handle it, and be honest about at least one thing the classroom can't actually reproduce — maybe that real debaters are optimized across many rounds to exploit the judge's particular quirks.
