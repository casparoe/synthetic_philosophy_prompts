I've been trying to work out whether several things I believe about the emotions and the good life actually fit together, and I'd like an independent audit of the set before I build anything further on it. On one side, I'm drawn to the ideal of freedom from emotional disturbance — the kind of thing the Stoics called apatheia and others called ataraxia: a state in which one's inner condition is not knocked around by fortune. On the other side, I find myself unable to let go of the idea that human life is at its best when it is built around particular attachments, and that some of the emotions we usually call negative are not errors. Here are the commitments as I would write them:

1. A good human life centrally involves deep attachments to particular people and to long-term projects that matter to one.
2. Freedom from emotional disturbance is a major constituent of that good: the person who meets loss or danger without being shaken is, in an important respect, better off than the person who suffers through it — not merely colder or out of touch.
3. Some negative emotions are fitting responses to the world, not merely useful or adaptive: grief at the death of someone one loves, fear in the face of genuine danger, anger at serious injustice. A person who never felt them would be missing something, not just wired differently.
4. Most of our misery comes from investing our happiness in things outside our control — reputation, health, other people's good opinion, even the continued existence of people we love — and we would be less fragile if we divested from those externals.
5. This divestment need not mean loving less. Love is better understood as a stable pattern of attention, well-wishing, and commitment than as need or dependence, so it is possible to love a person fully while one's peace is not hostage to how that person fares.
6. The right ideal is two-stage: not that a person never feels the initial stab of sadness or fear, but that they never assent to it or let it organize their state of mind into disturbance.

I honestly cannot tell whether this is one coherent picture or a set with real cracks in it, and I cannot see where the pressure would fall first. Please audit the set. Identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to be given up and at what cost. I'd like the diagnosis rather than a defense of any one commitment.
