I am writing a speculative novel set a generation from now, in a country where preimplantation genetic screening of IVF embryos is routine and where a statutory body, the Commission on Reproductive Technology, must license each permitted use of embryo selection. Much of the novel turns on my getting the Commission's next ruling to follow from its own record rather than from narrative convenience, so I want to think through what its precedents commit it to. The five decisions I have fixed are these. It licensed selection against a uniformly fatal enzyme disorder that kills in early childhood, and instructed clinics to choose an unaffected embryo when one was available. It licensed selection against a genetic variant resulting in moderate, easily corrected nearsightedness. It refused a Deaf couple's request to select an embryo carrying a hearing-loss variant so that the child would share their condition. It refused a request to select the embryo with the higher polygenic prediction of educational attainment, finding the evidence too weak at the time. And it refused sex selection for family balancing. In the novel's background is a recurring argument about whether the Commission is holding a disease-prevention line, whether it is drifting toward a liberal-eugenic policy that would license parental choices of advantage, or whether it is in practice running an evidentially chastened version of the principle the philosophical literature calls procreative beneficence: the idea that, where different possible children could be selected, one should select the child with the best prospective life.

The ruling I now need concerns a couple with several embryos, all healthy on every standard disease panel. A newly validated polygenic index of general cognitive ability has become available, and the Commission's own scientific advisory committee has now certified the index as a reliable predictor of adult functioning across many domains. The couple asks for a license to select the embryo that scores highest on this index. I would like you to predict, or if you prefer to recommend, how the Commission should rule if it is being consistent with its five earlier decisions. I am less interested in the bare outcome than in the reasoning: state the distinct rules that make sense of all five precedents, say how each applies to this new request, identify which precedent is the closest analog, give your best guess about the Commission with reasons for preferring one rule over its rivals, and then propose one further application, a sixth case, that would cleanly separate the rules you have identified. Please also say plainly what principle the Commission would be adopting either way.
