I've been doing shotokan karate for a few years — mostly kata, some kumite — and a friend recently dragged me to one of those rationality training workshops. The whole time I kept having this deja vu. The way they'd have us do a calibration drill (state 90% confidence intervals for ten trivia questions, then score ourselves) felt exactly like drilling a kata: a stylized, isolated move that never appears in that form in a real fight, but is supposed to build some underlying capacity. And the pre-mortem exercise — imagine your project already failed and write the story of what went wrong — felt like practicing a throw in slow motion before using it live. Both traditions seem to share a model: decompose the skill into moves, drill the moves out of context, and trust the transfer. Both also have this split between formal drilling and live resistance — karate kata versus sparring, and on the rationality side, solo exercises versus adversarial debate or live forecasting where you make a public prediction and get scored.

The parallel I can't decide about is whether rationality can be trained the way martial arts are — where 'kata' stands for a named, rehearsed exercise a student performs alone (calibration quizzes, pre-mortems, maybe 'steelman the other side' as a formal drill), and 'sparring' stands for exercises with live resistence (debate, forecasting against a real outcome). In karate there's a long-running debate over whether katas actually transfer to fighting or are mostly ceremonial — the 'aliveness' crowd says only live drilling transfers. If the parallel is deep, we could borrow that whole debate and predict the same about rationality training: that solo drills mostly don't transfer, and only predict-checked, adversarial practice does. But the obvious objection is that fighting is perceptual-motor and automatic, while the thing rationality training is trying to install is usually a pause — interrupting automatic reasoning — which might be the opposite kind of skill, so the training theory might not carry over at all.
