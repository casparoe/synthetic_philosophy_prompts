Risk-weighted expected utility (REU) is usually motivated by the Allais paradox: it is meant to show that a rational agent can care extra about the worst outcomes without us having to build that into the curvature of her utility function over money. The theory keeps outcomes on one utility scale but replaces the linear weighting of probabilities with an increasing risk function r, where a risk-averse agent has r(p) below p (and a risk-seeking one above). The original argument for introducing r is built on cases where the probabilities are crisp and given: 10% chance of this, 1% chance of that.

Now suppose the decision problem is genuinely ambiguous in Ellsberg's sense: the agent knows only that the probability of success is somewhere between 0.3 and 0.7 and cannot rationally sharpen that to a single number (she can think of it as a set of priors). In such cases people often display ambiguity aversion on top of any risk attitude.

Does the argument for introducing r survive once probabilities go imprecise? In particular, say whether r is forced to do double duty—representing attitudes to spread-out probabilities within a gamble and attitudes to missing information across the set of probabilities—and whether that is incoherent or just untidy. Then tell me whether REU survives, needs a repair, or is dead in this setting, with the weakest repair you can defend if you think it needs one.
