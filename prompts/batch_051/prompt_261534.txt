I want to know whether this should impress me as an engineer or whether it is a loose analogy dressed in technical language, and I'd like to test it through a translation exercise.

Restate HCH and factored cognition in the vocabulary of distributed systems as faithfully as you can. Treat the HCH process as a computational DAG: the root is the original question; each node reads a query plus whatever context was passed to it, produces a result, and may spawn child subtasks whose outputs it then aggregates; the root returns the final answer. Use the terms I actually use — decomposition, task graph, idempotency, partial failure, fan-out, consistency, consensus, latency, aggregation bugs, lossy context passing, and whatever else applies — and tell me which parts of the HCH picture map cleanly onto which engineering concepts.

Crucially, I want a residue report: once the translation is done, what did not survive? Are there features of good human deliberation that have no counterpart in this vocabulary, and is that because the distributed-systems lens is missing something real, or because the HCH idealization itself smuggled in the unargued assumption that deliberation is decomposable the way a compute job is? I suspect the interesting failure is not that subtasks crash or take too long, but that the root can receive correct, faithful subanswers and still assemble a wrong overall answer for reasons invisible at any single node — a consensus problem, not a latency problem. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, what in the translation supports or undermines it, and what the closest engineering analogue of each kind of failure would be.
