I have recently become the person who takes my mother to her appointments, and for the first time I will have to help carry out a decision of the kind my sister handles throughout her professional life. My sister is a geriatrician with unusually settled instincts about when it is right to override, steer, or leave alone an older person's choices for that person's own good. Her verdicts in the cases below have always seemed to follow some consistent line, though I have never been able to state the rule behind it.

1. A son asked whether he could crush his father's dementia medication into his food because the father, who had moderate Alzheimer's, refused it. My sister said he should not deceive, but should seek guardianship and then could give the medication over his father's objection, since his father could no longer hold onto the reason for taking it.

2. A mentally sharp 71-year-old woman refused chemotherapy for a cancer that had a meaningful but far from certain chance of being cured, because she found the side effects unendurable. After making sure the woman understood the probabilities, my sister said the refusal should be respected.

3. A mentally sharp 68-year-old man refused a routine screening colonoscopy, saying he would rather not know. My sister confirmed that he understood that screening sometimes finds treatable cancer, then respected the informed refusal.

4. A 65-year-old with diabetes was exhausted by daily insulin injections and kept refusing them. My sister found an alternative that could be given once a week, and once he accepted it she said that forcing the daily needles on him would have been wrong, even though his blood sugar was persistently high and the situation was serious.

5. An 80-year-old woman with mild dementia had been found wandering across town at night in winter. She refused both a GPS watch and a door alarm. My sister told the family to install the door alarm anyway and let her think it was a building repair; she disliked the lie but thought an unalarmed door was worse.

6. A mentally sharp 77-year-old man with elevated cholesterol but no history of heart attack refused a statin after reading about side effects. My sister documented the informed refusal and said no one should keep pressing him.

7. A care home rearranged its dining room so that fruit and water were the visible, easy options and sugary desserts required a request. My sister supported this, on the condition that anyone who asked for dessert got it without further comment.

8. A woman whose advance directive refused artificial nutrition if she became permanently unconscious had a severe stroke, and her family wanted a feeding tube. My sister said the directive should govern, even though some of the family believed she might have changed her mind.

The new case is my mother. She is 79 and cognitively intact. She has just been diagnosed with atrial fibrillation, and her doctors recommend a once-a-day oral blood thinner that would substantially reduce her risk of a major stroke; the main drawback is a modest bleeding risk. She refuses. She says she has always disliked pills, that a stroke would be awful but she is willing to take that chance, and that taking a pill every day would make her feel like a patient. She is not depressed, and her whole adult life is a pattern of avoiding medication even when she clearly wanted to get better. She has no advance directive.

I would like you to predict what my sister would say we should do about the blood thinner: respect the refusal, override, or something in between. More importantly, I want to know what rule or rules she seems to be running. The candidates include something about decisional capacity, something about the size, certainty, or reversibility of the harm, something about whether the refusal reflects the person's own settled values, and perhaps some position about how much steering is too much. I suspect more than one rule fits the record equally well. Tell me which rules fit her record, what each would recommend in my mother's case, and which is your best guess about her actual view. Finally, give me the further case, not already on the list, that would do the most to reveal which rule she is really using.
