I am a journalist who covers the mind sciences, and I have spent the past several months trying to determine what I actually believe about human concepts—the mental structures behind categories like BIRD, DOG, CHAIR, and BACHELOR. Rather than begin from the standard theories, I wrote down my verdicts on a series of concrete cases, and I want to know what position, if any, they add up to. Here are the cases, with the verdicts I find myself holding.

1. A robin strikes me as a better, more central instance of BIRD than a penguin; a penguin is a better instance than a kiwi; and a kiwi, while an odd bird, is still plainly a bird. A bat, which flies and in several obvious ways resembles a bird more than a kiwi does, is not simply an atypical bird; it is not a bird at all.

2. A newly hatched robin, naked, blind, and unable to fly, is still a bird to me. A porcelain figurine of a robin, painted well enough to pass for a living bird at arm's length, is not. Here I seem to treat look and behavior as neither necessary nor sufficient.

3. The same pull appears in a pair I keep comparing. A dog born with a mutation that leaves it three-legged and tailless, raised among cats and never having barked, is still a dog. A robot dog that barks, wags its tail, and is treated by every visitor as the family pet is not a dog. I cannot explain both verdicts by similarity of appearance or behavior.

4. My partner, who grew up with Siberian huskies, finds a greyhound an atypical dog. I grew up with greyhounds and find it entirely typical. Both of us agree that it is a dog; our disagreement seems to be about something other than membership, as if our personal histories set the standard for typicality but not for category boundaries.

5. When I read about a newly discovered bird species with a curved wading beak, webbed feet, and a tail fanned into strange plumes, nothing in my memory combines any of those features, yet I have no difficulty calling it a bird and even ranking how bird-like it is. My concept seems to be doing work that a storehouse of remembered examples alone cannot explain.

6. A tree stump at a picnic ground that families regularly sit on seems to me to have become a chair. A sculpture shaped exactly like a chair but bolted to the floor and impossible to sit on is not a chair. Function, or intended use, appears to be doing the categorizing here.

7. I would not call a Catholic priest a bachelor, though he is an unmarried adult male in the literal sense. I would call a recently divorced man of the same age a bachelor again, though he is also an unmarried adult male. The dictionary definition fails to capture either verdict.

These verdicts pull me in at least three directions: toward something like prototype or family-resemblance similarity, toward remembered particular instances, and toward a rough background theory of what makes a thing the kind of thing it is. My question is whether there is one account of concepts that yields all seven verdicts together with the boundaries between them, or whether some of my verdicts must be overturned or redescribed.
