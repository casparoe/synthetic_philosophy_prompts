A colleague and I have been working through the Löbian obstacle to self-trust, and we have assembled an argument whose conclusion neither of us accepts, but we cannot find the step that deserves to go. Here it is as we now have it.

(1) Let R be an agent whose reasoning is fully represented by a consistent formal system T extending Peano arithmetic, equipped with a provability predicate that represents “T derives φ” inside T.
(2) R counts as trusting one of its own conclusions φ only if R is entitled, for that φ, to move from “T derives φ” to “φ” — that is, to treat the fact that its own machinery produced φ as a reason for φ.
(3) If that entitlement is itself part of R's reasoning, then T must be able to prove, for each derived φ, the reflection conditional “if T derives φ, then φ.”
(4) By Löb's theorem, T proves “if T derives φ, then φ” only in the cases where T already proves φ outright; and the global soundness claim “everything T derives is true” cannot be proved in T at all unless T is inconsistent.
(5) So R cannot, from inside its own system, justify the bridge from its derivations to truth; any justification of T's soundness would have to be supplied from outside T.
(6) But a justification supplied from outside T cannot constitute self-trust: if R has no internal entitlement to that bridge, then R's readiness to assert its own conclusions is either an unjustified leap or a bare behavioral disposition, not rational trust.
(7) Therefore R, a consistent formal reasoner of this kind, cannot rationally trust any of its own conclusions.
(8) The argument generalizes: any sufficiently powerful consistent formal reasoner falls under (7), and on the standard idealization a human mathematician who has carefully checked a proof is just such a reasoner.
(9) Yet after checking a short proof, a mathematician is rationally entitled to believe the theorem; that is as clear a case of rational trust in one's own conclusions as there can be.
(10) So (7) and (9) cannot both be true, and something above must fail.

If I had to choose, I would keep (9) at almost any cost and would sooner abandon one of (1)–(6) than accept the skeptical conclusion. But I do not yet see which rejection is least costly, or what remains of the notion of self-trust if the reflection requirement in (2) is weakened, or whether the argument has gone wrong in a way I cannot name. Which premise or step do you reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
