I build consent flows for a living — cookie banners, app permissions, OAuth scopes, data-processing agreements, all the GDPR-shaped stuff. So I think in terms like authentication vs authorization, scopes, revocation, purpose limitation, clickwrap, dark patterns, and a threat model where the user is basically an untrusted principal operating on a resource that belongs to them. I have no philosophy background, but I keep hearing there's a whole ethics litterature on what makes consent valid, and that the mainstream account turns on three conditions: the person has to be competent, has to have the relevant information, and has to not be coerced or deceived. Restate that account in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. Some of it maps, obviously: deception is kind of like a consent dialog that withholds or buries the real purpose, coercion is kind of like consent extracted because the only alternative is loosing access to something you need, competence is kind of like an age gate or a user who plainly cannot understand the dialog. But I suspect the mapping stops being neat very fast — for instance, a permission screen that technically discloses everything but is designed to funnel you into hitting accept all feels like it's more than one of those three at once. Give me the systematic translation, and then the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which ones you had to fudge to get across, and which have no expression at all in the way I think. Then tell me whether the residue is because my vocabulary is missing something that matters, or because the philosophers' claims were vaguer than they look. I'm fine if the conclusion is that my mental model is good for compliance but not for ethics.
