I teach a second-year philosophy of language unit on empty names and truth in fiction, and I would like to set Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead beside the standard philosophical apparatus. Please write a critical reading of the play through that lens, about the size of a substantial seminar paper, for a reader comfortable with philosophy of language. You needn't cite page numbers or secondary literature; engage the text and the philosophical positions directly.

Take both directions seriously. From the philosophy side, work with the standard positions: the Millian worry that a name without a referent contributes nothing to what is said; the Fregean idea of sense without reference; Russell's treatment of fictional names as disguised descriptions whose existential commitments fail; and the widely used distinction between what is true in a fiction and what is true outright, with pretense accounts and possible-worlds accounts as competing explanations of the former. From the play's side, work with the actual texture of the text: the run of coin tosses that keeps coming up heads, the two protagonists' missing pasts and interchangeable names, the Player and the Tragedians, the scenes lifted from or staged in the wings of Hamlet, and the closing report from the English ambassador that supplies the title. Do not simply say the play is 'about' fictionality; show where particular scenes genuinely sharpen or complicate a philosophical point, and where the parallel strains or distorts the philosophy.

I want your view on three connected questions. First, what does the device of borrowing two named characters from an existing fiction and filling in their offstage lives show about how truth in fiction is generated — in particular, whether propositions about those offstage episodes should be understood as true in the source fiction, true in the derivative fiction, true in both, or true in neither? Second, what does the play's handling of the two names — their interchangeability, the characters' own uncertainty about their pasts and identities — dramatise about empty names, and which philosophical account of empty names does the play most naturally support, if any? Third, what does the play add to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot, and where does it oversimplify or mislead?

Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the play to a philosophical allegory, and do not bend the philosophy to fit the play. Where the philosophical material is contested, separate what is settled from what remains genuinely live among people who work on these topics, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. For instance, it is common ground that Shakespeare's uses of 'Rosencrantz' and 'Guildenstern' do not pick out real, flesh-and-blood people in the way that 'Copenhagen' picks out a real city; it is genuinely contested whether the names nevertheless refer to abstract artefacts, and whether truth in fiction is best analysed through pretense, possible worlds, or some other machinery.

After the reading, give a brief structured summary as a single JSON object with these fields:

- "thesis": a one-sentence statement of the reading's central claim;
- "key_evidence": an array of objects, each with "scene" (a short identifier of a scene or device) and "philosophical_work" (a sentence on the philosophical point it sharpens or complicates);
- "points_of_resistance": an array of strings identifying places where the play resists or muddies the philosophical reading;
- "settled_points": an array of strings stating points that are common ground among specialists;
- "contested_points": an array of strings stating live disputes and how the reading relies on one side of them;
- "verdict_for_teaching": a short string saying how, given all this, you would use the play in a philosophy of language unit.
