I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I work on software that manages randomisation for clinical trials, and part of my role is deciding which trial designs need to be flagged for human ethics review. Over the years I have accumulated a set of gut reactions to particular designs, but I do not know whether they hang together behind a single principle.

Here are my cases and my verdicts.

1. A double-blind trial randomises patients between two standard antidepressants. The published evidence genuinely splits the field, and competent psychiatrists disagree about which is the better first-line treatment. I would let this run without hesitation.

2. A cancer trial randomises patients between a new targeted drug and best supportive care, after a single-arm phase II study produced striking responses but no randomised evidence. I would let it run, though it makes me uneasy that some participants receive no active drug.

3. A trial randomises patients with knee pain to arthroscopy or sham surgery, because the placebo response to surgery is known to be large and many surgeons insist they already know the real procedure works. I would let it run; without it, I do not see how the question could ever be settled.

4. A non-inferiority trial tests a much cheaper new drug against a standard drug, with a wide non-inferiority margin. Everyone agrees the new drug might be slightly worse. I am genuinely unsure whether this is acceptable, and the question of how much worse is acceptable does not feel as though it has a principled answer.

5. A trial compares a new drug against an existing drug that is itself known to be inferior to a third drug that is already available. The sponsor chose the weaker comparator to avoid a harder comparison. If the decision were mine, I would block this.

6. A platform trial uses response-adaptive randomisation: before the trial concludes, the algorithm starts allocating new patients preferentially to the arm that is doing better. I would allow this, though I cannot fully explain why it differs from case 5, where information that one arm is worse is also used to shape who gets what.

7. During an outbreak, a vaccine trial randomises participants to vaccine or placebo after phase II data show a strong antibody response but no efficacy data. I would allow it, but as soon as an interim analysis crosses the pre-specified efficacy boundary, I would expect the placebo arm to be terminated and placebo participants offered the vaccine.

8. A trial randomises patients between an approved low dose of a drug and an experimental high dose. Open-label extension data strongly favour the high dose, though there is no randomised comparison. I would allow randomisation to continue only for a short, pre-specified window; beyond that it starts to feel wrong.

The only candidate principle I have ever been handed is that a trial is acceptable only while there is genuine uncertainty among the relevant experts about which arm is better. I cannot tell whether that is a real principle or just a slogan.

So my questions are these. Is there a precise principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? If the principle is something like that slogan, state it with enough precision to be applied, say which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to the case I am currently unsure about: an adaptive platform trial has been shifting allocation toward arm X on early outcome data, and before the scheduled interim analysis a large non-randomised registry study appears that strongly suggests X is better. Should randomisation between the arms continue, should allocation shift further, or should the comparison stop? What does your principle say, and is that the answer you would defend?
