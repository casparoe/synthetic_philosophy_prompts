I am a software engineer and I run a small reading group at my company. We are twelve adults — mostly engineers and product managers, none of us trained in philosophy — and we meet for ninety minutes every few weeks. We have chosen our next topic: whether the fact that someone is your compatriot gives you a stronger claim on your help than a stranger abroad has, when both need it. The question grew out of an argument in the group about donating to a local food bank versus a charity preventing malaria overseas, and we are fairly evenly split. Two members are dual nationals and one is recently naturalised, so the activity must not assume that everyone has a single tidy nationality, and the session should stay civil without blunting the disagreement.

I would like to open the session not with a lecture but with a structured activity or game that makes everyone feel the force of the question before we name and discuss it. Could you design one for us? We have ninety minutes, twelve participants, a room with a whiteboard and table space, and we can print cards or simple props in advance. I want the rules, roles, or incentives arranged so that participants run into the problem themselves: the genuine pull of partiality towards people who share their country or community, and the equally genuine pull of impartiality, according to which need — rather than shared nationality — should decide who receives help. By the end I want the group to have lived through both pulls and to have a sharper sense of where the disagreement actually lies, rather than a resolution.

Please give me a runnable activity — roles or rules, a timeline, any props — and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical disagreement rather than turning into a mini-lecture. Please also tell me what predictably goes wrong when such an activity is run and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator will have to supply in discussion.
