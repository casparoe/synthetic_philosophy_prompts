I am a doctoral student in political theory, and I have become more interested in a piece of disciplinary machinery than in my own current results. Across comparative surveys, respondents are routinely asked to place themselves on a 0–10 left–right scale; expert surveys assign each party a single left–right score; journalists describe a party as having moved to the right between elections; and in many chambers party groups are still seated along a left-to-right continuum, so that a spatial metaphor which began with seating arrangements in revolutionary France now functions as a kind of tacit ontology of political disagreement.

I should like to understand what this practice presupposes. When the discipline uses a single left–right scale as though it tracked a real axis of conflict, is it committing itself to a substantive thesis about what holds each side together — for instance, that the underlying dimension is equality versus hierarchy, or openness to change versus defence of tradition — or merely to something weaker, such as the claim that citizens and parties can be ordered relative to one another by whatever historically inherited bundle of positions currently travels with each label? The puzzle is sharpened by the fact that comparative researchers know the same label bundles rather different commitments in different countries, yet the scale is still treated as cross-nationally meaningful. Please reconstruct the implicit theory of ideology carried by the practice, distinguishing what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. I would also like to know what would have to change in survey design, party classification, or legislative procedure if one concluded that left and right are conventional alignments rather than a single real axis.
