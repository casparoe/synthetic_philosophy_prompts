Scientists and science-policy advocates often defend public funding for cosmology — a field that rarely promises any near-term practical payoff — by saying that the work is worth funding because knowledge is valuable for its own sake. I have heard the phrase used in funding meetings and in advocacy writing, and I cannot tell whether the people using it mean something modest (the public finds this research interesting, and serving public interest has some value), something strong (knowledge has a kind of value that does not depend on any use, pleasure, or effect at all), or something else entirely (it is the activity of inquiry, not the stored results, that really matters).

What I would like is an account of the distinct precise claims that "knowledge for its own sake" could be making, laid out as a menu from the weakest and least controversial to the strongest. For each reading, I want to know whether it is plausible, whether it is genuinely distinct from the others or collapses into them, and — most important for me — whether that reading, on its own, would justify spending public money on cosmology as against applied research, health, education, or other public goods. I would also like to know which readings the slogan typically needs to mean in a funding argument, and whether on those readings it does any real argumentative work or simply restates the conclusion that the research is worth funding. I care less about the dictionary meaning than about what a proponent has to be asserting for the phrase to carry the weight it is made to carry.
