In a metaphysics of causation seminar I keep returning to a puzzle, because an argument I can reconstruct from our discussions appears to lead from a claim I find hard to deny to a conclusion I find hard to live with, and I cannot tell which step is doing the damage. The starting case is familiar: a child drowns in a public pool while the lifeguard, seated in the chair, does not jump in. Most of us, myself included, want to say the lifeguard's failure to jump in was a cause of the death. But the following chain of reasoning seems to take that comfortable thought somewhere inhospitable.

(1) If the lifeguard's not jumping in caused the drowning, then by parity the not-jumping-in of any competent adult standing at the pool's edge caused it as well. Being the lifeguard matters for whether the omission was culpable or negligent, but it seems irrelevant to whether the omission was a cause; a person's job title does not enter into the causal facts.
(2) If the bystander's not jumping in counts as a cause, then so does the not-being-there of a competent swimmer who decided at the last moment to work late rather than visit the pool and who would have jumped in had they come. The bystander and the swimmer differ only in which missing event failed to occur and where each was, and it is hard to see why causal standing should hinge on such fine-grained differences.
(3) If that is right, then for any given harm, everyone whose doing something or being somewhere would have prevented it is among its causes. Since nearly every harm has an enormous cloud of such counterfactual saviors — people who might have been present, might have acted, might have arranged things differently — each of us ends up among the causes of a vast share of the bad things that happen, at one or two removes.
(4) But a notion of causation that indiscriminate cannot do the work we ask causation to do in explanation, law, and practical deliberation. It would no longer distinguish the lifeguard's relation to the drowning from that of the swimmer who worked late, and it would make the selection of causes from the indefinitely many absences that preceded an outcome a matter of arbitrary emphasis.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? The two claims I would most like to keep are that the lifeguard's omission is a genuine cause of the death, and that there is nevertheless a principled difference between the lifeguard's causal relation to the death and that of a distant person who merely happens not to have been in a position to stop it. I am willing to pay for that combination, but I do not know which step to surrender or what the surrender costs. I would be grateful for a diagnosis that names the weakest step or transition, explains why it initially looked plausible, and prices the main alternative ways of resisting the argument.
