I recently listened to an argument between two friends, an economist and a philosopher, about Daniel Bernoulli’s solution to the St. Petersburg paradox. They agreed on the setup: a fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads, and if heads first occurs on the nth flip, the player receives 2^n ducats; the expected monetary value is infinite. (1) The economist began by saying that Bernoulli’s 1738 paper Specimen Theoriae Novae de Mensura Sortis, translated as Exposition of a New Theory on the Measurement of Risk, settled the matter. He paraphrased a key passage from the paper as follows: the value of an item must not be estimated from its price but from the utility it yields, and in particular a given increase in wealth means less to a person already rich than to a poor person; Bernoulli proposes that the utility of a small gain is inversely proportional to the wealth one already possesses. The economist said this gives a logarithmic utility function, under which the expected utility of the St. Petersburg game is small and finite, so the paradox dissolves. (2) The philosopher replied that this changes the subject: the original question was what a rational person should be willing to pay for the game, and Bernoulli instead tells us how much a person with a particular concave utility function would pay. He added that if the payoffs were stated in utils rather than ducats, or if utility were allowed to be unbounded, the expectation would again be infinite; Bernoulli has only shown that the paradox disappears if one stipulates a special utility function, not that rationality requires it. (3) The economist called this a shallow objection. Bernoulli’s real point, he said, is not about logarithms but about the principle that the value of a risky prospect is the expectation of its utility, not the expectation of its money; that principle is independently motivated by the observation that an extra ducat means less to a rich person than to a poor one. (4) The philosopher accepted the motivational point but denied that it settled the puzzle. Concave utility explains why people refuse to pay enormous sums for the game, but it does not vindicate those refusals as rational unless one already accepts expected utility with that function as the correct normative criterion, which is exactly what was at issue. He concluded that Bernoulli had reparameterized the paradox rather than resolved it. Neither would budge.

I care less about your final side than about the specific reasoning at each step.
