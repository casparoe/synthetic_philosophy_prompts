I'm a behavioural economist, and I keep seeing philosophers argue about whether a "moral saint" — someone as morally good as it is possible to be — is an ideal we should actually want. My rough sense of one side is that a morally perfect person would have to crowd out the non-moral things that make a life appealing (a sense of humour, being good at something pointless but lovely, deep attachments that aren't chosen for their moral payoff), so maximal moral goodness looks like a poor life choice. The other side says that if moral reasons are as weighty as we claim, then deliberately aiming below that maximum is just not taking morality seriously. Some of them also distinguish a saint who is genuinely delighted by other people's welfare from a saint who has to drag themselves into doing the right thing, and the two seem to be treated differently in the argument.

I want you to restate each of these positions in the vocabulary I actually use — utility functions, preferences over bundles, constraints, opportunity cost, corner solutions, multiple objectives, satisficing versus maximising, intertemporal choice, revealed preference — as faithfully as you can. Then give me a residue report: what makes the crossing intact, what you had to distort or fudge to get it there, and what has no counterpart in my toolkit at all. Is my toolkit missing something important, or is the philosophers disagreement partly kept alive by not having formalised what they mean by "attractive", "taking morality seriously", and the appeal of judging a whole life from a detached standpoint? To put pressure on it: when someone says a moral saint would be boring or humourless, tell me whether that lands as a preference parameter, a prediction about how they'd spend a scarce budget, or a claim my model simply cannot state. I'm fine with equations where they genuinely help, but I don't want you to smuggle in the economist's assumptions and then declare victory.
