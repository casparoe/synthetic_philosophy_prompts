At my reading group on just war theory, Hana and Daniel got into a long argument about whether a soldier fighting on the unjust side can nevertheless have a genuine moral right to kill opposing soldiers, or whether that right belongs only to those on the just side. Hana said the right must be symmetric: ordinary soldiers are rarely in a position to judge their state's cause, so treating them as murderers when they fight cleanly would be unjust, and the moral equality of combatants is what makes any stable law of war possible. Daniel said that mistakes a legal convention for a real permission. If the war is unjust, the soldiers on that side are engaged in wrongful killing, and wrongful attackers do not acquire a right to kill those who resist them — a bank robber doesn't get to shoot a police officer just because he sincerely believes he is in the right. Hana replied that a soldier is not like a bank robber: the soldier's ignorance is usually reasonable and often not his fault, and in war each side confronts the other simply as an armed threat, so the relevant liability is threat-based, not wrongdoing-based. Daniel answered that this begs the question, because a threat you are not entitled to pose is not one you may carry out with force; calling both sides "threats" only works after you have already ignored who is entitled to be using force at all. Hana came back that Daniel's view, however theoretically neat, leaves no workable laws of war, since every belligerent believes its own cause is just and would claim the superior permission.
