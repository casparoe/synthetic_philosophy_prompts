I keep coming back to Keith Frankish's Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness. There's a stretch where he argues that phenomenal properties are best understood as intentional objects of introspective representation: we represent our own cognitive processes as having this raw, qualitative, for-me character, but nothing in the system actually instantiates that character. The representation is doing real work—it's what makes us report experience the way we do, and it probably has adaptive or functional payoffs—but the thing represented doesn't exist. I find the theory almost irresistibly tidy. It dissolves the hard problem instead of solving it, keeps everything physical, and explains why we're so convinced we have qualia without needing qualia. But then I stub my toe and the whole thing feels like a joke. The hurt is right there, and it doesn't feel like a representation of a hurt; it feels like the hurt itself. I know Frankish would say that reaction is exactly what the illusion predicts, but that feels like a theory eating its own counterexamples. I want a negotiated verdict. If illusionism is true, what would it take for the hurting-toe intuition to be debunked rather than simply reclassified as part of the illusion? Is there a way to say the pain is real in every sense that matters for action and aversiveness, while denying that introspection is tracking a nonphysical qualitative property? Or does the theory have to amend itself into something much closer to realism once it takes the felt urgency of pain seriously?
