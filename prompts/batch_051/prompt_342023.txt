I'm trying to get a handle on the boundaries of sentience question — specifically which computations matter morally — and I want a thought experiment built to order. Full disclosure: I lean toward an attribution view, where a computation counts as a patient only when there's something it's like to be that computation, and we treat "something it's like" as needing not just raw information processing but something like a point of view the system generates and then uses — a self-model, prioritization of some states over others, anything like a first-person report. Against that, set up an integration view: a computation matters morally to the extent that its parts constrain each other in a way that isn't decomposable, so the whole carries information the parts don't. (Integration is vague — pick a precisification, tell me which one, and use it consistently.)

I want the case where the integration view says there is a real, if modest, patient inhering in some subcomputation, and the attribution view says there is nothing it's like there and therefore no patient at all. And I want the hard version of it: not a big lookup table or a tape recorder that just prints "I am in pain." The integration has to be genuinely high — rich, two-way constraint among the parts. The features that would give the attribution view a hook have to be absent or actively misleading.

Constraints:
- Keep it realistic — infrastructure, a lab setup, a control system, a server cluster, that sort of thing. No brains in vats, no perfect simulations of a nervous system, no small Chinese nation.
- Dont stipulate any experience either way, obviously; that's the thing in dispute.
- Describe the parts, what information they carry, and why the whole does or doesn't amount to a perspective — concretely enough that both views have something to push on.

Then walk me through it:
1. what the subcomputation is, in plain terms;
2. why integration comes out high;
3. why attribution comes out empty (or why the only attribution-like features point away from patienthood);
4. which feature of the case is doing the real work, and what each side's verdict does if I delete just that feature;
5. how a committed defender of the integration view would honestly attack the case — not by denying the stipulations, but by arguing that the attribution view's verdict is the one I should stop trusting.

This is for a paper arguing that integration measures leave out the system's own use of its states, which I think is where attribution has to get its grip. So the case has to earn its verdicts from the theories, not have them written into the setup.

And if I've posed this badly — if the two views don't actually come apart the way I think, or the constraints force you to cheat, or my statement of the attribution view is too mushy to build against — say so plainly and tell me what I'd need to fix, instead of handing me a case that secretly begs the question.
