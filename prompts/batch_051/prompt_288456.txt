I am pulled in two directions about when an infinite regress is vicious, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a winner.

The theory I keep being drawn to is this: a regress is vicious when each item merely passes along the explanatory burden it was introduced to discharge — when it is the same kind of dependent item as the one before it and borrows whatever would make it explanatory from the next item down. A debt repaid by borrowing from a lender who pays it by borrowing from a further lender, ad infinitum, is never repaid; an inner viewer who sees the screen and therefore needs another viewer farther back explains nothing; a chain of beliefs in which each belief is justified only by the next never makes any belief justified. The theory earns its keep by its simplicity, by unifying these cases under one principle, and by giving me a nearly mechanical test: ask whether the work is ever actually done, or only rescheduled.

But I have a firm intuition about one case the theory seems to forbid, and I cannot honestly shake it. Suppose the past is beginningless: every event is caused by an earlier event. It seems to me that each event is then fully caused, because each event has its own anterior cause; nothing in the world is left waiting for a cause that never shows up. The question what caused the entire infinite series? strikes me as a category mistake. A series of events is not itself an event; it is not the sort of thing that happens, so asking for its cause seems to mislocate the demand. I feel none of this about the inner viewer or the justification chain, where every stage does look like a deferral. So the theory as it stands appears to condemn a benign regress along with the vicious ones.

When I try to state the principled difference, I reach for formulations I do not trust because I chose them after the fact: the causal chain is ordered in one temporal direction only; each link depends only on the link immediately before it; the causal work is discharged and recharged at each step rather than transferred. Part of my difficulty is that regress arguments slide between steps in the world, causes, and steps in the space of reasons, justifications, and I suspect the word vicious may mean something different in each register without my being able to say how.

So my question is what should give. What would a successful debunking of the intuition about the infinite past have to show — what would I have to learn or notice for that question to stop feeling like a category mistake and start feeling like a genuine explanatory failure? What would a successful amendment or abandonment of the theory have to show — which cases or considerations would expose the unified principle as false or overstated? Or is there a principled reading of the term that does the real work — depends, explains, or series — on which both survive without special pleading? Please separate what is settled in this debate from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested account as the consensus.
