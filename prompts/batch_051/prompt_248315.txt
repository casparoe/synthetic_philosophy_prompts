A long-form science piece I read summarized the quantum suicide and quantum immortality arguments under the many-worlds interpretation like this:

"Everett's many-worlds interpretation says that whenever a quantum measurement is made, the universe splits into all possible outcomes, and every branch is equally real. So imagine a device that measures a particle's spin and detonates a bomb if the result is up, while doing nothing if the result is down. There is always a branch in which the result is down and you survive, and you can only ever observe branches in which you are still alive; therefore you should expect to survive every single trial. After enough trials, the surviving version of you will have witnessed an astronomically improbable run of down-results, while the versions that saw up are dead and not around to be surprised. Generalizing, quantum mechanics implies that from your own first-person perspective you are immortal: for any accident or illness, some branch exists in which you pull through, and that is the only kind of branch you can experience. The article then treats this as a refutation of many-worlds: quantum immortality is absurd, it follows directly from the many-worlds picture, so many-worlds must be false."

I'm not sure how much of this to trust. I suspect the step from "there exists a branch where I survive" to "I should expect to survive" is doing more work than the article admits, and I also doubt that the absurdity of a consequence, by itself, refutes an interpretation. But I don't know whether the article is being fair to what defenders of many-worlds actually claim.

Could you go through this summary claim by claim and tell me which parts are accurate, which are simplifications or conflations, which are overstated, what a careful defender of many-worlds would object to, and whether the conclusion that many-worlds is false follows from the accurate parts?
