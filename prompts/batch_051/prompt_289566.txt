I run the direct-investment sleeve of a family office. Every week we decide whether to join a financing round that other funds have already committed to. The standard argument is that sophisticated investors' participation is itself evidence; but I know enough about rational herding to worry that once a few early movers act, later ones are rationally copying the action rather than the information, and a cascade can rest on very little independent judgment. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in what is actually known about informational cascades and rational herding, for deciding when "others are in" is genuine evidence and when it is mostly cascade. It must be usable by an analyst in about an hour using only what we already have (cap table, round structure, the track records we happen to know, and the limited things investors say publicly), with no access to other investors' actual reasoning, and it must be something I can defend to our investment committee. It should specify which data to pull and in what order, how to read the order in which commitments came in, where a public statement should be discounted as cheap talk, which distinctions from the theory must survive the simplification, and where judgment cannot be eliminated. Apply it to three cases: (1) a seed round in which two respected funds committed early and twelve others followed quickly; (2) a late-stage round led by a deep industry expert who is simultaneously selling down an existing position; and (3) a round where our own due diligence has found a red flag no one else appears to have priced in. The procedure should flag its own predictable failure modes and say explicitly what it cannot do, and at least one worked case should end with an answer I will not like.
