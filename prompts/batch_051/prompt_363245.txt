I've been turning over an asymmetry in how we talk about guilt across time, and I want someone to actually commit to a position instead of laying out the options.

Take a forty-year-old who feels guilt about something he did at nineteen—bullying a classmate, say. Even his friends who think he's a completely different person now don't tell him "that wasn't you, drop it." They treat the guilt as intelligible, maybe even fitting. The nineteen-year-old self is gone in every way that matters, but guilt is allowed to travel across the decades.

Now switch the frame. Someone says "I feel guilty about what my country did before I was born," or "our nation is guilty of what our grandparents generation did." A lot of the same people will immediately correct it: you weren't there, you didn't do it, that's not guilt, that's shame at most. Guilt, they insist, doesn't cross generations the way it crosses the years within a single life.

So why is a person's past self the same self for purposes of guilt, while a nation's past population isn't the same nation? Run it the other way and it gets stranger: we let a corporation stay guilty across complete turnover—Exxon is still on the hook for a spill decades after everyone personally responsible is gone—but we insist that a nation's present citizens can't inherit guilt. Corporations and nations are both collective bodies that persist through time under a name, both can sign treaties and owe debts, but we treat one as a guilty agent and the other as a crowd of innocent bystanders.

I remember Jaspers tried to split the difference with the claim that there is political guilt, which all citizens share just by being citizens of a state (including ones born later), and moral guilt, which is strictly individual. That lets a nation be liable without letting a newborn be guilty. But the distinction feels like it's doing more work then it can bear: what kind of thing is political guilt if no individual who bears it did anything? Is it just guilt renamed so people can pay reparations without flinching?

I don't want a survey. Give me the strongest principle you can find that either justifies the asymmetry (guilt travels within a life, and maybe through corporate time, but not from nation to citizen) or dissolves it, then tell me which side of Jaspers' line you'd actually defend and which cases you'd give up.
