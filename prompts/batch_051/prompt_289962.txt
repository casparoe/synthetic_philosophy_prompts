I'm a distributed systems engineer, and I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether a company can itself be blamed for harm, or whether only the people inside it can be. My friend kept saying that a corporation is just processes and incentive structures, so blaming the company is like blaming a server farm for dropping packets. I kept wanting to say that the system did it — that no single node held the whole decision — but I could not say exactly what that amounts to. I work daily in the vocabulary of nodes, state, replication, consensus, message-passing, control loops, failure modes, emergent behavior, and root-cause analysis. I am fluent in that vocabulary and can follow precise technical uses of it, but I am not a moral philosopher, so do not lean on unexplicated philosophical jargon. And please do not cite authors or works; I only care about whether the translation itself succeeds.

Please restate the following three positions about corporate blame in that vocabulary, one at a time:

1. The atomist position: only individual persons are the sort of thing that can be morally blameworthy. Talk of blaming a company is shorthand for blaming some identifiable set of individuals, and where no individual is properly at fault, the company is not a blameworthy agent.

2. The emergentist position: a corporation can be a blameworthy agent in its own right, because its decision structure can produce policies, choices, and a continuity of purpose that no individual employee intends or fully holds.

3. The accountability-without-culpability position: a corporation can appropriately be held answerable, fined, or required to make amends even if it is not the kind of entity that can be culpable in the way an individual is.

For each position, give:
(a) the translation — the position's claims expressed honestly in terms of nodes, state, message-passing, control loops, failure modes, and the rest;
(b) the distortions — claims that can be forced into the vocabulary only by stretching the ordinary meaning of the engineering terms;
(c) the blanks — claims that have no counterpart in the vocabulary at all.

In giving each translation, say explicitly which engineering object the corporation is being identified with — the set of nodes, the protocol they run, the aggregate state trajectory, or the system-level service — and flag where that identification is doing silent argumentative work. A translation counts as clean only if a competent engineer with no philosophy background could, after reading it, state the position's commitments using only the engineering terms. If you find yourself using intention, culpability, desert, or blame unanalysed inside a translation, mark that as a failure of translation.

Then step back and compile the residue: the main things that failed to translate across the three positions. For each, rule on whether the failure shows that the distributed-systems vocabulary is missing something morally important, or that the original philosophical claim was doing less work than it appeared to — a metaphor rather than a mechanism. Finally, return to my argument with my friend: after this exercise, does it look like a substantive disagreement or a vocabulary mismatch, and what is the strongest remaining reason to think a company can be blamed?
