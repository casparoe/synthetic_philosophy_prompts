I was having it out with a friend who teaches sociology and she has this line she repeats: generations—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z—are not real kinds, just arbitrary 15-year bins we use to make charts and headlines. Her argument is that the cutoffs are pretty much made up, since the exact year shifts depending on who you read, and that if you control for age and the current moment, most supposed "generational" differences go away or at least shrink badly.

I dont think that quite settles it. Fuzziness at the boundaries doesn't mean there's no real difference in the middle, any more than day and night aren't real because dusk is gradual. Some cohorts do share pretty specific formative experiences—growing up with smartphones, or entering the job market around the 2008 crash—so it seems at least possible that the labels are tracking something real even if the exact birthday cutoffs are conventions.

So here's what I'd like an answer to: how would you actually test the claim that "Millennial" or "Gen Z" is a real kind rather than a convenient bucket? Don't just say the cutoffs are arbitrary or give a reading list; I want the strongest concrete empirical test you can design, assuming you had full survey data across many cohorts and countries. What pattern would count as evidence that there's a real kind underneath, and what pattern would count as evidence that it's just grouping by birthday? And then be honest about what a positive result would still leave open—is "real kind" the sort of thing an empirical test can settle, or does it just come down to how useful the category is for prediction? Please write the answer in British English, and at the end say how confident you are in the test you design and which part you're least sure about.
