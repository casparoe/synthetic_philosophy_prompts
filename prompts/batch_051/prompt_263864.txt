Article 231 of the Versailles Treaty, soon known as the war-guilt clause, did not merely require Germany to pay reparations: it made the legal basis of payment Germany's and its allies' responsibility for all the loss and damage inflicted by their aggression. Germany signed under protest, and the clause poisoned interwar politics. Fritz Fischer's archival work in the early 1960s reopened the question on the historical side, contending that Germany's leadership had taken Europe into war in 1914 while pursuing expansionist aims; the ensuing controversy was as much about whether a nation can be guilty as about what the documents showed. After 1945, by contrast, international criminal law moved decisively toward blaming individuals: the Nuremberg tribunal held that only natural persons, not abstract entities such as states, commit crimes and can be punished for them. So the twentieth century left three different ways of blaming a war sitting side by side — a treaty that assigned responsibility to a state, a historiography that argued about national guilt, and a criminal law that would blame only individuals.

Reconstruct the theory of collective responsibility that the war-guilt clause presupposes. Does the practice treat a nation as something capable of acting, intending, and bearing blame; is it better read as a reparations device that deliberately bypassed the metaphysics of guilt; or is it doing something else again, such as assigning an outcome-level responsibility that an individualist could accept? Be precise about what the clause strictly presupposes, as opposed to what it merely fits well with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then use that reconstruction to assess the Fischer controversy and the post-1945 individualist turn: do they show that the clause depended on a dubious metaphysics, or that the metaphysics was never doing the work? Finally, say what state apologies, reparations, and national-guilt debates would have to look like if an individualist theory of blame were adopted consistently throughout.
