I've been turning this over since a neighbor got away with something. A guy on my street defrauded a bunch of elderly people out of their savings maybe eight years ago. Everyone knows he did it; he basically admitted it at a party once. But the prosecutor said the statue of limitations had run, so he can't be charged with anything. The weird part is, if they'd charged him the week after he did it, it would've been the exact same crime. Nothing about what he did changed. Time just passed and the crime sort of evaporated, legally speaking. And it's uneven: murder has no time limit—they'll still prosecute a thirty-year-old killing—but fraud, assault, most things, after enough years it's as if you didn't do it.

I keep trying to work out what picture of wrongdoing you'd have to hold for that to make sense. Maybe it presupposes that the moral stain of a wrong fades, the way grief does? Or that after enough time the person is basically a different person from the one who did it, so we'd be punishing the wrong man? Or maybe it's not about the wrong at all—it's just that evidence gets unreliable and witnesses forget, and we're willing to let some guilty people go to avoid convicting innocent ones on stale cases. But if that were the whole story, why wouldn't a confession or a clear video that turns up twenty years later restart the clock? It doesn't, as far as I know.
