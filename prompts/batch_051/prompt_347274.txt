I've spent enough years on rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums that I can't unsee "where do you draw the boundary?" as a move, which is maybe why I can't tell if my own views here are actually coherent. I'm supposed to send a short "philosophical profile" of my commitments about language change to a reading group, and I want an audit before I hit send.

My topic is the usual one: when does Latin become Italian? I know the question is partly about what we mean by "same language", but I honestly hold all of the following, and each one feels stable on its own:

1. There is no determinate moment when Latin became Italian. Not a year, not a decade, probably not a century. The spoken Latin of central Italy turned into something we'd now call Italian by a long, smooth series of changes, and any line through the middle is a choice.

2. But Latin and Italian are two different languages. I don't want the "Italian is just modern Latin" line; if someone said that, I'd count them as wrong rather than as having redefined words.

3. Language identity over time is a matter of lineage, not resemblance. A language can change any amount of its grammar and lexicon and still be the same language, so long as there was unbroken transmission, each generation learning it from the previous one. Languages are more like species or lineages than like bundles of features.

4. Vague boundaries are in the map, not the territory. There is no fact of the matter about exactly when "Italian" begins, because vagueness is semantic indecision: there's a bunch of acceptable places to draw the line and no priviledged one.

5. If two varieties are mutually unintelligible, they can't be the same language. I know intelligibility isn't sufficient, and dialect chains make it messy in the other direction, but the one-way rule I want to keep.

6. The Romance family tree is basically right. Latin is the common ancestor and Italian, Spanish, French and so on are separate descendants — a lineage can branch, and a language doesn't have to be extinct for its descendants to count as new languages.
