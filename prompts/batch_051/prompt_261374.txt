I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been drafting a short essay that I would like feedback on. The notes below give the argument I want to make. Please comment on three things specifically: whether my characterization of evidential and causal decision theory is accurate, whether the objection I raise actually applies to the position I am criticizing or rests on a misunderstanding, and where the essay would be most likely to overclaim or embarrass someone without formal training. Suggest revisions where the reasoning is weakest.

Working claim of the essay: evidential decision theory looks much more cooperative once one takes seriously the fact that the world contains many agents whose decision procedures are correlated with one's own — the idea usually called evidential cooperation in large worlds. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, a causal decision theorist defects because defection strictly dominates. An evidential decision theorist, however, can reason that choosing to cooperate is evidence that sufficiently similar decision procedures elsewhere will also choose to cooperate, which is evidence that one lives in the kind of world where cooperation is the better deal. On this view, the apparent irrationality of cooperating in a one-shot interaction comes from treating the interaction as smaller than it really is.

My worry is that this move only works after one has chosen which agents count as sufficiently similar — that is, after one has fixed the reference class over decision procedures. Make the class broad, and almost any action can be made to look prudent by imagining enough correlated counterparts; make it narrow, and one is back to defection. As an engineer, this looks like an under-specified parameter: a similarity metric the framework does not derive, and which can be tuned to produce whatever conclusion one wanted in advance. The essay would argue that the interesting question is therefore not whether to cooperate, but who gets to count as a correlational peer, and that this question sits beneath decision theory rather than inside it. Is that a fair statement of the issue, and is it a strong enough spine for a short essay?
