my friend who's deep into whole brain emulation keeps deflecting the "uploading is death" worry with an analogy: moving a mind to a computer is just like moving your files to a new machine. you copy the files over, verify the copy, wipe the old drive, and nobody claims the files died. they're the same files on new hardware. from this he concludes that a destructive upload (scan the brain, run the emulation, discard the original) is a relocation rather than a killing, and that people who insist on continuity of consciousness are just sentimental about meat.

i want you to audit the analogy. give me the explicit mapping first: old drive, new drive, the files, the copy operation, the checksum, the wipe, the hardware/software split. then go through the disanalogies and tell me which are cosmetic and which do real work. in particular dig into: (a) there's no fact about "what it's like to be a file," but there is about me; (b) a brain has no clean hardware/software distinction, since the "program" is realized partly in synaptic weights and ion channels that a destructive scan is precisely what destroys; (c) file copying lets you delete the original or keep it, and the official story about which copy is "really you" seems to change depending on which branch survives; (d) a file is inert and doesn't care about pauses or slowdowns, while i'm a running process, and it's an open question whether a slowed or suspended simulation preserves whatever matters about me. then say whether his conclusion survives once these are on the table, and if not, what a better analogy would be, or whether analogies are doing too much of the work here. keep it readable for someone who isn't sold on uploading.

and at the end, add a short note: what would change your mind about whether the analogy can carry it's conclusion, which argument or case would make you reverse the verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
