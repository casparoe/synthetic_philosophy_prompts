A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about inheritance taxes, and I came away less sure of the argument than when we began. My friend defended the familiar equal-opportunity case: a child's prospects should depend on what the child actually does, not on the accident of being born into the right family; large inheritances carry unchosen competitive advantage across generations; a society committed to equality of opportunity should therefore tax inherited wealth. I could not simply accept or reject this argument, and my hesitation came from a specific shift.

The argument was built for a world in which what parents pass on is mostly property and the moment of transmission is death. But much of what the well-off now transmit moves while the parents are still alive: private schooling and tutoring, the difference between graduating debt-free and not, help with a first home, internships arranged through family contacts, gifts small enough to fall below the threshold at which they are taxed. In many families the taxable estate is only the residue of a transmission that has already been completed. If that is right, the equal-opportunity argument is aimed, in principle, at the entire intergenerational transmission of advantage, while the tax instrument it historically supported is aimed at one narrow and late channel of that transmission.

What I want to work out is what happens to the argument once this is noticed. Does it survive intact, because the estate tax remains an imperfect but still usable way to reach dynastic advantage even if it misses much of the flow? Does it become stronger, because its premise now implicates a much wider range of parental behavior, making the real object not the estate but the family as a transmission mechanism? Or does it need repair into a principle that taxes advantage through the channels by which it actually travels? I want to know which premise the shift puts under pressure, and whether a repaired version can say something weaker but still important. I am less interested in what the tax code should say than in whether the argument still does the work it was supposed to do, and what the repaired version would have to say about the difference between an inheritance, a gift, and an advantaged upbringing.
