In three weeks I'm defending a short paper at a formal epistemology workshop, and the Q&A is the part I'm worried about. The thesis I'm stuck defending is this: decision theory should not treat an expected value as well-defined merely because the relevant sum converges; it should require absolute convergence, and the Pasadena game shows why. The room will be mostly decision theorists and philosophers of probability, plus two mathematicians who work on divergent series, and theyre the sort of people who find just ignoring the pathological cases lazy rather than tempting. My setup is the usual Pasadena game: flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if the first heads is on toss n you get (−1)^(n−1) times 2^n/n dollars. The expected value works out to the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 but not absolutely, so by reordering the states you can make it sum to anything you like. My line is that an expectation that changes when you relist the states is no expectation at all, and the only principled fix is to say the game has no expected value.
