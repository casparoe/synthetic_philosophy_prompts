Two friends of mine got into a long argument at dinner last week about whether big historical events are inevitable, and I've been turning it over since. They were both making points that sounded right in the moment, but I couldn't tell where the real disagreement was. The exchange went roughly like this (im compressing a little from memory):

A: "The First World War was overdetermined. By 1914 the alliance system, the naval race, and the war plans with their rigid mobilization timetables meant almost any spark could have set it off. Saying the assassination of Franz Ferdinand caused the war is naive—there'd been half a dozen other flashpoints in the previous decade that nearly did the same thing."

B: "That's hindsight talking. 'Any spark would have done it' is completely untestable; we only got to watch one run of 1914. The war also hung on a string of specific decisions that summer—the blank cheque, the Russian mobilization order, the Belgian refusal. Counterfactuals are a normal part of explanation. If I say I missed my meeting because the bus was late, I'm committing myself to a claim about what would have happened otherwise."

A: "Sure, but bus counterfactuals are checkable against regularities—buses run every day. History gives you one run, no control group, and thousands of interacting variables. You can't hold all of 1914 fixed except the assassination and rerun it. So these detailed what-if stories people write are basically just stories."

B: "But by that standard you can't call the war inevitable either. 'Inevitable' is itself a counterfactual claim—it says that in all the nearby versions where the assassination fails or doesn't happen, a great-power war still breaks out. You're making a modal claim while telling me mine are unscientific."

A: "Okay, but there's a difference in degree. The structural pressures I'm pointing to existed before and after the assassination and had been building for years. I don't need to say any particular bullet was necessary. I just need to say the system had made a general European war extremely probable, and then it broke out."

B: "But 'extremely probable' is doing a lot of work without any method behind it. And if the system made war so likely, why did the earlier crises keep getting resolved peacefully? I suspect the war only looks overdetermined once it's already happened."

Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each of A's and B's turns, give a mini-verdict: which specific claims hold up, which are overstated, and which are just wrong. Then say what you think both of them are missing in the contingency-versus-inevitability debate.
