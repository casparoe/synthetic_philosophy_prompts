For a paper I am writing on transhumanism, I have developed a distinction between accretive and substitutive self-change, and I want an honest assessment of how much weight it can bear. Accretive change adds a capacity while preserving the values and desires that could evaluate the addition: learning a new language, or training to run a marathon. Substitutive change reconstructs the valuing self, for example deliberately erasing a paralyzing grief, or extinguishing an aesthetic passion because it interferes with one's ambitions. My thesis is that only substitutive change raises a distinctive moral problem, which I call the vanishing-objector problem: the person whose consent matters is the very person the change would eliminate, while the person who remains afterward can no longer judge from the inside whether the transformation was a gain or a loss. I worry that this either recapitulates familiar debates about personal identity or quietly assumes that the earlier self's values have authority over the later self. Please reconstruct my view in its strongest form, identify the nearest existing positions, and then give the strongest objections. I am especially interested in whether the accretive/substitutive line marks a real difference in kind or only a difference of degree, and whether any objection is fatal rather than fixable.
