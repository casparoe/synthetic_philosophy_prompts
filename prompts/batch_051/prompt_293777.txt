I work in software and not in philosophy, so I'm going to sound naive here. The argument about whether an AI should be a "legal person" always seems to run aground on whether the AI is "really" a person, which to me feels like a type error: corporations are legal persons and nobody thinks they have minds. I'd like you to make an argument for the following position: that the corporate-person analogy is more useful than its critics admit, because legal personhood is best understood as a tunable bundle of capacities (owning property, entering contracts, suing and being sued, bearing certain duties), not a claim about consciousness or moral status. On this view we could grant a limited, purpose-specific legal personhood to some AI systems — say, an autonomous agent that enters contracts or causes harm — without thereby granting it rights in any thick sense or conceding it has interests of its own. Don't just assert this; build the argument. But also do me a favor: as you go, seperate what's actually settled among people who study legal personhood from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested take as the consensus. In particular I suspect the real fight is over whether a bundle-of-capacities picture actually does all the work here, since a corporation's legal personhood is ultimately a way of organizing human interests, whereas an AI might be in a different position if it can act in ways no particular human directed. So if there's a hidden step in the argument that assumes that difference doesn't matter, call it out. Keep it grounded in concrete consequences — who pays when something goes wrong, whether a contract is valid, when a court can name the system itself as a party — rather than abstract personhood talk.
