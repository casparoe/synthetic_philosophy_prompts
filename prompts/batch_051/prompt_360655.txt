I am a novelist, and I am building the constitutional logic for a near-future political novel. In the book, a reform movement has replaced courts, regulatory agencies, and legislative committees with continuous smartphone referenda. The movement's founders defend the design in almost mathematical terms: they invoke the Condorcet jury theorem, which they take to show that as long as each voter is even slightly more likely to choose the correct of two alternatives than the incorrect one, a sufficiently large electorate is almost certain to have a correct majority. In one scene, the constitution's chief architect responds to a plainly disastrous referendum result by insisting that the only possible explanations are that too few people voted or that some citizens were wrongly excluded, never that the public could have been wrong.

For the novel to work, I need the opponents of this system to be more than reactionaries; I need to know whether their worries have any mathematical force. If the theorem says what my characters say it says, then it seems to prove that scale alone washes out individual error and that fears about uninformed, misinformed, or demagogically manipulated voters are just superstition dressed up as civic virtue. I suspect I am missing something about what the theorem actually licenses. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
