In my linguistics club we have been arguing about whether there is any such thing as bad grammar, and one member offered the following argument. It convinced everyone at the time, but it seems to prove too much, and I cannot tell whether it is sound or, if not, where it goes wrong.

(1) A charge of bad grammar must be grounded either in how native speakers actually use the language or in an explicit rulebook.
(2) Grounded in actual use, no construction is ungrammatical within the speaker's own variety: every variety has its own internal rules, so a form like "I seen it" is well-formed in the grammar of the varieties that regularly produce it.
(3) Grounded in a rulebook, the charge records only conformity to a convention. Rulebooks disagree—some ban split infinitives, others accept them—and no procedure settles the disagreement from outside all conventions.
(4) So every verdict that a sentence is bad grammar is either false or a statement that the sentence departs from whichever convention happened to be selected.
(5) It follows that when a teacher marks a student's sentence as bad grammar, the teacher is not identifying a real defect in the sentence; the mark records the preference encoded in the school's rulebook or the social standing of the student's variety.
(6) The same reasoning seems to apply to clarity: when a teacher marks a sentence as unclear, the teacher is also not identifying a real defect, since readers disagree about clarity, standards of clarity change across periods, and no rulebook for clarity has more authority than one for grammar.
(7) Yet a sentence that careful readers cannot recover a reading from despite genuine effort is unclear in a way that is not merely a record of someone's preference.
(8) So (5) appears false, and grammar judgments must track something beyond the conventions of a rulebook after all.

I most want to keep (7), but I am not sure what that costs me. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the question whether there is any such thing as bad grammar? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up?
