In my philosophy of mind seminar we spent three weeks on the extended mind stuff, and my study group has ended up with a rough list of criteria for when something outside your skull should count as literally part of your cognitive system, not just a useful tool. We settled on four: (1) parity — if you moved the thing inside your head, what it does would uncontroversially count as remembering, reasoning, or perceiving; (2) reliable availability — it's there and working when you need it, with roughly the same hit rate as ordinary memory; (3) transparent access — you go to it the way you go to your memory or your vision, not by deliberately checking an external source and then evaluating what it says; and (4) endorsement — you take its outputs up as your own beliefs or memories, and losing it wouldn't feel like losing a gadget but like losing part of yourself.

The case we're stuck on is my roommate, Ana, who is writing a dissertation and keeps an enormous linked database of everything she has ever read, thought, or noticed, partly typed in her own words and partly imported and tagged. The system now resufaces old notes beside new ones and suggests connections between them — sometimes connections she has no memory of ever having made. When she's writing or arguing, she actually asks the database things like what did I think about supervenience and reads the answer, often forgetting she wrote the note until she sees it. She trusts most of what comes up without going back to the original source. But not everything: sometimes she reads a note and thinks no, that's not me, that was a bad Tuesday idea, and she deletes or rewrites it. She told me the database is her memory, that losing it would feel like losing years of her own thought, but she also talks about it as if it occasionally has opinions she has to talk back to.

So: does the database count as part of Ana's mind under our four criteria? Where exactly does it pass or fail? And do you think the case shows one of our criteria is doing too much work or is drawn too sharply — for instance, can something be part of your mind even if it sometimes surprises you, or if access to it is more like searching than like remembering?
