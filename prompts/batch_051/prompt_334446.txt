Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether deplatforming is best understood as punishment. By deplatforming I mean a cluster of informal sanctions: a platform removing a user's account; a university withdrawing a speaking invitation after protests; a crowd of strangers pressuring an employer to fire someone for something written years earlier. My friend insisted none of this is punishment, because no one has a right to an audience, a speaking slot, or a job. I kept returning to a passage from John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (Chapter IV, "Of the Limits to the Authority of Society over the Individual"):

The acts of an individual may be hurtful to others, or wanting in due consideration for their welfare, without going the length of violating any of their constituted rights. The offender may then be justly punished by opinion, though not by law. As soon as any part of a person’s conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it, and the question whether the general welfare will or will not be promoted by interfering with it, becomes open to discussion.

Mill seems to treat informal social penalties as penalties, legitimate even when no right has been violated, yet he binds them to a distinction between conduct that affects others and conduct that is merely self-regarding, and to a question about the general welfare. I want to know what a practice like deplatforming presupposes about punishment, blameworthiness, standing, and proportionality. Which of those commitments does the practice strictly presuppose, which does it merely fit with, and where is it underdetermined? Does treating deplatforming as a justified sanction commit its defenders to a retributive, consequentialist, or expressive theory of punishment, and can the practice instead be reconstructed as non-punitive—as refusal of association, self-defense, or disendorsement—without simply relocating those same commitments? Are the parts of the practice (platform moderation, campus disinvitation, employer firings under public pressure) consistent with one another, or do they presuppose different things? What would have to change in the practice if one of those implicit theories were replaced?
