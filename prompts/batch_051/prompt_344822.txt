I keep going back and forth with a friend about Benatar-style antinatalism, and there's a move I keep making that I want someone to stress-test. As I understand it, the asymmetry says the absence of pain is good even when there's no person around to enjoy the absence, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there's an actual person being deprived of it. That's supposed to tip the comparison in favor of never existing: the not-born get the good of no pain without incurring the bad of no pleasure. Fine. But it seems like the asymmetry gets turned off at birth, and I don't see why.

So I've been calling my version interval-indexed asymmetry. Take an existing person and divide the rest of their life into slices: the next hour, next year, the decade after. Now consider just one future slice. The pain in that slice doesn't exist yet, so its absence is good in the same way the never-born's absent pain is good. The pleasure in that slice also doesn't exist yet, so its absence isn't bad, because the "future person who would have enjoyed it" isn't there yet as a subject to be deprived. The comparison for that interval then comes out the same way the birth comparison does: not living through the slice beats living through it. Repeat for every slice and an alive person should prefer dying now to living the next stretch, and then the next. That can't be what he want. But I don't think it's just a gotcha. It pushes me toward the view that the asymmetry, if true, is really about intervals or possible states, not about birth, and then antinatalism either slides into pro-mortalism or owes us an explanation of why the birth boundary is special while every later boundary isn't.

My friend says interval-indexed asymmetry is smuggling in a bad analogy: the living person has wants, plans, and a subject that reaches into the future, so their future goods are possessed by someone now, while the never-born has no subject at all; my "would-have-been slicer" is not in the ontology. I can't tell if that's a real point or special pleading, because it sounds like just restating the birth boundary instead of justifying it. So: is interval-indexed asymmetry a coherent extension, or does it quietly change the topic from who exists to who might have existed? Does it already have a name (it feels too obvious not to have been tried)? And where exactly does it break in its strongest form?
