I am a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I keep running into a version of a problem I know from writing specifications. In my work, a specification is usable only if it can be compiled into a decision procedure: for every input in the domain, the code either flags it or does not. Some time ago I had to translate parts of a regulatory scheme into an automated screening system, and I began noticing something odd about criminal law. In the United States, courts can invalidate a criminal statute as void for vagueness. As I understand it, the doctrine has two main rationales: a statute must give ordinary people fair notice of what it prohibits, and it must not be so open-ended that police, prosecutors, and juries are effectively improvising the standards as they go.

What unsettles me is how selectively this standard seems to bite. The same legal system tolerates vague language all over: the reasonable person standard, bans on conduct that is unfair or deceptive, materiality determinations, and criminal tests that turn on community standards. Many of those would fail a compile test too, yet they are not automatically treated as invalid on that ground. The same system also insists that ignorance of the law is no excuse, and it leaves substantial charging and plea-bargaining discretion to prosecutors. From an engineering standpoint, that looks like a system demanding a clean public API for some rules while quietly routing other decisions through runtime discretion.

I would like to understand what this practice presupposes. What view of law, of citizens, and of official discretion does the void-for-vagueness doctrine commit a legal system to? Does it only make sense if law is addressed to ordinary people as usable instructions, rather than a set of standards meant to guide officials? Does it presuppose that discretion is primarily a danger to be limited, rather than a necessary instrument of individualized judgment? Which rival views does the doctrine quietly rule out, and which does it merely fit better or worse with? Are its parts consistent, given that the same system pairs a demand for clear notice with a rule that ignorance of the law is no excuse and with broad prosecutorial discretion? And where is the practice underdetermined, so that several different theories of law or fairness would each license it? I want a clear separation between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely suggests, and what it leaves open, plus a sense of what would have to change if one of those background commitments were replaced — for example, if law were understood as directives to officials rather than instructions to the public.
