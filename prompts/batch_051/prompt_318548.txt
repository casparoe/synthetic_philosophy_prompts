I run the peer-review process for a journal that deliberately publishes work from rival research programs in the same discipline, and a recurring problem has become impossible to ignore. A referee will submit a report objecting that a paper misuses a central term, such as gene, information, or planet, when the real situation may be that the paper and the referee are using the term inside different conceptual schemes of the kind Kuhn described as incommensurable. My editors cannot reliably tell the difference between a referee report that lands a genuine criticism and one that simply fails to translate the paper into the referee's own vocabulary, and under deadline pressure they tend to treat every such report as a substantive objection.

The practical constraints are these. The decisions come up weekly, in fields where specialists disagree about the meaning of a shared term, and a wrong call is costly in both directions: rejecting a paper because it will not translate into a reviewer's vocabulary can kill work that a bilingual expert would judge sound, while publishing a paper that genuinely contradicts well-tested results in the referee's framework is also a failure. The editors are not bilingual in every relevant framework, and they cannot become so in the time available. I want the procedure grounded in what Kuhn's view actually licenses, rather than the caricature of it: incommensurability is local, it concerns the absence of a neutral vocabulary into which both frameworks can be translated without loss, and it does not imply that comparison or rational argument across frameworks is impossible; understanding the other side typically requires learning a lexicon rather than finding an already shared set of meanings.

Please write the procedure as an ordered checklist my editors can run in under half an hour on any paper where a reviewer's central criticism appears to be that the author is using a term or a category incorrectly. For each step, say what question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a decision, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question has become one of judgment rather than procedure. Then run the procedure on two worked cases: first, a paper using gene in a systems or network-role sense that is opposed by a reviewer using gene in a molecular or sequence sense; second, a paper using planet to include large round icy bodies beyond Neptune that is opposed by a reviewer using a dynamical definition under which those bodies are not planets. Arrange at least one of the worked cases so that the procedure returns an answer the journal will not like, and say explicitly which parts of the decision cannot be reduced to a checklist no matter how carefully the checklist is written.
