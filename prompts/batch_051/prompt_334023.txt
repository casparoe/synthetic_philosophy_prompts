Last week, two members of my epistemology reading group, Dana and Ravi, got into an argument about a pair of cases. In both cases, Lena, a water-quality engineer, has exactly the same evidence — the same sampling run, the same instrument readings, and the same background reports — and in both cases the evidence points strongly but not conclusively to the same true proposition: that the water is safe to drink. In the low-stakes case, she tells a homeowner that the water is safe for the household garden. In the high-stakes case, she must decide whether to certify the same water as safe for the drinking fountains at an elementary school. Lena's evidence and degree of confidence are the same in both cases, and the proposition is true in both; only the cost of being wrong differs.

Dana and Ravi's exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Dana: The two cases are epistemically identical, so Lena either knows in both cases or knows in neither. Stakes are not evidence, and whether someone knows is fixed by evidence, truth, and belief — nothing practical. If the cost of error could change the verdict, a person could know something at noon and stop knowing it at one o'clock merely because someone raised the cost of being wrong. That makes knowledge unstable in a way that undercuts its usefulness as a description of a person's epistemic position.

(2) Ravi: The shift in what we'd say isn't instability; it's context-sensitivity, like the word "flat." A sidewalk can be flat enough for a skateboarder and not flat enough for a precision optical alignment, and neither claim has to be false. "Knows" works the same way: in a low-stakes context, Lena's evidence meets the operative standard for knowledge; in a high-stakes context, it doesn't. So the low-stakes "she knows" and the high-stakes "she doesn't know" can both be true in their respective contexts, with no need to retract either.

(3) Dana: That relocates the problem from Lena to our sentences. On your view, if I, a low-stakes bystander, say "Lena knows the water is safe," my lower standard makes my sentence true, while Lena herself, in the high-stakes situation, says "I don't know" and speaks truly too. You then have to say that it is true that Lena knows and true that Lena doesn't know in the very same situation, the only difference being who is talking. But the question we asked was about Lena's epistemic position, not about who can get away with saying what. Your view doesn't tell us whether she knows; it tells us only how to interpret the word.

(4) Ravi: But you're the one insisting a theory must tell us whether Lena knows, full stop. If you won't allow the standard to shift with the word, the only way to respect both the low-stakes ascription and Lena's own high-stakes denial is to let how strong her evidence must be depend on what's at stake for her. That is pragmatic encroachment: her practical situation partly determines whether her true, evidence-based belief is knowledge. So you face a choice. Either knowledge ascriptions are context-sensitive, or knowledge itself is stakes-sensitive. What you can't be is someone who thinks stakes are irrelevant to knowledge while also insisting that Lena's high-stakes denial gets something right.

(5) Dana: I can be exactly that, because I don't have to say the high-stakes denial gets it right. The invariantist says it's an ordinary mistake. When Lena says "I don't know it's safe," she's really registering the true thought that she shouldn't act on this evidence, given what would happen if she's wrong. But the standard for action and the standard for knowledge are different. She can know the water is safe and still reasonably demand more evidence before certifying it, because certification is governed by a practical norm of caution. Knowledge doesn't have to do that practical work.

For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right (a short scale is fine) and briefly justify the rating, distinguishing what the move gets right from what it misses. Then say which distinction Dana and Ravi most need to keep clearly in view but keep running together, and explain what that conflation costs each of them.
