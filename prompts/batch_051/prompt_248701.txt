I recently stepped in as a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course in political philosophy, after the original TA had to take medical leave partway through the term. The material has become personally relevant to me as well: I am drafting a paper on interpersonal comparisons of well-being, and I keep running into the difference between comparative and non-comparative principles of distribution, so I want to be careful not to let my own reading of that distinction color how I grade. The midterm contained the following short-answer question: "In your own words, explain the leveling-down objection to egalitarianism, and explain how prioritarianism is supposed to avoid that objection. Is prioritarianism best understood as a version of egalitarianism?"

One student, who otherwise did well on the rest of the exam, wrote: "The leveling-down objection targets telic egalitarianism, the view that equality is good in itself. If equality is intrinsically good, then moving from an unequal distribution to a more equal one by lowering the better-off to the worse-off's level should count as an improvement in at least one respect, even though no one is made better off and at least one person is made worse off. Parfit raises this objection to show that we should reject egalitarianism and adopt prioritarianism instead. Prioritarianism says that the moral value of a benefit to a person is greater the worse off that person is in absolute terms, which is why it escapes the objection: leveling down harms the better-off and helps no one, so under prioritarianism it is never an improvement in any respect, because a benefit to no one has no positive value. Prioritarianism is therefore best understood as a sophisticated version of egalitarianism, since it still gives extra weight to benefits to the worse off and will tend to reduce inequality in practice."

I have to assign a numerical mark out of 10 and attach written comments that justify the mark. Could you assess this answer point by point? I want the comments to say what the student got right and to pinpoint any slips or conflations with enough precision that the student understands exactly what to revise; a general impression would not be useful to them.
