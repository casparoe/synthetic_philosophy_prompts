Everyone always runs the same sort of tragic-conflict case — choose which child to save, Sophie's Choice, that kind of thing — and it never settles anything because the two sides just talk past each other about whether the leftover distress is guilt at wrongdoing or just the cost of having done something awful-but-permissible. So I want a case built to isolate the tie itself.

Construct me a thought experiment in which an agent's obligations tie with exactly equal strength, arranged so that a defender of genuine moral dilemmas and a denier of them must give different answers to the question 'does she act wrongly whatever she does?' Constraints: realistic setting (a hospital, school or workplace), the scenario itself no longer than a paragraph, and the tie must not be stipulated from nowhere — it should be generated by the structure of the case, for instance by two promises of the same kind made to two people with identical claims, or by the same value being at stake twice. No trolleys, no Nazis, no choosing which child dies.

After the scenario, walk through what each view says and why: the dilemma-denier (I'm happy for you to take a Rossian line or any other, just say which one you're taking) and the dilemma-affirmer. Then identify the single feature of the case that forces the divergence, and say what would happen if you removed it. Then give the best reply available to each side: how the denier argues that the tie is only apparent, or that exact ties make both options permissable; and how the affirmer argues that the tie is precisely what makes the conflict genuine rather than a mistake of weighting. Finish by saying how confident you are in the set-up as a whole and which step you are least sure of.
