I'm teaching a unit that pairs King Lear with Jane English's friendship model of filial duties — the view that what grown children owe their parents is not a debt incurred through benefits received, to be repaid on demand, but something closer to the voluntary, mutual responsiveness characteristic of friendship, which may lapse if the relationship itself has been allowed to wither. I'd like a critical reading of the play through this model, one that treats neither the play as a mere illustration of the theory nor the theory as the final word on the play.

Start by making the conceptual machinery precise in your own words: the difference between obligations modeled on repayment and obligations modeled on friendship; why English thinks the debt model particularly misdescribes the relation between parents and adult children; and what her view implies about a parent who is aged, needy, or demanding. Then work through Lear with that machinery. Consider the scene in which Lear divides his kingdom by demanding public, quantified professions of love in exchange for shares of the realm: Goneril and Regan are fluent in exactly that currency, while Cordelia refuses to trade her affection at that exchange rate, and Lear treats her refusal as a failure to pay what he is owed. Does the play show the debt model as not merely politically reckless but morally and emotionally self-defeating — a way of converting filial love into an enforceable account, thereby guaranteeing its own disappointment? Where does the play complicate English's position rather than confirm it? For instance, Lear in his later condition is not an equal friend whom one might drop when affection has cooled; he is a vulnerable, dispossessed, increasingly unhinged old man, and his need seems to pull toward an obligation the voluntary-friendship model has trouble stating. Likewise, Cordelia's own sense of what she owes her father may be neither a debt to be discharged nor a friendship freely chosen. Carry the reading in both directions — where the theory illuminates the play, and where the play resists the theory — and end with what the play adds to the philosophical argument that the argument alone cannot.
