I've been running a reading group with a few grad students on formal epistemology, and we've been stuck for weeks on van Fraassen's Reflection principle. The loose idea is that your current credence in a proposition, conditional on your future self assigning it value r, should itself be r — you should defer to your expected future credences in roughly the way you'd defer to an expert. Two camps have formed. The Reflectionists think the principle is a genuine requirement of rationality or diachronic coherence, and that the standard counterexamples (anticipated memory loss, a drug that predictably misleads you, known cognitive decline) are only apparent because the principle is meant to apply to future selves who are at least as well informed and no less rational than you are. The anti-Reflectionists think that move guts the principle: once you restrict it that much, either its empty or its no longer doing any interesting work, and the unrestricted principle is just false because there are perfectly rational violations. The two camps keep talking passed each other, and I can't tell whether they have a real disagreement or just two different ideas of what "deferring to your future self" amounts to.
