A familiar pattern in contemporary anti-discrimination law protects certain grounds and not others. An employer may not refuse to hire someone because of her sex, religion, race, or disability, but may refuse to hire someone because of a regional accent, short stature, weight, a visible tattoo, a bankruptcy on her record, or her political opinions. The same legal order may let an employer refuse a smoker in order to control health costs, while not letting it refuse a woman because clients would prefer a male account manager. The protected grounds are also protected unevenly: a religious body may often prefer co-religionists for certain positions, and disability protection is generally conditional on the employer being able to adapt the job without disproportionate burden. What theory of suspect grounds does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit criterion or criteria—for example, that a ground is suspect if it is immutable, visible, historically subordinated, irrelevant to the task, or tied to group status rather than individual conduct. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the places where the pattern is underdetermined by any single criterion. Say, too, which rival accounts of wrongful discrimination—for instance, that it is wrong because it treats persons as mere members of a group, or because it entrenches a group's subordinate status—the pattern quietly rules out and which it leaves open. Then press the implicit theory against the excluded grounds: can a principled line be drawn between refusing someone because of sex or religion and refusing someone because of accent, weight, or political opinion, or does the practice secretly depend on an unstated ranking of which traits and interests matter? Finally, say what would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced by a general presumption against decisions that track unchosen attributes associated with systematic disadvantage.
