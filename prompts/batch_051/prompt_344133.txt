For a piece I am researching on how group decision procedures can fail to respect both individual autonomy and unanimous agreement, I worked through a toy reconstruction of the impossibility argument usually called the liberal paradox, associated with Amartya Sen, and I have reached a conclusion strong enough that I now suspect my reconstruction. The argument appears to show that no decision procedure can, at once, defer to unanimous preference and to each person's say over even one small private matter. I want to know which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs.

The setup. A group decision procedure is assumed to take any profile of complete, transitive individual rankings over a set of arrangements and return a complete, transitive group ranking. Two conditions are then imposed.

(1) Pareto. If every member strictly prefers arrangement a to arrangement b, the group strictly ranks a above b.

(2) Minimal decisiveness. For each member, there is at least one pair of arrangements differing only in matters private to that member over which the member is decisive: if the member strictly ranks a above b, the group strictly ranks a above b. Each person, that is, has final say over at least one corner of their own life.

Now consider two housemates, Ana and Bo, and three arrangements:

x: Ana wakes early to practice violin; Bo cooks fish curry.

y: Ana sleeps late; Bo cooks fish curry.

z: Ana sleeps late; Bo cooks pasta.

Ana is decisive over {x, y}, which differ only in her wake-up time; she prefers x to y, holding dinner fixed. By (2), the group ranks x above y. Bo is decisive over {y, z}, which differ only in what he cooks; he prefers y to z, holding Ana's schedule fixed. By (2), the group ranks y above z.

So far, nothing is strained. Now add two meddlesome preferences. Ana dislikes fish enough that she would rather sleep late and eat pasta than wake early to a fish dinner, and Bo finds the early violin unpleasant enough that he would rather give up fish curry for pasta if it buys a quiet morning. Both therefore strictly prefer z to x. By (1), the group ranks z above x.

But from x above y and y above z, transitivity of the group ranking gives x above z. The group therefore ranks z above x and x above z, which violates the asymmetry of a strict ranking.

The argument generalizes from two people and three arrangements: no procedure can satisfy (1), (2), and the background demand for a complete, transitive group ranking over all preference profiles. That seems to prove too much — it would condemn nearly every familiar procedure that claims to balance individual rights against shared efficiency. Which step should go, and what does giving it up cost? If I had to choose, I would rather keep the Pareto principle and transitivity than surrender decisiveness; but I want to know precisely what that preference commits me to, and what the main alternatives sacrifice.
