Consider two regress arguments and use them to work out a general criterion for deciding when an infinite regress is vicious.

The first is familiar from the theory of justification. Suppose a belief is justified only when it is supported by some further justified belief. Then any chain of justification must either terminate in a belief that is not itself justified, form a circle, or continue without end. Many philosophers have held that the endless option does not really confer justification: each link passes on an epistemic debt that is never discharged.

The second concerns relations. Suppose a relation is a genuine entity over and above its terms. One can then ask what makes it the case that this entity actually relates its terms rather than merely standing alongside them. If a further relation is introduced to bind the original relation to its terms, the same question arises again for that further relation, and so on. Many philosophers nevertheless regard this regress as benign, or as showing that the initial demand for a connecting entity was confused rather than that nothing is ever related.

Compare the two cases. Is there a single feature that explains why the first regress is often thought vicious while the second is often thought harmless? Or do the two cases differ in ways that resist a unified account? In answering, propose and defend a general test for viciousness, and apply it to both examples.
