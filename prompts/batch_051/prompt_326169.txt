For the past year and a half my climate policy reading group has been stuck on the same fight about the social discount rate. Half the group (mostly the economists) say you have to start from observed behaviour: people really do prefer jam today to jam next year, markets price future goods at a positive rate, so a policy model that discounts far-future climate damages at a couple percent a year is just being realistic. The other half (mostly the philosophy students) say that is a category mistake: the question is how we ought to weigh future wellbeing, and from an impartial point of view the mere fact that a benefit arrives later rather than sooner should not matter at all — a life in 2200 counts the same as a life today. Someone always gestures at Ramsey's line about the pure rate of time preference being indefensible and a product of weakness of the imagination; then someone brings up hyperbolic discounting in individuals; then someone else says hyperbolic discounting is a bias we correct in ourselves, not a normative input; then someone says the whole thing is mute because there is already an annual extinction risk that gives you a discount for free; and then the conversation dies. We have read Stern and Nordhaus and some Weitzman and a couple of surveys, and nobody has moved.

I am not asking you to settle the discount rate. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement we are actually having. Is it a covered-up factual question about how future generations' preferences would reveal themselves if they could? A dispute about whether the question is descriptive or normative to begin with? A bedrock difference about impartiality that no empirical fact could settle? Or are Stern and Nordhaus answering two different questions under the one word "discounting"? My hunch is that the normative/descriptive split is doing most of the work, and that "pure time preference" is being used for at least two different things, but I have been wrong before. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or should we just expect the impasse to be permanent?
