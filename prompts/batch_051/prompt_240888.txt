Give me a working definition of the term word — a criterion that tells us, for any relevant pair or sequence of forms, (a) whether we have one word or more than one, and (b) whether two forms count as forms of the same word rather than two different words. Test the definition against these cases, and tell me where it breaks or needs repair:

1. cat / cats. I take these to be forms of the same word, and each is uncontroversially one word.
2. go / went. I also take these to be the same word, even though no sound or letter is shared.
3. child / children. The same word, with an irregular plural.
4. sing / singer. I take these to be two different words.
5. greenhouse / green house. Two different words, and only the second is a phrase.
6. quick / quickly. I have no settled view. The -ly suffix is regular and transparent enough that it feels almost inflectional, yet it is usually classed as derivational, which would make quickly a different word.
7. happy / unhappy. I have no settled view here either. It could be a negated form of the same adjective, or a new adjective built from it.
8. good / better / best. I lean toward saying these are forms of one word, but the total absence of shared shape makes me less sure than I am about child / children.
9. don't. One word or two? It is written as one but is standardly treated as a contraction of two, and I do not know which criterion should win.
10. John's, in John's book and in the king of England's crown. Is the possessive 's a word at all? It seems to attach to a whole phrase rather than to a single noun.
11. un-freaking-believable. One word, and the same word as unbelievable? If I say yes, my criterion still needs to explain where the inserted freaking sits relative to the word's boundaries.
12. kick the bucket. Three written words, and I would not call the whole string one word, but it behaves like a single lexical unit whose meaning is not composed from kick, the, and bucket.

Propose the definition, run it through all twelve cases, and for each case say whether it gives the right verdict, the wrong verdict, or no determinate verdict. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts, and identify which of your final verdicts is least secure.
