Locke's Second Treatise of Government, chapter V, section 27, contains the following clause: "for this labour being the unquestionable property of the labourer, no man but he can have a right to what that is once joined to, at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others."

Consider a case Locke plainly did not anticipate. A launch company has begun placing small, inexpensive "beacon" satellites into geostationary orbit. Geostationary slots are a finite natural resource: a satellite in one stays fixed over a particular point on the equator, and two satellites cannot safely use the same slot and frequencies without interference. The company's beacons do almost nothing beyond holding position and broadcasting an identification signal. The company has now parked them in most of the highest-value slots serving the Americas and Europe, and it leases these positions to broadcasters, weather agencies, and internet providers. Its owners justify this by Locke's labour theory: they built the satellites, launched them, navigated them into the slots, and keep station there, and that labour joined to a previously unowned natural resource makes the slots theirs. Critics reply that this is exactly what the clause forbids, because the company has not left "enough, and as good" in common for others. Set aside the Outer Space Treaty and today's licensing regimes; the question is whether Locke's principle, read well, permits this acquisition.

Then give your view on which approach should control this case, and whether the proviso as written is adequate to this kind of resource or one whose limits the case exposes.
