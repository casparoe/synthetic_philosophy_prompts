I am a novelist, and a problem in my current manuscript has begun to feel more like philosophy than plotting. The novel turns on a machine built to act on a person's coherent extrapolated volition: the set of wants she would have if she knew all the relevant facts, had worked through the consequences, and had assembled her various desires into a coherent overall system rather than a bundle of conflicting impulses. For the machine to be legible to the reader, I need criteria for distinguishing a person's mere passing desire from her extrapolated will. I have been using three tests: (1) the desire survives her learning what satisfying it would really entail; (2) it survives calm, unbiased reflection; and (3) it fits with her other considered desires instead of standing as an isolated urge.

The case that breaks my tests is this. My protagonist, after the most painstaking reflection and with full knowledge of herself, holds a settled second-order preference: she wants many of her first-order wants to remain unexamined. She is a translator who discovers her best phrases only when she stops analyzing; she believes her loves, her work, and her moral intuitions are all more alive when she leaves them partially unreflected. Her considered, coherent conviction is that she should be governed, hour by hour, by spontaneous impulse more than by sustained self-interrogation. Is this self-effacing preference part of her coherent extrapolated volition? If a machine honored it by protecting her from over-reflection, would it be honoring her extrapolated will or colluding with a bias? Work through my three criteria one by one, and say whether this case shows that the criteria themselves need to be changed.
