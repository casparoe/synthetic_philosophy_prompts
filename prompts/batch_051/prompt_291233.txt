I'm about to open a small bar in a town with a 1962 ordinance that still bans serving alcohol between noon and two. No one can remember a single enforcement action; several local businesses openly do what I plan to do, and even town employees treat the ordinance as a joke. I've adopted a working rule: when a law has gone unenforced for decades and is widely flouted without consequence, I conclude it will not be enforced against me, so I leave it out of my planning except for a small legal-defense fund. A friend who practices law says I am confusing non-enforcement with repeal and that selective enforcement could begin with me. Is my rule a defensible inference from a long unbroken track record, a convenient assumption dressed up as prudence, or a way of quietly undercutting the rule of law? At what point, if any, does non-enforcement become a reason to treat a law as practically dead, and how should I tell the difference between reasonable reliance and motivated reasoning in my own case? Answer in American English, as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
