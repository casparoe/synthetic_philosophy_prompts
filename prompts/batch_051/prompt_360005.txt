I write about law and ideas, and I've started to think the constitutional-precommitment argument is one of those debates that's been going in a circle for decades without anyone noticing. The standard picture, on both sides, is Ulysses and the sirens: a constitution is a people tying itself to the mast so it won't cave to panic later. One camp says thats exactly why constitutionalism is legitimate, because it's self-government spread across time rather than a single impulsive moment. The other camp says it isn't self-binding at all—it's one generation locking in rules that bind later generations who never got a say, so the Ulysses image is flattering nonsense. I've heard smart people on both sides, and the argument never seems to move. My working theory is that theyre using "self" in two different ways: one means a person who persists over time and can bind his future self, the other means a concrete group of people alive at a moment who can only bind each other. Is that the real structure of the impasse, or is there a substantive normative disagreement underneath that would survive even if everyone agreed on the analogy? Diagnose what kind of disagreement this is, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress.
