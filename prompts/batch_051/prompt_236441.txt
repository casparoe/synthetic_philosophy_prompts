A draft international agreement on the governance of stratospheric aerosol injection contains two provisions. The first provides that no deployment may begin unless every party consents; any single party may veto a proposed start. The second provides that, once deployment has begun, no party may unilaterally withdraw, withhold its assessed contribution, or otherwise disable the injection system if doing so would likely bring about a termination shock, defined as a sharp additional warming exceeding a specified rate or magnitude. Suppose that twenty years into a deployment that has suppressed roughly one degree of warming, a newly elected government in one party repudiates the agreement and moves to halt the flights it has been funding. Do the two provisions conflict in this case? Give the strongest readings on which they do not conflict, distinguishing, for example, between consent to initiate deployment and a continuing obligation to maintain a system that has become a shared risk, or between the government that gave consent and a successor government that repudiates it. Consider also whether either reconciliation is open to a moral-hazard objection, for instance that a party may consent to deployment expecting that the lock-in clause will not really bind it, or that other parties may underinvest in mitigation because deployment, once begun, is hard to stop. Say which reconciliation, if any, the drafters could honestly have intended and which is special pleading, and say what the agreement should tell a state that never consented to deployment but would suffer from its abrupt halt.
