I keep noticing that the arguement about whether famines are "natural disasters" or "political crimes" never actually moves, even though both sides keep pointing to the same handful of well-known cases. The natural-cause side mentions the potato blight in Ireland or the droughts and crop failures during the Great Leap Forward, and says the immediate killer was biology and weather. The other side says that grain was still leaving Ireland while people starved, and that Mao's procurement and collectivization policies turned a bad harvest into a catastrophe; weather just set the stage. Sen's entitlement work complicates it further: in Bengal in 1943 food availability per person doesn't seem to have dropped nearly as much as the death toll would suggest, yet rural laborers starved anyway because they lost the ability to command food. That should've changed the terms of the debate, but as far as I can tell it mostly gave both sides new vocabulary. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one label: one is asking about the proximate trigger and the other about who could have prevented the deaths, with "natural disaster" doing double duty. Is that right? I want a diagnosis of why this disagreement is so sticky — is it verbal, is it empirical, is it a moral disagreement disguised as a causal one, or something else — and not a verdict on which side is correct. Then tell me what would actually count as progress given the diagnosis, even if the answer is that progress shouldn't be expected.
