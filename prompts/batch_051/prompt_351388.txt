I've been reading Bentham's Anarchical Fallacies, the piece where he tears into natural rights, and I've tried to turn the argument near the beginning—where he says that a substantive right is something created and protected only by law, and that rights supposedly generated by imaginary or metaphorical laws are a kind of monstrous counterfeit offspring—into numbered steps. Here's what I have:

(1) A right, properly so called, exists only when some actual law assigns and protects it.
(2) An actual law is a command issued by a determinate sovereign and backed by sanctions.
(3) What natural-rights thinkers call laws of nature are not commands issued by any determinate sovereign; they are, at best, inventions of poets and rhetoricians.
(4) Therefore any right said to arise from a law of nature is not a right properly so called, but an imaginary right.
(5) If natural rights are imaginary in this sense, then appeals to them in political argument are either confused or purely rhetorical, and we can set such appeals aside when deciding what actually ought to be done.

I can't tell if this is valid, sound, neither, or both. My hunch is that step (1) is doing the heavy lifting by definining "right" in a way that simply excludes natural rights from the start, so a natural rights person would just reject that premise. But maybe the argument is still valid and the real question is whether the premises are true. Which step, if any, fails? Is the argument valid but question-begging, or invalid, or actually sound? And if the argument fails, what's the strongest reconstruction of Bentham that doesn't cheat by definition?
