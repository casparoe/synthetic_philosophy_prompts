I have been trying to think through what Benjamin Libet's experiments on self-initiated movement actually show about free will, and I have compressed the line of thought into numbered steps. I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis: is the argument valid, and if it is not, which step fails first and how?

As I understand the setup, a subject watches a fast-moving dot on a clock face and is told to flex their wrist whenever they feel like it, noting the dot's position at the moment they first become aware of the urge. Electrodes over the motor areas record a slow buildup of electrical activity, the readiness potential, which begins roughly 550 milliseconds before the wrist flexes, while the reported moment of the urge comes only about 200 milliseconds before the flex.

The argument is this:

(1) In each trial the readiness potential, a brain process involved in producing the flex, begins before the subject is aware of any urge to flex.
(2) Becoming aware of the urge to flex is the same thing as consciously deciding to flex.
(3) A movement cannot be caused by a conscious decision that occurs after the movement has already begun.
(4) The beginning of the readiness potential is the beginning of the movement in the sense relevant to causation.
(5) Therefore the conscious decision occurs after the movement has already begun.
(6) Therefore the conscious decision does not cause the movement.
(7) There is no relevant difference between this wrist flex and an ordinary deliberate action when it comes to the causal role of the conscious decision.
(8) So if the conscious decision does not cause the flex, no conscious decision causes any deliberate action.
(9) Free will requires that conscious decisions cause at least some deliberate actions.
(10) Therefore there is no free will.

Two things I have come across make me unsure where the weakness lies, if there is one. The first is Libet's own suggestion that subjects could still consciously stop the flex in the final fraction of a second, so perhaps what is free is not the starting of an action but the vetoing of one. The second is the suggestion that the readiness potential may be less like a preconscious command and more like noise accumulating toward a threshold, so its early rise would not yet amount to the start of the movement. I cannot tell whether these are mere details or whether either of them disables a specific numbered step. Please say which step, if any, fails first, why it fails, and whether these two points bear on the diagnosis.
