I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I've been going back and forth over an argument a friend gave me that seems to show intergenerational national guilt is incoherent, and I'd like someone to tell me where it breaks or what it costs to resist it. I've been reading Jaspers on German guilt — specifically his distinction between criminal guilt and what he calls political guilt, which on my understanding attaches to every citizen of a state for what the state does, simply in virtue of membership, whether or not the individual did, wanted, or voted for any of it. I find that compelling because it seems to explain why a German born decades after 1945 can still stand in some special normative relation to the Holocaust, or why an American born after abolition can owe something with respect to slavery. But my friend turned it into an argument that seems to force me to give all of that up.

(1) If a nation can be guilty of a past wrong, then the guilt attaches to the nation as a persisting collective, not merely to the specific individuals who perpetrated it; otherwise 'national guilt' is just shorthand for a list of individual criminals.

(2) A nation persists through time: the Germany of today is, in some real sense, the same collective as the Germany of 1933–45, even though the membership has turned over almost completely.

(3) So if the nation is guilty, current members inherit that guilt simply qua members — the guilt of the collective transfers to each later member.

(4) But guilt, in the only strict sense, is blameworthiness; you can be guilty only for what you did, intended, authorized, or at least could have prevented.

(5) Later generations did none of those things with respect to the past wrong; they were not there, did not choose it, and cannot now prevent it.

(6) Therefore current members both are guilty (from 3) and cannot be guilty (from 4 and 5). Contradiction.

(7) So premise (1) is false: a nation cannot literally be guilty across generations. 'National guilt' is either metaphor, a disguised reparations claim, or a way of predicating blame of people becuase of an accident of birth — which is exactly the kind of thing we would call unjust in any other context.

I don't like (7). I think there is a real relation a contemporary German or American stands in to their country's past wrongs, something more than 'metaphor or reparations' but less than personal criminal guilt, but I can't find the step to reject without losing something I want. Rejecting (1) makes me a pure individualist; rejecting (4) makes guilt weirdly non-agential; rejecting (5) seems to blame people for unchosen circumstances. So: which step actually goes wrong, and what does rejecting it commit me to? And does Jaspers's category of political guilt survive the argument, or does it turn out to be something other than guilt in the strict sense?
