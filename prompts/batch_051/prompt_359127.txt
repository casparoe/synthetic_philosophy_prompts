I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, 16 students, one 80-minute session. Design a classroom game that makes participants feel the force of the two-bitser objection to the distinction between original and derived intentionality — the Dennettian thought that once a group tries to state what original intentionality has over derived intentionality, each proposed marker can be matched by a cheap copy, so the boundary looks like a stance or local policy rather than a deep natural difference. The game should be runnable without lecturing first; students should not hear the terms original intentionality, derived intentionality, or two-bitser until the debrief.

The core mechanism I have in mind, which you should develop or modify: students act as a certification tribunal that must sort a series of items into an “Original” box and a “Derived” box. One rotating student plays a two-bitser auditor whose role is to take anything the tribunal has placed in the Original box and produce a specific doppelgänger — a cheap device, a blind physical process, an accidental duplicate, or a functionally identical construction designed with a different purpose — and demand either (a) the copy also goes in the Original box, or (b) the tribunal states, in public terms, what extra feature the original has. The tribunal holds a fixed number of tokens for revising its criterion; each revision costs a token. Provide case cards ranked from uncontroversial human cases to thermostats, honeybee waggle dances, stop signs, a vending machine that prints thank-you notes, a copied brain, and a language model generating first-person reports. Include the auditor’s list of stock challenges and rules for what they may not say.

Specify roles, materials, a timeline with approximate minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened to Searle’s distinction and Dennett’s two-bitser. The debrief should not settle the issue by fiat; it should show the students which moves they made, which stopping places were conventions, and what it would cost to hold Searle’s line. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle those failures, including the group that solves it too quickly by defining original intentionality in biological or source terms, the group that refuses to amend its rule and accepts that everything becomes original, and the student who appeals to first-person phenomenology. Be explicit about the feature of the dispute this activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply after the game.
