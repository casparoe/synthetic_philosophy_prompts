I've spent the last year or so treating rationality like a martial art, and I'm starting to wonder if the whole thing is just fancy procrastination. My routine is: calibration drills (I guess 90% intervals on trivia and weather and check my hit rate), rewriting messy internet arguments into premises-and-conclusions to find the assumption the person didn't say out loud, a weekly steelman-the-view-I-cannot-stand session, and before any big decision a one-page pre-mortem where I pretend the decision already blew up and work backwards to what probably killed it. I've gotten decent at the drills. My calibration is usually close to honest, and I can clean up a bad argument fast. But in real life I don't see much transfer. I still get defensive when someone challenges me in the moment, I still fall in love with my own plans immediately after writing the pre-mortem, and my actual predictions about things that matter — like whether a freind will really follow through on something, or which of two projects I'll still care about in six months — are as bad as ever.

The policy I've fallen into is this: before any argument or important decision, I run the relevant drill first, as a non-negotiable. I formalize the other side, put numbers on my confidence, write the pre-mortem. I treat it like a martial artist doing forms before sparring. But a lot of the time it feels like a ritual I do to feel rigorous, and the conclusion comes out the same as my gut would have said anyway. So my question is whether this policy is actually defensible. What would need to be true for drills like these to transfer to live judgment and live arguments the way a kata or a positional drill is supposed to transfer to actual sparring? And if the transfer story doesn't hold, is there a version of my routine that trains the real skill rather than the drill — or should I just drop the whole thing and stop fooling myself?
