I cover science and ideas for a living, which mostly means deciding which experts to trust about things I can't check myself. Somewhere along the way I developed a rule and I'm no longer sure whether it's good epistemology or a bias with tenure. The rule: I weight a source's explanations by their hands-on exposure. Someone who can articulate a theory but has never done the relevent thing gets discounted, sometimes to zero, while a practicioner who does the thing but garbles the theory still gets high weight about what's actually going on. Last month I was reporting on why a particular forecasting model failed. The quant who had built similar models but not this one gave me a crisp causal story; the forecaster on the ground, who had been burned by the model's errors, gave a confused, metaphor-heavy account. I found myself trusting the confused forecaster over the articulate quant, and wrote the piece that way.

On reflection, the worry is that my rule just is the old knowing-how vs knowing-that distinction doing unlicensed work in my head: I'm treating propositional knowledge as cheap unless it's backed by ability, and treating ability as a license to ignore whether the person can actually represent what they know. Maybe the rule tracks something real – tacit knowledge, the kind that lives in your hands and won't come out in words, or the fact that a fluent explanation often doesn't survive contact with the system – and the intellectualist line that knowing-how is just a disguised kind of knowing-that is the overrated bit. Or maybe I've built a tool for downgrading people whose only sin is being able to explain.

Is this rule defensible? Be specific about the conditions under which it works and the conditions under which it fails, and whether there's a restated version that keeps what's good without the collateral damage. I don't need a reading list; I want a verdict on the policy.

Give me the verdict as a JSON object with these fields:
- "verdict": one of "defensible_as_stated", "indefensible_as_stated", "defensible_if_modified"
- "bottom_line": one sentence summarizing the verdict
- "what_the_rule_tracks": the real difference between sources, if any, that the rule is sensitive to
- "what_it_confuses": cases it gets wrong
- "restated_rule": the modified policy, or the original if no change is needed
- "when_restated_rule_fails": residual failure modes or boundary conditions
- "philosophical_commitment": which side of the knowing-how/knowing-that debate the rule quietly assumes and whether that assumption is plausible
