I teach an undergraduate transition-to-proof course for a mixed group of mathematics and philosophy majors, roughly twenty-four students, in a seventy-five-minute block. The recurring pattern that bothers me is this: students can audit an induction proof line by line, confirm that each step is licensed by the previous one, and reproduce the same template on near-identical problems, yet they cannot say why the theorem is true or predict whether the argument would survive a change in indexing, base case, or domain. Last term I tried to close this gap by lecturing about the difference and then showing them the picture proof myself; the insight evaporated as soon as they saw an unfamiliar variant on the exam.

Please design a single seventy-five-minute classroom activity that stages the distinction instead of asserting it. Build it around the claim that the sum of the first n positive odd integers is n squared. Give different groups different access to the theorem: one group receives a template-style induction proof and practice generating near-copy proofs, while another receives the gnomon dot-grid picture — each new odd number adds an L-shaped layer that turns an n-by-n square into an (n+1)-by-(n+1) square — and practice reasoning from the geometry. Then run a gamified round of variations, such as the sum of the first n even integers, the sum of n odd integers beginning at some odd number other than 1, and the sum of the first n odd multiples of 3, or similar perturbations, under a token or point scheme that rewards correct answers, correct justifications, and answers to a second-order question about whether the argument would still work under some specific further change. Arrange the incentives so that students themselves notice where each style transfers and where it breaks: the template group should feel the cost of having no mechanism to inspect, while the diagrammatic group should feel the cost of mistaking a suggestive picture for an argument. Include group instructions or roles, a concrete point scheme, handout or board materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the distinction between verifying that a proof is valid and grasping why the theorem holds. In the debrief, introduce the idea that understanding tends to reveal itself through competent answers to what-if-the-conditions-had-been-different questions, and say explicitly which aspect of understanding the activity cannot capture or produce and what I will need to supply myself. End with a trouble-shooting section: what is most likely to go wrong the first time I run it — for example, groups swapping proofs, or alert students guessing the pedagogical target and performing to it — and how I should handle each failure without turning the session back into a lecture.
