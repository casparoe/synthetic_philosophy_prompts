A decision theorist I know gives the following verdicts on preference puzzles.

In Quinn's self-torturer — a person is connected to a device with settings 0 through 1000; each move from n to n+1 brings a fixed payment and an increase in pain too small to discriminate, although setting 1000 is unambiguously much worse than setting 0 all things considered — she says that no individual advance, considered on its own, is irrational, but the person is not thereby committed to preferring setting 1000 to setting 0; she also says the person should reject the entire sequence when it is offered as a package.

In a strict money pump — a person strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, with the options saliently different from one another — she says the preferences are irrational.

In a control case — a chain of small but clearly noticeable improvements, each strictly preferred to its predecessor and ending in a state plainly better than the initial one — she says the person must prefer the final state to the initial state.

Next week's case is a self-torturer subject who, after running the sequence, reports a strict preference for each advanced setting over the one before it while also reporting a strict preference for setting 0 over setting 1000. Predict her verdict, say which principle she is implicitly running — for example, a transitivity requirement on strict preference combined with a discriminability constraint, or a two-level view on which local preference and global betterness may diverge — and specify the further case that would settle between the candidates.
