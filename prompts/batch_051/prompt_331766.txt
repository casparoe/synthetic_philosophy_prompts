I keep almost convincing myself of an argument about the end of physics, and I want someone to show me exactly where it breaks—or, if it does not break, which premise I should actually give up and what that costs. The argument started as a forum post and now looks too strong to be sound, which is usually a sign that I am committing a fallacy or that one of my plausible premises is doing more work than I thought.

Let T be a mature physical theory, accepted on the strength of a finite body of observations.

(1) To be justified in believing that T is the final theory, we would need our total evidence to favor T over every rival that says T will turn out wrong in some not-yet-observed way.
(2) Such rivals always exist. For any T and any finite set of data consistent with it, there is a rival T* that matches T on all data in hand and on every observation we can feasibly make, but departs in a regime we will never probe: at an energy scale beyond any planned experiment, after a time so far off that no observation will be made, or in a decimal place no measurement will ever reach.
(3) No finite body of evidence can discriminate between T and an open-ended family of such rivals.
(4) If evidence cannot discriminate between T and a rival, then evidence cannot justify accepting T over that rival.
(5) Therefore, no evidence can ever justify the belief that any current T is the final theory.

So far the conclusion is one I could live with. But the argument does not stop there.

(6) The same construction applies to ordinary theory-choice. For any accepted T there is a rival T** that fits all available data at least as well and diverges only beyond present precision, in an unprobed domain, or under conditions not yet tested. If those rivals cannot now be ruled out, then by (4) we are not now justified in accepting T. And when future evidence rules those out, yet further rivals will take their place. Iterating, we are never justified in accepting any empirical theory.
(7) That conclusion is absurd. The history of physics is a history of theories being rationally accepted, refined, and rejected, and the very idea of updating on evidence presupposes that evidence can warrant some theories over others.
(8) So something in (1)–(6) must be wrong—or at least one of its premises must be less cheap to hold than it first appears.

My own allegiances: I want to keep (7), and I would rather keep the anti-finality conclusion (5) than surrender ordinary theory-choice. My felt sense is that the parity between (5) and (6) is the weak spot—that a claim to a final theory has to rule out a different and much larger class of alternatives than a claim to ordinary empirical adequacy does—but I cannot make that asymmetry precise without appealing to considerations like simplicity or unification, and I do not know how to defend those as evidence-like rather than merely pragmatic. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Name the step, say why it looked fine, and spell out the alternatives I commit myself to once I reject it.

Also, please say what would change your mind: which consideration or counterexample would lead you to locate the flaw in a different step, and where your diagnosis is most likely to fail. If a Bayesian framework helps, I would like the diagnosis stated in those terms, but I do not require it.
