I teach a 90-minute seminar on Arendt's account of political lying to about fifteen undergrads. The idea I want them to *feel* rather than have me explain is the difference between a private one-off lie and what Arendt thinks is distinctive about lying in politics: it isn't mainly a false statement, it's the organized replacement of a shared factual record with images or stories shaped for an audience, and it works because factual truth is held together by testimony and agreement rather than proof, so once the record frays you can't just point back to the facts to settle the dispute. I'd like a 45-minute in-class exercise — some kind of reconstruction commission or joint public statement exercise with roles, cards, and modest incentives — in which the class starts by trying to establish what happened in a contested event and then, because of the roles and incentives, collectively slides into massaging the record until the shared timeline itself comes apart. Give me concrete rules, materials, and a debrief that maps moments in the exercise onto Arendt's idea — e.g., the moment a fact becomes a "matter of opinion," or the moment the group stops asking what happened and starts asking what will play well. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which element of her view the exercise can't reproduce and I'll have to supply with a short lecture.
