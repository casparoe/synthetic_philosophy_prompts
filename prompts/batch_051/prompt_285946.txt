I am teaching a graduate seminar on inferentialist theories of meaning, and I want to start with a clear specification of what any theory of meaning-as-inferential-role must accomplish before I set Brandom's version against its critics. The working picture I will use is this: the content of an assertoric sentence is its place in a network of inferences — which commitments one undertakes in asserting it, what would entitle one to assert it, what it is incompatible with, and what it licenses one to infer. The view gets its distinctiveness from the claim that at least some material inferences, not merely logically valid ones, are meaning-constitutive: that the inference from 'a is a dog' to 'a is a mammal' is to be treated not as collateral zoological belief but as part of what 'dog' means, in a sense the theory itself must explain. Logical vocabulary, on this picture, is not an extra topic but a device for making explicit the inferential articulations already implicit in ordinary practice. I am not asking whether this picture is right. I am asking what would count as an adequate version of it, where adequacy is judged against the job of explaining how assertoric language can have objective, shared content — how a claim can be assessed as correct or incorrect by members of a linguistic community, not merely by its utterer.

Write the desiderata. For each requirement, give the concrete problem or case that forces it — among others, I have in mind: that meanings be shared across speakers with different collateral commitments, so the view does not collapse into private idiolects; that observational vocabulary remain answerable to how things are rather than floating free in a closed web of sentences; that there be an account of subsentential, word-level meaning and not just whole-sentence roles; that the system face the compositionality worry, since a finitely graspable inferential machinery must generate meanings for unboundedly many novel sentences; that the line between meaning-constitutive and merely believed inferences be drawn on principle rather than ad hoc; and that truth and reference talk be either reconstructed or honestly explained away rather than ignored. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for any genuine inferentialist, which are hostile-camp demands dressed up as neutral constraints (I suspect classical compositionality and word-world reference are candidates here), and which are genuine but difficult for any such view. Then give me what I most want: which subsets, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single desideratum does the most work in excluding naive or popular versions of the view. A short scorecard of Brandom's own version against the list at the end is welcome, but keep the weight on the standards themselves.
