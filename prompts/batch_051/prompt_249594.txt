I'm a retired physical oceanographer and I spent fifteen years on the advisory board of a private foundation that funds basic science. Since retiring I've been reading Feyerabend, and his claim that there's no single scientific method — that anything goes, meaning no fixed set of rules captures how science actually progresses — keeps making me wince about how we used to triage proposals. We had an implicit picture of good method: clear hypothesis, pre-registered design, controls, falsifiable predictions, and anything else got quietly killed. I want something I can hand to my old colleagues: a reusable procedure for evaluting a proposal that doesn't look like textbook hypothesis-testing science. The panel meets three times a year, has about 25 minutes per proposal, and is full of mainstream scientists who get nervous when a proposal smells like it has no method at all. The procedure should be a checklist or decision tree they can actually run: what questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer, where the proposal should be rejected anyway because it really is just crankery, and where the panel has to admit the remaining call is a judgment about values or risk tolerance rather than method. Then run it on two cases: (1) a purely exploratory data-mining proposal on a large oceanographic dataset with no pre-registered hypothesis, and (2) a proposal for a long-term observational monitoring program with no intervention and no testable model, justified mainly as building a baseline for future science. Make one of the worked cases come out as fund-this-even-though-the-panel-would-normally-kill-it.
