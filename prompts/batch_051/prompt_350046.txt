I'm taking a seminar on pain and emotion this term, and a recent conversation with my uncle has unsettled a view I'd always taken for granted. Following a small stroke during heart surgery, my uncle now reports that he feels pain but isn't bothered by it. If he touches a hot pan, he can tell you exactly where the burning sensation is and how intense it feels, but he doesn't wince, doesn't pull his hand away, and has to remind himself to remove it; he describes the sensation as a sharp burning, then adds that it simply doesn't trouble him. Until now I would have thought this combination was impossible: I assumed the awfulness of pain is built into the sensation itself, so that feeling a pain just is being in a state that hurts. Now I'm not sure what to say. Two friends in my seminar have split on it. One says this shows pain's badness is a separate affective response that is normally fused with the sensory component and can in principle come apart from it. The other says my uncle isn't actually feeling pain at all — he is receiving a kind of neutral bodily signal, and since pain by definition includes the unpleasantness, it would be a misuse of the word to call his state pain. I don't want to dismiss my uncle's self-report out of a commitment to a definition, but I also don't want to abandon the thought that pain is essentially bad just because one clinical case complicates it. How should I think through this?
