In Quine's argument for the indeterminacy of translation, a field linguist is imagined trying to translate a previously uncontacted language on the basis of nothing but observable behavior: which sounds speakers produce in which stimulus situations, and how they assent or dissent under questioning. Quine's conclusion is that such evidence cannot, even in principle, decide between rival translation manuals — an utterance of gavagai in the presence of a rabbit might be rendered as a rabbit, as undetached rabbit parts, as a temporal stage of a rabbit, and so on — and that there is no fact of the matter about which rendering is correct. The engine of the argument is the claim that translation must be justified by behavioral evidence alone, and that behavioral evidence underdetermines any analytic hypothesis about how to segment the world into objects, properties, and events.

Suppose now that the translator has access to what a modern machine translation group has: an enormous parallel corpus of texts produced or accepted by bilingual speakers, and a neural model trained simply to predict, from such data, what a fluent speaker would say in the target language. The system is never given an ontology, a lexicon with compositional semantics, or rules for how to divide experience into objects and properties; it nonetheless produces translations that competent bilinguals tend to judge uniquely adequate, even in cases where alternatives like the rabbit-versus-undetached-rabbit-parts renditions would seem to diverge. Does the success of such systems undercut Quine's argument, leave it essentially untouched, or force a repair of some premise while conceding the rest? I am interested in which specific premise is under the most pressure — the evidential base, the underdetermination claim, the no-fact-of-the-matter conclusion, or something else — and whether a weaker Quinean claim survives in a form still worth keeping.
