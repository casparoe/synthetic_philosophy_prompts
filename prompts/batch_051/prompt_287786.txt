I am a PhD student in the history of economic thought, and I am preparing a seminar session on the monetary essays in Hume’s Political Discourses. I would like you to give a quantitative assessment of the surviving analytical value of five Humean claims about money and trade. For each claim below, assign a score from 0 to 1, where 0 means the claim should now be treated purely as an antiquarian view and 1 means it remains a basically sound explanatory principle for contemporary monetary or trade phenomena. Then rank the five claims from most to least defensible, and explain the basis of your ordering.

The five claims are:

1. Money is not, properly speaking, the substance of trade but only an instrument that lubricates exchange; within a single economy, the absolute quantity of money is of little consequence once prices have adjusted.
2. An increase in the quantity of money has no lasting effect on domestic real magnitudes; its main secular effect is to raise prices, while its main advantage to the state is felt in foreign wars and negotiations.
3. Fears that a thriving nation will lose all its gold and silver are mostly groundless, because an outflow of money lowers domestic prices relative to foreign prices, which restores the balance through trade.
4. The prosperity of one nation does not generally undermine the prosperity of its trading partners; the growth of a neighboring state tends to widen one’s own opportunities for commerce and industry.
5. High or low interest is not determined primarily by the quantity of gold and silver but by the level of commercial profits together with the abundance or scarcity of loanable capital.

Please commit to definite scores and a definite ranking rather than hedging by saying each claim has some truth. I also want you to identify which of these, if any, should be demoted to a purely historical role in an economics syllabus, and which one you would place first as still doing active explanatory work.
