I teach a final-year undergraduate philosophy of science seminar devoted to the question of whether physics could have a final theory and, if there were one, whether we could ever know we had reached it. The group is sixteen students and we meet for 90 minutes. I want a session in which students feel the problem rather than hear it described: the gap between a theory that has fit every observation made so far and a theory that is in fact the final rule governing a domain, together with the difficulty of telling, from inside a finite history of testing, which situation one is in. I would like this done as an in-class game built around a hidden rule. The facilitator secretly commits to a rule that governs some small toy universe (sequences of symbols, responses to interrogable "experiments," or something similar), the class divides into research teams, and the teams may run experiments, propose laws, and score points by successful predictions. The heart of the game should be a moment where a team can declare that it has found the final theory and stop the game; the design should make that moment genuinely tempting and genuinely unsafe. I would like the hidden rule set up so that at least two inequivalent rules agree on everything observed so far, or so that the true rule can be extended in ways the available data cannot distinguish, so that a confident declaration can be rewarded by the scoring and later defeated by a cheap new experiment. Teams should pay something for experiments so that over-testing has a cost, and the scoring should work without real money.

I need a runnable plan, not an essay: materials, rules, a 90-minute breakdown, the scoring scheme, instructions for how the facilitator administers the hidden rule without dropping hints, and a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time (a group gaming the scoring, a student guessing the trick early, the rule leaking, teams stalling rather than declaring finality). Please keep the concepts out of the setup entirely; students should not hear "underdetermination" or "empirical equivalence" until afterwards. The debrief should map the game's moments onto a few distinctions: being adequate to all data collected so far, being empirically equivalent to the true rule over all possible outcomes of the game's allowed experiments, and being identical to the hidden rule; it should also connect the false-confidence moment to the question of whether the final theory of physics would announce itself. Finally, be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce — a discrete rule-guessing game is only a partial model of real physical theorizing, which is constrained by mathematics, unification, and independent lines of evidence — and say which part of the philosophical question the game might accidentally rig.
