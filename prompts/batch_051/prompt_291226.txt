I have been defending a version of the expanding circle account of moral progress in my philosophy of history seminar, partly on the strength of William Edward Hartpole Lecky's observation in his History of European Morals from Augustus to Charlemagne (1869). Lecky claims there that one can trace a long historical movement in which the sphere of beings toward whom people feel moral duties has widened: first confined to the family or clan, then extended to class, then to nation, and in his own day beginning to embrace all of humanity. I take Peter Singer's later formulation to be a generalization of Lecky's, with the circle now potentially including sentient animals. I want to hold this view honestly, not as a Whiggish article of faith. What would count against it? I'd like a map of the defeaters, sorted by how decisive they would be: which historical episodes, which cross-cultural cases, which results from moral psychology or evolutionary anthropology should move me, and which would merely qualify the view? I particularly want to know whether the claim that the circle has expanded is falsifiable at all, or whether its defenders routinely reinterpret every apparent regression — the collapse of Roman cosmopolitanism, the Crusades, colonial slavery, the twentieth century's organized slaughter — as local turbulence within an overarching trend. Which of these apparent regressions should a committed expanding-circle theorist concede as genuine counterevidence, and which would they dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the view's reliance on a hidden standard of moral progress is a deep problem or a feature.
