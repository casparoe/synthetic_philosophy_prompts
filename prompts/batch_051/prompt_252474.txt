I'm taking a course on Aristotle's four causes and the fate of teleological explanation, and the slogan "nature does nothing in vain" keeps appearing in my notes with more confidence than precision. My lecturer uses it as a one-line summary of the final cause in Aristotle's natural philosophy; a week later he invoked it to argue that Darwinian functions are a descendant of Aristotle's teleology rather than its abolition; and a visiting philosopher of biology used it to state an adaptationist principle about traits being optimally fitted to their functions. To complicate matters, the seminar texts sometimes restrict the claim to what is possible given the materials available, and sometimes leave it unqualified, which makes me suspect several distinct theses are being passed under one short sentence. Could you lay out the precise claims that "nature does nothing in vain" could be expressing — from something like a harmless heuristic about looking for functions to a substantive cosmic teleology about nature as a whole — and give a verdict for each: trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or empirically vacuous? I would also like to know which reading each of the three uses above actually needs, and whether Aristotle is best read as asserting something narrower than the unqualified English sentence suggests.
