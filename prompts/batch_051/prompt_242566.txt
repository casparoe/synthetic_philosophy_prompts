I have been trying to get clear about a recurring disagreement over what it takes to be complicit in a wrong. We agree on the easy cases: someone who both knows about a wrongdoing and makes a causal contribution to it is complicit. We disagree about the marginal cases. My colleague defends a causal account on which causal contribution is necessary for complicity and, other things equal, sufficient for it: a person can be complicit in virtue of being a causally necessary link in the machine even if they neither know about nor endorse the wrong, while someone who tries to help or sides with the wrong but makes no difference is not complicit. I defend a participatory account on which what matters is knowingly taking part in, advancing, or aligning oneself with the wrong: an ineffectual but willing participant is complicit, while a causally necessary cog who works the machinery without awareness of the wrong is not. Our examples keep conflating the two variables, and neither of us can produce a case that cleanly separates them.

I would like you to construct a single thought experiment whose purpose is to isolate which of those two features is doing the intuitive work, and to make the two accounts come apart in both directions. Use a realistic setting — a workplace, municipal agency, or similar institution, nothing violent or science-fictional — and a single wrongdoing involving two agents. The scenario should be no longer than one paragraph, and it should not stipulate the verdicts: just describe what each agent did, knew, and intended, and what causally mattered to the wrong. Then do the check. State for each agent what the causal account says and why, and what the participatory account says and why. Identify the single feature of the case doing the work, say what would happen if that feature were removed, and finish with the strongest attack a defender of each account could make on the verdict that goes against them.
