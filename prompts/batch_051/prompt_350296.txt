I keep going back and forth on an argument I've been reconstructing about the Anthropocene. Here it is, numbered:

(1) If there's a fact about when the Anthropocene began, then exactly one of the candidate boundary markers is the right one — the late-Pleistocene megafauna extinctions, the spread of agriculture with its pollen and soil changes, the Industrial Revolution's carbon spike, or the mid-twentieth-century plutonium layer from nuclear tests.
(2) All of those are real, global human fingerprints in the rock record, but they pull the start date to very different times, and each one is pushed by a different field for its own reasons.
(3) There's no way to show that any one of them is the correct boundary rather than just the one our discipline happens to find most significant.
(4) If there's no way to show which candidate is correct, then there's no fact about which candidate is correct.
(5) So there's no fact about when the Anthropocene began.
(6) So as far as the rocks are concerned there's nothing to discover; any start date is a choice about which human impact to count as the beginning, and the real question is who gets to make that choice.

I'm torn: half of me buys it and half of me is sure it goes wrong, probably at (4). It sounds like a slide from saying we can't tell which marker is right to saying there's no fact about which marker is right. But I can also hear a version of (4) that's a real metaphysical principle: maybe for historical kinds like epochs, what it is to be the correct boundary just is to be picked out by the relevant scientific practice, and then there's nothing more to be right about. I can't tell which reading is the stronger one.
