Every other week I facilitate a discussion group at my local library. It is a dozen or so adults, none of us trained in biology or philosophy, and we have an hour and a half. Recently I have been working through something on my own that I cannot quite settle: natural selection is usually described as acting on genes or on individual organisms, yet there is a serious line of thought that it can also act on whole groups. The case that bothers me most is a trait or behavior that is costly to the individual who has it but makes groups that contain many such individuals outcompete groups with fewer of them, so the trait spreads even though any single carrier would have been better off without it. I can put that argument on a whiteboard, but I can tell from the room that it feels like bookkeeping rather than something real, and I do not think a lecture will fix that.

Design a game or simulation for this group, playable with ordinary materials like tokens, index cards, or paper, with a payoff scheme that does not use real money, all fitting inside the ninety minutes with time left for debrief. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants first experience the tension from the inside: an advantage at the level of the individual player pulling one way, an advantage at the level of a team or the whole group pulling another, and nobody names the point until it has already happened. In the debrief, map what occurred in the game onto the distinctions among gene-level, organism-level, and group-level advantage, and be careful to separate the weak idea that groups can differ in their average behavior from the stronger idea that those group differences themselves determine which groups send more members into the next round. Also say plainly what the game cannot reproduce about the real biological debate and what I should supply afterward in discussion. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, clever players bending the rules, the game collapsing into one behavior too early, or the takeaway coming out backwards — and how to handle each without turning into a lecturer.
