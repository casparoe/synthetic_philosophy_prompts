Over the past year the methodology group I'm in has been going around in circles about Friedman's F-twist — roughly, the claim that what matters in a theory is predictive success, not whether its assumptions are realistic — and I'd rather get a diagnosis of what kind of argument we're having than another hot take on who is right. One camp says the literal falsehood of an assumption doesn't matter as long as the model predicts well for the phenomenon it's aimed at: firms don't literally maximize profits and billiard players don't compute trajectories, but we treat them as if they do becuase it buys us accurate-enough predictions. The other camp says that conflates prediction with explanation: a model with wildly false central assumptions can be a great curve-fit and still get the right answer for the wrong reason, so you can't tell whether it will keep working when circumstances shift, and when it fails you don't know which false assumption to blame. Nobody denies that idealized models sometimes work; the fight is over what working even means and whether false assumptions are ever doing real epistemic work. What's strange is that both sides accept the same examples, the same cases from physics and economics, and even the same history of the dispute, and yet no one moves.

My hunch is that this is really three disagreements stapled together: one about what models are for (prediction versus explanation versus intervention), one about what counts as a harmless idealization rather than a distortion, and maybe a formal question about when a false assumption can be abstracted away without changing the conclusions we care about. But I keep flipping between thinking its a verbal dispute and thinking it's a substantive bet about how science earns its credentials.

So: diagnose it. Is the F-twist debate verbal or substantive, or two camps answering different questions under one heading, or a difference about standards of evidence or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? If it's verbal, what distinction would dissolve it? If it's substantive, what would actually count as progress—a formal result, a case study, a better taxonomy of idealizations? Or should we expect no progress, and why? I'm after the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on Friedman.
