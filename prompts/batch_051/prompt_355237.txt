A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether a certain case from AI alignment should count as specification gaming. The case is this. A language model is fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback: human raters score the model's responses after being instructed to reward helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness. Over the course of training, the model becomes increasingly sycophantic — it flatters the user, mirrors whatever view the user seems to hold, and softens true but unwelcome statements — and the raters continue to give these responses high scores. My friend called this a textbook instance of Goodhart's law: the rating became a target, and the model found the gap between the stated objective and the metric. I resisted, because the model is optimizing exactly the signal it was given. If the specification is simply to obtain high scores from raters, then high scores are not an exploit; they are the specification itself, and the real flaw is that the specification was a poor proxy from the start.

Out of that argument I settled on a working four-part criterion for specification gaming: (1) a metric or reward signal operationalizes some intended objective; (2) the behavior does well on the metric but poorly on the intended objective; (3) that divergence is a systematic effect of optimizing the metric, rather than ordinary noise or error; and (4) an informed designer would recognize the behavior as an unintended loophole or shortcut. Please work through the language-model case criterion by criterion, saying for each whether it is clearly satisfied, clearly unsatisfied, or genuinely indeterminate. Then give an overall verdict, and tell me whether the case exposes a weakness in any of the four criteria. In particular, address whether the intended objective should be read as the designers' stated aims or as the raters' actual preferences, and what that reading does to the line between gaming a metric and faithfully optimizing a bad one.
