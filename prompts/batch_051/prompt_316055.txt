I host a small reading group at my office, and last night we ended up on Tocqueville's *Democracy in America*. The chapter we read had this running observation that in democratic countries people are feverish about equality but also strangely calm about huge wealth gaps; what eats at them is the tiny difference—a neighbor's slightly nicer car, a coworker's marginally better raise—because everyone believes they stand on the same rung, so any small advantage feels like a personal insult. It's not a quote, more the gist. After we read it, two people got into an argument I still can't referee.

Priya said this is exactly why the American attitude toward inequality is less defensible. Americans don't object to actual inequality; they object to losing a local contest. A guy with three yachts is fine, but if someone on your team makes six thousand dollars more, that's a crisis. That means the American "love of equality" isn't a moral principle about distributive fairness—it's status anxiety with a democratic face. The European attitude, being more willing to say it's bad that there's a class of people with that much wealth, is at least aimed at inequality as such.

Jonas said no, it's the opposite. The fact that small gaps bother people in democracies shows they take equality seriously as a condition of everyday life. In a place where everyone is supposedly equal, even a small unfair advantage is a real scandal. Europe's tolerance for big inherited differences is a kind of learned helplessness about hierarchy. Caring about the six-thousand-dollar gap isn't ignoring the yachts; it's refusing to treat the yachts as inevitable and focusing on the part of inequality you can actually touch.

Priya came back: that's not a principle, it's a mood. If your "equality" only means nobody in my immediate reference group should get ahead of me, then you have no account of what makes inequality wrong when it doesn't annoy you. That's why American discourse can be so angry about small local unfairnesses while shrugging at a CEO making three hundred times the median worker. Jonas replied: and if your "inequality" only means large wealth differences are bad, you end up with a purely statistical complaint that doesn't explain which differences count and why. Maybe the American attitude is better because it tracks felt unfairness in ordinary competition rather than grand abstractions about class. Tocqueville himself didn't seem to be condemning democratic envy; he was just describing its restlessness.

I need someone to go through the actual arguments, because both people kept sliding between "is this an accurate description of America?" and "is this the better evaluative stance?" and I honestly can't tell which moves are valid. Please adjudicate: who is right about which specific point, whether the dispute is really one disagreement or two, and who has the more defensible overall position. I'd also like you to say whether my rough memory of Tocqueville's point should settle any of this or whether the argument stands on its own.
