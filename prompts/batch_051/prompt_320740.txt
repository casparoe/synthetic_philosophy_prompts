I keep going back and forth on a case and I'd like my criteria stress-tested rather than the case just filed under a label. I'm pulling together a short position piece for a small software preservation group on when a codebase should be treated as orphaned rather than merely unmaintained.

Here are the criteria I've been using. A codebase counts as orphaned when:
(a) after a genuine good-faith effort you cannot identify, or cannot get a response from, the person or institution who holds the copyright;
(b) there has been no commit, release, or maintainer statement in the canonical copy for at least five years;
(c) no living person holds themselves out as able to grant or deny permission to use, modify, or redistribute the thing;
(d) it still has real users, so the permission question is live rather than academic;
(e) its lisence status is murky enough that a careful organisation would normally have to ask someone before relying on it.

Now the case. There's a small parsing toolkit called Skelf, written in the mid-2000s by a single maintainer and released under GPL version 2, at least according to the original readme. The maintainer died in 2011 without leaving any note about the code and without appointing a successor. His personal site, which was the canonical repo, went dark soon after. Three contributors had patches accepted over the years. One of them, call him Tom, is still around: he answers the occasional question on a forum thread and will tell people how to patch around a bug, but he has said several times that he doesn't own the project and can't speak for the estate. The other two contributors used long-dead handles and are untraceable. The maintainer may have written the first version while he was a research assistant at a university, which could mean the university has some claim to a share of the rights; repeated enquiries to the university have gone unanswered. The maintainer's brother, who is presumably the main heir, answered one polite email saying he knows nothing about computers and doesn't know what permissions his brother gave anyone, then didn't respond to follow-ups. There's an unofficial GitHub mirror made seven years ago by someone who just didn't want the code to vanish; the mirror has open bug reports and a couple of draft pull requests but no commits, and the mirror's owner says he can't give anyone permission for anything.

The same question keeps coming up from hobby projects and two small companies that still vendor an old copy: can we keep using this, can we absorb it into our own project, can the licence be changed? Nobody can give a straight answer. On my criteria the thing seems to wobble: there's plainly no maintainer activity, and the obvious rightsholder is dead, but there are identifiable living contributors and possibly an institutional rightsholder, so (a) and (c) only partly hold. Tom's forum answers are a kind of activity but not maintainer activity, so (b) depends on what counts as the canonical copy and as a maintainer statement. And (e) is odd because there is an express GPL grant on the face of it, except its scope might be clouded by the university's possible share.

Is Skelf orphaned on these criteria? Work through criterion by criterion, flag where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say which criteria the case shows should be amended or dropped. Write the answer in British English.
