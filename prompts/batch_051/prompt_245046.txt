A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about aesthetic testimony — whether you can legitimately take on an aesthetic judgment, say that a film is good or a dish is sublime, from someone else's say-so. She argued that a trusted critic's word is a reasonable starting point: you can rationally hold the belief, even if you haven't yet made the judgment your own. I took the harder line that aesthetic judgment cannot be transmitted at all, because it is constituted by one's own felt response; secondhand, it is just information about the critic's response, not a judgment of the work. We kept circling because neither of us could say exactly what state is acquired when testimony changes your expectations without changing your taste.

I want to keep thinking about this through Ratatouille. Read the film through this debate. It is a movie about a critic whose verdicts determine what the dining public takes to be excellent, about a rat whose extraordinary palate cannot be shared except through what he cooks, and about a final review that turns on a private childhood memory. Where does the film stand on whether aesthetic judgment can be passed from one person to another? Does it treat Anton Ego's published reviews as genuine aesthetic communication, or as something more like reputation, influence, and power? What does Remy's situation — his private taste, his need to work through a human stand-in, the public's misattribution of the food — show about the gap between private experience and public judgment? Which scenes would you point to, and where does the film resist a clean philosophical reading?

I would like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical debate seriously, rather than reducing either to an illustration, and that ends by saying what the film adds to the argument that the philosophy alone does not. Also, please say where your reading is most likely to fail, and which consideration — about the film or about the nature of aesthetic judgment — would most plausibly change your verdict about where the film stands.
