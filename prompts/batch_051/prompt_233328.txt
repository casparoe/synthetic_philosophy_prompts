I'm putting together a two-page handout for an undergrad seminar on the philosophy of probability, and I want a "which view of single-case probability do you actually hold?" quiz. The angle is that the reference class problem isn't just a statistical nuisance: how you pick the class for a one-off case lines up with what you think probability fundamentally is. Eight questions, each a concrete case with 2–4 answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto five leaves: strict frequentism (probability is only defined relative to a reference class, and choosing the class is a pragmatic decision or "posit," not a fact about the world), subjective Bayesianism (probabilities are degrees of belief; there's no correct class, just priors and updating on your total evidence), objective Bayesianism (the total evidence determines a unique rational probability, so the problem dissolves if you condition on everything instead of lumping into a class), propensity or causal-explanatory views (single-case probabilities are real, fixed by the chance setup or the causal factors in the situation), and pluralism about risk (there are several legitimate probabilities for the same event, each relative to a different purpose or question).

The quiz should sort people by what they say about cases, not by labels. Don't ask "do you think probabilities are degrees of belief?" Use cases where the positions come apart. Cases I want included: (1) a 54-year-old man newly diagnosed with early-stage prostate cancer being told his 10-year mortality rate — once you add his exact lab values, comorbidities, and genome you get a class of one, so which class should the doctor quote, if any? (2) an insurer pricing life insurance for a woman who falls in an actuarial cell with only three past policyholders, and the regulator requires a "statistically credible" class — does a class of three have a probability, and if not, what do you do instead? (3) a space agency assigning a failure probability to the first launch of a new launch vehicle, which is a class of one but has many loosely similar past systems. And (4) a fair-looking coin about to be tossed once — is "the probability of heads" a fact about this coin, a summary of a class of tosses, or just a degree of belief?

For each question, give options that map cleanly onto at least two or three of the leaves, and make the scoring key handle ties. Include a short "unoccupied or unstable combinations" note at the end: which answer patterns have no leaf, and whether those combinations are incoherent, merely unattractive, or could be occupied by a position nobody happens to defend. Also flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually seperate.
