I can't settle something about Abelard. His whole line is that the sin sits in the consent, not in the doing — the external act adds nothing, so someone who fully decides to kill but gets interrupted on the way is, in his view, just as guilty as someone who kills. But that psychology of sin seems to assume that behind a wrongful act there's normally a moment where the person registers that the act is wrong and consents to it anyway. Milgram, the Stanford prison thing, and the wider situationist/automaticity literature push the other way: a lot of real harm gets done by people following scripts and conforming, with the clear intention maybe filled in afterward. Does Abelard's argument survive that, or does it need a repair? I'd like you to say which premise is actually under pressure — that there is always a consent to be found, or that consent is what carries the guilt when there is one — and whether there's a weaker rebuilt version, like sin is in consent or in the culpable failure to notice what your doing, that still keeps something core. And if no repair really works, tell me what should replace the consent story.
