For a while I've been trying to follow a dispute about rule systems that can amend themselves, and it seems stuck in a way I can't quite explain. The standard case is this: a system has a rule R stating the only procedure by which any of its rules, including R itself, may be changed. One side treats using R to change R as a genuine paradox: the amendment gets its authorization from the very norm it replaces, so the new rule appears valid only if the old rule remains in force, yet the amendment succeeds only by extinguishing that old rule. The other side thinks this is no more paradoxical than a legislature voting, under its own standing orders, to change those standing orders for future business: an act can be authorized by a norm at the moment of the act even though the norm is gone afterward, and the talk of paradox comes from confusing validity-over-time with authorization-at-a-time. The argument recurs in discussions of games like Nomic and in debates about constitutional clauses that attempt to make something unamendable, and I have not seen anyone change position. My working hypothesis is that the two sides answer different questions under one heading: one is asking whether the amended system can be derived from the old one, the other is asking what an actor may permissibly do. Diagnose what this disagreement actually is — substantive, verbal, a clash of starting intuitions, or an ill-posed question — and say what, if anything, would count as progress rather than another round of the same moves. Please also state how confident you are in your diagnosis and identify the point you are least sure of.
