I'm on a working group that advises whether the next edition of our diagnostic manual should include a new category. The current fight is about whether to add complex post-traumatic stress disorder (C-PTSD) as a free-standing diagnosis, rather than folding it into PTSD as a specifier or subtype. Some members say a separate category would help people get the right treatment and signal that their difficulties aren't just 'PTSD but a bit different'; others say it would reify a fuzzy boundary and medicalise normal responses to sustained trauma. I keep coming back to Hacking's point about looping effects: when you classify people, the people classified take up the label, reshape their self-understanding around it, and start acting and presenting in ways that change the meaning of the category, which then feeds back into the classification itself. I'm sure this matters for our vote, but I cant articulate how it should actually change what we do.

Could you draft a short policy memo for the working group that (a) explains how looping effects should bear on a decision like this, (b) gives a clear recommendation among adopt, reject, and adopt-with-safeguards (and if you pick safeguards, say what they should look like), and (c) gives a fair account of the strongest argument for the option you don't recommend and why it shouldn't win. Please write in British English. End the memo with a final line of the form \answer{adopt}, \answer{reject}, or \answer{adopt-with-safeguards}, whichever matches your recommendation, so we can log the bottom line.
