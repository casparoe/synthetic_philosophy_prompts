A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about South Africa's post-apartheid Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and by the end I suspected we were disagreeing about something neither of us had actually articulated. The practice that unsettled us is this: the Commission ran an amnesty process in which perpetrators of gross human rights violations — killings, torture, abductions — could receive individual amnesty if they made a full disclosure of their acts and showed that the acts were committed in pursuit of a political objective. These same acts remained crimes under the ordinary law, and the process did not require applicants to express remorse or to have been morally justified. So one institution simultaneously treated certain acts as grave wrongs and as eligible to be set aside in exchange for truthful testimony. I want to know what philosophical commitments that practice presupposes. Please reconstruct the implicit theory of dirty hands, responsibility, and punishment, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with. In particular: does the practice presuppose that the perpetrators did wrong and that the wrong persists as a kind of moral remainder even after amnesty, or is it compatible with the view that political violence in such contexts falls outside ordinary moral categories? Does it presuppose that the wrongness of these acts lay partly in their concealment and official denial, so that disclosure is not just evidence but a partial remedy? And what would have to change in the institution if a retributivist were right that serious wrongs must be punished regardless of social consequences? Finally, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories of political wrongdoing could each, on their own terms, license it?
