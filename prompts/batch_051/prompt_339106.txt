You are a decision theorist who works on the foundations of rational choice. I keep seeing Newcomb's problem litigated one case at a time — someone points to the opaque box, someone else to the smoking lesion, a third to a transparent version — and I want the full specification before I take any verdict seriously. Give me the requirements list for an adequate normative theory of rational choice under uncertainty: the decision problems it has to get right (at minimum Newcomb's problem; a transparent version where you can see the box's contents before choosing; the smoking-lesion case where a gene causes both the craving to smoke and the cancer, with no causal link from smoking to cancer; and at least one case where another agent is certain to mirror your choice), the distinctions it cannot collapse (causal vs. evidential relevance, good news vs. good choice, transparency vs. opacity, one-shot vs. iterated), and the principles it must respect or explicitly qualify (dominance, expected utility, and any sure-thing-style reasoning it wants to keep). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is negotiable and what would have to be true for it to be dropped. Then say which single requirement does the most to exclude the main contenders — evidential and causal decision theory — and whether the full set of requirements is jointly satisfiable; if it is not, identify the tightest conflict. A table is fine, but the motivation of each requirement matters more than the scoring. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming candidate cases and principles before you commit to the list.
