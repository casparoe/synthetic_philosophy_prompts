I teach an upper-level seminar on the moral emotions, and we have been working through what gratitude demands and what it does not. A colleague whose views my students keep bringing to class holds that the phrase "debt of gratitude" should be read as a structural claim rather than a dead metaphor. On his account, a benefit creates in the beneficiary a liability essentially like a financial debt: it has a creditor; it arises only when the creditor intentionally conferred something at some cost to herself; it can be called in by the creditor; and it is extinguished once the beneficiary repays in the appropriate currency and amount. From these features he draws three conclusions. We cannot literally owe gratitude to inadvertent benefactors, because producing good results without beneficent intent creates no liability. We cannot owe gratitude to the dead or to the past, because there is no creditor to whom the debt could be discharged. And we cannot owe gratitude to luck or favorable circumstance, because fortune is not an agent and keeps no accounts. He concedes that ordinary speech talks of gratitude in these cases but insists the debt model shows this is loose talk.

I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, and I would like to hand him a precise audit. Please lay out the correspondences between the source (debt in its ordinary financial sense) and the target (gratitude) as he uses them; identify where the mapping breaks; and separate the disanalogies that are incidental from those that actually matter for his three conclusions about inadvertent benefactors, the dead, and luck. Then say whether any of the three conclusions survives once the disanalogies are made explicit. Finally, where the debt picture fails, say whether some better master-analogy captures what we owe to benefactors, luck, and the past—or whether no single analogy will do.
