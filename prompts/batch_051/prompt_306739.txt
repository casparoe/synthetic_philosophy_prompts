Here is the student's answer:

The octopus thought experiment has two people, A and B, on separate islands communicating over a telegraph cable, while an octopus taps into the cable. The octopus has no access to anything on the islands—just the sequence of signals—but after enough data it can imitate B's side well enough that A can't tell the difference. The usual moral is that the octopus has learned to control the form of the language without learning what any of it means: since it never had access to the things the signals refer to, it can't have recovered what the speakers mean. The stochastic parrot argument says LLMs are doing the same thing at scale: they splice together chunks of training text according to learned probabilities and never refer to anything. I think both arguments show that distributional information from text cannot support any semantic competence whatsoever, so a model trained only on text has literally no semantics—not even word-level similarity. And that's why I read the octopus example as a proof that any system without sensorimotor grounding is incapable of understanding, full stop. Since LLMs have no bodies or perceptual access to the world, they understand nothing, and the parrot analogy holds because a parrot also produces words without referents.
