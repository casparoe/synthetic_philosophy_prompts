I'm in an upper-division philosophy of language class and we've been doing two-dimensional semantics for a few weeks. The way I understand it: the primary intension of a term asks you to evaluate a world "as actual," so you look at what the term would pick out if that world turned out to be the real one. The secondary intension asks you to evaluate worlds "as counterfactual" after the actual world has already fixed the reference, so "water" comes out H2O in every world, even Twin Earth worlds where the clear drinkable liquid is XYZ.

Here's the parallel I keep seeing and can't decide about. I used to write interpreters for toy programming languages, and this looks a lot like dynamic versus lexical scoping. Dynamic scoping resolves a variable using the environment at the call site; lexical scoping resolves it using the environment where the binding was created. Descriptivism and the primary intension seem dynamically scoped: take whichever world you happen to be evaluating at, and look up whatever satisfies the description there. Kripkean rigidity and the secondary intension seem lexically scoped: bind "water" once in the actual world, then carry that binding into every counterfactual world. On this picture, two-dimensional semantics is basically its just a formal language with two different name-resolution rules.

Is this a real structural identity or am I just pattern-matching? What would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer? If it's a real analogy, do things like variable capture, shadowing, or the classic dynamic-scoping bugs have counterparts on the philosophy side — for instance, could a term's primary intension get "captured" by an unintended feature of the world it's being evaluated at? And where does the analogy break first? I'm more interested in where it comes apart than in being told it's interesting. Please answer in American English.
