I am a novelist at work on a near-future thriller about an AI race between two rival powers. The novel turns on a strategic question I want to dramatise as precisely as I can: in the rationalist tradition of explaining war as a bargaining failure, associated above all with the work of James Fearon, a race may become violent through private information and incentives to misrepresent, through commitment problems, or through issue indivisibilities. I want to explore the first two mechanisms as rival keys to interpreting an AI race, and I would like you to write a scene for me in which two specialists argue the matter out.

The first specialist, whom you may name and characterise, should defend the commitment-problem interpretation. Their position is that, even if both sides were fully informed and neither desired war, the first state to approach a strategically decisive AI capability could not credibly commit to leave its rival uncoerced afterwards; knowing this, the lagging rival has a rational incentive to strike preventively while it still can. On this view, the race is structurally war-prone, and the appropriate response leans towards containment, counterforce options, and accepting some provocation risk in order to slow the rival's programme.

The second specialist, who must be just as able, should defend the private-information interpretation. Their position is that the binding failure is not commitment but observability: capabilities, timelines, red lines, and the exact point at which an AI advantage would become decisive are all substantially hidden, and each side has ordinary reasons to conceal progress or bluff. The commitment problem, they should argue, is less severe than its analogue in nuclear first-strike logic because no single moment of decisive advantage is cleanly observable and because restraint can be made verifiable through monitoring, inspections, and staged agreements. On this view the danger is mainly miscalculated escalation, and the rational response is transparency and verifiable restraint rather than preventive containment.

The disagreement should therefore concern not whether Fearon's framework applies, but which of its mechanisms is doing the real causal work in the AI case and what that implies for policy. Please write a sustained dialogue, not two set-piece speeches. Each character should respond to the other's actual examples and arguments; each should be forced at least once to qualify or revise their position; and neither should be a strawman. Please avoid the stock caricatures of AI doomerism and AI optimism, and avoid easy resolutions in which one simply capitulates.

Before you write the dialogue, think carefully. First, set out privately the strongest version of each position, including the background conditions that would have to hold for it to be correct. Then identify the two or three points at which the positions most sharply clash, and note one concession each side would find costly but honest. Only then draft the scene.
