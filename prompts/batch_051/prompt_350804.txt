I have gradually talked myself into a position on cultural value that I can state clearly but find increasingly hard to defend without hedging. The position is that at least some cultural forms — natural languages, musical and craft traditions, and similar practices — are intrinsically valuable, where that means their value does not reduce to the preferences, welfare, or interests of the people who currently hold them. In roughly the way a biological species or an ecosystem is sometimes thought to matter beyond its present members, I hold that the extinction of a language with its last speaker, or the disappearance of a weaving or singing tradition with its last competent practitioner, would be a real loss even in the imagined case where no living person prefers that it continue, no future person would have been better off if it had survived, and no one is made worse off by the dying-out. What is lost, on this view, is a historically developed pattern of possible activity and thought: a space of distinctions, moves, and forms of attention that goes away when the practice goes away, regardless of anyone's attitudes toward it.

The trouble is that I keep having to refine the view when it is pressed, and I am no longer sure whether the refinements are clarifying the position or insulating it. Against the objection that an abstract pattern cannot be the bearer of value, I say the value resides not in the abstract structure alone and not in any token performance, but in the ongoing capacity of a community to reproduce and improvise within the form. Against the objection that an archive of grammars, recordings, and descriptions preserves everything observable, I say it does not preserve the value in full, because the value lives partly in live competence and in the possibility of new instances — though I cannot say exactly why the archive falls short given that it stores all the observable regularities. So I would like you to treat this as a position and tell me what would count against it. Please map the defeaters rather than merely listing them: which cases, intuitions, and results in value theory should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward; which of them a committed defender of the view should concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss; and whether the retreat to the capacity to reproduce and improvise has made the view unfalsifiable. If it has, I want an honest judgment about whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or simply the price of talking about intrinsic value. The defeaters I expect to find most persuasive are ones that show the view collapses into person-affecting considerations, or into an aesthetic preference of mine presented as a property of the world.
