I've been turning over a puzzle about the color phi effect ever since I came across a description of it, and I'd like to work through it in a structured way. In the demonstration, two brief lights of different colors — say, red and green — are flashed in quick succession at two nearby positions. Observers do not report two separate flashes; they report a single light that travels smoothly across the gap and changes color around the middle of its path. The strangeness is that this report seems to require the visual system to have information about the second, green flash before the moving light reaches its apparent midpoint, as if part of the experience is assembled after the fact and then inserted into an earlier moment of the sequence.

I gather there are two broad camps about where that insertion happens. On the Stalinesque account, the editing occurs before anything reaches consciousness: the conscious stream is already the finished, revised version, and the subject never consciously experiences the raw sequence. On the Orwellian account, the subject does consciously experience the sequence as it arrives, and it is the memory of that experience that is rewritten afterwards, once the later information is available. The two camps can agree on what the subject reports and on what the subject now takes himself to have experienced; they disagree about what was conscious at each instant and about the stage of processing at which the revision took place.

Could you act as a philosopher of mind specializing in consciousness and the temporal structure of experience, and draft the joint statement that a committed Orwellian and a committed Stalinesque could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the color phi case? Start by brainstorming the strongest version of each position. Then produce the document with a numbered section of empirical and conceptual claims both parties can sign; a section formulating the remaining disagreement precisely, in wording each side would accept for its own view; and a disagreements table of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — with, for each crux, a kind of case, experiment, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction stated in advance. Finally, identify any part of the dispute that is merely verbal or empirically equivalent, and say why you classify it that way.
