I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, working on memetics and the conditions under which content fails to take hold. Our lab is hosting a small student conference themed around making ideas stick, and as a joke the organizers asked me to do the satire slot: a piece from a fictional government bureau responding to an outbreak of an antimeme. For anyone who hasn't run into the term (the organizers definitely haven't): an antimeme is an idea or piece of information that resists being remembered or transmitted — a meme with a negative retention rate, basically. You read it, you understand that it matters, and then it's gone; if you try to pass it on, the conversation drifts, or you forget what you were about to say.

What I'd like you to write is a deadpan, bureaucratic public-health advisory of about 500 words, issued by the Bureau of Memetic Hygiene, warning the public about an antimeme that is currently, well, contained is the wrong word, but you get the idea. Please format it like a real advisory: a situation summary, case counts with a confidence interval that is meaningless because any reported case is by definition one someone managed to remember, recommended precautions, and a hotline. The jokes have to come from taking the logic of antimemes seriosuly — for instance, a directive that tells the reader to memorize the very thing it is about, a field office that cannot recall its own staffing levels, or an attached memo nobody read. Please include at least one numbered precaution that is impossible to follow for precisely the reason the advisory exists, and at least one italicized interjection from a frustrated enforcement officer. Write it in the voice of a competent but exhausted civil servant who fully understands the self-concealing character of the problem and is slowly realizing no one will remember this advisory anyway; keep the prose flat and official, no winking, no melodrama. By the end, the satire should be legible: a reader should feel that the culture-wide assumption that sticky, shareable content is always good is doing a lot of unexamined work, and that forgetting is sometimes protective rather than a mere failure.
