Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about why people love the particular people they do, and the argument had a familiar, frustrating shape: the more we clarified our positions, the less either of us moved. He defended a bestowal view: love is not a response to value that is already there; loving someone confers value on them, and the beloved's interests become reasons for you because you love them, not the other way around. I defended an appraisal view: love is, and ought to be, responsive to the beloved's qualities — their wit, their steadiness, the specific way they see things — and a love that answered to nothing about the person would be ungrounded. A third friend kept breaking in with cases neither of us could handle cleanly: a parent who loves an ordinary child more than a stranger's kinder, more charming child; a spouse who remains loved after an injury has destroyed the traits the other fell for; an adult who loves a cruel parent simply because she is his mother. Afterward it struck me that our argument has the same structure as the long-stalled philosophical debate between appraisal and bestowal accounts of love's reasons, with a relationship-based account sitting uneasily between them.

I am not asking you to adjudicate who is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is: a verbal one about the phrase 'a reason for love'; a substantive one that bottoms out in bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for; a difference about which cases count as data; or two camps answering different questions — one about justification from a third-person standpoint and one about what it is like to love from the inside — under a single label. My working hypothesis is that the last diagnosis is closest and that the relationship cases are the pressure point, but I want your independent diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, a kind of case, an empirical finding, a change of question — or an argument that the debate should be expected to stay stuck. Lead with your diagnosis in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
