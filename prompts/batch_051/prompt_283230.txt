I'm partway through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and the move I keep tripping over is the running anology between a Gödel sentence and a conscious self. My understanding: a Gödel sentence is a formula that, via Gödel numbering, manages to say in effect that it is not provable in the system, and under the usual assumptions it comes out true but unprovable from inside that system. Hofstadter then treats this as a kind of strange loop that is supposed to work the same way in minds, so that self-awareness is a system referring to itself the way the formula refers to its own provability. I want to know exactly how far this analogy is meant to go. What maps onto what — the formal system, the formula, provability, the numbering, the truth of the sentence? Where does the mapping actually do real work, and where does it start importing features of arithmetic that a mind might not have? For instance, the easiest way to see the Gödel sentence is true seems to require hopping outside the system — appealing to its consistency or reasoning about it from a stronger system — which sounds like a mind would need an outside observer to be aware of itself, the opposite of what introspection feels like. Also, a formal system is a fixed set of axioms and inference rules, but a mind revises its own rules over time. Does the conclusion that self-reference is the core of consciousness survive those disanalogies, or is self-reference doing two different jobs in the two halves of the analogy? And if the analogy does break, is there a better way to cash out the intuition that noticing yourself is different from just processing?
