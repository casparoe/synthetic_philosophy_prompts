Ok so I teach an undergrad applied ethics seminar, 16 students, one 80-minute block. The unit is on griefbots—chatbots trained on a dead person's texts, emails, and voice memos that let mourners keep "talking" to them. I don't want another pro/con discussion where half the class mails it in; I want them to feel the pull of the decision themselves. Design a structured role-play around a small group deciding what to do with a griefbot they've been using for a few weeks after the funeral. I'm picturing four groups of four. I'd like one twist where one member of each group plays the bot itself, so the group has to decide whether its "feelings" count for anything—if that's too cute, say so and give me the better version. The thing I want them to come away seeing is the conflict between the mourners' comfort and the fact that the person who generated all this intimacy isn't around to consent, plus the weird authority of things the bot says that the real person never said but totally could have. The rules and role cards should push them into that problem before anyone names it in debrief. Keep it runnable by a single facilitator with no props beyond role cards, a timer, and maybe a whiteboard. Include exact role-card text (four cards), a timeline for the 80 minutes, the complication to drop in halfway through and what it's for, a debrief that mappes specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual issues, a paragraph on what predictably goes wrong and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the role-play can't reproduce about real grief or real deployed bots.
