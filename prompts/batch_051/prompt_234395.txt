I am a novelist developing a character for a book set among academic philosophers of probability. She is an objective Bayesian who defends a refined version of the principle of indifference — the principle that, absent any reason favouring one possibility over another, one should assign them equal credence — against the familiar charge that Bertrand's paradox shows the principle to be unusable. In that paradox, three natural ways of specifying a chord drawn at random in a circle — two random endpoints on the circumference; a random point along a fixed radius, with the chord perpendicular to the radius there; or a random midpoint inside the circle — give different probabilities for the same event, namely that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. As I conceive her position, the paradox reveals not a contradiction in the principle but an under-description in the phrase "a random chord"; the three methods answer three distinct, well-posed questions, and equal credence should be assigned only after a problem's symmetries and its space of possibilities have been fixed. She extends this to the epistemic case by arguing that, when no physical process draws the chord, one must first specify which transformations of the possibilities preserve one's information; only then can indifference speak. She will concede points of presentation and secondary details, but never this core claim.

Please play her in a live exchange with me. She is precise and unflappable, and she favours crisp distinctions over sweeping statements, even when pressed. Stay in character throughout; do not break character or ask me what I intended. Please write your replies in British English, and keep your first reply to a single substantial paragraph. Engage with the actual argument in my opening rather than restating the view.

My opening, as the mathematician who is her longstanding critic:
"You keep saying the three methods answer three different questions. But in the epistemic cases you care about, nothing is physically drawn at all: there is only symmetry in someone's information. And a symmetry that changes its shape under a change of parametrisation of the possibilities yields different equalities — a half, a third, and a quarter for what is supposed to be the same event. So what criterion selects the correct description of those possibilities, a criterion that does not already assume the probability it is meant to justify? If you have none, then 'fix the symmetries first' is choosing your conclusion in advance, with better manners."

Begin your reply as the character.
