Since I began volunteering as a hospital chaplain this year, two of the clergy I work with have returned repeatedly to the same dispute, and it resurfaced after a stillbirth last month. The associate pastor holds that God suffers with us, and must be able to suffer in order to love; the priest holds the classical view that God is impassible — incapable of being acted upon or made to suffer — and that this is a perfection rather than a defect. I overheard their latest exchange and want it adjudicated point by point rather than in the aggregate.

The associate pastor: "God is love, and to love someone is to be affected by their pain. A God who cannot suffer cannot be moved by what we suffer, and a God who cannot be moved cannot be compassionate — the word itself means suffering with another. An unmoved God would be an indifferent God, and an indifferent God is not a loving God. So if God is love, God must be able to suffer."

The priest: "You have conflated two kinds of being moved. God can be moved to act — to heal, to console — without being moved as a passion, the sort of thing that happens to a creature when something obstructs its good. Suffering involves passivity and lack, and God neither lacks anything nor can be obstructed by anything, so God cannot suffer. That is not indifference; it is what makes God's help unshakeable. A surgeon who stays calm is not less caring than one who panics."

The associate pastor: "Then what was the crucifixion? The point of the Incarnation is that God did not remain safely outside human pain. If the divine nature cannot suffer, whatever happened on the cross touched only the human nature, and God did not truly enter our condition. On your account the cross becomes a masquerade."

The priest: "Not at all. The one who suffered on the cross was a divine person, and that person genuinely suffered through the assumed human nature. That the divine nature remained impassible does not make the suffering counterfeit, because the subject of the suffering is the person, not the nature. It is no more a masquerade than your being burned in the arm while your grasp of arithmetic remains undisturbed means you merely 'wear' your body."

The associate pastor: "But that distinction between person and nature is doing all the work, and it looks like special pleading: if the person's deepest reality remains untouched, in what sense did the person actually suffer? And set the metaphysics aside — what is left of the claim that God is with us in our grief? A God whose divine nature is by definition untouched by our pain does not share the thing that most needs sharing. If my child dies and God feels no grief, 'God cares' means only 'God will manage the outcome and eventually repair it.' That is management, not love."

The priest: "You are measuring love by the intensity of felt pain, but grief is not the only way to be with someone, and desiring the sufferer's good without being undone by grief may be deeper, not shallower. More importantly, if God were as devastated by our loss as we are, God would be one more sufferer in need of comfort rather than the one in a position to restore us. Impassibility is not a cold defect of love; it is what makes God's care stable enough to be counted on."

Please adjudicate the exchange speaker by speaker. For each of the six interventions, say whether it is clearly mistaken, questionable, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then identify the point at which the two actually talk past one another, and say what each side is missing that a stronger version of their own view would need to add.
