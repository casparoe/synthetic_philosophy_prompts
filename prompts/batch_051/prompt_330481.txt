South Africa's post-apartheid Truth and Reconciliation Commission ran on a deal: perpetrators of gross human rights violations could get amnesty — no trial, no prison — but only if they came forward and gave a full public account of what they had done. Victims were given a forum to testify, but their testimony did not by itself lead to punishment of the people they named; that depended on whether the perpetrator had applied for and been granted amnesty. And the whole enterprise was framed as reconciliation rather than retribution.

Here's the puzzle. In ordinary criminal law, the point of prosecuting and punishing a murderer is usually understood to include both retribution (the wrongdoer should suffer something) and deterrence (others should be warned off). But the TRC suspended exactly that for some of the worst political crimes, and it did so in exchange for truth-telling. So the practice seems to presuppose something like: for at least these crimes, retribution and deterrence can be traded away, and a truthful public account is a partial or full substitute for punishment.

I want you to reconstruct, as precisely as you can, what theory of punishment — or of what criminal justice is for — this practice presuposes. Does it commit itself to the view that truth is a form of accountability rather than a poor substitute for it? Or is it actually compatible with retributivism, because being made to confess publicly counts as a kind of punishment? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined: which parts could a retributivist, a utilitarian, and a restorative-justice person each accept without strain? Finally, is the practice coherent when it sits alongside the ordinary view that certain crimes must be prosecuted no matter what, or does it quietly swap in a different rationale for punishment?
