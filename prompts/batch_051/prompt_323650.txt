Last spring I took over the rulings register for a small land-trust association whose constitution is self-amending in a way I still find vertiginous: the clause that gives the membership its power to amend the constitution is itself one of the clauses that power reaches, and no provision is exempted from revision. Next week I have to advise the board on a disputed amendment, so I have spent the past month reconstructing the principle behind my predecessor's rulings. The constitution says an amendment is adopted by two-thirds of the members present at a regular meeting, and that when two provisions conflict the later one controls. My predecessor's recorded rulings include these. She held out of order a motion to suspend the quorum for a single evening, on the ground that the quorum is fixed by the constitution and can be changed only by amendment, not by suspension. She held valid an amendment lowering the quorum from forty percent to twenty-five percent, effective immediately, though the meeting adopting it had met the old quorum. She ruled that a proposal touching the amendment clause was not out of order merely for that reason: no clause is immune from amendment. And she repeatedly resolved conflicts between an older clause and a newer one by applying the newer, reasoning that the constitution speaks in its latest voice.

The present case is this. A proposal has been put whose operative language would replace the two-thirds threshold with a majority of members present for all future amendments, and it expressly states that the new threshold is to govern the vote on the proposal itself. The count was fifty-eight percent in favor: more than the proposed majority, but short of the current two-thirds. The sponsors argue that the present membership is the source of constitutional authority, and that the new rule should therefore count the very act that adopts it. The opponents answer that no proposal can amend the procedure under which it is being considered until it first satisfies that procedure. I am less interested in a bare answer than in the structure behind it: which of my predecessor's rulings is the closest analog, which candidate principles fit her record, and what each principle predicts here. Give your best guess as to how she would have ruled and what we should rule to remain consistent, and if the record underdetermines it, say what principle each ruling would commit us to and what future case would separate the candidates.
