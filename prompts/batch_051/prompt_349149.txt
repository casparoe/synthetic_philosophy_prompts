I keep going round in circles on what deplatforming and 'cancellation' actually presuppose. A university rescinds a speaking invitation; a platform deactivates an account after an old clip circulates; a publisher drops a book over the author's tweets. The stated rationale is usually that no law was broken, but a community can decide who it wants on its stages and platforms. Yet the practice also looks like punishment — it destroys income and reputation, and institutions often comply partly out of fear of being next. The confusing bit is that many of the same people would be horrified by a state punishment without a trial, a chance to respond, and some proportionality between offence and penalty. So what theory of punishment (or of membership, or of speech as action) is actually doing the work here? Does the practice strictly presuppose retributivism or expressivism about blame, or is it fully compatible with a purely deterrent/consequentialist story? And what would have to change about how these campaigns are run if we swapped in a due-process, proportionality-centred view of punishment? Act as a professional philosopher working on punishment and social norms, think carefully — perhaps sketch two or three candidate reconstructions before settling — and be clear about which parts of the practice are seperately underdetermined between rival theories and which genuinely force a particular view.
