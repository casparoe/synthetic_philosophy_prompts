You are an expert in the philosophy of history and historical methodology. I am a working historian of modern political institutions, and I have settled into the following six commitments. I want you to audit them for joint consistency: first restate the strongest defensible version of each commitment, then identify where they genuinely pull against one another. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, clearly separate them from pairs that only appear to conflict, and for each real tension say exactly what has to give and what the cheapest repair would cost.

(1) Whig history — writing the past as an ascent to present freedoms and institutions, sorting past actors into ancestors and obstacles — is a methodological vice: it anachronizes motives, takes the present as the standard, and flattens contingency.

(2) A central task of the kind of history I write is to explain how present institutions came to be; a history that refuses to trace outcomes forward into the present is refusing to explain.

(3) Historical significance is retrospective: an event, process, or agent matters in virtue of what it shaped — the differences it made to later developments, ultimately up to and including the present.

(4) Narrative history must select, and selection must answer to some criterion of significance; there is no neutral way to include everything, so the only question is which criterion is governing the selection.

(5) We can and should say that some historical changes were improvements — the end of chattel slavery, the extension of the franchise — without thereby committing ourselves to inevitable progress or to treating our present arrangements as the end of history.

(6) We should not judge past agents by our own menus of options and information, but that does not place them beyond evaluation altogether.

I would rather not give up any of these commitments. But I want to know what holding all of them together is actually costing me, and which claims are doing the most hidden work.
