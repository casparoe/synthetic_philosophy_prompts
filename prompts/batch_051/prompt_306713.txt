My friend's been on this charter cities kick, and every time I push him on the worry that a private city could have genuinely awful rules — debt peonage for missed rent, a legal system you can't vote on, whatever — he falls back on the same analogy: a city run by a company is just a country club. You join voluntarily, you signed up for the rules, and if you stop liking it, you walk. That's the entire source of its legitimacy. A state, by contrast, is a club where you're enrolled at birth and the exits are blocked. I suspect there's something off here, but I can't quite say where the country-club picture stops being a good model of a city-like thing with its own legal system. Could you audit the analogy the way he's using it? I'd want: an explicit mapping of what's supposed to correspond to what, the dissanalogies that actually matter for his legitimacy conclusion versus the ones that are just incidental, what gets quietly imported from the club setting that does't carry over to a polity, and whether the conclusion — that exit makes private-city authority non-coercive in a way state authority isn't — survives the audit. If there's a better analogy for what's actually going on, I'd take that too.
