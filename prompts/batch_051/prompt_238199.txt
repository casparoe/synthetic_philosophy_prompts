For an advanced undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of death, I should like a one-page decision tree that sorts students among the main criteria of death by their answers to concrete cases rather than by asking which label they prefer.

Please construct the tree as follows. Each internal node should be a single yes/no question about a concrete case, phrased in plain English and without naming the position it leads toward. The cases below should appear somewhere in the tree:

- a patient in a permanent vegetative state who breathes unaided but is irreversibly unconscious;
- a patient with no brain function at all who is mechanically ventilated, has a beating heart, and whose kidneys continue to work;
- a patient whose heart has stopped, declared dead after several minutes without circulation when no resuscitation is attempted;
- a patient whose heart has failed but whose brain is uninjured, kept alive on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation;
- if the logic permits, a brain-dead pregnant woman kept on a ventilator so that a foetus can be gestated, as a stress test for the whole-brain criterion.

The leaves should include at least: the whole-brain criterion; the higher-brain (or capacity-for-consciousness) criterion; the circulatory-respiratory criterion; and a conventionalist or pluralist view holding that death is a line drawn for social and legal purposes rather than a single biological fact. You may add one further leaf if the logical space requires it, naming and justifying it.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of positions: first set out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions within that space. Where a combination has no known defender, mark it unoccupied and diagnose in one sentence whether it is internally incoherent, thoroughly unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please also flag, rather than conceal, any positions the tree cannot cleanly separate. The whole-brain and circulatory-respiratory criteria agree in many cases but come apart in donation after the circulatory determination of death and in extracorporeal support, so wherever two leaves cannot be distinguished by any question in the tree, say what further case would separate them, if anything would.

The handout should run to no more than seven questions and five leaves, each leaf named and followed by a single sentence identifying the implication its defenders find most difficult to accept. Kindly write the piece in British English.
