I'm a distributed systems engineer who works on fault-tolerant consensus. I'm fluent in the epistemic interpretation of distributed systems: local and global states, runs, message histories, indistinguishability relations, the knowledge operator, common knowledge as the greatest fixed point of everyone-knows, synchrony and asynchrony, and impossibility results such as FLP. I know much less about Bayesian epistemology and formal game theory.

Restate Aumann's agreement theorem in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. I understand the theorem roughly as follows: two agents share a prior probability distribution; each updates on private information given by a partition of the state space; and if their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge, then those posterior probabilities must be equal. Go component by component — common prior, partitional information, Bayesian updating, posterior probability, common knowledge of the posteriors, and the conclusion — and for each component give the closest distributed-systems counterpart, or say explicitly that no faithful counterpart exists.

Then give me the residue report: which components translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion and what the distortion is, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. I especially want to know whether the theorem's notion of agreement is the same as consensus in my field, and if not, where exactly they diverge. End with a verdict on the residue: is it evidence that my vocabulary is missing something essential about belief and rationality, evidence that the theorem's conclusion is largely carried by assumptions — the common prior above all — that have no analogue in the systems I build, or some of each? Be careful not to smuggle in a mapping such as common prior equals common initial state or common protocol unless it genuinely preserves the work the prior does in the theorem; if the mapping needs fudging, say what is being fudged.
