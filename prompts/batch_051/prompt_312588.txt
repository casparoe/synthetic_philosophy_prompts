I serve on a standing expert working group that advises a psychiatric association on whether to adopt, revise, or reject proposed diagnostic categories. We meet twice a year, take up two or three proposals per meeting, and our recommendation is almost always accepted by the board that signs off. We work under sharp constraints: we cannot commission new studies, we must vote at the end of the meeting, and the group mixes clinicians, researchers, and service users who do not share a theoretical vocabulary. The decisions matter in a way I want to get right: a new category can open access to care and legitimize suffering, but it can also put a name on a life that people then live into, and the category itself may begin to shift as the people classified, and the clinicians, researchers, insurers, and advocates around them, respond to it. I take this to be the territory of Ian Hacking's account of looping effects: classifications of people are not inert labels, because the people classified come to understand themselves through the classification, alter their behavior accordingly, and thereby alter the expert and institutional practices around them, which loops back and changes the classification itself. But I do not know how to run this insight as a procedure at a meeting.

So here is what I want: a decision procedure my group could actually use before voting, to decide whether a proposed diagnostic category should be adopted, revised, or rejected, grounded as faithfully as possible in Hacking's looping-effects framework rather than in a generic risk-benefit checklist. Give me the questions in the order we should ask them; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say where the procedure stops with a no; and say where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values, clinical judgment, or a prediction about the future that no framework can settle. Also be explicit about which of Hacking's distinctions must survive the simplification, for example the difference between a classification that merely labels a pre-existing group and one that loops back on the people it classifies, and the role of expert knowledge and institutions in stabilizing a category, and which can be dropped. Flag the points where the procedure is most likely to misfire.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals, telling me for each what the committee would have to establish, whether the procedure passes or fails it, and where judgment outside the procedure remains: prolonged grief disorder, a proposal to make persistent, disabling grief continuing roughly a year or more after a bereavement a distinct disorder; internet gaming disorder, a proposal to classify a sustained pattern of gaming that crowds out other activities and continues despite harm as a behavioral addiction; and sluggish cognitive tempo, a proposed attentional pattern of excessive daydreaming, mental fogginess, and slowed thought or movement long debated as a possible condition distinct from ADHD. For at least one of the three, make it a case where the procedure returns an answer I would not like, and say what that tells us about the procedure itself.
