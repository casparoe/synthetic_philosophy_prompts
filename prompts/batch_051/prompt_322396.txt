You are to act as an experienced professor of political economy and the philosophy of social science, with a research focus on causal inference in comparative historical analysis. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Why Are Some Nations Rich?" and I am drafting the final exam. Please produce one long-form essay question on the institutions-versus-geography-versus-culture debate, together with a model answer of roughly 900–1,100 words and a marking scheme.

The question must be designed so that a student cannot pass by reciting the three standard positions or summarizing the inclusive-versus-extractive institutions story. I want it to test whether students understand that historical comparisons do not hand us a controlled experiment: institutions are themselves shaped by geography, culture, and prior wealth, countries are not independent draws, and any natural experiment intended to isolate the institutional channel must satisfy strong identifying assumptions. For instance, a candidate should be able to see why the observation that Europeans could not settle in large numbers in some colonies and left extractive institutions behind is not, by itself, decisive evidence that institutions caused subsequent poverty, because the same disease environment that deterred settlement might shape later outcomes through geography, health, or human capital rather than through institutions alone. They should also be able to explain why "institutions" is not one treatment but a bundle, and why that matters for the claim that institutional reform can be exported as policy. Whatever empirical setup the question uses should be stated inside the question itself; students should be graded on reasoning about the inferential problem rather than on recognition of particular texts, and the question should not call for citations or reading lists.

Before writing, spend some time brainstorming candidate crux questions: list at least three possible formulations and note the specific shallow answer or misconception each would catch. Then choose the one that best separates students who can reason about identification from students who have only memorized labels. After that, produce the three deliverables: (1) the exam question for a 90-minute essay; (2) a model answer; and (3) a marking scheme of 25 marks. The marking scheme should name five specific moves a first-class answer must make, allocate the 25 marks across them, and for each describe what a B-grade answer typically omits and the one error candidates most often make. Think carefully; in particular, do not mistake a plausible mechanism for evidence of causation.
