I teach a one-day executive seminar on decision-making under uncertainty for mid-career professionals — procurement managers, policy staff, and product leads — who are comfortable with spreadsheets and basic probability but have never taken formal decision theory. Next week's session covers Good's theorem: the claim that, for an expected-utility maximizer who is free to ignore information, acquiring additional information can never reduce expected utility ex ante. My problem is that the audience arrives with two contrary reactions. Half think the theorem is obviously true and not worth 75 minutes; the other half think it is obviously false, because they can point to cases where more information seemed to paralyze a team, leak a strategy, or make a final decision worse. Last year the Q&A collapsed into confusion about ex ante versus ex post evaluation, and I want the plan to build that distinction in early rather than let it surface at the end.

Plan how I should teach it, rather than explaining the theorem to me. Map the misconceptions to expect, in the order they will surface, and say exactly where in the sequence to head each off; I especially want you to handle regret and hindsight, information that changes other people's behavior, and the slide into the slogan that knowledge is power. Tell me what to leave out and what I should explicitly flag as out of scope. End with one question or short exercise for the last five minutes that will show whether they have the theorem as a decision-theoretic claim rather than a slogan.
