I'm trying to work out what I in fact think about how credences should defer to objective chance, and I want you to help me find the principle behind my gut verdicts on a set of cases.

By objective chance I mean mind-independent, time-indexed physical chance — the quantity expressed when we say that a particular coin toss has a particular probability of coming up heads in virtue of the physical setup, not anyone's degree of belief. The Principal Principle is the standard starting point: roughly, that one's credence should equal the known chance, at least when one's other information is admissible. I accept that as a starting point, but I think the interesting work is in the messy cases, and I want to know whether one principle can cover them all.

Here are my verdicts.

First, a coin I know to be fair is about to be flipped, and I have no other relevant information. I am at 0.5 that it lands heads. This one is uncontroversial for me.

Second, same coin, same known chance of 0.5, but an infallible crystal ball — one I know with certainty to reveal the actual future outcome — reveals that it will land heads. I am at something effectively equal to 1, not 0.5.

Third, a fair die is about to be rolled, so the objective chance of its showing a 2 is 1/6. I then learn from a perfectly reliable source that it will show an even number. I am at 1/3 that it shows a 2. I think of this as the chance of 2 conditional on even.

Fourth, a fair coin is about to be flipped. A stranger tells me it will land heads, and I know that this kind of stranger's predictions about coin tosses are right exactly half the time. I stay at 0.5. The stranger's report feels irrelevant rather than merely outweighed.

Fifth is the case I cannot settle, and the reason I am writing. Suppose I am in a world where objective chance is a Humean construction: chances are not primitive propensities but are whatever an ideal best-system systematization of the entire history of the world says they are, in something like the usual best-system analysis of laws and chance. I have overwhelming evidence for the true theory of chance at this world, and that theory assigns a particular coin a chance of 0.9 of landing heads on its next toss. I then learn from an utterly reliable source that the future will unfold in a way that would undermine that assignment: the coming run of tosses will be mostly tails, long enough that the best systematization of the total history of the world — past plus this future — would assign the coin a chance much nearer 0.1 than 0.9. My gut verdict is that I should not keep my credence at 0.9. The 0.9 no longer has authority over me once I have learned a future that, if actual, would make that chance assignment false. But I cannot say what number I should move to: I waver between roughly 0.1 and the thought that the setup no longer determines any definite credence at all.

I keep wanting a single principle that handles the first four cases cleanly and says something principled about the fifth. The candidate I keep coming back to, stated loosely, is this: the known chance statement constrains my prior in whatever way the Principal Principle describes, and then I simply conditionalize on any further evidence I receive — with the caveat that I cannot conditionalize on evidence that is incompatible with the chance statement I am also conditionalizing on. But I do not know whether that caveat is enough, or whether following it forces me to abandon one of my verdicts.

Please do the following. State the deference principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts, as precisely as you can. For each clause of the proposed principle, say which of my cases motivates it. Then give the principle's verdicts on cases I have not listed — in particular, a case where I learn a trusted but non-infallible expert's precise credence about the outcome, and a case where I receive a slightly less-than-perfect crystal-ball report. Finally, say honestly which of my verdicts the proposed principle cannot accommodate, or which verdict I would have to give up if I insist on the simplest version of the principle. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on chance and credence from what is genuinely contested — the undermining-style case especially — and do not present a contested view as the consensus. No citations or historical survey; I want the principle argued out.
