I retired a few years back from a career in physical chemistry, and I've gotten into philosophy since — mostly philosophy of economics and the bits of moral psychology that rub up against it. In a reading group last month, someone waved off an entire empirical literature on self-reported life satisfaction with the line "put your money where your mouth is." His point was that what people say they want is cheap talk, and only choices that actually cost a person something reveal what they really value. A second member said this is just revealed preference theory, and that in that setting the slogan becomes nearly a definition: utility simply is whatever a person's choices maximize, so of course actions reveal preferences — there is nothing else to reveal. The first guy then doubled down: fine, that's exactly why self-reports are useless. If preferences are defined by choices, then the worker who says she's unhappy in her job but dosen't leave has revealed that she prefers staying, whatever she says about her mood.

I recognize the instinct from the lab — don't ask the instrument what it's measuring, read the output — but I suspect the proverb is being made to carry more than it can. I also notice it slides from "choosing something tells you something about a person" to "there is nothing to know about a person's preferences beyond what they choose," and those aren't the same claim. Could you take the slogan "put your money where your mouth is" and lay out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing in this kind of argument? For each, say whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which of those theses, if any, would actually license the conclusion that self-report data is worthless and that the unhappy employee has revealed a preference for staying.
