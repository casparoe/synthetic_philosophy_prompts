I have a rule about how I let people express uncertainty, and I can't tell whether it's a defensible discipline or a bias wearing a theorem. The rule is this: when someone supports a claim by saying it is "likely," "probably true," "a real possibility," or anything similar, I refuse to treat the claim as informing my judgment until they attach a number to it, or at least an interval. If they will not, or if they say the matter simply cannot be quantified, I treat them as having said only that the claim is not ruled out—which, for my purposes, is close to saying nothing at all.

My justification runs through Cox's theorem, or anyway through what I took away from it. As I understand it, the theorem shows that any coherent way of grading the plausibility of propositions must, whatever words it happens to use, satisfy the sum and product rules of probability; a qualitative "probably" is therefore either a vague number in disguise, in which case I am entitled to ask which number, or it is not a coherent judgment of plausibility at all. So I ask. I apply the rule especially to predictions and risks: someone says a treatment will "likely" help, a law will "probably" reduce offending, a system "could well" fail under load. "Likely" could mean 0.6 or 0.95, and what I should do, and how much more evidence I would need, is quite different in the two cases.

The worry is that I may be confusing coherence with precision. A theorem that says any coherent degrees of belief behave like probabilities does not by itself say that every honest epistemic state admits a single real number, or that a number thrown out to satisfy me is more informative than a frank "likely." I may be imposing false quantification on people who rightly feel that their evidence does not determine a number, and I may be discarding real information carried by ordinary uncertainty-talk. Is my rule defensible as stated? If not, is there a version that keeps the good part—coherence, assessable claims, no hiding behind vagueness—without the bad? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I am doing the logic of plausible reasoning or just policing other people's speech?
