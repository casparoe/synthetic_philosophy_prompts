I'm a final-year philosophy student, and this term I'm doing a module on the philosophy of art where we spent a few weeks on fakes, forgeries, and what makes an original valuable — which has started bleeding into my day job. I work part-time at a small local museum, mostly scanning things and writing label copy, and we keep having to decide whether we can display a copy in place of an original object. In the last few months we've had three of these calls: a high-resolution photographic facsimile of a fragile watercolour so the real one can stay out of the light; a 3D print of a looted carved object that's going back to its country of origin but whose story the museum still wants on display; and a digital reconstruction of a mural we only hold fragments of, which the curators want to present as if it were the original for an immersive room. At the moment the staff basically improvise — sometimes the label says 'facsimile', sometimes 'reproduction', sometimes nothing at all — and I'm worried were either misleading visitors or forcing everyone to fuss over the wrong thing.

My manager knows I'm taking the module and has asked me to write a decision procedure the whole team can actually run. The people using it are curators, volunteers, and a marketing person, none of them philosophers, and they'll give each object about ten minutes. The stakes are visitor trust, loan agreements, our reputation, and one trustee who keeps saying the entire point of a museum is "the real thing." I don't want a reading list or citations; I want whatever the philosophy actually says about copies, originals, and the history of objects translated into questions to ask in order, with what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it should hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. If there's a principled difference between kinds of work where the object's history is part of what it is and kinds where a faithful copy is just as good, make that an explicit step rather than a footnote. Then run the procedure on the three cases above — the watercolour, the repatriated carving, and the mural reconstruction where the label would be hidden to preserve the immersion (and part of me thinks the curators are right about that one, so I'd like at least one of the cases to come out in a way I won't like). Also say clearly what the procedure can't settle. And can you write the whole answer in British English? I'll probably be quoting from it in a staff meeting.
