In my philosophy club, a classmate gave a presentation on something called "imaginative resistance" and distributed a handout that summarized the idea this way: "When we read fiction, we happily accept outlandish factual additions — time travel, talking animals, a world where water isn't H2O. But when a story asks us to accept a moral claim that clashes with our own, such as 'torturing the prisoner was the right thing to do,' readers simply cannot do it: our moral convictions are too strong and they shut down the imagination. That is why people reject novels that treat evil as good, and it shows that the imagination is ultimately fenced in by our real-world values." I have to write a short response evaluating whether this is a fair account of the idea. Which parts would a philosopher working on fiction accept, which parts are oversimplifications or wrong, and does the last claim about why people reject such novels actually follow from the accurate parts? The part I am most suspicious of is the phrase about the imagination being "shut down," since I can picture evil scenarios — what I resist is accepting that they are good.
