I'm a TA grading a third-year philosophy of science midterm and I could use a second set of eyes on one answer. Here's the question and the students answer. I need a grade out of 10 and point by point comments that justify the grade, not just a general impression of whether it sounds smart.

Question: Suppose a past theory T made a successful novel prediction using a differential equation E, an auxiliary simplifying assumption A, and a theoretical picture S (say, a posited medium or substance) that never actually appeared in the derivation. T was later replaced: E survived in the successor theory as a limiting case, while A and S were dropped. Explain how a selective realist would use this case to reply to the pessimistic meta-induction, and then evaluate whether that reply gives a usable rule for a currently successful theory T*, before any successor to T* exists, about which parts of T* it is rational to believe.

Student answer: The selective realists point is that T was not wholly false, so the pessimistic meta-induction's premise is overstated. The success-generating parts of T were the ones that survived, paradigmatically E; what was false were the idle parts, A and S, which did not carry the predictive load. So the historical record does not support the idea that successful theories fail wholesale. For a current theory T*, the realist can pick out the same kind of success-generating parts in advance by asking which components are actually used in deriving T*'s successful novel predictions. Those are the parts it is rational to believe; the rest, like S, can be treated as mere scaffolding. The obvious objection is that A was also used in deriving T's prediction and yet got dropped, so the test would have licensed believing A. But that only shows the test needs to be sharpened: count only the components that are indispensable to the derivation, not every component that shows up in it. Indispensability can be checked at the time by asking whether the prediction would collapse if the component were altered or removed. If it would, the component is safe to believe now. So there is no special retrospective problem. The pessimistic meta-induction is therefore defused: even if T* later gets labeled false, the parts we were warranted in believing are exactly the parts that will survive.
