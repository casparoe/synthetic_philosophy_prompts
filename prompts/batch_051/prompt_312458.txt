I'm in a metaphysics class on persistence and identity, and after the Ship of Theseus unit I can't stop seeing these problems in real life. Last weekend I visited a maritime museum with my suitemate. They have a 19th-century wooden schooner that's still afloat, and the museum has been maintaining it for almost a hundred years. Most of the hull planks, decking, and even some of the internal framing have been replaced at different times, but all the old material gets saved in a basement archive and displayed as authentic original pieces. The staff were very clear that the ship in the water is the original schooner and that if someone reassembled all the saved timbers into a second ship, they'd label that a replica. I'm trying to figure out what theory of how objects persist this practice is actually committed to. Does it presuppose that identity follows the spatiotemporal continuity of the ship as a maintained object, not the original material? Or is the practice compatible with basically any view of persistence, because original and replica here are just curatorial labels about historical value? And if the museum had to be philosophically consistent, what would they need to say the moment someone actually rebuilt the ship out of the stored timbers—would they be forced to pick one as the real one, or could they coherently call both, or neither, the original schooner?
