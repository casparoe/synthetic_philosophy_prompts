I'm a PhD student working on the ethics of oaths, and I keep running into the same move in the literature: people treat swearing on the Styx as the limit case of an oath with a built-in enforcement mechanism — the penalty is fixed, automatic, and non-negotiable, so the gods' oaths are as binding as oaths get. I want to stress-test that intuition by building a human version and seeing whether the bindingness survives.

Here's the mechanism I'm imagining (treat it as a mechanism, not first as a moral position). A guild of professional arbitrators amends its rules so that every full member must, on induction, swear an oath to uphold the guild's code, and must lodge a bond equal to three months' fees. If another member files a complaint that the oath was broken, a panel of five members drawn by lot hears it. If the panel upholds the complaint, the bond is forfeited — half to the complainant, half to the guild's common fund — and the member is barred from any guild role that involves swearing for a year. If the complaint fails, the complainant forfeits one tenth of their own bond to the accused. A member who loses a bond may re-swear after the year, but now with a bond of six months' fees. Membership privileges require an unanceled oath.

I'm curious how this actually plays out, not whether it's a nice idea in the abstract. Concretely: who ends up swearing, and who self-selects out? What does it do to what counts as a breach — does the code get rewritten so that oaths are cheap? How do people game the complaint-and-panel system (collusion, strategic withdrawal, side-indemnities for complainants, forum-shopping among panels)? Does raising the bond turn serious oath-breaks into a priced option rather than a wrong? And here's the bit I care about philosophically: in the Styx case the sanction is automatic and inescapable; in this human case it's fallible, delayed, and someone has to initiate it. Does the scheme approximate the Styx ideal as the bond gets larger and enforcement more reliable, or does it stop being an oath the moment it starts working through incentives? Put differently, is the binding force of an oath a matter of the sanction you invite, or a matter of what swearing does to the relationship between the swearer and the person to whom the oath is sworn, regardless of enforcement? I suspect the answer depends on which account of oath-binding you already hold, but I want that chased out rather than asserted. And if the scheme drifts from the Styx ideal, can any of its failure modes be fixed without giving up whatever made the Styx case look attractive in the first place?

Also, since this topic has real disagreement in it: please separate what's settled (like basic incentive logic) from what's genuinely contested among people who work on oaths and promising, and don't present one contested account of why oaths bind as the consensus. No citations or reading lists needed — I care about the analysis, not the bibliography.
