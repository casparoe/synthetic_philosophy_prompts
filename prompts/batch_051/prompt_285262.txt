I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar, and for this year's 90-minute final I want one essay question on Douglas Hofstadter's critique of statistical machine translation: his argument that such systems rearrange tokens with convincing fluency while understanding none of the content, and that this is shallowness rather than translation. The question must be impossible to answer well by summarizing that argument. It should force students to locate where the defect lies — in the system's internal processing, in its outputs, or in the norms of the practice of translation — by comparing two rival standards for a good translation: faithfulness to what the source text says, and usefulness to the reader. It should then ask students to judge whether a system that passes ordinary fidelity tests must be credited with understanding when it produces fluent, confident mistranslations of exactly the kind a comprehending human translator would not make, and to say what, if anything, understanding would add over and above producing the right output. Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names five must-hit moves, allocates marks among them, and for each one notes the characteristic student confusion it is designed to catch.
