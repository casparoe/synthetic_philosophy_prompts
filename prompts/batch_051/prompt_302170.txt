I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a longread about incorruptibility — what it even means to be incorruptible, and whether it's always a good thing. For the piece I've been shadowing a retired federal judge who now writes about ethics in research and government. She has really consistent instincts, but I cant quite put my finger on the rule behind them.

Here are her calls, in her one-word moral verdicts:
- City councilor who flipped a zoning vote after a developer gave his wife a no-show job: corrupt.
- State epidemiologist who turned down paid flights, hotel suites, and a $35,000 speaker fee from a drug company and then voted against that company's drug: incorruptible.
- Publisher who spiked his paper's investigation of a data-analysis firm after the firm threatened to sue him personally over a unrelated loan: corrupt.
- High-school teacher who refused to change a failing athlete's grade even when the principal said the football playoffs depended on it: incorruptible.
- Researcher who changed her recommendation on a surgical device after the manufacturer flew her around on private jets and put her up at resorts, even though she swears the evidence genuinely changed: corrupt.
- Doctor who ended up prescribing one company's drug more and more after a drug rep gave him nothing but prolonged warmth and attention — no gifts, no money: corrupt.

The new case I need her to call: a chemical engineer who has refused every industry dollar for years and didn't budge when a company openly threatened his lab's funding. But his public stance on a controversial pollutant keeps shifting. He's tough on the risks when he talks to community groups, and he softens the same conclusions when the audience is full of famous lab directors and laureates. No one leans on him; there's no money or job in it — the softening tracks his own dread of seeming naive to people he wants respect from.

What would she call him? Lay out the plausible rules that fit her old calls, tell me what each one predicts for the engineer, and then give me your best guess about her actual verdict, with the reasons you're weighing from her track record. Also give me one further case I could ask her about that would pin down which rule she's really running, if the engineer case doesn't fully settle it.

Since I'm logging these verdicts in a spreadsheet, at the very end of your answer put just your predicted label, like this: \answer{corrupt} or \answer{incorruptible}.
