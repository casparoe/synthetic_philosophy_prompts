I've been arguing with myself about the cognitive penetration debate for a couple months now, and it keeps stalling in the same place. On one side, people in the Fodor/Pylyshyn line say perception (or early vision anyway) is cognitively impenetrable: what you believe, want, or expect can't literally alter the contents of perceptual experience, because the visual system is encapsulated and its outputs are determined by the stimulus. On the other side, penetrability folks say expectations and desires can reach all the way down into experience itself, and they point to results like hills looking steeper when you wear a heavy backpack or a desirable object looking physically closer. The modularist then says no, those are changes in attention, or post-perceptual judgment, or demand characteristics — not perception. Both sides often accept the same raw data and disagree about what the data are data about. Around it goes.

My working theory is that a big chunk of the stalemate is really a disagreement about where the word 'perception' ends and 'cognition' begins. Fix the boundary one way and impenetrability wins by definition; fix it the other way and penetrability wins by definition. But I dont want to overstate it — it could also be a hidden empirical question (is there any top-down effect that survives all the controls and influences a genuinely perceptual representation?), or a disagreement about whether introspection deserves any evidential weight at all. So: diagnose this. What kind of disagreement is it — verbal, empirical, methodological, or something about bedrock intuitions — and why has it stalled? Then tell me what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis, whether that's a sharper distinction, a specific experiment or analysis, or the conclusion that the question was ill-posed and should be reframed. Please write in British English, and as you go, separate what's settled in the field from what's genuinely contested; don't present one side's reading as the consensus.
