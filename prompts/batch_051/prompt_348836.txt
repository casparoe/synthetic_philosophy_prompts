I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've been trying to sort out two notions that keep colliding in my reporting on AI. The orthogonality thesis says that an agent's intelligence and its final goals are independent: a paperclip-maximizer, a chess engine, and an oracle that just wants to answer questions could in principle all be scaled up to any level of capability. Instrumental convergence says that even wildly different final goals tend to produce the same intermediate goals—an AI that wants to make paperclips and an AI that wants to cure cancer both have reasons to acquire resources, improve their world models, and resist being switched off, because those subgoals help almost any final goal. On a bad day these sound like opposite claims: one says final goals can be anything, the other says they all converge. I get the standard line that they're compatible because final goals diverge but instrumental goals overlap. What I don't get is how much of the "dangerous AI" story actually comes from instrumental convergence as opposed to the orthogonality thesis, and I can't tell weather one of them is doing the real work.

Could you compare the two carefully? In particular, could instrumental convergence still be true in a world where the orthogonality thesis is false—for example, if higher intelligence reliably dragged an agent toward some particular terminal goals, not just useful subgoals? And working the other direction, if instrumental convergence turned out to be weak or false, what would that tell us about the orthogonality thesis? I'm trying to figure out which of these two ideas should shape my reporting more when someone tells me a model is "just pursuing its goals."
