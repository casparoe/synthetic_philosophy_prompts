Analyze wartime rationing and the government-run thrift campaigns on the American home front during the Second World War as a case study in two readings of frugality: a deontological reading on which being frugal is a duty owed irrespective of its aggregate effects, and a consequentialist reading on which frugality matters because promoting it conserves scarce resources and maximizes welfare. I know the broad facts: the U.S. government rationed goods such as sugar, gasoline, tires, and meat, issued ration books, and ran saturation campaigns urging households to use things up, wear them out, make do with less, and go without; compliance was widespread, though black markets and hoarding also appeared. What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a summary. For each reading, identify the relevant players and say what that reading tells us each of them faced and should have done — the government, the compliant household, the black marketeer, the hoarder, and the household already living near the margin with little left to cut — and say what the propaganda was actually doing: prescribing a duty or managing outcomes. Then give an honest account of which features of the episode each reading explains and which it misdescribes, and say whether the episode taken as a whole supports the deontological reading, the consequentialist reading, or neither. Close by identifying one feature of wartime frugality that both readings struggle to capture. Write in American English; do not include citations or reading lists.
