I'm a high school student who spent several years living in Germany and has now moved back to the United States, and one everyday difference has stuck with me more than any other. In Germany, I noticed that pedestrians — including people I otherwise thought of as independent-minded — would stand at a red light late at night with no car visible in any direction, because crossing against the signal was simply not done. Back in the U.S., I see people glance at an empty street and cross against the light all the time, and they regard the signal more as a suggestion to be checked against their own judgment. Each side seems to find the other's behavior faintly unreasonable, yet both act as though they are being obviously sensible.

I want to understand what philosophical commitments each practice presupposes. Does the German habit presuppose that a rule's authority comes from something other than the individual's own assessment — from the value of social order, or from a duty to obey legitimate rules as such — so that following the rule does not depend on the particular consequences at 3 a.m.? Does the American habit presuppose that the individual's judgment of harm and reasonableness is the final standard, so that a walk signal is just a useful device rather than a source of obligation? Say where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change in each culture's everyday relation to rules if the other side's presupposed view were substituted for it.
