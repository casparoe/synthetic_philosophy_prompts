I am a novelist, not a philosopher or a computer scientist, and I have been reading the long-running dispute over machine translation in connection with a novel I am writing. By now the debate seems to me to have stalled completely. On one side, Hofstadter and those persuaded by him argue that statistical machine translation, neural or otherwise, is fundamentally shallow: it produces target-language text by manipulating surface patterns and co-occurrence statistics without grasping what it renders, so that even its successes are the successes of large corpora rather than of understanding. On the other side, defenders of modern neural translation reply that these systems demonstrably learn structured internal representations, track dependencies across languages, resolve ambiguity in ways that require contextual sensitivity, and generalize beyond any sentence their training data could have contained verbatim; from this they conclude that the systems encode something semantic enough that the difference between their shallowness and our competence is one of degree, not kind.

What strikes me is that both camps largely accept the same technical facts about how the systems are trained, and they mostly cite the same behavior as evidence: one side's polished output is the other side's proof of mere fluency, and one side's howler is the other side's anecdote. Yet nobody moves. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict for either camp. Is this a verbal dispute about the word "understand"? Is it a disagreement about what could ever count as evidence for mental predicates as opposed to evidence for behavioral adequacy? Is it a disagreement about what explanation is supposed to do: one side demanding a mechanistic story about the process, the other satisfied by input-output competence? Do the sides differ over whether understanding is a threshold concept or a graded one, or over whether ascribing it requires capacities the systems lack by construction, such as the ability to refuse a task, to be uncertain, or to mean something by the rendered sentence? Or is there a hidden empirical bet doing the work underneath, a bet about scale, grounding, interaction, or what future systems will be able to do, which the present conceptual vocabulary is obscuring?

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the two camps are largely answering different questions under one heading: one is asking whether a translated text works for a reader, the other whether the process that produced it resembles human semantic competence, with the single word "understanding" blurring the two. But I want that hypothesis tested, not assumed.

Diagnose this impasse with concrete attention to what each side actually maintains, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, an experimental design, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
