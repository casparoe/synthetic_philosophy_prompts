I'm putting together a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on judgment and decision-making, and I want an instrument that sorts students by what they actually believe about whether—and how—rationality can be trained, rather than by which label they prefer. Please write a nine-question quiz, titled something like "Which theory of rationality training do you actually hold?", with a scoring key.

Each question should present a concrete case: someone practices a specific form of reasoning (a base-rate drill, an "imagine the plan has already failed and ask why" exercise, a structured self-critique, a forecasting practice, a repeated decision journal, a rote expected-value calculation kata), and then faces a new problem that is structurally similar but superficially different—or a stress test such as time pressure or a politically charged dispute. Each question should offer two or three answer options that differ in the outcome the respondent predicts or in how they explain the outcome. No option may name a position or ask the student which account sounds right.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto five positions, each named and followed by one sentence on its most awkward commitment:

(a) General skill—rationality is a domain-general capacity, and deliberate practice in one demanding epistemic setting transfers widely, the way footwork or breath control transfers across martial arts.

(b) Drill and procedure—training improves judgment mainly by installing mechanical procedures, checklists, and recognizable cues, so transfer is real but mostly near transfer.

(c) Epistemic virtue—training works less by supplying algorithms than by shaping intellectual character: open-mindedness, willingness to revise, resistance to motivated reasoning.

(d) Craft and domain-specificity—good judgment is local expertise, and generic rationality training mainly yields the appearance of competence.

(e) Deflationary—what generic training mostly improves is confidence, fluency, and post hoc narrative skill, not accuracy.

To keep the quiz diagnostic rather than encyclopedic, build it from three axes rather than from the labels: optimism about far transfer; whether the supposed mechanism is procedural or dispositional; and whether judgment is general or domain-local. Include at least one far-transfer case (for example, from a spreadsheet expected-value habit to a decision about whether to end a relationship), at least one kata case in which a rote form is practiced repeatedly without deep understanding, and at least one case involving stress or motivated reasoning. Indicate which pairs of positions the instrument cannot cleanly separate and why; in particular, say where near-transfer questions leave general-skill and drill-and-procedure respondents indistinguishable, and where self-report makes epistemic-virtue and deflationary respondents hard to tell apart. Also note any answer pattern that scores into an empty or unattractive region of the logical space and briefly diagnose it. The handout should be self-contained: no citations, no named authors, and no reliance on technical vocabulary being defined elsewhere.
