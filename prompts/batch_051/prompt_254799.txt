I spent my career in the physical sciences and have come to philosophy only recently. The problem I keep circling is what deterministic chaos does to the Laplacian picture of a fully computable future. I would like to work through a variation on the standard thought experiment.

Start with the familiar demon: at a single instant it is given the exact positions and momenta of every particle together with the laws, and it has unlimited capacity to derive from them the entire future and past. Now change the case. The world remains deterministic—a complete state fixes exactly one successor state—but it is chaotic in the technical sense, with a positive Lyapunov exponent; take the error-doubling time to be one hour. The demon really is given the exact microstate, so there is no initial measurement error. What it lacks is not information but the ability to carry out a completed infinite computation. To produce an actual forecast, it must run a finite procedure—a numerical integration with a nonzero step, a truncated series expansion, or a simulation on a finite state space—and any such procedure carries some local error δ at every stage. That unavoidable local error is then amplified by the chaos exactly as an imprecise initial condition would be. For any fixed δ there is therefore a finite horizon beyond which the computed trajectory is no better correlated with the true trajectory than a point drawn at random from the same attractor; shrinking δ pushes that horizon out only logarithmically.

Work through what this variation does to the original argument. Does the Laplacian conclusion tacitly depend not merely on determinism but on a completed infinite computation, and is that idealization doing more philosophical work than the standard reading acknowledges? Is the resulting gap between determinism and predictability purely epistemic—a limitation of our measuring instruments and computing hardware—or does the case show something stronger, namely that even an agent holding the exact state cannot know the distant future by any finite procedure, so determinism does not by itself yield in-principle predictability? And if we additionally place the finite demon inside the world it must predict, so that its own computation becomes part of the dynamics, does the difficulty shift from chaos to self-reference? I would like a rigorous reconstruction that separates what the original argument strictly requires from what it merely suggests, and that says which standard view of chaos and determinism, if any, survives these modifications.
