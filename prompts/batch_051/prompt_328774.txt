For a seminar on medieval moral psychology I've been asked to write up where I actually stand on Abelard before the rest of the group takes turns picking at it, and the more I do it the less sure I am that my comittments hang together. I'm not interested in Abelard exegesis here; I just want an audit of my own list.

Here's roughly what I think: (1) Abelard is basically right that sin lies in consenting to what you take to be wrong, not in the outward act and not in the desire or impulse itself. (2) So a person who consents and then is stopped before doing anything is exactly as guilty as the person who carries the act out. (3) But the outward act and its consequences should weigh heavily in how a community punishes, reconciles with, and regards the offender — just not in guilt before God. (4) A desire or fantasy you entertain without consenting to it is not a sin, no matter how vivid or how often it returns. (5) At the same time, I think there is such a thing as sinning through culpable negligence — coasting along inside a grudge or a pattern of inattention without any explicit moment of consent. (6) I also think failing to resist a thought can amount to a kind of tacit consent built up over time. (7) And I think a person can be blameworthy for habits he acquired through past consents even when, at the moment of acting, he's on autopilot and not deliberating at all. (8) But I don't want to count genuinely accidental harms done with no awareness as sins.

Could you go through these and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other and how badly, which pairs only look as if they're in tension, what the cheapest fix is for each real tension, and which single revision would clean up the most? I have a sense something has to go, but I can't tell where the real problem is, and I'd rather not find out live.
