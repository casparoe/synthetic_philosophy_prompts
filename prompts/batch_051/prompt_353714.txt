I'm putting together notes for a seminar on proof and legal epistemology, and I keep noticing the same pattern: a lot of the papers spend two pages on the blue bus or the gatecrasher, use it to motivate their one big claim, and then build a whole theory on top. I'd rather work backwards. Before I read any more theories of legal proof, I want the full spec of what such a theory has to get right. So: write the desiderata for an adequate account of when bare statistical evidence is enough to support a finding against someone, in civil and criminal law. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Cover at least the blue bus (a plaintiff is run off the road by a bus, 80% of the buses on that route belong to one company, and there is no other evidence) and the gatecrasher (1,000 people attended a rodeo but only 499 paid; can the organizer win a penalty against an arbitrarily selected attendee, with nothing else to go on?). But don't let the list reduce to those two. I care about the fine structure: the difference between a high probability and good evidential weight or sensitivity; the fact that DNA match probabilities are statistical yet almost always admitted, so no theory can just say statistical evidence is always inadmissible; the distinction between what licenses belief, what licenses a verdict, and what licenses punishment or liability; and any result from the reference class problem, base-rate neglect, or probabilistic independence that an adequate theory can't quietly contradict. Also include something like the algorithmic case: a black-box risk score says 73% likelihood this particular defendant will flee before trial, with no human-narratable story attaching to them; does that pattern with the bus or not?

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of particular camps (say, is it a genuine requirement that the evidence be about the defendant as an individual, or is that just a causalist's prejudice?). Tell me which single requirement does the most work to exclude the standard views—the probability-plus-threshold picture, the knowledge account, the sensitivity or weight accounts, whatever else you know—and if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable given what we know about reference classes, just say so; that's the part I most want to see. Skip a detailed scorecard; I'll do that in my paper.

Write in American English. Then, as literally the last line of the response, give a machine-readable \answer{...} containing a short label for the single most exclusionary requirement.
