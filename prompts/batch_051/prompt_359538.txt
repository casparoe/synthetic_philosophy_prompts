After a seminar on calendrical conventions, I listened to two colleagues, Lena and Ravi, argue about whether the global civil calendar should keep its present epoch or adopt a new one, and about what role a year zero should play in whichever calendar we use. The argument went through several rounds, and by the end I genuinely could not referee it. I report it here from my notes.

Ravi opened: "The current global year number is not a neutral piece of bookkeeping. It takes the presumed birth of Jesus as the pivot of history, and it does so without a year zero, so the sequence literally goes 1 BCE, 1 CE. We could keep the same months and length of the year but renumber from the start of the Holocene, roughly when agriculture and the first settled societies emerged, so that this year would be about 12,025 HE. A date like that makes the calendar a genuinely common human project rather than a parochial inheritance, and a reform would also let us insert a year zero and remove a standing arithmetical incoherence."

Lena answered: "That gets the point of a civil calendar backwards. A calendar is a coordination device, not a historical essay. Nobody who writes the date is asserting anything about Jesus; they are using a token we all recognize. The present epoch is justified by the enormous network that already uses it. The Holocene proposal just replaces one arbitrary pivot with another: agriculture did not begin in a single year, and 'the Holocene' is a geological interval, not a datable event. Paying large transition costs for a different arbitrary pivot is not an improvement."

Ravi replied: "The alternatives are not equally arbitrary. Any cutoff through continuous history will be somewhat vague, but a cutoff can be chosen because of what it says about us—that we share a planetary and human history—rather than because one tradition's sacred history became administratively convenient. And the present epoch is not inert: it is a default endorsement that stays in place precisely because people refuse to see it as a choice. You cannot defend it by calling it a mere token while also refusing to consider what the token conveys."

Lena responded: "I am not refusing to consider it; I am saying the meaning has faded. Most users of the calendar never think about its origin, and for them the date is purely referential. If we make the calendar a meaning-bearing institution, we make every group's disagreement with the chosen meaning a reason to reopen the numbering. You think you are solving parochialism, but you are really installing a new orthodoxy—why the Holocene, rather than the first city, the first writing, the first humans, or the first moon landing? A neutral tool is preferable to a better sermon."

Ravi said: "That understates what 'neutral' requires. The status quo is not the absence of a sermon; it is a sermon so old it has become furniture. Saying 'keep it because it is familiar' grants the inherited framing a permanent incumbency, and that is not neutrality, it is path-dependence. A calendar used by everyone should be justifiable to everyone, not merely tolerable because they have no choice."

Lena came back: "But the justifiability of a unit is not the same as the justifiability of a creed. We do not ask whether meters, hours, or time zones are justifiable to everyone as symbols; we ask whether they coordinate well and how costly a change would be. The relevant harms of a new epoch are concrete—errors in record-keeping, software failures, retraining, the perpetual ambiguity of old documents—while its benefits are largely a symbolic gain that many people will not notice. That is a poor trade, and it remains poor even before we settle the year-zero question."

Ravi answered: "The year-zero question actually counts in my direction. You say we can leave the epoch alone, but the present scheme's missing year zero is a real defect: calculations that span the BCE/CE boundary require an extra correction, and it confuses anyone who learns that 1 BCE is not followed by 0. A reform is the natural occasion to adopt astronomical numbering across the board. One-time transition costs spread over centuries of future use are not a decisive objection; they are the price of correcting a flaw we would otherwise preserve forever."

Lena replied: "We can correct the flaw without renumbering the calendar: astronomers already use a year 0, and we could simply specify that the Common Era is computed astronomically when arithmetic crosses the boundary. And transition costs are not cleanly one-time; every old record, date-stamped document, and legacy system continues to carry the old numbering, so the two systems would coexist for a very long time. The burden of proof is on the reformer to show that the new frame is more than a change of label."

Ravi said, finally: "That burden can be met. 'Symbolic' is not a synonym for 'unreal.' The frame of a calendar is part of the public background that shapes which histories are salient and whose vantage point counts as the default. I would rather justify that background as a shared human era than as an accident of one tradition's administration. That is a reason, not merely a sentiment."

Lena concluded: "And I would rather justify a calendar by whether it is reliable and universally used than by what it says about history. A calendar that tries to speak to everyone will eventually say something that someone rejects, and then we are back in this argument with a different date. The present system may be an accident, but it is an accident that works."

Lena and Ravi then asked me, as the only person still listening, to say who was right. I could not. Please help me adjudicate their exchange. Specifically: where is each of them right and wrong about the nature of epoch choices, the force of transition costs, the meaning-bearing versus merely coordinating function of a civil calendar, and the proper role of a year zero? Then give your overall judgment: which of the two is, on balance, more right, and why?
