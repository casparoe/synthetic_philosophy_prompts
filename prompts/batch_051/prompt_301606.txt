Okay so I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm trying to keep the ethics in my book from being easy to pop. The setup: a bioengineering firm has created a servant species—call them squills—that are clearly bright enough to suffer but maybe not fully self-conscious. My protagonist, an ecologist, pushes a position through the whole book, and I want it to be defensible enough that a smart reader can't shred it in ten seconds. You're a professional philosopher working on value theory and moral status: think carefully, and before you answer, brainstorm the objection space a bit.

Here are my notes on her view. She starts from Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. IV, where the felicific calculus gets its ingredients:

"To a person considered by himself, the value of a pleasure or pain considered by itself, will be greater or less, according to the four following circumstances:

- Its intensity.
- Its duration.
- Its certainty or uncertainty.
- Its propinquity or remoteness."

Bentham then adds fecundity and purity, and, for a population, extent: the number of persons affected. My ecologist's claim is that this list is missing a a factor—how much of a mind is in the experience. She holds that the value of a pain episode is roughly consciousness × (intensity, duration, certainty...), a multiplier view. So a squill's agony gets intensity and duration, maybe even certainty, but a low coefficient on consciousness; ten thousand squill-days of suffering could be outweighed by one broken arm in a fully self-conscious human. She also flirts with a stronger, lexical version: below some threshold of having a self, pleasures and pains don't enter the ledger against a fully conscious being's interests at all. She calls the first the multiplier and the second the cutoff. The plot turns on wether the cutoff is monstrous.

Comment in these four sections, in order, with rough lengths:

1. The proposal restated (~80 words): put her multiplier/cutoff view in its strongest form, and say exactly what work the consciousness coefficient is supposed to do.
2. What Bentham supports (~120 words): from the quoted passage, say whether adding a consciousness multiplier is a natural extension of his calculus, or whether it quietly changes what the calculus is for.
3. Hard objections (~200 words): give me the two or three sharpest attacks on the multiplier version, and separately on the lexical version.
4. What I should keep (~120 words): advise me which version my ecologist can hold without becoming a strawman, and what she'd need to concede to survive a decent interlocutor.
