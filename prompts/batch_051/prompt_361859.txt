A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about whether the hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy are occasional defects in historical explanation or something close to its permanent condition, since the historian already knows how things came out and can hardly avoid turning a messy, contingent past into something that looks inevitable and fully explainable. I have just re-read Ian McEwan's Atonement, and the argument kept intruding: Briony Tallis turns a scattered sequence of half-understood episodes into a settled story that ruins Robbie and Cecilia, and the final section then reveals that the one consoling ending she wrote for them, in which they survive the war and are reunited, was her retrospective invention, framed as confession and atonement. I would like a careful reading of the novel through that argument rather than a reduction of the novel to an illustration. Where does the book dramatise the way hindsight lends events a spurious inevitability and coherence, and where does it resist the idea that any retrospective account can be trusted? Does Briony's narration at the end fall back into the very narrative fallacy the ending seems to expose, or does the novel manage to show something the philosophical argument cannot, perhaps about the difference between explaining the past and making reparations for it? I would also like a verdict on whether McEwan lets Briony have it both ways by supplying a beautifully shaped ending while telling us that beautifully shaped endings falsify the past.
