I am a PhD student in the philosophy of psychiatry, and I keep returning to a problem about Ian Hacking's looping-effects argument. On Hacking's picture, human kinds differ from natural kinds because they are interactive: the people classified can become aware of the classification and react in ways that alter the kind itself, forcing the classification to be revised — the kind loops. The mechanism seems to presuppose that the person can encounter the classification in a recognizable form, such as a diagnosis or a publicly circulating label. But classification is increasingly carried out in a very different way: predictive models trained on passively collected digital traces — phone-use patterns, sleep and movement data, message content, voice features — assign psychiatric risk scores or latent categories to people who may never be told the result. The features that drive the assignment need not correspond to any category the person could recognize or adopt as a self-description, and the classified person may have no opportunity to respond to the classification at all. Does Hacking's argument survive this development? I would like to understand which premise of the argument the development bears on, and whether the argument can be repaired in a form that preserves its central conclusion — for example, by relocating the loop to the model or the institution — or whether the looping-effects framework simply stops applying. I am interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in whether algorithmic classification is good or bad.
