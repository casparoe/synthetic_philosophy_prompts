Im prepping a unit on free riding for a discussion section, and I keep hitting the same problem: the stock examples (unions, public radio, shared sidewalks) all leave room for someone to say the free riders contribution would have made some small difference, or that it demoralizes the cooperators, or that if everyone did it the whole thing would collapse. I want a case that closes those exits, because the point of the unit is to see whether the fairness complaint against free riding can still stand once the harm-based complaint is taken away. So: construct a thought experiment where a person knowingly and freely benefits from an ongoing cooperative practice without contributing to it. The benefit is non-excludable in the technical sense (the practice can't stop them from using it), but they aren't forced to use it—they could decline at minor cost. Their contribution would genuinely make no difference: the practice is already producing its good at a stable level, extra help would be refused or left unused, and their non-contribution goes unnoticed. They aren't spiteful or anti-social; they value the practice and would chip in immediately if it ever looked like it might collapse. One paragraph, ordinary setting (neighborhood, workplace, community garden, whatever), no lawbreaking, no deception, no hidden stakes. The case should generate the verdicts from the theories, not stipulate them.

Then walk me through it. A harm-based or act-consequentialist view should say not contributing is permissible—maybe a bit shabby, but not wrong. A fairness or fair-play view should say it is wrong to take the benefit without sharing the burden. For each theory, show why it gives that verdict. Then isolate the feature that does the real work in the fairness verdict—my guess is it has to do with voluntarily accepting the benefit rather than merely having it land on you. Check what happens if you remove that feature: does the case collapse back into the familiar thought that unsolicited benefits impose no obligations? Finish with the strongest attack a defender of the harm-based view could make on the case, and say whether it can be blocked.
