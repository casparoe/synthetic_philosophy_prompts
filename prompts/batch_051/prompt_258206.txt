I'm a novelist working on a book about two evolutionary biologists who've spent years arguing past each other, and in the novel their department chair locks them in a room and won't let them out until they draft a joint statement of where they actually agree and where they genuinely part company. I need that document to be plausable enough that a working biologist reading it would nod and not cringe, so I'd like you to draft it for me.

The two positions:

(1) A gene's-eye view in the Dawkins/Williams line: the right default question about any trait is how the underlying variant affects its own expected transmission, and talk of organisms or groups as "units" is at best a shorthand.

(2) A multilevel selectionist in the Sober/Wilson-ish line: selection has to be tracked at multiple levels at once, and treatng the gene as the unit is a bookkeeping choice that quietly picks a winner.

Now write the document they'd produce. It should have:

- A numbered "What we both accept" section. Real substance, not bromides: what natural selection needs to get going (heritable variation, differential reproduction), what Hamilton's rule does and doesn't settle, the fact that genes replicate and organisms die, when individual and group interests line up and when they come apart. Each claim should be concrete enough to check.

- A disagreements table. Columns: the issue; the gene's-eye wording; the multilevel wording; and "verbal?" where you mark the rows you think are just the two camps using "unit" or "level" differently. Each side's wording has to be something a card-carrying member of that side would sign—no strawmen.

- A cruxes section: three or four sub-questions where a surprising empirical or theoretical result would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, name the concrete case, model, or observation they'd both agree bears on it—think a segregation distorter that biases its own transmission while lowering organismal fitness; sterile workers; a trait that lowers its bearer's fecundity but raises the local deme's or colony's productivity—and have each side write down, in advance, what it predicts and what would make it concede ground. Make the cases specific enough that the reader can see the experiment or comparison.

If part of their quarrel is just about words rather than biology, say which part and why.

After the document, the very last line should be a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that summarizes what the two of them jointly sign.
