For some years a colleague of mine in the philosophy of cognitive science has been assembling what she calls a ledger of reflective cases: occasions on which making someone think explicitly about their own thinking either improved or impaired the very performance or judgement at issue. Her settled cases, in the shorthand she records, are these:

- Students solving algebra problems while explaining each step to themselves outperform those who solve the same problems silently: she records an improvement.
- Experienced golfers instructed to attend closely to the mechanics of their putting stroke putt worse than when they are told simply to sink the putt: she records an impairment.
- Eyewitnesses who describe a face in words before attempting an identity parade are worse at identifying the face than those who complete an unrelated filler task instead: she records an impairment.
- Mock jurors who write out the reasons supporting their verdict before deliberation reach more accurate verdicts than those who do not: she records an improvement.
- Expert wine tasters who write tasting notes are later worse at matching the wines to their own earlier samples than tasters who judge silently: she records an impairment.
- Children learning a new board game improve faster when, before each move, they say aloud which rule they are testing: she records an improvement.

Next week's case is this. A professional chess player, during a timed tournament game, is asked to state aloud the principle or plan behind each move before making it. She must enter a single verdict: improvement or impairment.

Please do three things. First, set out the candidate rules that fit the ledger, and show briefly how each rule handles the settled cases; do not assume there is only one. Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about the chess case, and give your best prediction with the reasons you prefer that rule. Third, identify a further case that would do the most to separate the remaining contenders, and say what result on that further case would settle matters.
