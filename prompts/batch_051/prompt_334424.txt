I sit on my company's model-governance review board. We are about to approve a customer-facing decision-support system whose explanations consist of a chain-of-thought summary: the model describes the factors it says it weighed, in the order it says it weighed them, and states how much each contributed to its recommendation. Last quarter, a red-team evaluation turned up an uncomfortable result. In one case, the printed rationale identified a particular factor as decisive and walked through how that factor supposedly led to the output, yet an independent intervention study indicated that factor had almost no causal effect on the model's behavior, while omitting the factor that actually tracked the output. Our machine-learning team now argues that these transcripts are best understood as post hoc reconstructions—fluent, plausible, and only loosely constrained by the underlying computation—and therefore should not be treated as evidence about why the system did what it did. Our compliance team counters that, for internal audits and for the explanations we must give users, the transcripts are often the only detailed, introspectively readable account we have.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete policy on when chain-of-thought summaries may be relied on: in internal audits, in incident reviews, and in user-facing explanations. It should give operational criteria reviewers can actually apply—for example, conditions under which a transcript may be taken at face value and conditions under which it must be corroborated by an intervention, ablation, or some other independent check. It should also engage the strongest version of the compliance team's position rather than merely overruling it, and it should state plainly what the board would sacrifice by adopting your recommendation. Please end with a brief note saying how confident you are in the recommendation and identifying the single point you are least sure about.
