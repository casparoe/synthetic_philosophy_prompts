I have spent a good deal of time reading rationalist and effective-altruist-adjacent commentary, and I keep noticing how often an argument originally designed as a criticism of utilitarianism gets reimported into longtermist discussion more as a slogan than as a live piece of machinery. The argument I have in mind is the separateness-of-persons objection: the charge, associated above all with John Rawls, that utilitarianism treats society as if it were a single person whose pleasures and pains can be totaled up, and thereby fails to respect the fact that lives are separate and that each person's good makes a distinct claim. Rawls died before effective altruism and longtermism were available as self-conscious positions, so he never had to face strong longtermism directly—the view that, because the number of possible future people is so large, reducing existential risk is the dominant moral priority and may reasonably crowd out benefits to currently existing people.

I would like you to construct a counterfactual account of what Rawls would have thought about strong longtermism had it been formulated during his lifetime. Work strictly from commitments that are well established in his thought: the original position and the veil of ignorance, the priority of the basic liberties, the difference principle, and his particular diagnosis of where utilitarian aggregation goes wrong. Would he have said that the separateness-of-persons objection transfers straightforwardly to aggregation across merely possible future people, or would he have insisted that the original position already absorbs the problem by asking what principles would be chosen by parties who do not know which generation they will occupy? In particular, would he have regarded an expected-value argument that trades a certain benefit to existing people for an astronomically larger expected benefit to possible future people as the very kind of illicit interpersonal trade-off he was criticizing, or as a case his objection does not reach?

Finally, I would like the answer to be specific about how actuality and possibility matter on his view. Under Rawls's own framework, does the fact that future people are possible rather than actual, and that their number and identities depend on present choices, make a principled difference to how their claims may be weighted against the claims of existing people? And how would he have resisted or accommodated the longtermist claim that near-term poverty alleviation is dominated by far-future existential-risk reduction?
