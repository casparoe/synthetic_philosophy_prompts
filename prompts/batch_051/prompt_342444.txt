I've been arguing with a friend about whether a life of maximal moral goodness is actually something we should want people to aim at, and I keep reaching for a distinction I haven't seen defended anywhere. I call it concentrated versus distributed moral goodness. Concentrated goodness is what the moral-saint ideal seems to demand: pile as much of the moral point of view as possible into one life, until moral considerations saturate that person's motives, projects, and relationships. Distributed goodness starts from a different observation: the morally good behavior a society actually gets seems to be powered by a motley ecosystem of non-moral motives — curiosity, love of particular people, ambition, humor, pride in craft — which the saint would have to prune wherever they compete with impartial duty. My half-formed theory is that the familiar debate about the desirability of moral sainthood commits a concentration fallacy. It takes all the moral goodness that a society achieves through many ordinary, lopsided lives, imagines it concentrated into a single person, and then concludes either that morality is tyrannical or that we should approximate the concentrated version anyway. But moral goodness may be less like a balance that one diligent person should accumulate, and more like an ecosystem's productivity, which you maximize by letting many unimpressive species do their own thing rather than replacing the whole system with one high-yield crop.

The conclusion I'm tempted by: "Is maximal moral goodness desirable?" is the wrong question; the better one is "Which distribution of moral motives and projects across persons tends to produce the most morally good world overall, and is the saintly distribution optimal?" My friend thinks this is just self-serving relabeling — a way for ordinary people to count their moral mediocrity as a contribution to the social ecosystem. I want to know if she's right. Give me an honest assessment of the construct: is the distinction coherent, has someone already worked out this idea under a better name, what does it get right, and where does it break? If you had to name the three strongest objections to it, which of them, if any, are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable?
