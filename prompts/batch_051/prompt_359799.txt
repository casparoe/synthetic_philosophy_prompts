I am a philosophy professor preparing a colloquium paper, and what I need is a Q&A prep sheet rather than help with the paper itself. The thesis I am defending is a form of political pacifism: not the claim that individuals may never use lethal force in self-defense or in defense of others, but the claim that no modern state as actually organized may morally wage war. My argument runs roughly as follows. Even when a war satisfies the familiar just-war conditions on paper, the state's decision to fight is structurally defective: it is made by officials who do not bear the costs they impose; it depends on conscription, taxation, and propaganda directed at persons who cannot meaningfully authorize those impositions; and the epistemic situation of state officials—oversimplified intelligence, bureaucratic optimism, fixed timetables—makes the proportionality and last-resort conditions systematically unverifiable at the moment of decision. The problem, then, is not merely that wars tend to go badly in hindsight, but that the modern state is the wrong kind of agent to be entrusted with this decision in the first place, so the just-war requirement of a "competent authority" is never actually satisfied.

The audience is my department's political philosophy workshop: three just-war theorists (one Thomist, one Walzerian, one law-of-war scholar), a Kantian who works on authorization and public reason, a democratic theorist who writes on political obligation and civil disobedience, and two graduate students already sympathetic to pacifism. They are not hostile to pacifism in principle, but I expect them to think my view lets states off too easily and ordinary citizens off too easily, and they will press on whether refusing state war is a dereliction toward the vulnerable people such wars sometimes protect. Please act as an experienced just-war theorist who has sat through many such workshops. Think through the likely objections carefully before ordering them, and then give me a prep sheet ordered by how likely and how damaging each objection is: the objections phrased as the relevant member of this audience would phrase them, each with a short reply, a note on what I should concede and what I should hold, and an explicit indication of where the reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me honestly how likely it is that someone asks it anyway. If the thesis as I have framed it collapses internally, or if the audience I describe would not in fact press the objections you list, say so plainly rather than supplying a polished prep sheet for a doomed talk.
