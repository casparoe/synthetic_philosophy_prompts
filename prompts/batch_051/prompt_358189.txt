Write the desiderata that a theory of immigration control must satisfy to be adequate for deciding when, if ever, a wealthy liberal democratic country may exclude a non-citizen who wishes to enter and settle. I want the specification itself, not one more round of open-borders rhetoric; fix the purpose as that of evaluating candidates across the whole spectrum, from open borders to a near-unqualified right to exclude.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say which requirements are negotiable and which are not. Among the things a serious theory must handle are: the difference between a border and a landlord choosing tenants; the citizen's right to return; family reunification; refugees and climate-displaced persons; guest-worker and skilled-migration programmes; the fiscal and welfare-state objection; illiberal migrants whose integration may fail; temporary visitors who overstay; and the supposed right of a political community to self-determination. Do not let the list quietly presuppose either that migration is like trade in labour or that it is like membership in a club; these are among the things the desiderata must force a theory to confront.

Then, in a brief table, score the main existing positions against your list, and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude weaker candidates. Please answer in American English. At the end, say how confident you are in the overall framework and single out the point you are least sure of.
