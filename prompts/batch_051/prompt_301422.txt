I write about ecology and ideas, and I keep running into the same move in debates about wild animal suffering. The anti-intervention side says something like: nature is a balanced, self-correcting system; wild animals largely live the lives they are adapted to; so interfering is hubris and will just cause more harm somewhere else. But most ecologists abandoned the balance-of-nature picture a while ago. Populations overshoot and collapse, a huge fraction of animals die before reproducing, and the slow deaths—starvation, parasitism, being eaten alive—aren't a malfunction of an otherwise healthy system; they're part of how the system actually runs. I'm trying to figure out whether this kills the leave-nature-alone argument, or just forces it to claim something weaker. I'd like the answer in four sections, in this order: 'The argument's working parts' (100-150 words, what the anti-intervention position has to assume), 'What the new ecology undercuts' (100-150 words, which premise is under pressure), 'What survives' (100-150 words, the strongest repaired version of the argument, or why none is available), and 'Bottom line' (40-60 words).
