I keep coming back to Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self, and I've started to wonder whether it survives the one development he obviously never had to deal with.

The argument, as I understand it: the I is not some final homunculus sat behind the symbols; it is a self-referential symbol, a loop. The brain's symbols point at each other, eventually the system comes to refer to its own referring, and the thing that says I just is that loop, not a substance peeking out from behind it. The original evidence for this was Gödel-style self-reference and his analogy-making programmes — cases where you could, in principle, point at the machinery looping back on itself.

But now the systems that produce the most fluent self-talk are LLMs, and they don't look like the theory's diagrams at all. They are trained to predict text; whatever self-model they show is assembled in context and can be dropped or contradicted a prompt later. There is no persistant loop in the weights that keeps running and re-entering itself over time — at least none anyone has pointed at — and yet the thing says I and reasons about its own outputs rather well.

So does the original argument survive this? I can steelman both answers. Either the loop was always meant functionally, a pattern that counts as self-reference no matter what substrate runs it, in which case an in-context loop might qualify and the argument just sheds some old implementation assumptions. Or the argument depends on the loop being a long-lived, self-sustaining structure, in which case LLM self-talk is a convincing imitation and the account needs a new premise (persistence? embodiment? something else) to stay relevant. The thing I actually want to know is: which premise takes the hit, and is there a repaired version that still explains why the I feels like a single continuing thing rather than just a pronoun that got generated.
