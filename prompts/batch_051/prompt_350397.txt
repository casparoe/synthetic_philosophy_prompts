I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, working on a feature about whether the concept of a moral right still earns its place in public argument. Please write an explanatory dialogue for my readers between two equally sophisticated philosophers with opposing views. The prose and dialogue should be in polished British English.

The first character defends rights as side constraints rather than trumps. On this view, a right is not simply an especially weighty interest that usually wins in practical calculation; it is an agent-relative restriction on what one may do to a person, even in pursuit of good ends. This character should not be a caricature and should not merely insist that rights are absolute. The dialogue should force them to explain how constraints can have thresholds, how rights can conflict, and how the side-constraint view differs from the claim that rights are just very strong interests.

The second character is an instrumentalist about rights. On this view, rights language is useful but derivative: it compactly marks especially weighty interests, assigns institutional roles, and raises the burden of justification, but it does not name an independent moral kind. This character should be equally sophisticated and should grant that rights vocabulary often does genuine practical work; their claim is that it adds no distinct normative force.

Set the dialogue in a university hospital research ethics committee. The committee is considering whether to approve a study that would link previously collected genomic and lifestyle data to neighbourhood-level location records to improve early detection of a serious illness. The data would be de-identified, but the linking makes a minority of patients re-identifiable in practice. Most patients have given broad consent, but a small number have explicitly objected. The committee must decide what those objections should mean.

The committee setting should remain the dramatic frame, but the characters should use it to reach the underlying question: what rights are, whether they constrain institutions in a way ordinary interest-balancing cannot, and whether the language of rights does any work beyond marking strong interests.

The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth, with each character answering the other's strongest point rather than talking past it. It should include at least the following moves:

- an exchange on whether proceeding over someone's objection would wrong that person or merely set back an interest;
- an exchange on how a defender of side constraints avoids the charge that their view lets small risks of harm hold large populations hostage, and whether a threshold on constraints is distinguishable from the instrumentalist's balancing;
- a passage in which the instrumentalist argues that side constraints, once leavened with thresholds, are simply trumps in disguise and asks what further work the idea of agent-relativity is doing;
- a response from the side-constraint theorist explaining that a constraint is not just another interest to be aggregated, because it concerns what the decision-maker may do rather than what merely happens;
- a passage in which the instrumentalist develops a deflationary account of rights as tripwires that trigger closer scrutiny and shift the burden of justification but do not themselves settle outcomes;
- a rebuttal from the side-constraint theorist arguing that tripwires cannot capture the directed character of rights, the sense in which a right is owed to the bearer rather than being a general reason to promote or protect something;
- a counter-reply from the instrumentalist explaining how directed duties might be fully explained by the roles institutions assign and the goods those roles serve.

Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words. Do not use footnotes, references, or named citations, and do not attribute the positions to particular authors; the piece should stand on the arguments themselves.
