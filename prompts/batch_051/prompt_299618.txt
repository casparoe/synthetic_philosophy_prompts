You are grading an upper-level undergraduate exam in a course on moral psychology. Below are the question and one student's answer.

Question:
Explain the situationist critique of virtue ethics. Your answer should do three things: state the psychological commitments the critique attributes to virtue ethics, describe the main experimental evidence the critics appeal to, and assess how damaging the critique is, distinguishing the conclusions that do follow from the evidence from those that do not.

Student answer:
The situationist critique, associated with John Doris and Gilbert Harman, holds that virtue ethics rests on a false moral psychology. Virtue ethics, especially in Aristotle, is committed to the existence of stable global character traits, such as honesty and compassion, that reliably produce appropriate behavior across a wide range of situations. The social-psychological evidence is supposed to show that no such traits exist.

In Darley and Batson's Princeton seminary study, theology students on their way to give a talk, in some conditions a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan, encountered a man slumped and apparently in distress in a doorway. Whether they stopped to help depended heavily on how much of a hurry they had been put in, while measures of their religiosity made no difference. In Isen and Levin's study, people who had just found a dime in a phone booth were far more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers than people who had not. In Milgram's obedience experiments, ordinary people were willing to administer what they took to be painful or dangerous electric shocks to another person when instructed to do so.

These studies are taken to show that behavior is controlled by small features of the situation rather than by stable dispositions. People do not, on this picture, have global character traits: if they did, there should be high correlations between how they behave in one kind of situation and how they behave in another, but the observed correlations are low, around .3 or less. Character, as ordinary thought understands it, is therefore largely an illusion. Since virtue ethics instructs us to cultivate exactly these global traits, the critique shows that the central normative project of virtue ethics is undermined: there is nothing of the required sort to cultivate. Virtue ethics is therefore empirically refuted, and attempts to save it by weakening virtue into narrowly situation-specific dispositions merely replace the theory with something else.
