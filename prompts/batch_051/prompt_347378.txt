I have been developing a distinction for thinking about Wittgensteinian hinges, and I want an honest assessment of it. I distinguish 'standing hinges' from 'deciding hinges'. A standing hinge is a certainty whose removal would dissolve the subject matter of an inquiry: the certainty that there is an external world, for instance, is what lets empirical questions be about anything at all. A deciding hinge, by contrast, is a certainty whose removal would leave the subject matter intact but deprive the inquiry of its capacity to reach verdicts: the certainty that perception and memory are generally trustworthy, or that the future resembles the past, is what lets evidence settle which claims to accept. My central claim is that these two kinds of hinge fail in different ways, and that this explains a real difference between two skepticisms. External-world skepticism attacks a standing hinge, and I take the right response to be showing that the doubt presupposes what it challenges. But Humean skepticism about induction attacks a deciding hinge, and the same response seems unavailable there, because any argument for the relevant certainty already employs the very capacity to settle inductive questions that is at issue. Is the distinction coherent, or do the two kinds collapse? Does something like it already exist in the hinge-epistemology literature under another name? What does it get right, and where does it break?
