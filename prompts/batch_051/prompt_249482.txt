I recently began working daily with a large language model, and I find myself oscillating between two reactions. On some days it seems to display exactly the kind of open-ended, context-sensitive flexibility I associate with genuine understanding; on other days I remind myself that it is, at bottom, a fixed set of weights computing a probability distribution over next tokens. This has made an old philosophical caution personally urgent for me: the story of the digger wasp and the idea of sphexishness.

As I remember the story, a digger wasp returns to its burrow with a paralyzed cricket, leaves the cricket at the entrance, goes inside to inspect the burrow, comes back out, and drags the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside, the wasp carries it back to the entrance, inspects the burrow again, and repeats the whole sequence indefinitely, never simply dragging the cricket straight in. The behavior looks purposeful in the normal course of events, but the small perturbation reveals it to be a fixed routine that cannot adapt.

The argument the story was used to support is, compressed, something like this: do not infer genuine intelligence, understanding, or choice from behavior that merely looks flexible in the environment in which it normally occurs, because that apparent flexibility may be an illusion produced by a rigid mechanism that happens to fit that environment.

The development that now unsettles me is the language model itself. Such a system responds coherently to novel prompts, follows instructions it has not literally seen before, and revises its output when challenged. On the surface it passes the very perturbation tests the wasp fails. Yet the process producing this behavior is in an important sense no less fixed than the wasp's: a static network, trained once, mapping each context to a distribution over continuations, with no persistent goals, no monitoring of its own activity, and no preferences among the outcomes it describes. The flexibility sits in the behavior; the rigidity has moved down a level, into the parameters and the training objective.

I want to know what has happened to the sphexishness argument in light of this. Does it survive intact, with the lesson now being that even behaviorally flexible systems can be sphexish when the flexibility is generated by a fixed mechanism? Does it need repair, because its original test — whether the system adapts when the environment is perturbed — is one the language model passes, so the defect must be located elsewhere, perhaps in the absence of a self-model, of reasons-responsiveness, or of intrinsic goals? Or is the argument dead, because the wasp story did its work through the narrowness of the wasp's repertoire rather than through anything about the underlying mechanism? I would like an answer that identifies the exact premise of the old argument the new case bears on, and says whether that premise can be restated so the argument still does useful work.
