I follow a small online discussion group for people who like hard theological puzzles, and since I have no formal background in philosophy or theology I mostly just listen, re-read the threads, and try to make sense of what people are saying. A while back, two members I'll call Daniel and Ruth got into a long argument about the first fall — how an angel such as Satan could have made the very first evil choice if he was created good, with free will and no prior temptation to explain it. I've never been able to sort out who was right. The exchange went roughly like this.

(Dan) We start with an angel created good — no body, no passions, no bad company, nothing outside his will that could pull him toward evil. So the first bad act had to come entirely from inside him, as a free act. But that is the puzzle: how does a will that is entirely good, on its very first bad move, produce evil out of itself?

(Ruth) I think the puzzle is worse than you're making it. A person can only choose what appears good to him. A good will sees the good accurately and loves it. So before the first sin there was nothing in the angel that could prefer a worse thing to a better one. If he chose badly, then either he knew it was bad — and nobody chooses a known evil as evil — or he didn't know it was bad, in which case he made an honest mistake, and an honest mistake isn't a sin. Either way, the first sin looks impossible.

(Dan) He didn't choose evil as evil. He chose something genuinely good — his own excellence — but ranked it above a greater good. The sin is in the ranking, in putting a lesser good first, not in wanting something bad. And he wasn't ignorant of the greater good the way you mean. He let his attention settle on himself instead of keeping the higher good in view. The failure is in what he attended to, not in what he knew.

(Ruth) But "letting his attention settle" is already the wrong ranking. You've moved the sin from the choice to the attending, which doesn't solve anything — it just relocates it. An angel doesn't have wandering attention the way we do, with distractions and fatigue. Either attending to self over God looked good to him or it didn't. If it looked good, that's a false appearance — an intellectual defect, not a sin. If it didn't look good, then he chose it as not-good, which you and I both said nobody does.

(Dan) I don't think the attending and the preferring are two separate events. The attending is the preferring — it is one act. There's no earlier moment in which the ranking was already bad, and no earlier bad character to cite, because there was no earlier bad character. A free act can be its own origin. If you demand an earlier bad state to explain the first bad act, you've made "first bad act" contradictory by definition, and that shows something is wrong with your demand, not with the idea of the fall.

(Ruth) Then here's my problem. If the first bad act is its own origin, with nothing in the angel's nature leading to it, then a good nature spontaneously produced evil — which is the very thing we were trying to explain. If the first act simply arises without anything determining it, then whether it is good or bad looks arbitrary, and "free choice" becomes a name for a bare fact rather than an account of how evil could arise. I can accept that the origin might be a mystery. I can't accept your answer as an explanation of the mystery.

(Dan) I'd accept that, if I thought any full explanation were possible. But notice what a full explanation would have to do: it would cite a reason for the first bad act, and if that reason justified the act, the act would be good rather than evil; if it didn't justify the act, it wouldn't fully explain it. So a complete explanation of the first evil act is exactly the thing we should not expect. The best available account cites a real good he wanted, a failure to attend, and a will capable of putting itself first without a prior cause. That is a partial explanation. The leftover is what freedom costs. I wouldn't call it a bare fact; I'd call it the price of a creature who can love rather than merely comply.

(Ruth) But I never asked for a justification — that's your word. I asked how the wrong preference could form at all. A partial explanation is fine. Show me how a will that is entirely ordered to the good actually produces a wrong ordering without already being wrong. "Freedom makes it possible" tells me why God might allow it; it doesn't tell me how the angel's will did it. I'm not even saying your conclusion is false. I'm saying that, so far, we can't tell the difference between "nobody has found the mechanism" and "the very idea is incoherent." And those are very different places to end up.

I reread this exchange several times and could never decide who had the better case, because it seemed to me that each of them was right at some steps and possibly overreaching at others. Could you go through the argument move by move and adjudicate it: who is right about which specific point, where each person's reasoning slips — including any step that sounds reasonable but isn't, or sounds wrong but is actually on to something — and what, if anything, both of them are missing? Then give an overall verdict, but only as strong as the arguments support. And so that I know how much to lean on your verdict, please also tell me which single argument or consideration, if it went differently, would most change your overall judgment, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
