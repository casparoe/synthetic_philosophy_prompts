In my philosophy of criminal law seminar we have been considering what a line of decided cases commits a court to when a legislature is silent about fault. I would like you to work through the following problem about punishment without fault.

Consider the Court of Review, a fictional appellate court in a common-law jurisdiction whose statutes frequently omit any mental element and which has no general provision telling it how those silences are to be interpreted. It therefore decides new cases by analogy to its own past decisions. Its track record on liability without fault is as follows.

In re Harland Dairies. A dairy sold milk adulterated with a banned preservative. The adulteration would have been caught by the routine batch testing that was standard among dairies of that size, though the statute did not require it and the owner had performed no tests. The Pure Food Act punished the offense by fine alone. The court affirmed the conviction without proof of knowledge or negligence, describing food-safety statutes as public welfare measures for which the legislature may impose liability without fault.

In re Penn. A defendant took a stranger's near-identical coat from a cloakroom under the honest and reasonable belief that it was her coat. She was convicted of petty larceny, an offense of dishonesty punishable only by a fine. The court reversed. It held that theft is a true criminal offense whose conviction carries stigma, that a reasonable mistake of fact negates the mental element unless the legislature says otherwise with unmistakable plainness, and that the modest penalty did not make the offense one of strict liability.

In re Toller. A haulage proprietor operated a commercial truck whose safety inspection had lapsed because a bookkeeper failed to file the renewal; the proprietor had no actual knowledge of the lapse. The offense carried a fixed small fine. The court affirmed the conviction, holding that inspection and permitting requirements are public welfare offenses and that a commercial operator is taken to know the condition of its operating papers.

In re Nave. A factory's settling pond discharged sediment into a salmon stream during a storm. The company's own operating plan required weekly inspections of the pond, but the inspections had not been performed for two months, and the prosecution offered no separate proof of fault. The Clean Waters Act punished the violation by fine. The court affirmed without proof of negligence, emphasizing that environmental protection is a public welfare subject and that the defendant voluntarily operated a facility whose characteristic risks it was in a position to understand.

In re Garth. A defendant remarried after a solicitor told him that his first marriage had been dissolved; the advice was wrong, but the defendant's reliance was reasonable and followed sincere inquiry. He was convicted of bigamy under a statute silent about fault and punishable by up to seven years' imprisonment. The court reversed. It held that bigamy is an offense of moral turpitude, that its penalty and stigma take it outside the public welfare category, and that a reasonable mistake of fact negates liability.

Now the same court must decide In re Mire. The chef-owner of an oyster bar served raw oysters bought from a licensed distributor that had passed a mandatory inspection shortly before delivery. Several patrons became seriously ill from a pathogen that, at the time, could not be detected by any commercially available test and was not indicated by the appearance, smell, or paperwork accompanying the oysters. The prosecution conceded that the chef had no actual knowledge, that she had followed all established handling practices, and that no failure of care was alleged; it rested entirely on the Food Safety Act, which covers food-borne injury and authorizes a fine, imprisonment of up to one year, and disqualification from running a food business. The only state-of-mind evidence the prosecution offered is that the chef knew raw shellfish carry some risk of food poisoning. The question before the court is whether liability attaches without proof of at least negligence.

Write the analysis a law clerk should produce for the bench. Reconstruct the most defensible candidate rules that would fit the earlier decisions, stated precisely enough to decide cases; say what each candidate rule requires in In re Mire; identify the closest precedent and explain whether the precedents govern the case or underdetermine it; and recommend a result if the court wishes to remain coherent with itself, stating explicitly which principle it would be adopting under each available choice. If more than one principle remains live after Mire, specify a further case that would best separate them and say what each principle predicts for that case. Where the earlier decisions do not force a unique answer, say so plainly, and make clear which rule the court would be committing itself to by each possible disposition of Mire.
