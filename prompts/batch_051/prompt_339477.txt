I cover the mind-and-brain beat for a science magazine, so I sit through a lot of consciousness talks, and one from last spring has been nagging at me ever since. The speaker was defending epiphenomenalism — the view that conscious experience is real but causally inert: the brain causes your experiences, but your experiences never cause anything back, not even the next brain event. You still have the pain, the red afterimage, the pang of regret; none of it does any work. During the Q&A someone pressed her on what people call the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and she kind of sidestepped it, and I've been trying to figure out whether she was entitled to. Here is the objection as I scribbled it down afterwards.

(1) If epiphenomenalism is true, then a conscious experience — say, the pain in my hand — never causes any physical event, including anything in my brain. (2) My belief that I am in pain, and my saying 'I'm in pain,' are physical events. (3) So if epiphenomenalism is true, my pain does not cause either my belief that I am in pain or my report of it. (4) A belief about one of my own conscious states is justified only if that conscious state, or something appropriately tied to it, helps produce the belief — otherwise the belief matches the state by luck, and luck isn't evidence. (5) So if epiphenomenalism is true, my belief that I'm in pain is not justified. (6) But our own first-person judgments are the main evidence we have that conscious experience exists at all. (7) So epiphenomenalism, if accepted, destroys it's own evidential base.

I keep coming back to step 4: maybe the epiphenomenalist can say the brain state causes both the experience and the belief, and that's connection enough. But then the experience is still causally out of the loop, and the belief would have formed even without it — which sure sounds like luck. So: is the argument valid, and if one step fails, which one? If it doesn't fail, is there any honest way for an epiphenomenalist to keep saying 'I have experiences' without that belief being a fluke? Walk me through the moves.
