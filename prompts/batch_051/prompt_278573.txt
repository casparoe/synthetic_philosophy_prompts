I'm a retired chemist, and I've been reading alot of decision theory and philosophy since I left the lab. Last weekend at a dinner party, a friend ran a dollar auction: she held up a dollar bill, the highest bidder pays their bid and gets the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays their last bid but gets nothing. Two guests, Maya and Jon, then argued about it in the kitchen and I honestly couldn't settle it. Here's roughly what they said.

Maya: The whole game is a trap. A rational person shouldn't enter at all. Once two people start, each one keeps topping the other because dropping out means eating your previous bid, and the next bid always looks cheaper than that. It's a machine for turning a dollar into a loss for everybody.

Jon: Sure, not entering is smart. But that just pushes the problem back. Suppose you're already in. If I've bid ninety cents and you've bid a dollar, then dropping costs me ninety cents, while bidding a dollar ten costs me only ten cents. At that moment, bidding past a dollar is the less bad option, so you can't call it irrational.

Maya: You can, because the ninety cents is sunk. It's gone whatever you do. The rational comparison is only about future costs: dropping costs you nothing more, while bidding again costs you another dime. Wincing about the ninety cents is the sunk cost fallacy.

Jon: But that's not how this auction works. The ninety cents isn't gone if I win; I never pay it. It only becomes a loss if I stop and end up second. So it's not sunk, it's conditional on how the auction ends. The real comparison is pay ninety and get nothing, or pay a dollar ten and get a dollar. That's negative ninety versus negative ten.

Maya: Fine, the accounting is trickier than I said. But your negative-ten outcome assumes the other bidder stops. They face the same logic: if you bid a dollar ten, they're choosing between losing a dollar and bidding a dollar twenty, which loses only twenty cents. The local calculation just ratchets up. So treating each bid as "the smaller loss" is myopic; it ignores the next step you're inviting.

Jon: But each next step is a separate decision, and I can drop at any one of them. The fact that the other person might keep topping me doesn't make any particular bid wrong. What's the alternative at ninety cents, deliberately losing ninety cents to avoid a hypothetical bigger loss? Given that we're already in, continuing is the best available option each time.

Maya: Exactly, and that's why a genuinely rational agent would see this coming and either not enter, or commit to a hard cap beforehand. If you don't have an enforceable stopping rule, you're not deciding at each step; the auction is deciding for you.

Jon: But a cap you're free to abandon is worthless, because the same local logic will tell you to break it as soon as it binds. Unless you bring in something external, like a promise to your spouse or some penalty for breaking the cap, commitment isn't available. So the real lesson isn't that people are too weak to ignore sunk costs; it's that the rules plus ordinary rationality can force a ruinous race where no individual step is to blame.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? I'd like to know who's right about which specific claim, whether there's anything they're both missing, and which of them is more right overall.
