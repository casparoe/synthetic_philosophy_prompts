In Plato's Meno, Socrates is using this image (I'm quoting from the Perseus text): "To possess one of his works which is let loose does not count for much in value; it will not stay with you any more than a runaway slave: but when fastened up it is worth a great deal, for his productions are very fine things." He then says he means true opinion, which is a fine possession while it stays.

I've been arguing with a friend about this because of our neighbor. He's an old farmer who predicts the weather three days out better than the local met service. He's not doing it from any understanding of physics; he goes by whether the bees are staying close to the hive, how the smoke from the sugar factory bends, the feel of the wind, stuff like that. When you push him for a why, he says something like "the air's got a heavy smell" or just shrugs. His forecasts are right a lot, but he can't tie them down with the kind of account Socrates wants. My friend says Plato would still rank the meteorologist's knowledge over the farmer's true opinion, even on the days they say the same thing, because the farmer's opinion is one of those un-tied statues. I'm not so sure, because the farmer's opinions have been stable for forty years; they don't actually run away. So what does the Meno passage actually commit Plato to here, as a test for why knowledge beats true belief? And if his test says the farmer is worse off epistemically, should we accept the test, or does the case expose a flaw in it? Please keep the answer grounded in the actual text, not just the slogan, and separate what's settled in Plato scholarship from what's genuinely contested.
