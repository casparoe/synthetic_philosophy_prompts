You're a legal philosopher who works on the rule of law and how statutes live or die. Think carefully and walk through the old argument premise by premise.

I keep going back and forth about whether the classic rule-of-law objection to desuetude survives a world where enforcement is automatic and basically free. The objection, as I understand it, runs: rule of law requires congruence between the law on the books and the law as officials actually enforce it. When legislators leave a statute sitting around for decades while enforcers quietly ignore it, you get a gap between the written rules and the real ones. Citizens can't use the statute book to predict what will happen to them, and officials end up governing through unannounced non-enforcement instead of through public rules. So desuetude on that view is a defect, not just dead-letter trivia.

Now suppose a jurisdiction installs near-zero-cost universal enforcement: sensors and algorithmic systems detect every violation and issue sanctions automatically, so any statute still on the books gets enforced. Desuetude becomes technically impossible—the gap the objection worried about dissappears.

My question: does the objection actually survive that change, or was it doing its work behind an assumption it never stated? I can picture two moves. One says the mismatch is gone, so the rule-of-law problem is solved, and you're just left with ordinary policy worries (privacy, overbreadth, stupid old laws). The other says the real target of the objection was never the bare mismatch—it was something like the idea that enforcement effort is the only ongoing signal of a law's democratic warrant—and automatic enforcement just relocates that problem rather than cures it. Which premise in the old argument carries the weight, and does it still hold under the new assumption? I want a verdict on whether the argument survives, needs repair, or inverts into an argument against enforcement-by-default—and if repair is possible, what the fixed version would claim.
