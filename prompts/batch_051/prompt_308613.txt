Exam question (20 points): Explain what Gödel's modal version of the ontological argument is designed to achieve that Anselm's original formulation does not, state as precisely as you can what the modal proof establishes if its axioms are granted, and explain where the argument is most vulnerable to philosophical objection.

Student answer: Anselm's version begins with the concept of God as the being than which no greater can be thought. If such a being existed only in the understanding and not in reality, then a greater being could be thought, namely one that also exists in reality; therefore God must exist in reality. Kant's objection is that existence is not a predicate that adds content to a concept: to say that x exists does not alter the concept of x, so the argument is trying to derive real existence from the mere contents of a concept, and that move is illegitimate. Gödel's modal version is meant to avoid Kant's problem. Formally it is set in the modal system S5 and employs a family of positive properties. Gödel defines a positive property as one that it is better to have than not to have, so an axiological claim is doing real work from the outset. A God-like individual is defined as one that has all positive properties, and necessary existence is stipulated to be a positive property. The proof then shows that if the existence of a God-like individual is possible, then such an individual exists in every possible world; by S5 this yields necessary existence and hence actual existence. The proof does not on its own establish the possibility premise, so the debate ultimately turns on whether a God-like individual is possible. Because the proof still treats existence as a positive property of individuals, Kant's criticism applies to Gödel's version in essentially the same way it applies to Anselm's: nothing about going modal removes the original problem.
