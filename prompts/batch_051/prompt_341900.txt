Last night a friend and I argued deep into the evening after a seminar on the replication crisis, and I left unsure which of us had yielded what. I am drawn to the Bayesian account of evidence: it is coherent, it satisfies the likelihood principle, and it says optional stopping is irrelevant because the posterior already reflects exactly what the data support. But my friend kept pressing an intuition I cannot shake: science is a repeated, public enterprise, and what matters to a community of inquirers is how often its methods go wrong in the long run. A literature that reported posterior quantities would have no built-in analogue of error control; researcher degrees of freedom in model and prior choice could generate overconfident findings without end, whereas frequentist methods at least make false positives rare across replications. Present both positions at their strongest. Then tell me what it would take for the long-run-error intuition to be debunked and abandoned, what it would take for the Bayesian account to be amended (prior registration, calibration checks, a separation of private belief from public decision), and whether there is a reading of "inference" on which both can be retained.
