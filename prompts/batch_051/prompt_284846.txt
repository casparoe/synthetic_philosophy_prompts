A friend and I spent Saturday evening arguing about whether large language models can make genuine intuitive leaps — the sort of move that is not merely a novel-looking recombination of material already in the training data, but that reaches something genuinely outside the training distribution. By the end I was defending the leap view, and he was defending the recombination view. We have argued enough to know we are not going to settle it by restating our positions, so we agreed to write the document an adversarial collaboration between our two camps would produce.

The two positions to treat as parties:

- Leap view: a model can, under some conditions, produce conclusions or ideas that are genuinely beyond its training distribution in a sense strong enough to count as an intuitive leap, not just a high-dimensional interpolation.
- Recombination view: a model only ever recombines, interpolates within, or compresses patterns from its training distribution; anything that looks like a leap is a sampling artifact, and "outside the training distribution" has no non-arbitrary reading.

Please draft the joint statement they could both sign. This is not a request for a verdict on who is right, nor a transcript of our argument; it is the constructive statement both camps could put their names to. The statement should contain:

(1) a numbered list of claims both accept — about how LLMs are trained, what next-token prediction and in-context learning can and cannot show, what held-out benchmarks do and do not settle, and what "training distribution" can be made to mean;
(2) precise formulations of the residual disagreement, each phrased so a reasonable holder of that view would accept the wording as their own;
(3) the cruxes — the sub-questions that would move at least one side if settled (I suspect one crux is whether any non-arbitrary line between inside and outside the learned distribution can be drawn, and another is whether the recombiner's own test rules out human intuitive leaps too);
(4) for each crux, a concrete case, test, or experiment both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a table or tables for the disagreements and cruxes. Keep the whole statement under 1,100 words, write it in American English, and do not add citations, footnotes, or a bibliography — I want a clean text we could each put our names to.
