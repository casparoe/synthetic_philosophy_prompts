I keep noticing a pair of judgments in myself that I cannot easily reconcile, and I want you to tell me whether they can be reconciled or whether I should change one of them. Consider someone who acquires a puppy in order to frighten, hurt, and then abandon it. I take it nearly all of us regard this as serious cruelty, even if the harm is brief and no one else witnesses it. Now consider a "digital dog": a detailed simulation whose state variables include a valenced aversive module that registers damage, produces pain-like signals, and drives avoidance and escape behavior the way a biological pain system does. Suppose someone spends an evening tormenting the digital dog solely to watch it squirm. I do not feel the same horror at the second case, and I suspect most people do not, even after I stipulate that the two systems share the relevant functional organization: the state-to-state transitions, the causal role of the pain signal in reorganizing later behavior, and the input–output mapping are the same. If a mind is the computational pattern rather than the matter it runs on, then substrate should be morally inert; if it is not, I want to hear the principle that places the carbon case on one side of the line and the silicon case on the other.

The obvious reply is that my revulsion tracks fur, faces, and the smell of blood rather than abstract functional structure. But a causal or evolutionary story about why I react differently is not yet a justification; I asked for a principle. So here is the task: construct the strongest candidate principle you can that generates both judgments—that hurting the biological dog is seriously wrong, and that tormenting the functionally equivalent simulation is permissible or at most trivially wrong. Then stress-test that principle against nearby cases: a robotic dog running the same architecture, the same control system realized in biological neurons, an alien with different chemistry but an equivalent pain architecture, and a simulation about whose consciousness we have independent high-confidence evidence. Then take an honest position: should this asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up? Write this as a coherent, fairly structured essay that reaches a definite verdict rather than ending in agnosticism. Finish with a machine-readable line consisting only of \answer{keep}, \answer{reverse}, or \answer{give-up}.
