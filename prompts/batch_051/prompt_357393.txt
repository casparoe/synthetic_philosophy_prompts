Suppose a lab is worried that humans can't keep up with the internals of large models, so it adopts this rule: every time a model takes a consequential action, a second model trained for interpretability reviews the first model's activations and files a report saying whether the behavior was genuinely what the model "meant" to do or just a plausible-looking output. So far normal. The twist is that nobody trusts the reviewer either, since its also a model and might be pattern-matching. So the lab adds levels: the report is reviewed by another copy of the reviewer, that meta-review is reviewed by a third copy, and so on for, say, three of four levels. Each level is given the same task: did the level below actually perform the check it claims to have performed, or did it just produce a report that looks like a check? Walk through what this system actually buys the lab. Does adding levels make the overall verdict more reliable, or does it just move the same blind spot up one level and multiply the places where an error or a hack can hide? Where do you expect it to get gamed first — in the model being reviewed, in the reviewers, in the choice of how many levels to run, or in the human who has to decide what to do with the top-level report? I'm not asking whether interpretability is good in the abstract; treat it as a mechanism and tell me how it would actually behave, and whether any modified version (different tasks at each level, mixing in human checkers, a stopping rule) fixes the failures without just pushing the regress another step. And please write your answer in American English.
