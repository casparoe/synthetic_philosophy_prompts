I'm part of a loose collective of about forty people who work on research where one person can unilaterally do something that exposes everyone else — synthetic biology, open-weight AI models, that kind of work. We've been chewing on the unilateralist's curse: when each member has a private read on whether an action is net positive and any one of them can pull the trigger, the decision effectively gets made by whoever is most optimistic, which is exactly the person whose judgment you should least trust when the action is irreversible. Last year someone published a protocol that half the group still considers reckless, so now there's a proposal on the table.

The proposal: before any member takes an action that could irreversibly affect the rest of us, they must post a notice of intent to a public log and wait fourteen days. During that window, any other member can flag the action; a flag freezes it and triggers a blinded group vote (the proposal is anonymized, and voters don't learn who flagged). Flagging costs a small deposit, refundable only if the group votes to block. If nobody flags, the actor can go ahead unilaterally. If at least 60% vote to block, the actor is barred from proceeding, and if they proceed anyway they're expelled and publicly disavowed — which matters because we all depend on the same small set of funders. I want to know how this thing would actually behave in practice, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. Walk me through the incentives: who flags and who stays quiet, when actors bother posting a notice versus just acting without one, how the deposit and the expulsion threat get gamed, and what happens to borderline actions when everyones private signals are correlated. Does it fix the unilateralist's curse, or does it just relocate the selection pressure to the flaggers and the voters? Where does it fall apart first?

One more thing: in your answer, please separate what's fairly settled about the unilateralist's curse from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested take as if it were the consensus.
