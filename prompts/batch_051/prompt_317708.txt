I keep seeing the gender-equality paradox get used to settle way more than it can, and I want someone to sort out which of the moves people make with it actually go through. Stipulate for the sake of the prompt that the finding is real and robust: in more gender-egalitarian countries (the Nordics especially), men and women's educational and occupational choices diverge more, not less — more women in care work and nursing, more men in engineering, bigger sex differences in STEM degree share than in countries like Algeria or the UAE. People then run off in a bunch of directions. Some say it shows the relevant preferences are substantially innate, or at least canalized early enough that pushing for 50/50 in every field is fighting biology. Some say it shows egalitarian policy is self-defeating, or at least that equality of opportunity won't produce equality of outcome and we should stop treating the latter as a measure of the former. Some say the whole thing is a measurement artifact — the indexes used to rank countries as egalitarian are partly built out of the very outcomes being explained, and anyway its an ecological correlation, so you can't infer anything about any individual woman or man's preferences. And some say it points the other way: in rich, secure societies people can finally afford to follow intrinsic preferences, so the paradox is a point in favor of egalitarian policy rather than against it.

What I want is a defended ranking of these downstream claims (and any I've missed) by how much they actually follow if we stipulate the finding. Which are genuine implications, which are overreads, which are live but unsettled? For each one, tell me the extra premise it needs to go through, and whether we have any independent reason to accept that premise. And since this gets dragged into arguments about quotas and STEM outreach funding, flag the one or two claims someone could actually build bad policy on if they believed them too confidently.

Also: be straight about your confidence. Hedge where your hedging, and name the single point in your answer you're least sure of.
