For years now I've basically settled into the view that principled disagreement is a temporary thing: put two Bayesians with different priors in front of the same long stream of shared observations and their posteriors eventually merge, with the initial prior difference washing out. I've absorbed this from the merging-of-opinions results (Blackwell–Dubins and its descendants) and from the general rationalist folk theorem that priors only matter when data is scarce. In practice I lean on it a lot — when I see people with very different starting worldviews still at each other's throats after decades of seeing the same world, I default to thinking the failure is motivational or informational rather than their priors, and I mostly don't worry about how much my own early ideological accidents still steer me.

But I want to actually stress-test this before I keep treating it as settled. What would have to be true — or is already true — for the washing-out picture to fail or to be far weaker than I'm treating it? Don't give me the boring "humans aren't ideal Bayesians" answer; give me the map of where the formal results themselves are fragile. The things I vaguely know to worry about: the two agents' priors having to be mutually absolutely continuous, their sharing a likelihood and a hypothesis space, the true hypothesis having to sit inside the support of both priors, the data being stationary, model misspecification, merging of one-step forecasts versus merging over deep explanatory theories, and convergence being arbitrarily slow in high dimensions. Which of these would a defender of my view cheerfully concede, and which ones actually kill it? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, plus an honest verdict on whether my ordinary-life confidence in "enough data washes out priors" is doing work the theorems don't license, and whether the position's insulation is a bug or a feature.
