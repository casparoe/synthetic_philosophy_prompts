I have been rereading the Second Meditation and find myself caught between a theoretical principle and an experience I cannot set aside. As a scientist, I am disposed to expect that a proof of existence should establish the existence of some definite kind of thing; otherwise one is, so to speak, asserting a bare existential quantifier without a well-defined variable. This pushes me toward the deflationary reading of the cogito: that what Descartes is entitled to conclude is only that thinking is occurring, not that there is a persisting thing, an "I," that performs the thinking. The word "I" would then be a grammatical requirement of the act of thought, not a warranted ontological posit. Yet when I actually run the doubt for myself, I find this reading loses contact with the phenomenon. It is I who entertain the possibility of my non-existence, and I seem to grasp myself directly as the very subject of that act, not as an inference to an anonymous mental event. Descartes writes in the Second Meditation:

Doubtless, then, I exist, since I am deceived; and, let him deceive me as he may, he can never bring it about that I am nothing, so long as I shall be conscious that I am something.

I do not want a verdict on which side is correct. I want a negotiated diagnosis: is there a reading of this passage on which the deflationary claim and the first-person intuition are compatible, or must one yield? What exactly would justify abandoning the intuition as a product of grammar or self-identification, and what exactly would justify abandoning the deflationary principle as too sparse? In your answer, please also say what would change your mind: which counterargument or consideration would move you to the opposite diagnosis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
