I'm a retired chemist and I've only been reading philosophy properly for a couple years, so forgive me if this is old hat. In my reading group someone recently dismissed all of evolutionary psychology as irrelevant to ethics by saying "you can't get an ought from an is." When I pushed back that knowing how our moral emotions are actually produced might at least help us figure out which of them to trust, he said no — that's already trying to get an ought from an is, because from the fact that a judgment has a certain causal history nothing follows about whether we ought to distrust it. I get the narrow point that a purely factual premise can't deductively imply a normative conclusion without some kind of bridge principle. But I suspect the slogan is doing more work than that in his argument. Unpack the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be expressing — about logical validity, about the meaning of "ought," about reduction, about motivation, about action-guidance, whatever else you find — and say which are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which are false. Then tell me which reading his move actually needs, and whether that reading earns him the conclusion that facts about moral psychology can contribute nothing to how we should form or revise our moral beliefs.
