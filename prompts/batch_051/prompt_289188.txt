Just got back from a two-hour argument with a friend about whether the numbers count, and I'm stuck in a way that's kind of new for me. The line he was pushing — and it's the most compelling thing I've heard in a while — goes like this. In a case where you can save one stranger or five strangers, the only morally relevant things are the individual claims each person has, and the single person's claim to be saved isn't any weaker than each of the five's. Deaths don't sum across persons the way harms sum within one person's life. There's no extra entity, 'the five,' whose loss is bigger, and if you say five deaths are worse, the obvious question is worse for whom. I find myself attracted to this because it seems to be exactly what explains why you can't carve up a healthy person to save five transplant patients: each person counts separately, and you don't get to stand them in a row and total them up. It also has a clean, systematic appeal — one principle, take each claim on its own, don't aggregate; maybe the transplant case needs a separate principle about using people as means, but my friend thinks it's the same root, and I can't yet see where the joint is.

But then I think about actually being in the rescue case — one person trapped to the left of a collapsed building, five to the right, and only time to go one way — and I cannot honestly believe it's okay to save the one. It's not that I'd feel terrible about it; I'd think it was wrong, and I'd judge anyone who did it. So now I've got a theory I can't knock down and an intuition I can't shake, and the friend and I talked in circles for an hour over which one is doing the work. I don't want to be told to just save the five and stop philosophising, and I don't want to be told the theory refutes the intuition by definition. I want the strongest version of each side: the best case that the anti-aggregationist can make that saving the one is not merely permitted but maybe the genuinely respectful choice, and the best case that the save-the-five intuition should force the theory to be amended or dropped. Then give me a verdict about which should yield — and what it would take for each to be the one that goes. Is there a version where the intuition gets debunked as a kind of scope-insensivity artefact, a version where the theory gets amended so it still handles the transplant case but requires saving the five, and are they maybe compatible on some reading I haven't seen? I want the costs of each move, not just a winner.
