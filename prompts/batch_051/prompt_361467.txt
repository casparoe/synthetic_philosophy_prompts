I'm in a philosophy of action course, and last week our discussion section constructed the following argument. Each step looked plausible on its own, but the conclusion seems to prove far too much, and we can't agree on where the mistake is.

(1) An agent can rationally choose between options only if, at the time of choice, she can assign each possible outcome a value that reflects how she would regard that outcome from the relevant later perspective.
(2) For any outcome whose phenomenology is new to the agent, she cannot know before undergoing it what it is like from the inside.
(3) If she cannot know what an outcome is like from the inside, then she cannot know how she would regard it from the later perspective, and so cannot satisfy the requirement in (1).
(4) Therefore, no choice whose outcomes include new phenomenology can be rational.
(5) But virtually every non-routine choice includes some outcome feature one has not experienced before: an unfamiliar dish, a first trip to a new city, a new friendship, a different kind of work.
(6) So, by (4), virtually no non-routine choice is ever rational.
(7) That cannot be right, since it would make exploration, learning, and ordinary risk-taking systematically irrational.

We would most like to keep (2) and (7), but we can see that keeping both forces a cost somewhere. Which premise or step should we reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Please also say which further argument or distinction would make you change your mind about the diagnosis, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
