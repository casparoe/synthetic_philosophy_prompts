I run a reading group for adults who mostly know Stoicism through the recent self-help revival — the control-what-you-can slogan, daily Marcus Aurelius excerpts, resilience talk. Next session I want to spend ninety minutes teaching them the dichotomy of control as the ancient Stoics actually deploy it, and specifically the ways the popular two-bucket version quietly alters it. Don't give me the explanation; give me a plan for the explanation. Lay out the order in which the ideas must come and what in your sequence depends on what — for instance, why the audience has to grasp the Stoic diagnosis of distress before the distinction itself can do any work. Pick one running example that can carry the whole session, justify what it must have and what it must lack, and say why not a delayed flight or a traffic jam unless one of those genuinely earns its place (my own instinct is a workplace injustice or a relative's illness, but I'll defer to you). Then map the misconceptions this particular audience will bring to the room or form mid-session — I suspect at least three: that being in my control includes my emotions, that whatever isn't in my control should simply not matter to me, and that the dichotomy is a licence for passivity in the face of wrongs. For each one, tell me exactly where in the sequence to head it off before it hardens into their picture of the doctrine. Mark where, if anywhere, a three-category revision of the dichotomy — no control, partial control, complete control — belongs in the session, and whether it clarifies the ancient distinction or quietly imports a different notion of control. End with a single question I can ask at the close that would separate the people who actually have the distinction from the people who only have the slogan. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and why — I don't want the session to collapse into Stoic physics or the free-will debate.
