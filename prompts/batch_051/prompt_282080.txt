In my political philosophy course we have been discussing the justification of elections, sortition, and political careers, and I find that my own judgments about institutional design do not fit any single theory we have read. Here are the verdicts I keep coming back to. I think compulsory jury service is legitimate even though jurors are selected by lot and are not accountable to voters. I also have no objection to a small local planning board being chosen by lot from residents for a fixed term. But I would strongly oppose replacing a legislature with a randomly selected assembly holding full lawmaking power, and I would prefer a chamber of career politicians over a demographically representative chamber of randomly selected citizens. I favor term limits for a powerful chief executive, but I oppose term limits for legislators, because I think legislative expertise and long committee careers are genuinely valuable. I approve of a professional civil service insulated from elections, yet I support citizen-initiated recall of elected representatives in principle. Is there a single principle about who may properly govern, and under what constraints, that captures all of these verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I revise? Please state the principle precisely and tell me what it implies for two cases I have not made up my mind about: selecting delegates to a constitutional convention by lot, and whether a randomly selected citizens' chamber should be allowed to veto laws passed by an elected one.
