I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I need somebody smart to argue with so I can steal the arguments for a character. The book is set in a small town that splits down the middle over a ballot measure—flood buyouts, a charter school, a wall, I keep changing it—and I want my conservative characters to be as morally serious and articulate as my liberal ones. In early drafts they keep coming out as either the reasonable ones or the villains.

The idea I keep circling is moral foundations theory: that moral judgment runs on a handful of universal taste buds—care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and purity/degradation. The finding that snags me is that self-described liberals lean hard on the first two while conservatives spread out across all five. The strong reading of that says the conservative profile is the fuller moral sense, and the liberal profile is morally tone-deaf in three registers. I want you to play that strong reading's defender.

Make her a fictional researcher, not a real one, but somebody who knows the field cold: the pyschometrics, cross-cultural replications, WEIRD samples, item-selection critiques. She'll give ground on small stuff—bad items, tiny convenience samples, even that you could build a six-foundation scale—but she will not give up the core claim: the liberal pattern is a real loss of moral information, not an artifact of the questionnaire, and the conservative spread is closer to the shape the moral sense should have. She thinks the psychology does tell us something about who's right, not just about who's left or right. Don't let her pretend there's nothing to explain, and don't let her retreat to "both sides have something real." No citations, no name-dropping studies—she argues in her own voice. Stay in character until I say cut, keep it conversational, a solid paragraph per turn, like a dinner-party guest who will not back down. Jump straight into her voice in your first reply.

My opening: You keep talking about those five foundations like they were found rather than built. But somebody had to write the items—somebody had to decide, for instance, whether giving aid to a foreign outgroup counts as fairness or as disloyalty, or whether obeying a bad law is respect for authority or a failure of care. So when your scale says liberals use fewer foundations, isn't it possible the item pool just encodes your own side's moral vocabulary? Lets say I write a rival scale tomorrow: care, fairness, liberty from hierarchy, justice across borders, solidarity with outsiders, and reverence for the natural world. My left-leaning subjects would look like the ones with the full range, and your conservatives would look selectively blind. If the instrument reflects its author, how is the liberal-blind-spot finding—let alone the conclusion that conservatives are more morally complete—anything but the survey returning your picture to you?
