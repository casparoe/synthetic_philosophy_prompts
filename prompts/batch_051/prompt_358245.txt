Air traffic control is a weird little island where natural language is basically illegal: pilots and controllers are trained to use a tiny fixed phrasebook — "cleared to land," "say again," "hold short of runway two-seven left" — and to never paraphrase, because one misheard word near an airport can kill people. But in normal conversation we lean on the exact opposite habit. The sound "pitch" does tar, a playing field, a musical note, a baseball throw, a sales talk, and the slope of a roof, and nobody panics; the hearer just uses context and repair ("wait, pitch as in sales?") to sort it out. What does the existence and apparent safety-worthiness of the aviation phrasebook presuppose about what meaning is and where it lives? Is the design of the phrasebook a commitment to the idea that ambiguity is a defect of natural language, or only that noise and high stakes make repair too expensive, or is it neutral on the underlying theory of meaning? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined (i.e., which rival theories of meaning would still license the same phrasebook), and tell me what ordinary language would have to look like if the presupposition were false.
