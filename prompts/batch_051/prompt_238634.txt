In my philosophy elective we discussed the standard trolley problem: a runaway trolley is heading toward five workers, and a bystander can throw a switch that diverts it onto a side track, where it would kill one worker instead. I would like to work through a modification, and I want the analysis to center specifically on the doing/allowing (or killing/letting-die) distinction rather than just on minimizing deaths.

Suppose the switch is unreliable and everyone knows the exact probabilities. If the bystander does nothing, the five die for certain. If the bystander pulls the switch, there is a 70 percent chance that the trolley diverts and kills the single person on the side track, and a 30 percent chance that the switch fails and the trolley continues and kills the five, exactly as it would have if the bystander had never pulled it. There is no third option, and pulling the switch has no further costs.

Work through what a straightforward consequentialist should say, and then work through what a deontological view organized around the killing/letting-die distinction should say. In particular, I want to know whether the latter view can reject pulling the switch even though pulling lowers the expected number of deaths, or whether a probabilistic causing of death counts as a weakened form of killing, in which case the two views may prescribe the same action. At what switch reliability, if any, would the killing/letting-die view change its verdict, and is there a threshold that it can justify rather than merely assert? Finally: does this variation show that the doing/allowing distinction is a robust constraint, or does it show that, once uncertainty is introduced, the distinction quietly collapses into expected-value reasoning?
