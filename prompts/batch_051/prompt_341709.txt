People in my corner of moral philosophy have been arguing about how to act under moral uncertainty for years now, and I've started to think the debate is stuck because nobody has actually said what kind of disagreement it is. The predictable camps: you should maximize expected choiceworthiness across the theories you find plausible, weighted by your credences — expected utility, just aimed at being morally right rather than prudentially well off. The critics say this is a formalism without content, because choiceworthiness numbers from different theories don't share a scale or a zero point, so the comparison between, say, a utilitarian verdict and a Kantian verdict gets fixed by an arbitrary normalization that already presupposes a first-order moral view. The third camp says the whole approach is misguided and we should just act on the theory we think is most likely right, perhaps with some hedging at the margins, because uncertainty about which morality is true isn't itself a moral consideration.

My working theory is that this is a standoff about what the project is even trying to do. The maximizing camp treats moral uncertainty as a decision problem: you have credences, payoffs are choiceworthiness, now optimize. The comparison critics think the decision problem can't be set up neutrally, so "maximizing" is a pseudo-operation. The third camp doesn't think morality should be turned into a decision procedure over theories at all. If I'm right, the debate isn't substantive in the way people think it is; but I can imagine I'm wrong and there's a real question whether rational action under evaluative uncertainty requires comparable values or can get by with something weaker.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a survey: is it verbal, substantive, different questions under one heading, or starting intuitions nobody can argue for? Where exactly does the intertheoretic comparison problem bite — and is it a real formal obstacle or a artifact of framing the question as maximization? And what would genuinely count as progress given the diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves part of it, an impossibility theorem about neutral comparisons, a weakened decision rule, or a reason to drop the question? Be honest about whether anything would actually move people, or wether its a structural impasse.
