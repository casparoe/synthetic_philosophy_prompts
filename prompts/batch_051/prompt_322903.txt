Consider the familiar hitchhiker case used to motivate the idea of rational irrationality. A person stranded in the desert is offered rescue by a driver who will help only if he promises to pay a sum once safely home; but once home, the rescued man would be better off not paying, and a rational driver, knowing this, has no reason to trust the promise. Now suppose that in a near-future, otherwise ordinary liberal democracy, a safe, cheap drug is approved: for one year after taking it, a person is unable to break any promise made during the first active hour unless the promisee explicitly releases him; the compulsion holds even when breaking the promise would serve his self-interest, and he cannot release himself. He remains able to deliberate and may decline to promise in the first place. Predict, with reasoning, how this technology reshapes promising, contracting, and trust over the following thirty years. Who uses the drug, on whom, and in which transactions? Which kinds of cooperation and exchange become newly possible, and which existing arrangements are displaced? How do formal contract law, courts, collateral, guarantees, and reputation adapt? What new forms of exploitation, regret, or coercion should we expect and why? Make your reasoning visible: mark each link as robust or speculative, and state the assumptions whose failure would most change your predictions.
