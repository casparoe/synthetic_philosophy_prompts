I'm a software engineer who got pushed into an engineering manager role at a mid-sized company, and I've noticed our quarterly review process has the same smell as a nasty bug I can't reproduce. Every quarter we decide which long-shot projects get another round of funding. And every quarter, at least one project has missed its promised milestone and the owner says some version of "we were right, we were just early." The underlying bet was sound, the market hasn't caught up yet, the data volume isn't there, the tooling was immature. Sometimes thats clearly true. But the phrase "right but early" can be attached to literally anything, and I've watched projects eat budget for two years on it without anyone being able to say what would have proved them wrong.

I was reading about Malthus recently, and it felt like the same shape of problem. He argued population grows faster than the food supply, so you're heading inevitably for famine and collapse. It didn't happen on his timescale, partly because technology and changed birth patterns got in the way. And people now defend the claim as "he was right about the underlying mechanism, just early about how long it would take, and those countervailing forces delayed it." Maybe thats a fair rescue, maybe it's a dodge. And that's exactly how I feel in review meetings.

What I want is a decision procedure I can actually run, every quarter, on any project that missed a milestone and is now claiming "right but early." A checklist or a flowchart in words. The questions should be in order. For each question, say what a sufficient answer looks like, and say where the procedure stops with "kill it." Mark the places where no rule can settle it and a human has to make a call, because I don't want to pretend the procedure is more objective than it is. In particular, I want the procedure to force the person claiming "right but early" to specify, in advance, what "plain wrong" would look like at the next review — because if "wrong" can't even be described in advance, the claim isn't a prediction, it's just a story.

Constraints so the answer doesn't float away from reality: we have maybe 35–40 active bets, each one gets about 15 minutes in review, nobody in the room has a philosophy background (me included), and anything we kill has to be explained to a VP who personally sponsored a few of these projects, with the CFO signing off on the budget side. If the procedure requires me to lecture people about epistemology in the meeting, it won't survive contact with the finance director.

Then actually run the procedure on two or three concrete cases:

1. An ML recommendation feature that was supposed to lift conversion by 5% within six months. It has been eighteen months and the measured lift is basically zero, but the team says the model is sound and the only problem is we don't have enough training data yet, which will arrive as we sign more enterprise accounts.
2. A monolith-to-services migration that only pays for itself at roughly 10x current traffic. We're at 1.2x and traffic has been flat for a year. The team says the traffic is coming, the industry is just slow to move.
3. A project of your choice where "right but early" turned out to be true — include it so the procedure isn't just a polite axe.

Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot catch: the kind of project, or the kind of "right but early" claim, that will slip through no matter what rules we adopt.
