hey — I'm a linguistics PhD student TAing an intro language-and-mind course, and I'm trying to write a problem-set question that isn't just a rehash of the 'is ASL a real language' bit. The course uses a shortlist of criteria for what makes something a natural human language, so here's the working list, roughly:

1. conventional vocabulary: a set of form–meaning pairings shared across a community of users, not just one person's private code
2. duality of patterning: meaningless building blocks (like sounds, or handshapes and movements) combine into meaningful units, which combine again into bigger expressions
3. productivity: users can produce and understand novel utterances they've never encountered before
4. displacement: you can talk about things that aren't in the here and now — the past, hypotheticals, other people's minds, whatever
5. traditional transmission: children acquire it from earlier users in the ordinary course of development, without explicit teaching

The borderline case I want to use is homesign. That's the gesture system a deaf kid creates when they're born into a hearing family that doesn't sign and the kid never gets exposed to an established signed language. So it isn't taught — the child invents it. From what I know of the studies, homesign systems have stable word-like gestures, including productive pointing for reference; kids string them into two- and three-gesture utterances with fairly consistent ordering (like object before action, or whatever pattern the child settles on); and they can refer to absent stuff, so displacement at least partially shows up. But: no community of fluent co-users, parents usually use a reduced subset and lean on context, and there's no real duality of patterning because the gestures are mostly holistic rather than segmented into meaningless sublexical pieces. Also the system doesn't transmit across generations the way a language does — though when several homesigners get thrown together, their systems can become more language-like over time.

So here's the question. Check homesign against each of the five criteria honestly, and give me a bottom line: is it a language, or not? And the part I actually care about: where it fails, is that because the criteria are tracking something about the child's internal linguistic system, or something about the social/transmission side of language? I specifically can't decide about criterion 1 — is conventionalization a property of the language, or a property of a community having one? And does the fact that homesign seems to satisfy displacement and at least some productivity while failing every social criterion mean my list is smuggling in two different notions of 'language' under one heading? Be explicit about where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the case should make us revise the list.
