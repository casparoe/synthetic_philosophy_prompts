Last year I became both the custodian of my late father's papers and a member of our town's commemoration review board. The two roles have forced me to notice that my own judgments about what should be remembered and what should be forgotten do not seem to come from a single principle. I want to know whether one exists, and if not, which of my judgments I should give up.

Here are my verdicts.

1. The annual pageant that celebrates our 18th-century victory over the neighboring valley should end. It is presented as heritage, but it keeps a grievance warm and teaches children to think of the people downstream as hereditary enemies.

2. The plaque listing the workers killed in the old mill fire should stay on the mill wall, and the names should still be read each anniversary, even though the owner's descendants say it unfairly keeps their family's shame alive.

3. My father's letters to a woman who was not my mother are mine to burn unread. No one is owed them.

4. But the diary my father kept as a junior official in the colonial administration I may not destroy. It should go to an archive, even though it will embarrass him and complicate the family story.

5. The courthouse mural showing enslaved people as contented servants should come down from the courthouse and be moved to a museum with an explanatory label—not destroyed, but not left as decoration.

6. The high school should keep teaching the history of the county eugenics program, including the names of the families it targeted, even though some residents call it old news and bad for property values.

7. The annual reenactment of the witch trial, which ends with a mock execution, should stop. It turns an injustice into entertainment, and I would say that even though it has run for ninety years.

8. The recorded testimony of a survivor of the massacre at the river crossing should remain available to researchers, even though her children say that hearing her voice re-traumatizes them.

I find the tension between (2) and (7) especially troubling: both concern public commemoration of historical wrongs, yet I want one kept and the other stopped. And the tension between (3) and (4) is the one that keeps me up: the same man's papers, and I feel entitled to destroy one and forbidden to destroy the other.

My working hypothesis is that we may forget the glories and grievances of "us," but we may not forget harms done to others. But that says nothing about the difference between (2) and (7), and nothing about my father's two boxes. I also have an inchoate sense that my obligations to my own dead are different in kind from the town's obligations to its dead, but I cannot make that distinction precise enough to use.

Is there one principle—or a small set of principles—that delivers all these verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn? Please state the candidate principle or principles precisely, tie each clause to the cases that motivate it, apply the result to a further case I have not listed, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Test the working hypothesis against the cases and repair it if it can be repaired rather than discarding it.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the ethics of memory and forgetting, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
