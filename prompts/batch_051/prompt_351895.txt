Contemporary interpretability research on neural networks displays a striking tension. Its everyday vocabulary is unabashedly representational: researchers describe individual neurons, or directions in activation space, as standing for specific concepts; they say a model "tracks" an entity through a story or "computes" a syntactic relation; they describe "features" as stored in superposition across many neurons and reconstruct "circuits" that take one representation as input and produce another as output. At the same time, many of the same researchers insist that these descriptions are only predictive or descriptive summaries, that there is no single true decomposition of a network into parts, and that the goal is intervention and control rather than reading off a pre-existing internal code. The picture is further complicated by the fact that the main tools — linear probes (classifiers trained to recover a property from activations), sparse autoencoders (which learn an overcomplete dictionary of activation directions), activation patching (replacing an activation to see whether downstream behavior changes), and gradient-based attribution — can yield very different, non-equivalent decompositions of the same network, with no agreed procedure for deciding which, if any, is canonical.

Treat that practice as the object of philosophical analysis, and reconstruct the theory of representation it presupposes. In particular: does the practice strictly presuppose a realist view about interpretability, on which networks contain representations that analysis discovers, or is it equally compatible with a deflationary or instrumentalist view, on which "the model represents X" means only that a particular decomposition is predictively or causally useful under a chosen method? Are the two halves of the practice — its representational vocabulary and its pluralism about decompositions — consistent with each other, or does the pluralism quietly undermine the vocabulary? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it. Finally, spell out what would have to change in how interpretability research is done, reported, and used if the field explicitly adopted the rival view. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on or write about this, and do not present a contested philosophical view as the consensus.
