A friend of mine keeps an informal ledger of her verdicts on gratitude cases, and her calls have been stable enough across years that I suspect there is a principle underneath. A few of her data points:

She says substantial gratitude is owed to the stranger who pulled her back from a bus and broke his shoulder doing it, and to the coworker who swapped shifts for a month, at real personal cost, so she could care for her father after surgery.

She says no gratitude is owed to a driver whose brakes failed and whose skidding car happened to block the one that would have struck her; the benefit was enormous, but she insists there was no benefactor in it.

She says a passerby who noticed her bag was unzipped before anything fell out earns a quick thanks, not the kind of thing she would call gratitude.

She says no gratitude is owed for a windfall that came from a fund manager's self-serving trades in a stock she had forgotten she owned; she calls that luck rather than benefaction, even though someone's choices produced it.

The new case is this: her old manager spent six months and considerable political capital getting her appointed to a position she badly wanted, and he has been explicit throughout that he did it because he wants a loyal ally in that institution and does not care whether she is grateful.

Predict her verdict. Then say which principle you take to be driving the ledger, which rival principles remain consistent with it so far, and give me a further case you would use to force the rivals apart.
