I'm taking a philosophy of mathematics course this term, and I got into an argument with my brother, whose a physicist, about whether the four-colour theorem really deserves the word "proved." His line is that a proof is something a single person can, in principle, follow start to finish and see that it's right. But the four-colour theorem works by reducing the map-coloring claim to a finite list of configurations, and the checking of those configurations was done by a computer program—no human has actually surveyed the entire argument. Then there's the Kepler conjecture about the densest way to pack spheres, which was a long human argument plus enormous computer casework, and afterwards the whole thing was formalized and checked by a proof assistant. These arguments are, in the jargon, unsurveyable, and I'm trying to pin down what "proof" should mean when that happens. The definition I grew up with is starting to look too small.

I keep testing candidate definitions against these cases: (1) the usual proof that sqrt(2) is irrational; (2) the four-colour theorem, as I described it; (3) the Kepler conjecture in its original form, and then in its later machine-checked form; (4) an existence proof by the probabilistic method, which shows some object exists with positive probability but doesn't exhibit one; (5) a fully formal proof in Lean or Coq that no person reads but a small, reliable proof checker certifies; (6) a persuasive but gappy argument in a physics paper that "shows" a result in a way a mathematician would not count as rigorous; (7) a computation that checks Goldbach's conjecture for all even numbers up to some enormous bound. My gut says (1) is definitely a proof, (7) definitely is not, and the interesting mess is in the middle.

Can you give me a working definition of "mathematical proof" that handles these cases, run each case through the definition, and tell me where the definition breaks or forces a weird verdict? If you think the term is doing two jobs—say, one about certainty and one about social acceptance—I'm happy to get two definitions and a note on which cases move between them.
