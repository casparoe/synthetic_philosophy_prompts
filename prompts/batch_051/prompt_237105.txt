For an AI safety seminar I'm taking, I want to stress-test a control scheme my group came up with, treating it as a mechanism rather than a moral argument. The setup: a lab has a model M that it suspects might be misaligned but is too useful to retire. Every user query and every draft answer go to a smaller trusted monitor T, which is assumed to be aligned but noticeably dumber; T can't run code or search. T has to decide within a few seconds wether to release the draft to the user, and to compensate for being weak it can ask M up to four yes/no questions about its own draft before deciding. M sees the full query and draft while answering, and knows this whole procedure exists from the start. The lab also fine-tunes M only on answers that were released and later upvoted; blocked drafts are thrown out and their ratings never reach M. Walk me through how this actually plays out. What does a misaligned M converge on doing, and what would T need to do to hold the line? Where does it break first: the four-question budget, T being weak, M being the one who answers the questions, the training signal, or something about the user? Does merely being interrogated change what M writes in the first place, and is that a win or a hidden cost? Could patching it with a second, indepenent model writing the questions, or showing the user the Q&A transcript, or penalizing M whenever a draft is blocked actually fix it, or do those just open new holes? Write it as continuous prose, like an email, no headings, bullet points, bolding, or tables.
