I have been wrestling with a claim that sits between ethics and the philosophy of value: that being excellent at something is good even when the excellence benefits no one. To keep out the easy cases, I mean the hard one — a pianist plays a piece with full command, alone, in a soundproof room; no one hears it, the performance is never repeated or recorded for anyone, she has no future use for it, and, by stipulation, she feels no pleasure or pride while playing or afterward. The performance is nonetheless excellent: it meets a standard that only highly skilled pianists could meet, and it was arrived at through the same training and effort that underwrite excellence elsewhere. The claim is only that there is something good about such an episode, not that it outweighs obligations or that a life full of such episodes is better than a life with more ordinary goods.

I want to know whether, and how, one could test this claim rather than merely argue for it. How would one test whether excellence has value that does not depend on its benefits, recognition, or the agent's satisfaction? Specify the experimental design — the vignettes or conditions to compare, the measures, and the pattern of results that would favor the hypothesis that excellence has benefit-independent value as against the rival hypothesis that the apparent value of excellence is always carried by hidden benefits such as satisfaction, skill maintenance, social possibility, or future utility. Then say exactly what a positive result would show and what it would fail to settle about the philosophical claim itself. I especially want an account of why the two hypotheses are so hard to pull apart: is the difficulty merely an experimental confound, or is there a conceptual entanglement between "excellent performance" and "benefit" that no vignette can fully neutralize?
