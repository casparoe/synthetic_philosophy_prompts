I teach an upper-level political philosophy seminar, and I am drafting the final exam. I would like you to write one essay question on the Condorcet jury theorem that cannot be answered by restating the theorem or producing the usual "wisdom of crowds" gloss. The question should push students into the part of the theorem most summaries skip: the independence assumption. They should be made to confront the fact that the theorem's optimistic conclusion — that a large number of voters, each more likely than not to be right, will come very close to certainty in their majority verdict — requires their errors to be sufficiently independent, while the practices that actual democracies value, such as deliberation, shared evidence, common arguments, and party discipline, systematically make voters correlated. The question should therefore force students to take a position on whether the theorem, read honestly, is an argument for deliberation or against it, and whether the conditions under which it works are conditions any real deliberative body could know it satisfied. It should also force them to notice that the result cuts both ways: if individual competence is below one half, the large group becomes almost certain to be wrong, so the theorem cannot be a blanket vindication of majority rule.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of about six hundred words, and a marking scheme out of twenty. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each answer must contain — for instance, stating both assumptions precisely, explaining why the theorem flips when competence falls below one half, identifying the exact mechanism by which deliberation induces correlation, distinguishing the picture of a body aggregating independently formed judgments from the picture of a body forming a shared judgment through discussion, and reaching a definite conclusion about what the theorem does and does not establish for democratic theory.

No footnotes or bibliography are expected, and neither the question nor the model answer should depend on quotations or citations. Present everything as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Even the marking scheme should be written as prose sentences that say which point is worth how many marks, rather than as a list.
