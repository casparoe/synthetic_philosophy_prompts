For my course on death and well-being, I have been defending a restricted version of the Epicurean claim that death is not bad for the one who dies. The version I find strongest rests not on the familiar symmetry argument comparing death to prenatal nonexistence, but on a subject requirement. As I understand it, the argument runs like this: something can be bad for a person only if there is some time at which it befalls a subject who exists then; when a person dies, there is no time at which death befalls an existing subject, because once death arrives the subject no longer exists. So the state of being dead cannot harm the dead person. I am not making the stronger claim that dying cannot be bad — the process of dying can be painful, frightening, or a loss of further life — only that being dead cannot itself be bad for the person who dies.

I want you to test this position by mapping its exposure rather than merely listing standard objections. For each candidate defeater — a possible case, an intuition, an argument, or something empirical or formal if relevant — say precisely which premise or commitment of the subject-requirement argument it pressures, and how. Then grade it: would it be fatal, severe, or merely awkward if it succeeded? Separate the defeaters that are genuinely live from those that are merely logically possible but have nothing positive going for them. I want the map drawn from the inside as well as the outside: which defeaters should a committed defender of the subject requirement concede count against the view even while thinking they ultimately fail, and which would the defender reasonably dismiss as changing the subject, begging the question, or smuggling in an account of harm the position was never obliged to grant? Throughout, keep three claims distinct: that being dead is bad for the dead person; that dying is bad for the person who dies; and that a death is bad for others, or bad simpliciter. A recurring defensive move is to insist that an alleged defeater only supports the second or third claim; say when that reply is legitimate and when it has become evasive. Also say what the subject-requirement view commits me to that the main rival view — that death is bad because it deprives the person of a life they would otherwise have had — does not. I would personally be most moved by a defeater that shows the subject requirement, applied consistently across cases, forces me to surrender a harm I cannot accept giving up. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and end with an honest verdict: is the position well insulated, and if it is, does that insulation tell against it?
