I’m a high school student taking a philosophy elective, and I am writing a paper on whether Rousseau’s idea that a person can be made to obey the general will without being coerced in the ordinary sense—what he famously calls being forced to be free—is defensible. I don’t want to start evaluating that idea until I have a clear specification of what a successful account of the general will has to deliver. In Book II, Chapter I of The Social Contract, Jean-Jacques Rousseau writes:

> I hold then that Sovereignty, being nothing less than the exercise of the general will, can never be alienated, and that the Sovereign, who is no less than a collective being, cannot be represented except by himself: the power indeed may be transmitted, but not the will.

My request is this. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of Rousseau’s general will must meet. For each requirement, explain what consideration or case from the book makes it a requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are demands only one interpretive camp would insist on. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I especially want to know which requirements do the real work when someone defends or rejects the forced-to-be-free idea. For example, if a theory requires that the general will be genuinely shared by all citizens rather than merely imposed, then forced freedom looks like a contradiction—but is that a genuine desideratum, or is it a preference of Rousseau’s democratic critics? Finally, briefly test two or three standard readings of the general will against your list. Keep the focus on the requirements and their motivations, not on the scorecard; I will work out my own verdict in class.
