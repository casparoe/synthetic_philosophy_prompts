Some macrohistorians, following Peter Turchin, claim that agrarian states exhibit secular cycles: sustained population growth produces elite overproduction, which intensifies intra-elite competition, which weakens state finances and legitimacy, leading to political breakdown and then a reset. The claim is not merely that this narrative fits a few familiar cases but that the pattern is a genuine dynamical regularity that can be quantitatively detected and used to anticipate instability before it arrives. A skeptic can reply that the whole cycle is a post hoc construction: with enough variables and enough recorded past, one can fit a decline-and-collapse story to almost any polity, and the mechanism is really a sequence of labels applied once the outcome is known.

How could one test whether the secular-cycle account has predictive content rather than mere retrospective fittedness? Design a test that would fault the theory if it were only a flexible narrative in disguise. Specify the unit of analysis, how you would operationalize the core variables — elite numbers, fiscal health, demographic pressure, political breakdown — how you would keep the test data genuinely out of sample or otherwise prevent information about the outcome from leaking into the model, and what result would count as success and what would count as failure. Then make explicit what a positive result would and would not establish: whether it would warrant talk of cycles as real entities, whether it would support causal or merely forecasting claims, and which components of the theory would remain untested regardless of how good the forecasts are.
