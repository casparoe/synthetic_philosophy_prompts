You are a philosopher of science who has been asked to advise a national research council during a budget review. The council funds a substantial portfolio of theoretical and observational cosmology: cosmic microwave background surveys, dark-energy probes, gravitational-wave astronomy, and simulations of large-scale structure. Its published funding criteria say that every proposal is judged on two grounds, "excellence" and "impact." In practice, cosmology proposals satisfy the impact criterion by mentioning student training, public outreach, or speculative future spin-offs, and the council's own reviewers generally concede that these impact claims are weak.

A finance-ministry official has asked you, in writing, to identify what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. She points to an apparent inconsistency: the council refuses to fund astrology, homeopathy, or purely speculative metaphysics on the grounds that they have no application, yet it funds cosmology despite having no foreseeable application, while still requiring every proposal to receive an impact score.

Poincaré's *The Value of Science* is often invoked informally to justify such funding. As you know, he argues roughly that astronomy is worth doing even where it is not obviously useful, because it enlarges our conception of the universe, disciplines the mind, and gives humanity a sense of something grander than itself. But the official does not want an appeal to Poincaré; she wants the implicit theory made explicit enough to defend before a skeptical treasury.

Write a memo to the official that does the following:

1. Reconstructs the implicit theory of the value of knowledge that would make the council's pattern of decisions coherent. For each candidate view—instrumentalism, the thesis that some knowledge has intrinsic value, an objective-list perfectionist view, and a virtue-epistemic view on which the value lies in the intellectual activity—state whether the practice strictly presupposes it, merely fits it, or rules it out.

2. Distinguishes what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely tolerates. Pay special attention to the dual criterion "excellence + impact": is it a coherent expression of the presupposed theory, or does it force applicants to pretend that outcomes are predictable when the underlying theory says no such predictability is required?

3. Proposes a concrete revision of the funding criteria that would make the presupposed theory explicit. If the council should continue to fund cosmology, what exactly should its criteria say instead of "impact"? If it should not, what change is required?

4. Address the hard discrimination problem: if application is not the test, what principled difference exists between cosmology and pure mathematics on the one hand, and astrology or other application-free speculation on the other?

Before writing the memo, spend some initial paragraphs working through the candidate theories and state which one you find most defensible and why. Then write the memo, about 900 words, addressed to the official. The point of the exercise is to separate what the practice strictly commits the council to from what it merely happens to fit, and to show whether Poincaré's line of thought can be turned into an actual funding rule. Do not merely summarize Poincaré; use his argument as one candidate articulation of the implicit theory and test whether the council can consistently adopt it.
