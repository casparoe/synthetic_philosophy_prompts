I spent my working life in a lab and have only recently gotten into philosophy, so forgive me if this is half-baked. I've been roped onto the board of a private foundation that funds basic research, and I've been reading Plutarch — specifically the way he'd adjust a historical anecdote to bring out a moral point rather than to get the record exactly right. At our next meeting there's a vote on a reform modeled on that. Before any major grant decision, the board has to hear a short "life case" from staff: a two-page biography, real or composite, of a past scientist or benefactor whose career turned on a choice structurally like the one before us. The written life is allowed to sharpen the moral — minor facts may be changed — so long as it's labeled as embellished. Walk me through what this does over a couple of years: what staff end up writing, how board members actually use the stories in debate, who gets funded and who doesn't, and where the scheme gets gamed first. Would requiring a second life that argues the opposite, or else banning any invented details, fix the failure modes without turning the whole thing back into a dull staff memo?
