I've been stuck on this for a policy seminar on data and privacy, and I'd rather get a blow-by-blow analysis than a simple "it's good" or "it's bad" verdict. We're reading Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis's The Right to Privacy (1890, Harvard Law Review), and this passage keeps nagging at me:

This development of the law was inevitable. The intense intellectual and emotional life, and the heightening of sensations which came with the advance of civilization, made it clear to men that only a part of the pain, pleasure, and profit of life lay in physical things. Thoughts, emotions, and sensations demanded legal recognition, and the beautiful capacity for growth which characterizes the common law enabled the judges to afford the requisite protection, without the interposition of the legislature.

They go on to argue that the real principle behind privacy is protection of the person, not property. But a friend who works at a data brokerage keeps pushing a property-style fix, and I want to know how it would actually behave. Here's the proposal. Any company that holds records "about" a natural person — identifiers, behavioral logs, inferred traits, whatever — must pay a quarterly royalty, say five cents per person per quarter, into a public clearinghouse. Individuals can claim their accumulated pot by verifying their identity; anything unclaimed after two years rolls into a privacy-enforcement fund. Companies may deduct the fair value of the free services actually used by that person that quarter, as valued by an auditor the company itself hires and pays. Firms with fewer than 50,000 data-subject records are exempt. Treat it as a mechanism rather than as a moral position: what does it incentivize for users, for companies, and for the ad exchanges and data brokers in the middle? Where does it get gamed first — I'm picturing fights over what counts as a record "about" a person, derivative data laundered into "non-personal" form, and inflated free-service valuations? What second- and third-order effects should I expect, for instance around data being hoarded rather than sold, or around people being paid to claim on behalf of others. And here's what I really want to sort out: does the Warren and Brandeis passage suggest the property/royalty frame is the wrong one from the start, or does it actually support treating personal data as the sort of intangible property the common law grew to recognize?
