Suppose that, beginning tomorrow, every legal system that currently criminalizes blackmail abolishes it as a distinct offense, replacing it with this rule: a person may lawfully threaten to disclose true information that they obtained lawfully and hold under no duty of confidentiality, and may lawfully make nondisclosure conditional on payment, provided the disclosure itself would be lawful if made and no independent offense—deception, fraud, threats of violence, or misuse of stolen or confidential information—is involved. All other law remains as it is.

The change is interesting because of the familiar paradox: the underlying disclosure would typically be legal, staying silent is legal, and bargaining for lawful forbearance is a normal transaction; yet combining them in a conditional threat is treated, almost everywhere, as extortion. Give a reasoned, mechanism-level prediction of how a society operating under this rule for two decades would change, rather than a story or an appeal to authority. Address at least: the ex ante incentives of people who might be exposed; the incentives of firms and individuals who collect or archive discreditable information; the pricing and credibility of purchased silence; and background norms of privacy, reputation, and trust. In particular, evaluate whether the standard consequentialist worry—that legal blackmail would set off wasteful investment in discovering secrets and would mostly transfer wealth without producing anything—would be borne out, or whether countervailing effects, such as the deterrence of behavior people strongly wish to keep hidden, would dominate. Conclude by distinguishing the predictions you hold with high confidence, the ones that turn on assumptions you can imagine being false, and the observation that would most undermine your central prediction.
