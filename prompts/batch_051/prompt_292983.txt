I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether someone who once committed a serious wrong can later, after sincerely repenting and making what amends she can, have the standing to blame another person for the same kind of wrong. The argument stalled on a cleaner question than either of us had framed at the start: is what defeats standing the blamer's present posture toward the norm, or is it the bare fact of her earlier violation?

I would like a thought experiment built to order that separates those two considerations. Construct a realistic scenario—workplace, family, or civic life; one paragraph; no contrived or science-fictional elements—in which, under the two accounts stated below, a blamer has standing to blame on the first account but lacks it on the second.

The first account I will call the present-commitment account. On this view, a person loses standing only when her blame involves a present mismatch between the demand she makes and the norms she currently treats as binding on herself. Someone who now genuinely condemns her own earlier violation, accepts blame for it, and holds herself to the same standard retains standing, because her blame does not misrepresent her relation to the norm.

The second account I will call the clean-hands account. On this view, a person loses standing if she herself committed the kind of wrong she now blames. The demand carried by blame—that the target answer for the wrong and change—can be properly issued only by someone whose own relation to the norm is unimpeached. A past violator, even a sincerely repentant one, remains a compromised source, and the target may dismiss the blame with a version of you did it too.

The scenario should satisfy these constraints. The norm at stake should be a serious moral or professional norm, not a minor rule of etiquette. The blamer's prior wrong must be the same kind as the wrong she now blames, not merely adjacent to it, and she must no longer be committing it. Her repentance and reparative efforts should be shown in the scenario and should be adequate by ordinary standards, so that the only remaining obstacle is the bare fact of her earlier violation. The target's blameworthiness must be beyond question, so that the only disagreement concerns the blamer's standing. And the case must not smuggle in any additional defect—rudeness, self-interest, ulterior motives, disproportionate anger—that would independently explain why the blame misfires. My friend and I ended up on opposite sides of exactly this divide, so the case should not be rigged in either direction; the point is to isolate whether a sincere, self-applying condemnation from a reformed offender is disqualified by her record alone, and the verdicts should be generated by the two accounts rather than stipulated.

After presenting the scenario, walk through what each account says about it and why. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work in pulling the two verdicts apart, and say what would happen if that feature were removed—does the case collapse back into both accounts agreeing, and in which direction? Finally, explain how a defender of the clean-hands account would attack the case and how a defender of the present-commitment account would reply.
