I teach an upper-level seminar in decision theory for philosophy majors, about eighteen students, with an eighty-minute session. Previously we covered the Saint Petersburg paradox and the standard move of replacing expected monetary value with expected utility. Now I want the students to feel a stranger failure case: a game where the mathematical expectation does not diverge to infinity but fails to exist at all, because the sum of probability-weighted payoffs is conditionally convergent. You may know this as the Pasadena game or Pasadena paradox. I want an in-class activity, not a lecture, in which half the class plays a bank offering a series of bets with payoffs of roughly the form \((-1)^{n+1} 2^n/n\) when a fair coin first comes up heads on the nth toss, and the other half plays bettors. The bank and the bettors should set prices, observe cumulative winnings, and try to determine a fair value at different points, with the discomfort emerging from the activity itself before the paradox is named. Materials should be simple: coins, tokens or chips ledgers, and optionally a shared spreadsheet. No real money is used, but the game should make the large swings of fictional dollar amounts feel salient.

Please give me exact rules, a realistic timeline, a payoff sheet, pricing rounds, and a structured debrief. In the debrief, map the moments of the activity onto the mathematical distinction between diverging sums, absolutely convergent sums, and conditionally convergent sums whose terms can be rearranged to produce different limits. Tell me what will predictably go wrong: I expect students to rationalize their unease as ordinary risk aversion, or to think the problem is only that the numbers are large, or to conclude that any small fixed fee solves it. How should I handle those misreadings without deflating the activity? Finally, be frank about what the classroom simulation cannot reproduce about the idealized infinite game, since students encounter only finite truncations, and say exactly what I should supply inwords to bridge that gap. Participants should come away seeing that expected value is not always a well-defined guide, even when every term in the calculation looks perfectly legitimate.
