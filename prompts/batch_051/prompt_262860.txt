I cover science and religion for a living, and here's an asymmetry I keep noticing in otherwise evidence-obsessed people. The same person who says a homeopath is just wrong, maybe dangerously wrong, because there's no mechanism by which the remedy could work, will happily say that a devout Catholic and a devout Muslim are both fine with God. Two people who disagree about whether a mushroom is toxic can't both be right, and nobody reaches for a line like "two paths up the same forest." But on the ultimate life-or-death question — who's saved, and how — a lot of the same people do reach for "two paths up the same mountain." So we treat religious claims as the one domain where sincere, tradition-shaped belief gets a pass that being right doesn't, while staying hard-nosed objectivists everywhere else. Is there anything about religious claims that justifies this: that they aren't truth-apt, that salvation is about how you seek rather than what you affirm, that the traditions are describing the same thing from different angles? I want the best defense of the asymmetry, tested against a nearby case or two (a political ideology held sincerely? a parent's medical claim about a kid's treatment?), and then a verdict: keep the asymmetry, or admit its just special pleading and give it up. Please write the answer in American English.
