Ever since I moved to France for work I've been trying to pin down what this country actually thinks religious neutrality consists in, and the more I look the less it holds together. In the public schools, students can't wear ostentatious religious symbols — a headscarf, a yarmulke, a large cross — and the same logic gets extended to a ban on face coverings in public and to periodic fights over whether a mother wearing a headscarf can chaperone a class trip. Okay, so the state is supposed to be laïc: religion stays out of public institutions. But then the very same state pays the salaries of teachers in private religious schools that operate under contract with the ministry, a huge proportion of them Catholic, and up in Alsace-Moselle it still funds religious instruction under some old concordat arrangement. So the state is not neutral in the sense of "keeps its hands off religion"; it's deeply entangled with particular religions while policing the visibility of individual believers in state spaces.

I'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory of neutrality, citizenship, or religious freedom that this setup pressuposes — or a case for why there is no one consistent theory at all. Is the underlying idea that religion should be invisible in public life but can be subsidized in private institutions? That France has a historically settled religious identity it can quietly support, while minority practices have to be screened off? Lay out the theory as charitably as possible, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and then say what would actually have to change in policy if you swapped it for the Anglo-American multiculturalist alternative — the UK or US model where schools make accommodations for religious dress and the state doesn't have this same kind of entanglement. If you think the French setup is just incoherent, defend that; if you think it has a logic, show me what it is.
