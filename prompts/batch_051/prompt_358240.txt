Consider a philosophy of mind professor who has, over the term, issued explicit verdicts about which artifacts and systems understand Chinese. Her track record so far:

- Searle's original Chinese room, with a monolingual English speaker executing a rulebook: no understanding.
- The entire room-plus-book-plus-operator taken as an extended system: no understanding.
- The same rulebook implemented on a standard digital computer with no human in the loop: no understanding.
- A robot controlled by the room's rulebook, equipped with cameras and motor effectors and navigating a Chinese-speaking environment: no understanding.
- A complete, biologically faithful neuron-by-neuron simulation of a native Chinese speaker's brain during Chinese conversation: understands Chinese.
- An ordinary human who learns Chinese through immersion: understands Chinese.
- A giant lookup table pairing every possible input string with a fixed reply: no understanding.
- A formal semantics engine that translates Chinese into first-order logic, applies truth-conditional rules, and generates replies: no understanding.

Next week's case is this: an artificial system trained on Chinese text and on multimodal input from virtual environments. Its replies track Chinese usage in novel contexts, it passes counterfactual and referential probes about which objects and features its internal symbols correspond to, but its architecture is not brain-like, its learning rule is not biological, and no stage of its training involved human feedback in Chinese.

Do you think the professor will say this system understands Chinese? Which principle is she implicitly applying? Which other plausible principle fits her past verdicts equally well but yields the opposite answer for this new case? Finally, specify a further case that would most sharply separate those two principles.
