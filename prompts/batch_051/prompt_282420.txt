I'm a retired pharmacologist who got into philosophy of science a couple of years ago, and there's a practice from my old world I keep chewing on. Journals in my field, and even more so in psychology, now treat a result as less credible unless the hypothesis, analysis plan, and stopping rule were registered before the data were collected. The same p-value gets called confirmatory if the plan exists in advance and merely exploratory if you chose the test after looking around at the data. But I can look at an exploratory analysis and see no actual flaw in it. The data didn't change because the plan was written down later, and the statistics don't know the order in which the scientist had there ideas. So why should the fact that I could have run another test make me less convinced by the test I did run? The practice seems committed to the idea that I can know I had lots of analytical freedom, be unable to point to any specific mistake in my reasoning, and still rationally be less confident than my own first-order reading of the data tells me to be. Is that the implicit epistemology, or is pre-registration really a policy about protecting the published literature from false positives, and it only looks like a thesis about individual self-distrust? Reconstruct what the practice presupposes about evidence and self-trust. Say what it is strictly committed to versus merely compatible with, and tell me what would have to change in how journals treat unregistered work if we instead held that a scientist's sincere, careful first-order assessment is the only rational basis for belief.
