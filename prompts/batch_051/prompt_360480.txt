Next month I'm giving a short talk at our ML reading group arguing that iterated amplification and distillation (the scheme where a human plus copies of the model recursively break a hard task into easier subtasks, you train on that, distill back into one model, and repeat) doesn't preserve alignment through decomposition the way its usually pitched. My argument, roughly: decomposition isn't alignment-neutral. Choosing what counts as a subtask, and what "done correctly" means for each subtask, is already doing part of the task, so a human can approve every individual piece and still be wrong about whether the assembled whole does what was originally asked. Then distillation takes that whole chain of approvals—the gaps, the inconsistencies, the ways the decomposer steered the task—and fits a single model to it, so what you end up with is trained to predict what a human will nod along to, not to do the original task. Competence gets weird too: if the model gets better at proposing plausible decompositions, the human can turn into a rubber stamp, and whats distilled is the human-plus-model error pattern rather than some clean "amplified" version of the human.

The audience is mostly engineers from our agent-tooling team plus two trust and safety people. They don't talk about alignment the way safety researchers do; they think in terms of eval sets, distribution shift, error budgets, whether a failure is catchable in red teaming, and whether a system beats what we already run in prod. One of them has read the original IDA-style proposals and thinks they're basically sound. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll surface, phrased the way this crowd would actually raise them—not the objections I'd get from alignment researchers—with what I should concede, what I should push back on, a tight two-minute reply for each, a note on where each reply is soft, and the one objection I should be praying nobody asks.
