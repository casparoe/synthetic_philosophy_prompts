i'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and i keep running into a pattern in arguments about weird events that i don't have a name for.

Concrete case from work: our dashboards are huge. An oncall engineer noticed three 500 errors in a row from user ids ending in 919 and said the odds of that are about one in a billion, so something must be wrong with that id range. But he only noticed 919 because it happened to show up; if it had been 512 or 777 he would have flagged that instead. With the amount of traffic we serve, some repeating suffix like that is basically guaranteed to happen regularly, so the relevant number is more like "some pattern catches someone's attention and a ticket gets opened today," not "this exact suffix repeats three times."

A second version of the same shape: after a deploy, a PM sliced conversion by about forty segments and found one with p = 0.03, and he immediately had a story for why the deploy hurt only that segment. I know p-values and multiple comparisons are adjacent, but the first case doesnt have a pre-registered hypothesis at all. In both cases the pattern is seen first and the hypothesis is formed second, but the probability gets quoted as if the hypothesis came first.

I also see the same move in everyday coincidence stories: someone runs into a friend in a random city and says "what are the odds," so it must be meaningful. It always seems to ignore that the story is being told because it happened, and with enough people doing things, some story like that will happen to somebody.

I know the birthday paradox, and coworkers keep saying "that's just the birthday paradox," but that seems different to me: the birthday thing is about any two people colliding inside one fixed group, while here the pattern is selected after looking, and then its odds are computed as if it had been predicted in advance.

Is there an established name for this specific thing? If there are a few adjacent labels, I'd like the differences spelled out — when does a post-hoc pattern become an actual anomaly worth investigating, and is the core mistake a probability error, an inference error, or two separate mistakes? I'm not satisfied with just a label; I want the boundaries between this, the birthday paradox, p-hacking, and ordinary base-rate neglect. If there's no name, coin one and define it with a couple of edge cases.
