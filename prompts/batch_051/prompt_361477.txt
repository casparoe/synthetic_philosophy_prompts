You are an expert metaphysician specializing in the mind–body problem. I have been reconstructing an objection to physicalism — I believe it is sometimes called Hempel's dilemma — and I suspect that one of the steps is doing all the work, but I cannot see which. Before answering, work through the strongest reply a physicalist could make to each step, then give only your final answer.

Here is the argument:

(1) Physicalism is the substantive thesis that everything that exists is physical.

(2) For "physical" to give this thesis determinate content, it must mean either (a) whatever is posited or described by current physics, or (b) whatever would be posited or described by a completed, ideal future physics.

(3) If it means (a), physicalism is very likely false: current physics offers no settled account of conscious experience and is, by historical induction, probably an incomplete inventory of the world; but on this reading, whatever it omits simply counts as non-physical.

(4) If it means (b), physicalism is empty or trivially true: since we cannot specify what an ideal future physics would include, we cannot say what would fall outside it, so the thesis places no real constraint on what there is.

(5) Therefore, physicalism cannot be both substantive and well supported; it is either very likely false or a merely programmatic or trivial claim.

Is the argument sound? If a step fails, say exactly which one and give its corrected version; if it is sound, say what a physicalist should do next. The only escape I can imagine is a third reading of "physical" — something like a kind term whose reference is fixed by current physical paradigms but can be revised later without becoming empty — but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine third option or just a disguised form of (b).

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introductory or concluding material.
