I am a philosophy professor whose work sits at the intersection of the philosophy of history and the cognitive science of explanation. I want you to act as an expert in this same area, comfortable with both the experimental literature on hindsight bias and philosophical accounts of narrative explanation. Think carefully before you answer, and resist the easy move of treating the two concepts as synonyms.

Take, as your case, the standard retrospective interpretation of the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962: the thirteen-day standoff in which President Kennedy’s calibrated naval blockade—officially styled a quarantine to avoid the language of war—is said to have forced Khrushchev to remove Soviet missiles from Cuba, and which became a canonical success story of crisis management. For our purposes the salient facts are not in dispute: the blockade; the later-revealed secret understanding that American Jupiter missiles would be withdrawn from Turkey; and the fact that the episode’s reputation as a controlled, statesmanlike triumph hardened before the public learned, from documents and testimony released later, how many near misses and accidents had been folded out of the story.

Your object is not the events themselves; it is that retrospective account—its shape, its omissions, and its stubborn survival. Analyze it through the paired lens of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, keeping the two sharply distinct. Begin by giving a working definition of each and a criterion by which one could tell, feature by feature, which lens is doing the explanatory work. Then apply them: which features of the standard account are best attributed to hindsight bias—a distortion of perceived predictability once the outcome is known—and which to the narrative fallacy, understood as a preference for causally tidy, plot-like stories over probabilistic or conjunctive ones? Which features require both at once? Then reverse the direction of analysis: what does this case show about the two constructs themselves, especially about their supposed independence? For example, does the episode suggest that hindsight bias is just the mechanism that supplies a narrative with its allegedly inevitable endpoint, or that the narrative fallacy can structure historical explanation even where no hindsight distortion is operating? End with a bottom line about whether the distinction survives contact with this case, needs qualification, or should be dissolved.
