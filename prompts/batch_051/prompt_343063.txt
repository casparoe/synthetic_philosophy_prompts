I am a retired natural scientist — I spent four decades in the laboratory — and I have only recently begun reading moral philosophy seriously. I am preparing a discussion for a philosophy-and-literature group on Henrik Ibsen's An Enemy of the People, and I would like from you a reading of the play through the lens of loyalty, whistleblowing, and the ethics of betrayal.

The play sits at the center of the topic: Dr. Stockmann, a public health scientist, discovers that the town's baths — the source of its prosperity — are polluted; his own brother, the mayor, suppresses the finding; the newspaper that first champions him turns; a public meeting brands him an enemy of the people. Please write a critical essay that treats the play as more than a case study and the philosophy as more than a label. Specifically, I want you to do the following: distinguish the relevant kinds of loyalty in play — loyalty as partiality toward persons, loyalty to a community, and loyalty to a truth or standard of evidence — and say how each pulls on Stockmann and on those who silence him; reconstruct the standard conditions under which whistleblowing is usually judged justified, such as evidence of serious harm, exhaustion of internal channels, proportionality, and a legitimate public interest, and test them against the play, noting where Stockmann meets them, where he violates or bends them, and whether the play treats that checklist as sufficient; address the charge that Stockmann betrays his town and the countercharge that the town betrays him, and ask whether the language of betrayal is doing real moral work here or is just an instrument of power; and identify places where the play resists the philosophical categories — I am thinking of Stockmann's frank contempt for the majority he opposes, the way his motives and his message become entangled, and the final scene's embrace of solitary independence. Conclude by saying what the play adds to the philosophical debate beyond an argument: does it dramatize something that the standard ethics of whistleblowing misses, and is it ultimately a vindication, a complication, or an indictment of the demand that one expose serious wrongdoing regardless of loyalty? I am not asking for a plot summary, and please do not reach for quotations; paraphrase only what is needed. Keep the essay formal and self-contained, suitable for a scientifically trained reader who is new to the philosophical literature.
