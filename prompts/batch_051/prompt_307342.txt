I've been going back and forth about the pessimistic meta-induction in a philosophy of science seminar, and I cannot tell whether I have found a real problem or whether I am simply failing to see what the argument is actually for. The standard line, as it is usually put, is that many theories that were once impressively successful — Ptolemaic astronomy, phlogiston chemistry, the caloric theory of heat, the luminiferous ether — are now considered false, so the empirical success of a theory is a poor guide to its truth, and we should expect our own successful theories to meet the same fate.

What bothers me is this: the premise that those earlier theories were false is not something we read straight off the historical record; it is a verdict delivered by our current theories and instruments. The same later science that tells us phlogiston does not exist is doing an enormous amount of work on both sides of the ledger. But then the argument seems to want it both ways. If current physics is trustworthy enough to refute caloric, why is it not trustworthy enough to tell us, at least approximately, that electrons are real? If it is not trustworthy enough for the latter, then the inductive base — the list of refuted past theories — is itself the product of exactly the kind of confidence the argument claims is unwarranted. Either way, the pessimistic induction appears to saw off the branch it sits on. Where exactly does this line of criticism go wrong, or does it? I would especially like to understand how one can chain together a sequence of verdicts delivered by science while simultaneously drawing a conclusion that undermines confidence in those very verdicts.
