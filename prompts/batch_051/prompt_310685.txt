I can't stop thinking about a puzzle in how the British constitution actually works. In theory the monarch has real powers: no bill becomes law without royal assent, the King appoints the Prime Minister, and he's the formal head of the armed forces and so on. In practice every one of these is wired to the elected government — assent is automatic (it hasn't been refused in centuries), the PM is whichever MP can hold a commons majority, and the King's Speech is really just the government's agenda read out in a fancy robe. So the powers are real only on the condition that they are never used against a government with a majority; the moment a monarch actually refused assent or sacked a PM who could win a confidence vote, the whole institution would probably be abolished. That's what I find strange: an unaccountable veto that survives only because it's a costume. I used to think that made the monarchy obviously irrelevant, but people who know more than me insist the unused powers are the point — an emergency brake that never gets pulled, with the monarchy doing the legitimating while the elected machinery does the governing. And then Japan has an emperor with no such reserve powers at all, just a symbolic role, which makes me wonder whether the UK version is a different beast or the same symbolism with scarier paperwork.

My question: what view of democratic legitimacy does this arrangement strictly presuppose? I can see at least three readings — (a) the crown is a dormant emergency brake, so the practice presupposes that there can be moments when an unelected figure may legitimately override the elected will to protect the system; (b) the crown is purely expressive, a continuity device, and the powers are legal fossils we collectively agree not to notice; (c) something two-level, where legal sovereignty and political sovereignty are allowed to point at different places and the gap between them is doing the real work. Reconstruct the implicit theory as exactly as you can, say whether it's coherent, and separate what the practice presupposes from what it merely happens to be compatible with. Then tell me what institutions would have to change if you replaced that underlying view with a flat popular-sovereignty picture where every effective power traces to voters. And because I keep going back and forth between the readings: say what argument or fact would most likely get you to switch which reading you think is doing the work, and where your reconstruction is shakiest.
