The reductionist account of testimony, standardly traced to Hume, says that a hearer is justified in trusting a report only through an inductive inference: she has observed in the past that reports of the relevant kind tend to be true, and she projects that correlation onto the present case. That inference appears to require that the reports she has encountered form a roughly representative sample of the kind of report in question. Now consider an environment in which cheap technology lets anyone fabricate reports—in text, audio, and video—that are indistinguishable from sincere ones unless checked against independent evidence, and in which the fabricated reports are algorithmically selected to be maximally plausible to each hearer and to vastly outnumber the true ones. Does reductionism still license our ordinary trust in testimony in that environment? If not, state precisely which premise of the inductive argument fails and what a repaired version would need to add. Then say whether anti-reductionism, which treats testimony as a basic source of justification on a par with perception and memory, is threatened by the same development or only by a different one.
