ok so in my social theory module we've been doing habermas's communicative rationality and the ideal speech situation. the picture, roughly, is that you don't judge a norm by whether people actually agreed to it but by whether it would hold up in a discussion where everyone affected can take part, has an equal chance to speak and challenge, nobody is coerced or bribed, and the participants are actually trying to understand each other rather than just win. habermas also thinks that anyone who seriously enters into an argument is already tacitly presupposing something like this, because to argue at all is to offer reasons to someone you treat as able to be moved by them.

now suppose a development: a large share of the contributions to a public debate aren't produced by a person in the usual sense at all. they come out of a language model that can churn out fluent objections, expressions of hurt, concessions, and summaries of where the discussion has got to. the model has no beliefs of its own, can't be sincere or insincere about an inner life, can't be held to what it said a week later, and never changes its mind because one reason was better—it just updates a context window. and the people in the debate often cant tell whether they're replying to a person or a model.

does habermas's ideal speech situation survive this? i'm not after platform-policy answers like whether ai content should be labelled; i want to know what happens to the argument. does the whole test rest on a presupposition that machine-produced speech violates, or can the ideal be restated so it still does its work? if it survives, say what machine-generated speech should be treated as; if it breaks, say exactly which presupposition snaps and what, if anything, we're left with.
