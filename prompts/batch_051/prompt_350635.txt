I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, so I will frame this issue in terms of information systems. The standard lesser-evil defense of voting in a two-party system runs roughly like this. Both major candidates may be bad, but one is usually worse in expectation; the harm a voter might help prevent is huge; the probability that any individual vote is decisive is very small but nonzero; and multiplying that huge harm by that small probability still yields an expected benefit that outweighs the inconvenience of voting. So one should hold one's nose and vote for the less bad candidate.

Now suppose a mature forecasting infrastructure gives each registered voter a live, personalized estimate of the probability that their own vote will break the tie in their district or electorate, built from continuous high-resolution polling, precinct-level data, redistricting information, and machine-learning models. For the overwhelming majority of voters, that estimate is on the order of one in tens of millions or less, the forecasts are accurate enough that a rational agent would accept them, and voters see the number before deciding. Decisiveness is no longer merely small and unknown; it is quantified, visible, and effectively negligible for nearly everyone.

Does the lesser-evil argument survive this development? If it does, identify the premise that carries the weight and explain why a known, negligible probability of decisiveness does not defeat it. If it does not, say whether what remains should be reframed as an expressive act, a deontological duty, or something else, and whether the original argument was quietly relying on voters not knowing how small their chance was.
