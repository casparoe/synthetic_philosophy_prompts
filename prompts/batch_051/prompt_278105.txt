I'm a software engineer on an evals team, not a philosophy person, so maybe I'm missing the standard vocabulary here. We're building a monitor that reads a model's chain-of-thought before the model gets to take an action (run code, send a message, move money, that kind of thing), and I keep being asked what the monitor is actually supposed to guarantee. Every time I try to write real requirements instead of vibes — "the CoT should be faithful," "the model shouldn't be able to game the monitor" — I realize the requirements pull against each other and I can't tell which ones are load-bearing.

So here's what I want: if someone had an actual theory of chain-of-thought monitoring as a safety technique, what would it have to get right to count as adequate? I want the desiderata list. For each requirement, give me the case or failure mode that motivates it — a model writing a clean-sounding justification for an action it was going to take anyway, a model that learns to produce CoT the monitor likes while its real policy gets worse, a model whose CoT is faithful but whose actual reasoning is just bad. Also say which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of a particular camp, and flag any pairs you think can't be jointly satisfied at once. My intuition is that "read the real reasons" and "don't change the model's incentives by reading them" can't both fully hold once the model knows its being read, but I can't make that precise. Also, what distinctions can't the theory collapse — like the difference between the chain being evidence about what the model is doing and the chain being the actual process by which its done, or the difference between raw CoT and a summary the model writes of its own reasoning. And tell me which single requirement does the most work at ruling out the naive version we'd build first. I dont need a scorecard of existing approaches — I mainly want the spec itself and the places where it breaks.
