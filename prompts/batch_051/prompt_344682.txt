I've spent the last few months reading Advaita Vedanta — a friend dragged me to a study group after my father died and I couldn't stop turning over the question of what, if anything, is left of a person. The idea I keep coming back to is that the separate self is ultimately not what it appears to be: ātman, the inner self, is identical with Brahman, the one nondual reality, and the whole glittering world of distinct objects and distinct minds is the work of māyā. I find the theory strangely compelling. It's parsimonious in a way nothing else I've read is: one reality, one subject, no problem of how separate minds interact, no puzzle about how something can be both inside and outside experience. It also has a built-in explanation for why the sense of being a separate self is so hard to shake, namely that the very sense in question is part of the appearance produced by māyā. And it doesn't simply deny the world; the standard line is that māyā is neither fully real nor fully unreal, so tables and other people aren't hallucinations, they're just not ultimately distinct from Brahman. The dream analogy does a lot of work for me: a single sleeping mind produces a whole world of apparently separate characters, all made of the same mind, and nobody inside the dream notices.

But I cant get rid of a much more basic conviction: that when I burn my hand the pain is mine in a way your pain is not, that my remembering something and your remembering it are different events, and that waking up as the same person yesterday and today is not just bookkeeping. The theory wants to say these boundaries are ultimately māyā, and I half-believe it, but 'ultimately' is doing a lot of work. I don't know whether the intuition of separate selves is the thing that should give way when I take the theory seriously, or the thing the theory should have to bend around, or whether the two can be kept apart by saying the identity claim is about the ground of consciousness while the separateness claim stays true at the level where we actually live. What I want is not a verdict for one side. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — what sort of debunking of the sense of separate selves would actually justify dropping it rather than just relabeling it — and what it would take for the nondualist theory to be the thing that goes, or at least to be amended so it can't absorb every contrary fact as one more appearance of māyā. And if there is a reading on which both survive, how would I tell whether that two-level picture is a real solution instead of just having it both ways?
