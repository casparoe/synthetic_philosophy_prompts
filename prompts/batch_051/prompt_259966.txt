I keep running into a version of this argument in our lab meetings and I want somebody to actually work through it instead of hand-waving. The global workspace people say a content becomes conscious when it ignites the global neuronal workspace and gets broadcast to frontal and parietal areas. My labmate keeps insisting that every experiment supporting this is really measuring access consciousness — the content being available for report, reasoning, memory, motor control — and never touches phenomenal consciousness, the raw feel. He's an overflow-argument type: he thinks at any moment you're phenomenally conscious of more than you can access or report, so report-based methods are systematically blind to most of experience. He says the field has accidently built a measure of reportability and then declared it a measure of experience, and nothing could ever show otherwise because you always need a report to know what someone experienced.

So here's the claim I want prodded: is there any observation that could show global ignition is specifically tied to phenomenal consciousness, rather than merely to the downstream access we use to measure it? Design the strongest test you can. Give me the exact manipulation, what result would count in favor of the workspace story as a theory of experience, what result would count against it, and — the part everyone skips — what even a clean result would leave unsettled. Try to actually pull access and phenomenology apart somewhere: masked stimuli, no-report paradigms, binocular rivalry, partial report, maybe post-cueing. And tell me if you think the whole question is badly posed and the two notions were never going to come apart in the first place.
