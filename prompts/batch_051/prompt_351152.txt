In section II of "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), F. A. Hayek writes:

"In ordinary language we describe by the word “planning” the complex of interrelated decisions about the allocation of our available resources. All economic activity is in this sense planning; and in any society in which many people collaborate, this planning, whoever does it, will in some measure have to be based on knowledge which, in the first instance, is not given to the planner but to somebody else, which somehow will have to be conveyed to the planner. The various ways in which the knowledge on which people base their plans is communicated to them is the crucial problem for any theory explaining the economic process, and the problem of what is the best way of utilizing knowledge initially dispersed among all the people is at least one of the main problems of economic policy—or of designing an efficient economic system."

Treat this as the crux of Hayek's contribution to the socialist calculation debate: on his view, the design problem for any economic system is how dispersed knowledge is conveyed to those who plan, and the real dispute is over who does the planning. Suppose you are setting the agenda for a three-year conceptual research project, staffed by philosophers and economic theorists, whose aim is to determine what the calculation debate does and does not establish about contemporary proposals that use large-scale computation, machine learning, or platform-generated data to coordinate economic activity centrally. Which unresolved question in the debate should the project settle first, and why? Candidates might include whether Hayek's knowledge argument is independent of Mises's incentive argument, what exactly prices convey that non-price mechanisms cannot, whether Lange's trial-and-error reply answers Mises or Hayek or neither, and whether the argument's force is limited to static allocation and says little about innovation; but do not feel bound by that list. I want a defended ordering, not a survey: for each of the top two or three questions, say which other questions depend on it, how tractable it is for philosophers or economic theorists rather than engineers, and what evidence over the next couple of years would move it up or down the ranking.
