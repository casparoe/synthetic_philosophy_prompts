I've been chewing on a distinction I keep calling predictive overhead, and I'd like someone to stress-test it. Take a deterministic but chaotic system — weather, a double pendulum, whatever. To predict it out to some horizon you need to specify its present state to some finite precision, and in a chaotic system the precision you need keeps growing with the horizon: roughly, every extra unit of future you want forecastable costs some fixed number of additional bits about the present. So far this is just the usual point that chaos makes forecasting hard. But I think there's something stronger nearby. Determinism says there's exactly one trajectory; predictability requires that there exists a description of the present that is cheaper than just running the system. These aren't the same property at all.

I've started calling the cost of the difference the demon's rent. The claim I actually want checked is this: for long enough horizons in an unboundedly chaotic system, the predictive overhead ends up larger than the information content of the future being predicted. You need a description of the present that is more complex than the events you're trying to foretell — the forecast costs more then it reports. If that's right, then a Laplace-style demon can be exact only if it's at least as complex as the world it predicts, which means it can't be a neat little observer standing outside the system. I'm aware this brushes up against chaos theory, algorithmic information, and the old points about a predictor being inside the world it predicts. But I can't tell whether I've rederived something trivial or made a real point. Give me the strongest objections to the demon's rent, and tell me where it breaks. Have I smuggled an epistemic notion of information into an ontic claim about determinism, or does the idea actually do work?
