I am putting together the final week of an undergraduate seminar on philosophical justifications of hierarchy, and I want to pair Aristotle's discussion of natural slavery in the Politics with Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go. The pairing feels right, but I want to avoid the easy version of the reading, where the novel is treated as a generic dystopian warning against treating people as means. Aristotle's claim is more specific and more uncomfortable than that: a natural slave, for him, is not merely someone conquered or coerced, but someone whose nature suits them to be another person's possession and instrument. Such a person can recognize reason and respond to it, but does not possess it in the full deliberative sense that would make self-rule appropriate; their body and disposition are fitted for necessary labor; and being governed by someone who does have deliberative reason is, on Aristotle's account, genuinely beneficial to them rather than a mere injury. At the same time, Aristotle recognizes that slavery by law or convention can come apart from natural slavery, and that many who are enslaved in practice may not be natural slaves at all. Ishiguro's clones are created and raised to be carers and then donors: Hailsham gives them an education, a culture of art-making, and a language of being special, while the surrounding society treats their eventual donation as simply what they are for. The novel follows Kathy, Tommy, and Ruth as they love, scheme, remember, and pursue the rumor that a real love affair might earn a couple a deferral, all without the regime having to hold them in chains.

I want a reading of the novel through Aristotle's concept of natural slavery that works in both directions. Where does the lens illuminate something real about the clones' condition, and which scenes or choices should anchor that part of the discussion? Where does the novel falsify or complicate Aristotle's criteria, given that its protagonists plainly deliberate, plan, lie, and love? Does Aristotle's own distinction between natural and legal slavery ultimately condemn the world of the novel, or does the novel show that the distinction itself is too easy, because a legal and social system can manufacture the appearance of natural fitness by shaping people until they seem to lack deliberation, or until they experience their unfreedom as identity and even vocation? Finally, what does Ishiguro's treatment add to the philosophical debate that Aristotle's categories, read charitably, do not make visible? I would like the result to read as criticism rather than illustration: faithful to the texture of the novel and honest about the theory, without softening either Aristotle or Ishiguro to make the other look coherent.
