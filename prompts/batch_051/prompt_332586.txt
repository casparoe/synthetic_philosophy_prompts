After a long argument with a friend about whether two honest and rational people can legitimately keep disagreeing, I came away puzzled. My friend kept appealing to Aumann's agreement theorem. As I understand it, the theorem says that if two Bayesian agents share a common prior and it is common knowledge between them what posterior probability each assigns to some event, then their posterior probabilities must coincide; they cannot agree to disagree. Once those conditions are in place the proof seems almost trivial, yet the conclusion feels wildly out of step with the stubborn persistence of disagreement among thoughtful people.

I suspect the problem lies in an idealization the theorem takes for granted. The standard result assumes exact common knowledge: each agent knows the other's posterior, knows that the other knows theirs, and so on without bound, with every step in that regress perfectly precise. Real agents are computationally bounded. They can iterate the I-know-that-she-knows hierarchy only finitely many times, they reason to some fixed depth, and what they actually communicate is coarse or noisy rather than an exact posterior probability. Suppose we weaken the setup accordingly: common knowledge up to some finite level k, or common p-belief, where each level of the hierarchy is assigned probability at least p, or agents modeled as finite automata. Does the agreement result still go through under any of these weakenings? Which premise is doing the real work, and is there a repaired theorem that yields only approximate agreement under approximate common knowledge, or does bounded rationality break the conclusion entirely?
