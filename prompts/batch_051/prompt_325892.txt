For my ethics bowl team I've been messing with the idea that moral compromise is just trade: two people with different values can make a deal where each walks away thinking the world is better by their own lights, even though each still thinks the other's values are wrong. In econ they call it gains from trade, except here the things being traded are moral concessions. Sounded nice until I built this argument and now I can't find the exit.

Take any genuine moral compromise: two people, Ana and Ben, disagree on something serious, and they settle on a middle policy Q. Ana thinks Q is better than the status quo but still wrong because of what it gives Ben; Ben thinks the same from his side. Here's the argument that this is never actually justified:

(1) A compromise is justified only if each party has sufficient reason, all things considered, to prefer the compromise to the status quo.

(2) In a genuine moral compromise, each party has to help bring about something that, by their own sincere judgment, is seriously morally wrong — Ana helps lock in the part Ben wanted that Ana thinks is wrong, and vice versa.

(3) If you sincerely judge that X is seriously morally wrong, then no amount of getting what you want on other fronts can give you sufficient reason to deliberately help bring about X. Otherwise you have to admit that a good enough outcome on other fronts can justify deliberately doing something seriously wrong.

(4) So in any genuine moral compromise, neither party has sufficient reason to agree (from 2 and 3), which means the compromise can't satisfy (1).

(5) Therefore a genuine moral compromise between people who actually disagree on something serious is never justified. It either wasn't genuine (one of them didn't really think the concession was wrong) or it wasn't justified.

The reason this freaks me out is it proves to much. It doesnt just rule out compromise between strangers; it rules out all the everyday cases we'd want to keep. Like: a pacifist who thinks her taxes funding the military is wrong but still votes for a bill that funds schools and the military, because the schools matter that much to her. Or a prison abolitionist joining a coalition for a bill that includes some jail funding. Or a vegan agreeing to family dinners with meat on the table in exchange for her family doing two meatless nights a week. By the argument, every single one of those is unjustified, and that feels insane.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? My gut says I most want to keep (1) — if a compromise isn't better than the status quo by each person's own lights, why would they agree? — and I also don't want to give up (3), because the idea that you can buy off your own conscience with gains on other fronts seems plainly wrong to me. But I can't figure out how to keep both and still deny the conclusion. Also, after you answer, tell me where your own diagnosis is shakiest: what argument or counterexample would make you switch to blaming a different step?
