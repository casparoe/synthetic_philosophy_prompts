I teach a seminar on the ethics of memorialization and simulated persons, and I have been collecting a colleague's judgments in the hope of extracting the principle she is actually applying. She refuses to state it herself. Her record, so far as I know it, is this.

1. A widow, whose late husband had told her while alive to use their letters however helped her, trains a text-only model on their correspondence, with his parents' support, and uses it privately to get through the worst of her grief. She judges it permissible.

2. A firm scrapes a recently deceased essayist's published journals, sells subscriptions to a chatbot that carries on the correspondence, and ignores the family's objections. She judges it impermissible.

3. An archive, with the descendants' cooperation, builds a clearly labelled simulation of a long-dead diarist so scholars can query the letters; the tool is free and non-commercial. She judges it permissible.

4. A son secretly clones his late mother's voice from her voicemails, programs a smart speaker to give him life advice in her voice, conceals this for years, and refuses his siblings' requests to delete it. She judges it impermissible.

5. A studio, with the estate's permission, digitally inserts a long-dead actor into a brief, respectful, clearly credited cameo in a biographical film. She judges it permissible.

Now I have a sixth case. A terminally ill man gives his wife explicit written consent, well before his death, to simulate his conversational self afterward, and records detailed answers to questions he expects her to ask; he and his family support the plan. A year after his death, the wife has withdrawn from her friends, plays the simulation through the night, and her therapist reports that she is using it to avoid facing the loss. She refuses either to pause the simulation or to see a grief counselor. The simulation itself is faithful to his recorded wishes and contains nothing he did not authorize.

If my colleague is consistent with her record, will she judge this sixth case permissible or impermissible? Which earlier case is the closest analog? I can see two live hypotheses about her principle: one turns on authorization and control, the other on the welfare of the surviving user. I want you to commit to a single prediction and a single principle, with your reasons, rather than laying out the options and stopping, and to describe one further case that would tell the two hypotheses apart if I pursue the matter. Keep your answer to at most 400 words.
