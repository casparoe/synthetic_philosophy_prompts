I teach an upper-division philosophy of language seminar, and I want to spend a session on two-dimensional semantics using Borges's Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote. The story's premise: a fictional twentieth-century author named Pierre Menard sets out not to transcribe but to compose, from his own thought and in his own historical situation, a small body of prose that turns out word-for-word identical with passages from Part One of Don Quixote; Borges's narrator claims the identical wording means something entirely different in the two centuries. Write me a critical reading of the story through two-dimensional semantics. Work with the usual distinction between primary and secondary intensions — epistemic possibilities considered as actual, versus metaphysically possible worlds given how the actual world in fact is — and use it to state precisely what sort of difference Menard's feat is supposed to produce: a difference in primary intension, in secondary intension, in character, in what is merely pragmatically conveyed, or in something the framework has no room for. Pay particular attention to the narrator's running comparison of passages in which a sentence conventional in Cervantes becomes conceptually daring in Menard solely in virtue of when and by whom it was written. Then do the reciprocal work: say where the story tracks the two-dimensional apparatus surprisingly well, and where Borges blurs distinctions the apparatus keeps apart, such as the difference between the standing meaning of a sentence and what an author can be taken to have meant by tokening it. End with what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the formal machinery alone misses. I want sustained criticism rather than illustration: take the story on its own terms and let it push back on the theory when it should. No quotations from the story or the philosophical literature, no citations, no bibliography; the students have the text and I want the reading itself.
