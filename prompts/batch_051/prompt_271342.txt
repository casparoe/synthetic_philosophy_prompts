I am a retired organic chemist, and I have recently joined a philosophy seminar for lifelong learners. This term the tutor has been leading us through the concept of rights, and she has set out three broad accounts for the group to work with. On the first, rights are trumps: an individual right normally blocks a policy justified by the general welfare, and it may be overridden only in exceptional or catastrophic circumstances or in collisions with other rights. On the second, rights are side constraints: they are agent-relative prohibitions on how one may treat persons, so one may not intentionally use someone as a mere means even to the best overall outcome, though harms one merely foresees are sometimes acceptable. On the third, rights are instruments: they are rules justified by their tendency to secure welfare, autonomy, or coordination, and although they ought to be respected in ordinary cases, they may be overridden when the balance of reasons clearly favours doing so.

Over the term the tutor has given a series of firm verdicts without indicating which account she is drawing upon. She maintains that the state may not compel a healthy passer-by to donate an organ to save five transplant patients; that public health authorities may confine someone with a highly contagious and dangerous disease even against that person's wishes; that the state may not torture a suspect to force disclosure of the location of a bomb, however many lives are at stake; and that the police may not listen to a barrister's calls with clients without a warrant even when doing so would help convict a dangerous criminal.

This week's case is a proposal from a national health research authority. The authority holds a large archive of blood samples collected decades ago for newborn screening; the samples are stored in fully anonymised form and would otherwise be destroyed. It proposes to use them in a genomic study expected to improve early detection of a common childhood leukaemia, an advance that might save several hundred lives each year. The parents were never asked for permission, and the authority regards retrospective consent as impractical, though it would impose strict anonymisation and independent oversight. May the authority proceed without consent? I would be grateful if you would act as a professional philosopher and think through this carefully. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate rules that fit the tutor's earlier verdicts, then state how each of the three accounts would treat the new case, identify which account best explains her overall pattern, and give your best prediction of her verdict. If you judge that her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine her position, say so plainly and specify the further case that would most cleanly distinguish whichever accounts remain in the running.
