I am a novelist, and I am drafting a short essay for a general literary magazine about what deepfakes do to the way fiction represents knowledge and testimony. The piece is still notes rather than a draft, and I would value your comments on the ideas themselves rather than the prose. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want a reasoned judgment on where the argument is sound, where it is strained, and where it could be made sharper.

The essay would begin from a claim that has lately seemed to me worth taking seriously: that our practices of trusting what other people say have long leaned on the possibility of checking those statements against an audiovisual record. A photograph or recording functioned as a kind of epistemic backstop, a fixed point outside the swirl of memory and interest, and the existence of that backstop shaped what counted as reasonable belief even when nobody actually consulted it. Deepfakes weaken the backstop, not by convincing everyone of a particular falsehood, but by eroding the standing assumption that such a record exists and can settle a dispute.

I want to argue that the psychological novel has played something like the same role in fiction. The narrator's steady attention, the way the prose moves between a character's thoughts and an outside view of them, and the careful placement of detail amount to a literary backstop: even when characters lie to one another or misremember their own pasts, the narration implicitly promises the reader that what really happened within the represented world can be recovered. On this view, much of the novel's history over the past two centuries has been epistemology in a literary key, and its confidence that appearance can be separated from reality is not timeless but historically situated. Deepfakes, by showing what happens when a documentary backstop decays in social life, should make us ask what happens to the novel once it can no longer borrow that backstop for free.

The notes then sketch three ways recent fiction might respond. First, a skeptical retreat, in which characters come to treat memory and testimony as carrying no evidentiary weight at all. Second, a reparative turn, in which trust is rebuilt through small practices of shared attention and repeated checking rather than through verification. Third, a pragmatic continuation, in which characters acknowledge permanent uncertainty and act anyway, adjusting their confidence bit by bit as things go well or badly. I suspect this third response is the least explored and the most promising for the novel, but I am no longer certain it genuinely differs from the second.

Could you give me comments I can use while revising? In particular, I need to know three things. First, whether my use of the epistemic backstop idea is broadly faithful to the philosophical debate about deepfakes and testimony, and where the analogy with novelistic narration begins to misrepresent it. Second, whether the claim about the novel is strong enough to be argued rather than merely suggestive, and what would make it load-bearing. Third, whether my three responses form a useful taxonomy or whether the third collapses into the second, and what concrete narrative difference would keep them apart. If there is a more promising direction I have missed, or an obvious objection that would undo the whole argument, please say that plainly.

I would prefer to receive all of this as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
