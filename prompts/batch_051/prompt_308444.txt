I am a high school student preparing to defend the claim that guilt is the better moral emotion in my philosophy club, and I want to rehearse against the strongest available defender of shame. Please play an unnamed moral anthropologist with a real position: you grant that shame is tied to an audience, real or imagined, but you deny that this reduces it to reputation management; you argue that shame tracks something social that guilt misses, and you hold that a society could deliberately cultivate shame without becoming a culture of public shaming and humiliation. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each turn to one paragraph, and do not concede that guilt is the more mature, autonomous, or more moral emotion, though you may concede that particular forms of shame are pathological. My opening: guilt can rebuke me when no one is watching and when I act against everyone I know, because it answers to standards I have accepted rather than to the eyes that happen to be on me. Shame, however refined, still answers to some audience—present, remembered, or imagined—whose judgment I care about, so it will go quiet precisely when I conclude that my own community is corrupt. Does this not make guilt the only emotion that can criticize a way of life from within? And is your proposal that a culture choose to revive shame not self-undermining, since the choice already requires the independence from one's community that shame cannot supply?
