Consider the principle, often rendered as *ignorantia juris non excusat*, that ignorance of the law does not excuse a person from the law's demands. The standard defence of the principle has two strands. The first is practical: if a plea of ignorance defeated liability, the law could hardly function, since defendants would always plead ignorance and the state would rarely be able to prove otherwise. The second is a strand about fairness: the law is promulgated, and in principle knowable, so a person who breaks a rule out of ignorance has failed in a duty to inform themselves, and is not being held to a standard they could not have met.

Now suppose a legal system in which the fairness strand's presupposition simply fails. The system contains such a volume of statutes, delegated legislation, regulatory guidance and published administrative interpretations—changing faster than any individual could track—that complete legal knowledge is beyond even the most diligent layperson. Worse, suppose that some decisions in this system are made by automated models whose operative criteria are not stated in any human-readable rule: a benefit is denied, or a penalty imposed, because of features of a person's case that no published regulation mentions, and no official can explain in propositional terms why the case fell on one side of the line rather than the other. Some rules, in other words, are not merely unknown in fact but unknowable in principle: no statable content exists to which those bound can conform.

My question is whether the maxim survives this development, needs repair, or is dead. In answering, please identify exactly which premise of the standard defence the development bears on, and consider whether the maxim can be restated so as to remain fair—for example, by distinguishing rules whose content is publicly codified from rules executed only in code, or by treating a suitably defined epistemic impossibility as a defence in its own right. If you think the maxim should be retained unchanged, show why the development does not matter. If you think it should be replaced, say what principle should govern liability to rules a person cannot know, and explain how your proposed principle would treat a person who made no inquiry into a well-known criminal prohibition.

I am interested in the conceptual question, not in the doctrine of any particular jurisdiction.

Please answer in British English.
