Consider a family-policy regime of the kind common in much of Northern Europe, and in weaker forms elsewhere. It does three things at once: (i) it replaces a large fraction of a new parent's prior wage for roughly a year, so the payment is proportional to prior earnings rather than to the care given — a banker and a cashier receive different sums for the same care of a newborn; (ii) it makes this leave available only to a parent who is taking time away from a job, while a parent who was not employed before the birth receives nothing; and (iii) once leave ends, it subsidizes non-parental childcare, but only for parents who return to work or training, while a parent who stays home to care full-time receives no payment and no subsidy. Thus the same activity — attentive, skilled care of a small child — is paid work when done by an employed parent on leave or by a subsidized caregiver, but unpaid when done by a parent outside the labor force. What view of care, of work, of the family, and of the dual-earner household does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the points at which the policy is philosophically underdetermined. Then say what would have to change in the policy if the governing view were instead that caring for one's own child is work of the same kind as paid care and should be valued on the same terms. Please write your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
