I'm a graduate student drafting a survey paper on the question of whether philosophy makes progress, and I've been trying to lay out the main positive and deflationary answers before I argue for my own. The standard positions I've collected so far are these five. The wrong-standard view: the question presupposes that a discipline is in good order only if it converges on settled results in the way the sciences do, and that standard is simply the wrong one to apply to philosophy. The dissolution view: progress consists in exposing and discarding pseudo-problems that only looked like questions in the first place. The accumulation view: what grows is not a body of settled first-order conclusions but a stock of distinctions, counterexamples, argument schemas, and conditional results of the form "if you accept A, then you are committed to B." The absorption view: mature parts of philosophy spin off as sciences, and that is where the progress has gone. And the instrument view: progress lies in producing better tools—formal logic, probabilistic modeling, formal semantics, experimental methods—rather than in fixed conclusions. I have the nagging feeling that this list is missing something that a referee will notice immediately, and I'd like to be the one who finds it first.

Tell me which account I am most plausibly missing—the one that best slots into the survey alongside the five above—and then give four further candidates as a check against more blind spots, for five numbered items in total. Each item should be one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. For each, include a clause saying whether it is genuinely distinct from the five I listed rather than a rewording, special case, or combination of them. The candidates should be positions a serious participant in the debate could actually defend, not parodies; if one candidate is clearly stronger, mark it as the one to incorporate first.
