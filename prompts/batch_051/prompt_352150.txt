I'm a retired physicist and only recently got into philosophy, so maybe I'm reading this wrong, but I've been working through Bostrom on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, and the paperclip maximizer is always the toy case. The toy is always a maximizer though: utility linear in the number of paperclips, no upper bound. I'd like to see how much of the convergence story actually hangs on that. Take the same superintelligence in the same imagined world, but make its final goal satisficing instead: it gets utility 1 if at least a trillion (10^12) paperclips exist at some point, 0 otherwise, and it's genuinely indifferent to any extra paperclips and to everything else. Assume the count is crisp and verifiable. Now run the standard instrumental-convergence argument for this agent. Before the threshold is met, do the usual convergent drives—resource acquisition, self-preservation, goal-content integrity—show up with roughly the same strength as in the maximizer? After the threshold is met, does those drives just vanish, i.e. would shutoff be no loss at all? If the drives only converge before the threshold, is that a real restriction on the thesis, or is it exactly what the word instrumental was supposed to mean all along?
