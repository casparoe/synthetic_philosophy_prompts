I am a novelist writing a historical novel about Johannes Kepler during his Prague and Linz years, and a single characterization decision is driving the plot. Kepler was at once the most exacting mathematical astronomer of his generation and a working astrologer: he cast nativities and annual prognostications, drew up a horoscope for the general Wallenstein, published a 1602 dissertation arguing for the foundations of astrology, and in 1610 produced a pamphlet defending a reformed astrology against learned critics and vulgar practitioners alike. Yet the received image of him has him dismissing astrology as a foolish daughter who keeps her wise mother astronomy fed, which is usually read as a sign that he practiced only for income while privately holding the art in contempt.

The novel's final chapters depend on which Kepler I commit to: a man who genuinely believed that the heavens act on the sublunary world through real and discoverable physical causes — light, harmonic proportion, the geometrical aspects between planets — and that a cleaned-up astrology could therefore take its place within natural philosophy, or a man who treated horoscope-casting as a contemptible but remunerative courtly trade. I am not asking for another reading list, and I do not want the reply that the evidence is merely complicated. I want your credence in one proposition: Kepler regarded his reformed, physicalist astrology as an in-principle legitimate part of natural philosophy, rather than as a cynical or purely pragmatic accommodation.

Please give me a number for that proposition and then show the derivation. Assign rough probabilities to the sub-questions your credence rests on — how much weight you place on the public astrological writings being reformist rather than anti-astrological outright; on his private correspondence and his search for physical mechanisms behind the aspects; on the horoscopes as evidence of conviction versus client management; on whether the foolish-daughter remark attacks astrology as such or only its vulgar form; and on the prior question of whether the phrase "believing in astrology" is even a well-posed category before the astronomy/astrology boundary hardened. Show how the terms combine, then tell me which term you are least confident about and what evidence — a stray private letter, a single passage read more carefully, a different framing of early modern natural philosophy — would move your final credence the most. My researcher places the sincere reading near 0.8 and my editor near 0.2; say where that disagreement most likely lives.
