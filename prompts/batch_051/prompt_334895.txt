I keep coming back to an argument with a friend about whether a sentence like "BP was to blame for Deepwater Horizon" is literally true or just a sloppy figure of speach. Take the 2010 Deepwater Horizon blowout with the facts as commonly understood: BP was the operator and made the key well-design and schedule calls, Transocean owned and operated the rig, Halliburton did the cementing, a negative-pressure test was read as successful when it probably wasn't, the blowout preventer failed, eleven people died, and the Macondo well leaked for months. I don't need you to relitigate the engineering; I want the philosophy.

Here's the theory side. One view says only individuals are the kinds of things that can intend, decide, and be morally responsible; on that view, saying "BP cut corners" is shorthand for a bunch of scattered individual decisions, and the real ethical work is figuring out exactly which managers and engineers should bear blame. The opposing view says a coporation can be a moral agent in its own right if its internal procedures turn individual choices into corporate-level commitments—policies, risk tolerances, sign-off chains, budgets—so that the corporation has attitudes that arn't identical to any single employee's attitude. There's also the "problem of many hands": if a disaster is overdetermined by lots of small decisions spread across several firms, no individual may be fully culpable even though the collective outcome is terrible.

I want you to analyze Deepwater Horizon through this disagreement. First, do the mapping carefully: who or what is the moral agent on each of the two views, what would count as BP's "decision" or "intention" to tolerate risk, and which features of the episode fit each description. Second, say honestly where each view has to go quiet or force the case—for example, what do the multi-company layers (BP/Transocean/Halliburton) do to a picture in which the corporation is the unit of blame, and does the problem of many hands dissolve if we count BP itself as one hand? Finally, what does this episode do to the theory: is it a case the corporate-agency view handles especially well, an anomaly for it, or a case it never really anticipated? I care less about the final verdict than about the mapping being precise.
