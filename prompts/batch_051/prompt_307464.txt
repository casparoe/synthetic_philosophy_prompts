I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep noticing that a lot of policy debate treats old rules as guilty until proven innocent. I want to write a column about what happens when you actually build Chesterton's fence—the rule of thumb that you shouldn't tear down a fence until you understand why it was put up—into the machinery of government, not as a slogan but as a specific procedure. Treat the following proposal as a machine, not as a moral position.

A state legislature passes a Fence Review Act. It applies to any statute or regulation that has been in force for at least thirty years. Before such a rule can be repealed or substantially amended, the committee pushing the change must have a function report comissioned. The report has to reconstruct, from the legislative record or plausible inference, what problem the rule originally solved and what ongoing function it might still be serving. If the report finds no identifiable function, repeal proceeds normally. If it finds at least one plausible function, repeal can still happen, but only through a two-year pilot: the rule is suspended in a randomly drawn set of counties matched against comparison counties, with outcome measures pre-registered before the suspension, and the repeal becomes statewide only if those measures don't worsen beyond a stated margin. A two-thirds vote can waive the pilot, but only on the stated ground that the plausible function is genuinely obsolete. There's also a public window for "fence testimony" from anyone who claims the old rule is doing invisible work—this is the Burkean bit, the idea that old institutions encode accumulated trial-and-error knowledge nobody wrote down.

What does the Act incentivize among legislators, agencies, interest groups, and ordinary citizens? How would it be gamed—through how function reports are written, which counties get "randomly" selected, which outcome measures get pre-registered, or what gets passed now in anticipation of future review? What second-order effects should I expect, such as changes in the kinds of laws legislatures pass in the first place, or which old rules get left alone? Where does the thing fail cleanest, and can any of those failures be fixed without turning the whole thing back into a slogan?

Organize your answer under these headings, in this order:

The machine (150-200 words): how the Act would actually run in practice.
Incentives and gaming (250-300 words): who responds and how.
Second-order effects (200-250 words): what happens to lawmaking and to the stock of old rules overall.
Failure modes (200-250 words): where the mechanism breaks.
What's fixable (150-200 words): which failures can be repaired without gutting the mechanism.
