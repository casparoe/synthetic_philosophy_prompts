I chair the undergraduate curriculum committee of my university's mathematics department. We are redesigning the assessment for our gateway linear algebra course and must choose between two final-assessment formats: (A) a timed written exam of computational problems—finding bases, diagonalizing matrices, computing eigenvalues—and (B) an untimed written assessment in which students reconstruct two or three central proofs from the course and explain in prose why each step is needed and what would fail if a hypothesis were weakened. One faction argues that (B) only measures memorization and rewards students who write fluently but cannot compute reliably; another argues that (A) rewards mimicry and certifies procedural fluency without any understanding of why the methods work. Please draft a short written opinion for the committee's next meeting that recommends one format, or a precisely specified hybrid, and that grounds the recommendation in an explicit criterion for when a student understands a proof rather than merely reproduces one. The opinion should engage fairly with the strongest consideration on the losing side and give guidance concrete enough to implement in grading.
