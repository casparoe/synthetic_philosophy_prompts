I am a novelist, and this term, while drafting a novel about a character who consents to a callosotomy, I am also teaching an upper-division seminar called "Fractured Subjects: Split-Brain Cases and the Unity of Consciousness." Below is the take-home examination question I set, followed by one student's submission. I would like you to grade the submission as a careful examiner would: assign a letter grade and provide the specific, point-by-point comments that justify the grade, so that the result is a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression.

Examination question:
A patient whose main interhemispheric fibers have been surgically severed fixates on the center of a screen. A picture of a spoon is flashed briefly to the left visual field. The patient verbally reports that nothing was shown, yet with the left hand they can select the spoon from among several objects placed out of view. On the strength of this and similar results, some philosophers conclude that at least two subjects of experience are present during the trial; others conclude that there is one subject whose contents are only partly integrated; and a third group concludes that the question how many subjects are present may have no determinate answer here. Assess the evidence. Your answer should do three things: (a) say which features of the case are uncontroversial and which are underdetermined by the data; (b) state the extra premise or premises needed to move from the behavioral dissociation to the two-subject conclusion; and (c) make explicit which notion of unity — unity of the subject, unity of access, or unity of reportability — your own assessment relies on, and whether that choice of notion does more work than the evidence itself.

Student submission:
Split-brain patients have had the corpus callosum severed, so the two hemispheres cannot share information directly. Because the early visual pathways cross, a spoon flashed to the left visual field reaches the right hemisphere, which controls the left hand but has little control over speech; the left hemisphere, which controls speech, receives nothing and therefore reports seeing nothing. The accurate left-hand selection shows that the right hemisphere had information about the spoon and used it to guide a deliberate choice. This cannot be mere unconscious processing: selecting the correct object in a task like this requires conscious perception, so the right hemisphere must have consciously seen the spoon. There is therefore a conscious experience of the spoon at the moment of selection that the speaking subject cannot report. But a conscious state that its subject cannot report is not a state of that subject at all. The concept of a subject of experience is the concept of the owner of conscious states, and ownership requires that a state be available for the subject's report. Hence the unreportable experience cannot belong to the speaker; it must belong to a second subject. The one-subject view can escape this only by denying that the right hemisphere saw the spoon — which the accurate selection rules out — or by admitting that a subject can have conscious states inaccessible to it, which is incoherent. The no-determinate-count view merely refuses to draw the obvious conclusion.
