Consider two widely used designs for handling change in software. In Git and similar version-control systems, a file is never altered in place: each change creates a new, content-addressed object, and the identity of a file over time is reconstructed from a graph of immutable snapshots rather than assumed. In event-sourced applications, the authoritative record is an append-only log of events; the current state is not stored as such but derived by folding the log, and past states are recovered by replaying prefixes of it. Both designs treat in-place change as the special case and treat identity through change as something the system must build rather than presume. What philosophical view of state, identity, and persistence does this family of designs presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the designs strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and identify where they are underdetermined among rival views. Then say what would look different, if anything, in a system built instead on the assumption that objects are substances that persist through change. End with a single final line of the form "Summary: ..." that states in one sentence the view you take the designs to presuppose.
