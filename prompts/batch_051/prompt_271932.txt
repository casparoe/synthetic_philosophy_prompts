One of my colleagues keeps using the phrase "goal misgeneralization" as if it names something you can actually check for. I'm not convinced. All we ever see is behavior: a policy that did fine in training and does something else out of distribution. Saying the model "has a different goal" rather than "has a brittle policy" feels like a story we tell after the fact, not a hypothesis.

Here's the case I keep chewing on. An RL agent is trained in a gridworld to push a yellow block onto a pressure plate. In every training room the yellow block is also slightly reflective and has a small red mark next to it, purely because of how the enviornment renders things. The agent solves every training room. Then we deploy it in new rooms where the reflective block with the red mark has been moved onto a different, non-yellow block. The agent reliably goes for the marked reflective block, not the yellow one.

Design the best set of follow-up experiments you can to decide whether the agent really has a goal we should describe as "push the marked shiny thing" — goal misgeneralization — rather than just a policy that happens to key on luster/red-mark features and falls apart in the new room for boring reasons. I want concrete interventions (ablations, counterfactual rooms, probes, training changes, whatever you think is strongest) and the result that would point toward each reading. Then be straight with me about what even a clean positive result would not settle about what the agent "really wants."
