I am enrolled in a university seminar this term on constitutional gaps and interregna, and the professor has spent several weeks giving rulings on invented cases about who has lawful authority when a constitution stops answering the relevant question. She has not yet stated the general rule she is applying, and next week she will rule on a new case. I want to anticipate that ruling by reconstructing the principle or principles that best fit her decisions so far, and I want to know how much those earlier decisions underdetermine the answer.

Her rulings so far, in the form I remember them:

1. When the constitution expressly makes the vice-president the acting president during presidential incapacity, a vice-president who takes over during the president's short illness acts lawfully.

2. In a particular republic, the constitution creates a vice-presidency but says nothing about succession if both the president and the vice-president die. After they die in a plane crash, the speaker of the assembly assumes the acting presidency on the ground that the country needs a single executive. The assembly passes a resolution backing her, the public complies, and three months later the constitution is amended to codify the succession. The professor says the speaker's assumption of office was constitutionally legitimate.

3. In the same republic, in an otherwise identical crisis, the chief justice announces that he is now acting president. The assembly refuses to recognize him, regional governors decline to follow his instructions, and he never exercises any real executive power. The professor says he was not constitutionally authorized to act as president.

4. After a flood makes the constitutionally fixed election date impossible, the elections commissioner postpones the vote by fourteen days because no election could otherwise be conducted. Every major party complies, and no one challenges the postponement afterward. The professor says the postponement was within the commissioner's authority.

The new case is set in the same republic. The president and the vice-president are killed together, and the constitution is still silent on succession. The speaker of the assembly immediately claims the acting presidency and obtains a supporting resolution from the assembly, but before she can establish control the chief justice also claims the office. The military, the civil service, and a majority of regional governors publicly obey the chief justice and refuse to obey the speaker. The chief justice begins issuing executive orders that are followed in practice; the speaker begins issuing rival orders that are not.

I would like to work out what the professor will most likely say: will she treat the speaker, the chief justice, or neither as the lawful acting president? More importantly, I want to identify the principle or principles her earlier rulings commit her to, state how each would apply to this new case, offer my best guess about her ruling with the reasons for preferring it, and identify the further hypothetical case that would most cleanly discriminate between the candidate principles.
