I'm taking a course on the philosophy of achievement this term, and we've been going in circles on what "excellence" even picks out. Half the class uses it to mean just being extremely good at some activity; the other half uses it as though calling something excellent already says something about its value overall, like a verdict on the thing and not just a measurement of skill. Our tutor kept saying a working definition has to earn it's keep against hard cases, so I made a list of the cases people kept fighting about:

- a neurosurgeon with a near-zero complication rate who has operated on thousands of patients
- a violinist with impeccable technique who has genuinely never played for another person
- a chess-playing program that beats every human grandmaster
- a person with an uncanny memory for license plates and not much else
- a malaria parasite superbly adapted to dodge the immune system and antimalarial drugs
- a con artist whose long-game frauds are technically brilliant
- a person who is, no way around it, an excellent torturer
- a sprinter who shatters the world record but turns out to have been doping for years
- a perfectly fine, thoroughly average home-cooked lasagna

Can you give me a working definition of "excellence" that returns a definite verdict on each of these, and then run the definition through all nine? Tell me where it gives a surprising or uncomfortable answer, and whether you'd rather revise the definition or bite that bullet. If you think the word is really two words doing two jobs—one for skill, one for commendation—say that plainly, give both definitions, and say which cases switch camps between them. I mostly want to know whether being excellent at something is, by itself, a good thing about the person or thing, or whether the goodness has to come in from somewhere else, like the beneficiary or the moral character of the activity.
