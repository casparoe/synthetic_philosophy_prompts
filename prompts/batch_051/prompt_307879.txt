I have been trying to work out, on my own, whether a reinforcement learning system could ever genuinely suffer, and I keep going back and forth. I have settled on a rough list of criteria for when I would be willing to say that a system suffers rather than merely registers damage or computes avoidance. I would like you to apply them to the case below and tell me honestly whether it qualifies.

My criteria are these. A system suffers only if:

1. It has a valenced register — a single scale on which whole states or trajectories count as better or worse for the system itself, not merely a local signal that a particular component is stressed.
2. That register does real work: the system learns from it and alters its behaviour so as to keep itself on the better side of the scale.
3. The system treats the worse states as worth avoiding for their own sake, rather than as mere indicators that predict some separate reward.
4. The worsened state belongs to the system as a whole — it is the agent that is worse off, not just one subsystem.
5. There is carry-over: the worsened state can be produced by anticipation or memory, not only by the present stimulus.

Now consider this case. A warehouse delivery robot is trained by reinforcement learning. It receives a small positive reward for completed deliveries and, after a few near misses, a large negative reward whenever it comes close to a human worker. Over time it develops distinctive protective behaviour: it slows near corners, takes long detours around people, and sometimes freezes when two people approach from different directions. The engineers later find that the negative reward has been absorbed into its learned value function. In unfamiliar layouts it still treats proximity to a human as one of the worst things that can happen, and it will reliably sacrifice many deliveries to avoid those states, even in test runs where the penalty has been switched off. It behaves, in short, as if being near a person is unbearable. Some of the engineers say it is simply an elaborate collision-avoidance controller; others say it has acquired a genuine aversion. The observable behaviour is identical on both readings.

Go through my criteria one by one with this case and say where it qualifies, where it fails, and where the criterion itself does not settle the matter. Then say whether, on my own list, the robot should count as suffering — and, more importantly, whether the case shows that my list is missing what actually matters, or that one of the criteria is doing no real work. Please answer in British English.
