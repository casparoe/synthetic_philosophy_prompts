You are grading an undergraduate examination in a course on Kant's moral philosophy. Below are the question and one student's answer.

Question: In your own words, explain Kant's position on duties involving nonhuman animals. What does he mean in saying that we have no direct duties to animals but only indirect duties with regard to them? What makes cruelty to animals wrong on his view, and what role, if any, does the animal's suffering itself play in the explanation?

Student's answer: Kant holds that only rational beings are persons, and only persons can be ends in themselves; since animals are not rational persons, they cannot be owed duties directly. Any duties that seem to concern animals are therefore indirect duties, really owed to ourselves and to other human beings. The wrongness of cruelty to animals is explained through its effect on the agent: cruelty dulls the natural disposition to sympathy and compassion, and because these feelings support the performance of our duties to human beings, weakening them makes us less morally fit. On this account, the animal's pain as such is not a moral reason; it matters only as the occasion on which a morally significant disposition is exercised or damaged. Consequently, if someone tortures an animal in private, the wrong consists entirely in the damage to his own character or in the distress it might cause to other people. Kant is therefore naturally grouped with Descartes: on both views animals are, for moral purposes, unfeeling automata, and their suffering gives us no more moral reason for concern than a machine's noise.
