I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next month I am leading a 75-minute session in our newsroom's recurring background series for about a dozen colleagues — reporters and editors on the data, technology, and science desks, numerate but not trained mathematicians. I want the session to teach one idea well rather than survey a controversy: what it means for a mathematical proof to become unsurveyable, and why that should worry us. The idea I want the group to leave with is roughly this: a proof has traditionally been expected to be surveyable, in that a single competent reader could in principle follow the whole argument from premises to conclusion; computer-assisted proofs such as the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture strain that expectation, not only because the machine might err but because the finished product can exceed any one human being's capacity to inspect it; and that in turn forces us to ask what a proof is for — a certificate of truth, an explanation, a piece of communicable understanding, or some combination of these.

I am not asking for the explanation itself; I need the architecture of the session. Give me the sequence of components in the order they must be introduced, what each depends on, and an ordering that makes the problem of unsurveyability come to seem unavoidable rather than merely asserted. I need the single example or case that should carry the whole session, and a justification for it: in particular, whether the four-colour theorem is the right carrier, or whether it is too large and a deliberately small invented case would better isolate the concept, and what the carrying example must have and must lack. I need a misconception map for this audience in the order the misconceptions are likely to arise, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off and how to do it without breaking the thread. My own suspicion is that the dominant misconception will be that a computer checking a proof is just a human checking it faster, and I would like that one disassembled early; I also expect some version of the claim that a computer-assisted proof is merely high-confidence empirical evidence. But I want the full list, not only my guesses. I need a single closing exercise or question that will tell me whether they have grasped the idea rather than merely accepted that some proofs are very long. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and where I should say so explicitly, so that legitimate technical material does not turn a conceptual problem into a technical one. The constraints are fixed: no slides and no assigned readings; everything must happen in the room.
