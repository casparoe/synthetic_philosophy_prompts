I am a novelist working on a near-future novel, and the plot has led me into a philosophical problem I can't write my way around. In the manuscript, a city's freight-routing system — jointly operated by a logistics firm, a municipal transit authority, and a sensor-maintenance contractor — makes a sequence of individually defensible choices. It reroutes a convoy onto secondary roads because of scheduled work on a rail bridge. It re-weights arrival time against fuel economy in proportions each operator had separately approved. It updates a hazard flag from the contractor's repair log, which had been filed in the wrong format and therefore read as routine rather than urgent. A sinkhole opens on one of the secondary roads, and a driver is killed. The engineers did not foresee the interaction. The dispatcher followed the agreed procedure. The oversight committee had approved the general objective weights months earlier and had no reason to revisit them. No one intended the harm, and no one I can identify was negligent in a way that clearly explains it.

My settled conviction, before writing this scene, was that there are no genuine responsibility gaps: if a harm is a moral wrong rather than a bare accident, then some person or organized body of persons is responsible for it — something went wrong in design, deployment, oversight, or maintenance. The alternative struck me as magical thinking, as if blame could attach to a distributed system the way it attaches to an agent. But as the scene has taken shape, I cannot shake the intuition that my own narrative is demonstrating the opposite. Every attempt to pin the death on a human character rings false — a forced resolution imposed by the conventions of legal blame rather than discovered in the material. The morally troubling thing about this accident, as I find myself writing it, is precisely that the harm is jointly produced by many agents none of whom is culpable, and that the system itself is not the kind of thing that can bear guilt. If I force one character to be the villain, the scene becomes a different, simpler story than the one that unsettles me.

I want to know how to think about this conflict. Present the strongest version of each side. For the no-gaps view, I want to understand what is actually required for the conviction to be true: whether responsibility must always be traceable to some person or can rest with a corporation or collective; whether "someone should have known" can do the needed work even when no actual person could reasonably have known; and what is lost, morally and legally, if we allow that no one is responsible for a serious harm. For the intuition, I want the best case that my scene is tracking something real: that the concept of responsibility was built for individual agents with beliefs and intentions, and that a genuinely distributed harm can outrun it; and whether saying "no one is responsible" is necessarily an evasion or can be an accurate description of a new kind of case. Then give me a negotiated verdict. What would justify discarding the intuition as a fiction-induced artefact? What would justify amending or abandoning the no-gaps conviction? And is there a reading of "responsible" on which both survive — for example, by distinguishing culpability, answerability, and an obligation to repair?
