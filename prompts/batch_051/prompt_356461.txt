I've been reading Aquinas's On Kingship (De Regno) and I keep coming back to a passage I can't classify. As I understand him, he says that if a ruler becomes a tyrant, the people or the public authority that put him there may remove him, and if no human remedy is available, they have to turn to God. But he is also quite firm that a private individual may not go out and kill the tyrant, because that would be contrary to apostolic teaching. So the same act, killing a tyrant, seems to change its character depending on who does it and in what capacity: private revenge is condemned, communal action is allowed, and then there is a further fallback to divine remedy if the community's route fails.

I've noticed this two-level or three-level structure before in other political writers, but I don't know what to call it. It is not just the general debate over tyrannicide, because that covers both yes and no positions. It is not quietism either, since the community can act. I am after the specific concept for the division between private and public recourse, and the move from one to the next, with God as the last resort. Does that configuration have an established name? If not, can you coin one and define it in a way that distinguishes it from straightforward justifications of tyrannicide like the kind you find in Cicero or John of Salisbury? I want the carving, not just a label: what exactly changes between the private and public cases, and what does Aquinas's version leave unresolved?
