I am drafting a two-page research proposal for a philosophy of language workshop, and I want it to be built around a less-anthologized stretch of Frege's "On Sense and Reference" rather than the usual opening about the morning star and the evening star. The passage I have in mind is this:

"The words 'the celestial body most distant from the Earth' have a sense, but it is very doubtful if they also have a reference. The expression 'the least rapidly convergent series' has a sense; but it is known to have no reference, since for every given convergent series, another convergent, but less rapidly convergent, series can be found. In grasping a sense, one is not certainly assured of a reference."

My half-formed thought is that Frege's puzzle about identity statements is standardly framed as a problem about how a true identity statement of the form "a = b" can be informative when "a" and "b" refer to the same object, and Frege's answer is that the two signs express different senses that determine the same reference. But the quoted passage suggests that a sign can carry a definite sense even when it has no reference, and that grasping a sense is not by itself a guarantee of reference. I suspect this pushes the explanatory weight in Frege's account away from co-reference and toward the internal structure of sense itself: even in true, informative identity statements, what does the cognitive work may be the fact that the two senses would remain distinct even if no object answered to one or both of them.
