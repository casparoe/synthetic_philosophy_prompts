In a philosophy of science seminar the instructor kept falling back on the old idea that vision is basically a camera: light shines in, the retina records the image, and nothing about what you believe or want can get upstream into the picture. From that he concluded that observers with rival theories can still share neutral observations. I've read some of the cognitive penetration literature, and it feels like the camera comparison is doing some quiet heavy lifting for him. Audit it. Lay out the correspondence table: what maps onto the lens, the shutter, the film or sensor, and—this seems important—the person viewing the finished photograph? Then say where that mapping holds and where it breaks once training, expectation, emotion, or background assumptions are in the loop. I want to know which of the disanalogies actually threaten his theory-neutrality conclusion and which are harmless, and whether the conclusion survives once they're on the table. And if the camera is the wrong analogy, tell me what a better one would be—one that keeps what the camera gets right without quietly importing the neutrality claim.
