I'm looking for a working definition of a contested term: "proper name." I don't want a dictionary gloss; I want an account that draws the line between proper names and other ways language picks things out, and that can survive being run against a list of hard cases. The ordinary term is used confidently—introductions, forms, and databases all presuppose one—but the boundary gets contested quickly once you move past familiar English given-name-plus-surname examples.

Here are the cases. I mark my own leanings where I have one; you are free to defend a surprising verdict if your definition forces it.

(a) "Sofia" as the given name of a particular person. Clearly a proper name.
(b) "the last person to leave the room." Clearly not a proper name.
(c) "dog." Clearly not a proper name.
(d) "Smith" in "Joan Smith." I lean toward calling this a proper name, but it is inherited and shared across people who are not, in any ordinary sense, one family, so it seems to be doing different work from "Joan."
(e) "Ivanovna" in "Anna Ivanovna Petrova." It is a patronymic, formed from the father's given name. Is it a proper name, part of a proper name, or something else entirely?
(f) "Einarsson," as used in modern Iceland by a man named Jón Einarsson, whose father is Einar. Under this convention the surname is not inherited: his daughter would be "Guðrún Jónsdóttir," not "Guðrún Einarsson." So "Einarsson" looks like a surname but does not pick out a continuing family.
(g) "Taylor" in "Taylor Wilson" and in "Wilson Taylor." In English the same string can serve as a given name or a family name, and nothing about the word itself marks which role it is playing.
(h) "Lady Gaga" as the stage name of a living performer. It names a public persona; is that enough to be a proper name of a person?
(i) "Kleenex" in "pass me a Kleenex," meaning any facial tissue. Genericized trademarks hover between proper name and common noun.
(j) "Socrates." A single name with no family-name component: a case where one expression does all the picking out.

I want a definition that classifies these cases well. Please state the definition, run it against each case, say where it misclassifies or goes silent, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Then tell me how confident you are in the final definition overall, and single out the specific case or move you are least sure about. Please write in American English. End with a one-sentence summary of your final definition as the last line, formatted as \answer{...}.
