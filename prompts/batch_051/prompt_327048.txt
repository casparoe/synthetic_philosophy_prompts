I am an evolutionary biologist who works on speciation, and I think natively in terms of populations and demes (interbreeding local groups), gene flow between them, reproductive isolation, ring species, anagenesis (a lineage changing without splitting) versus cladogenesis (branching into descendant lineages), and the three species concepts I use: biological, phylogenetic, and ecological. I keep running into a debate about language identity that looks to me like the same problem in different material: when did Latin become Italian, and is there even a fact of the matter?

The positions I encounter are roughly these. (1) The boundary is vague: adjacent generations have always understood one another, so any line drawn between Latin and Italian is an arbitrary cutoff in a continuum. (2) Identity is mutual intelligibility: a language ends when the relevant kind of intercomprehension fails. (3) A language is a historical individual: the chain of transmission was continuous, so Latin did not become Italian; the lineage persisted, and Latin and Italian are only names for earlier and later stages of the same continuing object. (4) Language identity is a social-institutional fact: a language is the dialect that got standardized, named, and claimed by a community through a literary standard, an academy, schooling, and national media, not through any purely linguistic property.

Restate each of these four positions in my vocabulary, one by one, as faithfully as you can. For each one, make the mapping explicit: what in the language case plays the role of a deme, of gene flow, of reproductive isolation, of a ring-species endpoint, of anagenesis, of a species concept. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my framework at all.

Finally, tell me your verdict: does the residue show that my framework is missing something important about what a language is, or does it show that the language debate is partly a social-conventional or terminological argument dressed up as a question of identity? I want the translation to be honest; do not smuggle in the biology framework's assumptions and call that a translation, and treat the residue as the interesting part. Please write the answer in American English.
