At my cousins graduation party last weekend my uncle Rob and my cousin Maya got into a long argument about whether the switch from hunting and gathering to farming was a huge mistake in human history. Maya is a college sophmore taking an anthro class on early civilizations, and she was basically arguing that yes, for the average person it was a bad deal. Rob (her dad, software guy who grows tomatoes) was arguing that calling it a mistake is confused. I was sitting there eating cake and just listening. It went something like this:

(1) Maya: The skeletal evidence is pretty grim. Early farmers were shorter than the hunter-gatherers they replaced, with more cavities, more signs of malnutrition and joint damage, and they died younger. And foragers seem to have spent maybe half as much time getting food as farmers did. So on health and free time, the first farmers were worse off.

(2) Rob: Okay, but worse off per person is not the same as "mistake." Farming can feed way more people on the same land. So the comparison is between a small population of fairly healthy foragers and a much bigger population of less-healthy farmers. Until you say whether you care about total or average wellbeing, the health data doesn't settle whether the switch was a mistake.

(3) Maya: It also created inequality. Hunter-gatherers couldn't really hoard food, so they had to share. Once you can store grain in a granary, someone controls the granary, and next thing you get chiefs, taxes, social classes. And it's a one-way ratchet: once population grows and the old foraging skills and land are gone, nobody can just go back. So saying people "chose" it is too generous.

(4) Rob: Two problems. First, there were farming villages with basically no big chiefs or classes for thousands of years, so farming made hierarchy possible but not automatic — you can't blame the grain for the chief. Second, if it's a one-way ratchet, then calling it a mistake is weird, because a forced move isn't a bad choice, it's no choice. And honestly, you're judging the whole transition by its costs while still enjoying the surplus it made possible.

I can't figure out who to side with, so I'm curious how an outsider would score it. Please adjudicate it move by move: for each of the four moves, give a rating on this scale — clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, mixed, mostly right, clearly right — and a sentence or two justifying the rating; if a move has two parts, you can say which part pulls it up or down. Then say who is right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing. End with a one-sentence summary as the very last line, starting exactly with "Verdict:".
