I have been going in circles about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I want to know where the following reconstruction of it goes wrong, because as it stands it seems to prove both too much and too little at once. The argument has eight steps, and each of them looks close to forced when I read them in isolation.

1. Each individual has preferences over lotteries that satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each can be represented by a utility function u_i that is unique only up to positive affine transformation. 2. Society — understood as a social observer, or a planner — also has preferences over the same lotteries satisfying those axioms, represented by u_s. 3. Pareto indifference: whenever every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, the social observer is indifferent between them too. 4. Given 1–3, Harsanyi's theorem says u_s must be an affine function of the individual utilities: u_s(L) = Σ_i α_i u_i(L) + β, for some real weights α_i and a constant β. 5. So society is representable as maximizing a weighted sum of individual utilities, which looks as if the theorem has handed us utilitarianism from nothing but individual rationality, social rationality, and a Pareto condition. 6. But the weights are not pinned down by anything in the setup; replacing u_i by a_i u_i + b_i (with a_i positive) leaves individual preferences unchanged, and absorbing each a_i into the corresponding weight reproduces the very same social utility function up to an additive constant. Adding a stronger Pareto condition that constrains strict social preference can at most force the signs of the weights; it cannot fix their relative sizes. 7. So the theorem appears to establish no particular interpersonal trade-off: the same social preferences can be cast as weighting one person a billion times more than another, or the reverse. 8. Yet step 5 was supposed to be the substantive result — the one that rules out maximin, prioritarianism, and any nonlinear social welfare function — and now it appears the theorem simultaneously proves a controversial ethical conclusion and no determinate version of it.

I honestly cannot tell whether the problem is a faulty inference somewhere in 4–8, or whether the chain is valid and the real cost is in one of the premises — most plausibly the premise that society should be modeled as a von Neumann–Morgenstern reasoner over lotteries in the first place. If I had to declare which claims I most want to keep, they would be: (a) the affine form in step 4 does real work and does not collapse into bookkeeping about arbitrary units; and (b) the theorem by itself does not settle what relative weight one person's utility should receive. I want the answer to name the step that is false, or the premise that is least costly to surrender, and to be explicit about what surrendering it costs. Please also say how confident you are in the diagnosis, and single out the specific point you are least sure about. American English throughout, please.
