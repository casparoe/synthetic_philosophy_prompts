I'm a retired physicist who's been reading philosophy for a couple of years, and I keep getting tangled up in the identity of indiscernibles. I used to think I had a workable set of criteria for when two things are really two things and not just one thing described twice. My rough criteria for counting A and B as distinct objects are: (1) spatial distinctness — they're not both wholly in the same place at the same time; (2) seperate histories — you can trace each one's trajectory or causal chain backward without the lines every merging; (3) a difference-maker — there is at least one property or relation that A has and B lacks, so that a fully explicit description could label one as A rather than B; and (4) reidentifiability — in principle you can pick one out and track it through time. I realize (1) and (2) sort of overlap, but I'm keeping them apart because I'm not sure they always do.

The case that's been eating at me is Max Black's old thought experiment. Picture a universe with nothing in it except two iron spheres. They're exactly alike in size, shape, mass, temperature, composition, color, everything; they're a mile apart and sit motionless in otherwise empty space. Criteria (1) and (2) make them look like two. But there seems to be no difference-maker: neither sphere has a property or relation the other lacks, unless I quietly smuggle in an absolute position or a cosmic 'left.' And if I can't distinguish them, can I really reidentify one of them over time, or am I just counting the same sphere twice? Still, the straightforward description — two spheres, a mile apart — seems impossible to give up. My old training makes it worse: in quantum mechanics, identical electrons are like this, and the formalism handles it by saying you cannot label them at all. Swapping two electrons in the wavefunction changes nothing (or just the sign), and the antisymmetrized state is naturally read as: there are two, but no fact of the matter about which is which. That analogy is honestly what pushed me back to Black.

Act as a patient philosopher of physics. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming which of my four criteria are doing real work and which are just disguised versions of each other. Then go through the criteria one at a time and say whether Black's two-sphere universe qualifies as containing two distinct objects on that criterion, where the criteria underdetermine the answer, and whether the case shows that one of my criteria — I suspect the difference-maker, (3) — needs to be weakened or thrown out. I'd also like your verdict on whether the quantum identical-particle case is really the same problem or just a physical analogy that muddies the water.
