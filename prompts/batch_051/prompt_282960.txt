A friend of mine has been defending the fast-fashion industry, and he keeps reaching for one picture to make his case: a sweatshop job is a ladder out of poverty. A low rung is still above the ground, and kicking the ladder away because you dislike how low the rungs are only leaves people on the ground. From this he concludes that consumers should not boycott, and that governments should not impose minimum wages or costly safety rules on these factories, because any job is better than no job and the exchange leaves both sides better off than they would have been without it.

I want to know how far that ladder picture actually holds. What maps onto what in the analogy: the worker, the factory owner, the foreign buyer, the worker's fallback of subsistence farming or informal work, the rungs themselves? Where does the mapping break down? For example, a ladder is a stable object that nobody profits from when you climb it, whereas a sweatshop job is an ongoing arrangement whose terms are set by people who gain from how much of the benefit they can keep for themselves. And the person inviting her to climb may also have had a hand in making the ground below her as low as it is. It also seems to me that the picture quietly assumes the only comparison that matters is between the lowest rung and the ground, and that if the worker is better off than she would otherwise have been, the arrangement cannot be unfair. Is that assumption actually delivered by the analogy, or just imported by it? In other words, could a deal be genuinely better than a person's alternatives and still be exploitative?

I would like you to audit the analogy point by point, and then tell me which disanalogies matter for my friend's conclusion and which do not. Once those are on the table, does his conclusion survive, namely that a mutually beneficial deal cannot be exploitation and should be left alone? And if the ladder fails, is there a better analogy that keeps whatever is true in his argument without smuggling in the rest?
