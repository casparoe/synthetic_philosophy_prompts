I'm teaching a philosophy of law seminar and want a "which position on dead-letter laws do you actually hold?" decision tree for a handout. The setup is the desuetude problem: a statute stays on the books for decades but is never enforced (a Sunday-trading law, an old anti-blasphemy provision, a jaywalking ordinance nobody touches), and then a new prosecutor announces she'll start charging people under it. I want the tree to sort students into positions on whether the long non-enforcement itself gives defendants a defense or gives a judge a reason to refuse to apply the law.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of -isms, but make sure the leaves include at least: a strict pedigree positivist, a fair-notice/rule-of-law view, a desuetude-as-tacit-repeal (customary law) view, and something natural-law-ish. Each branch node should be a question a student could answer about the scenario without already knowing which label it leads to—things like: does what officials actually do change what the law is, or only what it would be wise to do? Is the problem that the defendant really relied on the non-enforcement, or something more systemic about congruence between law-on-the-books and law-in-action? Can a court do this itself, or is it something only the legislature should do? For each leaf, give it a name (or "unnamed" if nobody really defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment—the bullet a smart defender has to bite. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't separate, and flag any unoccupied combinations that fall out of your questions, with a sentence on why that seat is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Aim for 6–8 leaves and at most five decision questions. End with a one-sentence summary of the trees overall shape; put that sentence inside \answer{...}.
