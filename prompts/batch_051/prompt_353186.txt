You are an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of personal identity, and you write unusually clear pedagogical materials. I am putting together a handout for a discussion group of scientifically literate non-philosophers on the question of whether it is rational to sign up for cryonics. Please design a decision tree that sorts a respondent into a stance on cryonics based on what they accept about a sequence of concrete claims, not on which label they already prefer.

Before writing the final tree, think carefully about the branching structure: identify the questions on which the live positions genuinely come apart, work out which combinations of answers are logically possible, and only then locate the actual defended positions within that space. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of school labels.

Each branch node should be phrased as a yes/no or either/or question that a respondent can answer without knowing the name of any position it leads toward. Where a technical notion is indispensable, for example psychological continuity or expected value, gloss it in one clause. Each leaf should give the stance an informative name, followed by one sentence stating that view's most awkward commitment, the cost a defender has to pay.

The tree must use all of the following splits, though you may add others and order them as you judge best:

1. Whether death is a harm for the one who dies at all.
2. Whether the revived person would have to be identical to the current person, or whether a psychologically continuous successor would be enough for signing up to make sense.
3. Whether a rational agent may act on a credence as low as, say, one in a million or one in a billion in an enormous finite future good, or whether such Pascal's-mugging reasoning needs to be bounded, discounted, or rejected.
4. Whether the effective probability of revival should be the one advertised by cryonics organizations, or whether it should be lowered substantially in light of base rates, the risk that the organization or the surrounding civilization will not survive long enough, and conflicts of interest.
5. Whether the expected value of the bet survives comparison with the best alternative use of the money, such as effective charities or present needs, and whether there is some upper bound on what even a small chance of future life can rationally demand of you.

The leaves must include at least the following, but you may add up to two more if the logic requires them: a committed signer-up who judges both identity and expected value favorable; a Pascalian bettor who signs while conceding a minuscule probability; a sign-up-if-cheap insurance bettor; a successor altruist who denies that strict identity is what matters but signs to benefit the continuing person; an identity refusenik who thinks revival would not be them and therefore cannot benefit them; a base-rate skeptic who thinks the effective probability is low enough to make sign-up pointless; an opportunity-cost refuser who thinks the funds do more good elsewhere; and a death-indifferent refuser who sees no harm in death that a possible revival could remedy. If two of these leaves cannot be cleanly separated by your nodes, say so explicitly and explain what further information would separate them.

Keep the tree to roughly eight branch nodes and at most ten leaves. After the tree, add a brief note diagnosing any unoccupied regions of the answer space: for each empty combination of answers, say whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Aim for an instrument that someone who has never read anything about cryonics could still answer honestly, while still separating the positions that actually matter in the debate.
