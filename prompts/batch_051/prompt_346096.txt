I am a novelist planning an alternate-history novel about the early twentieth-century rebuilding of analysis and topology. The book turns on a divergence, and I want to work through its consequences before I commit to the plot. Suppose that, in the early decades of the twentieth century, the mathematical community standardizes on sequential convergence as the primitive notion for continuity and limits: a continuous function is one that preserves convergent sequences, a closed set is one that contains the limits of its convergent sequences, a compact space is one in which every sequence has a convergent subsequence, and so on. The neighborhood and open-set formulations are treated as secondary language rather than as the foundation, and the definitional choice hardens into textbooks, training, and the habits of researchers. I am trying to understand why certain definitions — the open set, the group axioms, the modern notion of continuity — come to feel indispensable, so I want to know how much of that indispensability reflects the mathematics itself and how much is path-dependence.

Predict, with reasoning, how twentieth-century mathematics would have developed under this sequence-first convention. Which fields would proceed roughly as they did, which would stall, and which would eventually be forced to rediscover something like open sets? What concrete problems or phenomena would put pressure on the sequence-first framework — for instance, spaces whose structure is too rich or too finely resolved for sequences to capture — and would those failures surface quickly or only once some field tried to generalize? Where would the sequence-first language mislead its users, and where might it actually have been a useful discipline? For each step, make your reasoning visible: say which consequences are robust, which are guesses, what assumptions they require, and which actual developments in the history of topology and functional analysis would have to be different for your prediction to be wrong.
