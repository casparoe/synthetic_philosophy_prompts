I'm in a cultural evolution class and I keep going in circles on the memetic view of beliefs — the idea that cultural items spread because they're good at spreading, not because they're true, and that being "good at spreading" just means being catchy, memorable, or good at making the host pass you on. The theory earns its keep: it explains chain letters, earworms, urban legends, and why careful true ideas can get outcompeted by nonsense. But I have a conviction I can't shake, which is that at least some of my beliefs — vaccines work, the planet is old, evolution happened — I hold because of reasons and evidence, and the reasons are the actual cause, not a story I tell afterwards. The two genuinely seem to clash. If the memetic view is right, the felt sense of "I believe this because its true" could just be another feature that makes a belief good at surviving inside me, like the self-defending memeplexes that come with built-in shields ("doubt is a sin", "if you question this you just don't get it"). But then the theory is debunking the very faculty I'd have to use to accept the theory. I want the strongest steelman of both sides, and then a verdict on what should give way — under what conditions should I debunk the intuition as a defense mechanism, under what conditions should I amend the theory (maybe truth is a replicative advantage in some environments, but only contingently), and is there a reading where both survive because one is about populations and the other about individual epistemology? And concretely: is there a way to tell, in my own case, whether the "but I have reasons!" response is reason-giving or just the meme's immune system doing its job?
