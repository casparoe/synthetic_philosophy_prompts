I'm an economist by training, so I naturally reach for utility functions, discount rates, existence values, externalities, and intergenerational welfare when I hear a philosophical claim. I keep hearing philosophers talk about Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture and the thought that human extinction would be bad even if it involved no extra deaths. As I understand it, the idea goes like this. Compare two worlds: in one, you die at your normal time and humanity carries on after you; in the other, you die at the same time and thirty days later an asteroid sterilizes the planet. You're dead either way before the difference shows up. Scheffler's claim is that these two worlds matter very differently to us now, and that this shows how much of what we care about depends on a background assumtion of a collective afterlife, meaning a human future that continues after our own lives end. Restate that position in the economist's vocabulary as faithfully as you can, using whatever notions you need from that toolkit. Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts only go through with some fudging, and which parts have no counterpart at all. My guess is the whole thing is just existence value plus a big externality, but I want to know what I'd be papering over. Is there anything left over because the economic vocabulary can't express it, or is the leftover a sign that Scheffler's claim is less precise than it sounds?
