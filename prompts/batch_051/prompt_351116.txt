I am a retired biochemist who has only lately begun reading philosophy and biblical scholarship, so I apologise if I am missing distinctions that specialists would find elementary. I have been puzzled by the long-running standoff between Paul and James on faith and works. In Romans and Galatians, Paul appears to say that a person is put right with God by faith, apart from carrying out the works prescribed by the Mosaic law; in the second chapter of his letter, James appears to say that a faith which issues in no deeds is lifeless and that a person is put right by deeds and not by faith alone. Since at least the Reformation this has been treated as a contradiction to be reconciled, and the standard reconciliations — that the two authors mean different things by faith, by works, and by justify — are so familiar that they have not ended the argument; each side still reads the other's proof-texts through its own commitments.

My working hypothesis, formed from a scientific habit of asking whether apparently conflicting statements employ their key terms in the same sense, is that the debate is largely verbal: Paul may use justify for God's declaring someone righteous, while James may use it for God's showing someone to be righteous, and the two men are answering different questions about different moments in the religious life. But I suspect this is too tidy, and that beneath the lexical ambiguity there is a substantive disagreement about whether standing with God is a status conferred once and for all or a condition made evident over time in a life. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Please answer as a professional philosopher of religion with a strong grip on the history of the debate.
