I am a retired natural scientist, and I have recently been working through Hellenistic and early modern debates about whether the passions should be cultivated, extirpated, or merely governed. In a reading group I was given the following five-part test for an affective episode to count as an emotion that could form part of a good life, rather than as mere physiological noise, reflex, or objectless mood:

1. The episode involves a representation or appearance that something good or bad for the agent is present, imminent, or likely.
2. That representation is in principle open to the agent's endorsement, rejection, or revision through reasoning.
3. The episode has a distinctive felt quality: there is something it is like to be in it, often connected with the body.
4. The episode supplies a characteristic action tendency, not merely diffuse arousal.
5. The episode's duration and intensity are sensitive to changes in the agent's assessment of the situation.

Now consider a case from a deep-brain stimulation report. A patient with treatment-resistant chronic pain has an implanted electrode in the periaqueductal gray matter. When the device is switched on, she reports a warm, buoyant sensation of well-being that begins almost immediately. She insists that the feeling is not about anything: she does not believe her life has improved, that some good is present, or that any danger has receded. Her judgments about her condition are, she says, unchanged. Nevertheless, while stimulated she is observably more likely to smile, to initiate conversation, and to comply with physical therapy. When the device is switched off, both the feeling and the behavioral changes disappear within minutes. She describes the state as being more like a colored light than like an emotion.
