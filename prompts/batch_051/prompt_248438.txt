I recently attended a public lecture on constitutionalism in which the speaker repeatedly invoked the slogan that ours should be a system of "the rule of law, not of men." He relied on it to argue that courts, rather than elected assemblies, ought to have the final authority over the interpretation of statutes; as he put it, allowing legislators or voters to settle the meaning of the law would be to allow men to govern, rather than law. I am fairly sure that the argument moved too quickly, but I have been struggling to say exactly how.

I would like you to take that sentence — "the rule of law, not of men" — and separate out the distinct precise claims it could be used to express, from the anodyne to the radical. For each candidate reading, please say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or nearly so, and explain why. I am especially interested in readings involving the generality and equal application of legal norms, the publicity of law, prospectivity, the independence of the courts, the principle that public officials hold only conferred powers, and the sense in which judges inevitably exercise human judgement when they apply the law. Finally, set out which of these readings, if any, would actually sustain the lecturer's conclusion that courts should have the final authority over statutory meaning, and which would not.

Please write your answer in American English.
