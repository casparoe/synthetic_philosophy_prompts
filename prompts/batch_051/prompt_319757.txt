I write about science and ideas, and I keep encountering a claim that American moral culture prizes individual conscience while German moral culture prizes duty and order. I want to test that claim against a primary text, not a stereotype. In Ralph Waldo Emerson’s essay “Self-Reliance,” he writes:

“A boy is in the parlour what the pit is in the playhouse; independent, irresponsible, looking out from his corner on such people and facts as pass by, he tries and sentences them on their merits, in the swift, summary way of boys, as good, bad, interesting, silly, eloquent, troublesome. He cumbers himself never about consequences, about interests: he gives an independent, genuine verdict.”

Emerson presents the boy’s lack of concern for consequences and social approval as a model of healthy judgment, and suggests that adult self-consciousness is a kind of imprisonment. The idea seems to be that conscience works best when it has not yet been disciplined by law, custom, or calculation.

Compare this Emersonian picture of conscience with a competing picture, drawn mainly from German sources—Kant’s insistence that a good will acts from duty rather than inclination, and Hegel’s view that freedom is realized not by standing apart from institutions but by participating in them. On that competing picture, the boy Emerson praises would be not a paradigm of moral health but a case of undeveloped caprice; genuine conscience would require submission to a universal law or to ethical norms embodied in social order.

I would like you to do three things. First, state as precisely as you can what Emerson is claiming in the passage and what makes it attractive. Second, state the strongest version of the German-style objection: why would someone think that binding oneself to duty and order is itself a form of freedom rather than its opposite? Third, evaluate which picture has the better of it. Is there a coherent middle position that accepts Emerson’s point about the authority of individual insight while also accepting the German point that unconstrained private judgment is insufficient for moral life? Be specific about how such a position would handle cases where individual conscience conflicts with established order.
