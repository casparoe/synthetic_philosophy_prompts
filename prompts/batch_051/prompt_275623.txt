A self-governing school's constitution includes this provision: "Every rule of the School is an experiment. No rule shall be adopted unless the community has first stated the problem it is intended to solve and the observable result by which it is to be tested; and every rule shall stand only so long as that result is achieved." The school's curriculum committee has quietly installed a machine-learning scheduler that assigns students to seminar sections. It has reduced scheduling conflicts and raised attendance, but the committee never stated a problem or a test in advance, and no one on the committee can explain why the system places any given student in any given seminar. The committee says the scheduler is not a "rule" but a tool, and that its success in practice is exactly the test the constitution demands. Does the scheduler fall under the provision, and does it satisfy it? Argue from the words, from the purpose of the clause as an embodiment of Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry, and from what its authors would most plausibly have said if asked; then say which of these approaches should settle the case, and why. In particular, address whether an arrangement whose results are good but whose workings no one understands can count as warranted by experience in Dewey's sense.
