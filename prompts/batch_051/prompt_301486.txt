I am a professor of philosophy, and I would like an assessment of a policy I have come to enforce in my own writing and in seminars and faculty discussion. I have found that the sentence "X is socially constructed" — where X is race, gender, money, or some other socially significant kind — is made to do very different kinds of work: sometimes it means that X would not exist without certain social practices, sometimes that X is not a natural kind, sometimes merely that X could have been otherwise, sometimes that X ought to be revised or abandoned. My policy is to discount any further conclusion drawn from such a sentence unless the speaker specifies, in the same breath, the sense of construction, the contrast being drawn (constructed as opposed to what), and the inference the construction claim is supposed to license. When these are absent, I treat the claim as doing no argumentative work and say so explicitly.

Last month I applied this policy to a colleague who argued that, because money is socially constructed, the university's apparent budget constraint is really a choice, and the austerity plan is therefore a political decision rather than a necessity. I asked in what sense money is constructed and how that licenses the move from constructed to chosen. My colleague answered that the demand was philosophical pedantry used to stall a true and urgent point.

I would like to know whether the policy is defensible. Is the demand for specification a sound guard against equivocation, or does it stack the deck against a legitimate kind of critique, one whose point is to unsettle a distinction rather than to operate within it? Under what conditions does my rule function as motivated skepticism rather than clarity, and is there a version of the rule that keeps its benefits without its costs?

In your answer, please say explicitly what would change your verdict: which argument or consideration would lead you to answer differently about the policy, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
