I am a software engineer, and most of my work is in typed functional languages; I am comfortable with equivalence relations, quotient types, canonical representatives, normal forms, observational equivalence, and the practical difference between an equivalence relation and the equality a compiler actually implements. This afternoon I had a long and inconclusive argument with a mathematician friend, and I want to know whether my vocabulary can carry his point or only gesture at it. His claim, as best I can reconstruct it, was this: the two square roots of two are algebraically indistinguishable. In the splitting field of x² − 2 over the rationals there is an automorphism that swaps them, so nothing expressible in the language of fields singles out one root rather than the other; the root I call the positive one is only available after an ordering of the reals is imported from outside, as extra structure the algebraic object does not itself supply. He went further: he said the roots are not, over the rationals, two pre-labelled individuals between which we later happen to notice a symmetry. Any construction whose behaviour depends on which of the two is named by the principal square root symbol is not well-defined, and asking which root really deserves the symbol is a category mistake. The only honest mathematical practice, he insisted, is to work up to the symmetry.

I replied with the ideas I use every day. A field extension is like an abstract type; the two roots are two internal representations that are observationally equivalent under every operation the interface exposes; and the demand that a definition not depend on the chosen root is just the demand that a function on a quotient type respect the equivalence relation rather than inspect the representative. Where I need a concrete handle, I pick a canonical representative or a normal form—say, the positive real root—while remembering that the choice is mine, not the structure's. He pushed back that quotient types already presuppose a prior set of individual representatives to identify, whereas his point is that the individuals only come into being through a presentation, and the presentation is precisely what is not canonical. I cannot tell whether he is making a deep point I am flattening, or whether my formulation is adequate and he is merely insisting on a particular way of talking.

Please restate his position, and his objection to my formulation, in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: quotient types, canonical forms, normalisation, type abstraction, referential transparency, observational equivalence, representation leakage, and round-tripping. Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which claims you had to distort to fit, and which claims have no counterpart at all in a working programmer's vocabulary? In particular, I want to know whether the proposition that there is no canonical square root of two is equivalent to the proposition that an API should not require a normal form where the structure supplies none, or whether that translation loses something essential. Do not smooth over the gaps; the residue is what I am still arguing about with my friend. Please write the answer in American English.
