Take the standard St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads, and if the first head occurs on toss n you receive 2^n dollars. Now change the payout rule. Fix an infinite set S of positive integers. The house pays 2^n dollars if the first head occurs on toss n and n is in S; otherwise it pays nothing. Everything else is unchanged: the coin is fair, the house has unlimited funds, and you play exactly once. Consider two concrete choices of S: (a) S is the primes, so you collect 2^p only if the first head occurs on a prime-numbered toss; (b) S is the powers of two, {2, 4, 8, ...}.

Work through how the standard analyses of the paradox change, if at all, under this modification.

1. Expected value. Compute the expected value for S = the primes and for S = the powers of two. Then characterize, in general, which choices of S leave the expectation infinite. Compute the probability of receiving any nonzero payout in each of the two concrete cases, and state the exact condition on S under which the median payout is 0.

2. Log-utility resolution. With initial wealth w > 0 and utility over final wealth u(x) = log(w + x), show that both concrete versions of the game have a finite certainty equivalent. Does this resolution do the same work here as in the original game, or does the probability mass on the zero payout become the dominant consideration as S gets sparse?

3. Finite-bankroll resolution. In the original game, limiting play to N tosses — first head on toss n ≤ N pays 2^n, and no head in N tosses pays 0 — gives expected value N. Give the analogous formula for the modified game with general S, and compare S = primes, S = evens, and S = powers of two for a fixed N.

4. Verdict. Does this modification strengthen one side of the original paradox? One could read it as making the infinite expectation more embarrassing, since with S = primes or powers of two you probably receive nothing, yet the expected value is still infinite. Or one could read it as exposing the infinity as an artifact of allowing infinitely many payoff states. Say which reading you endorse, if either, and state which standard resolutions survive the modification in spirit, which merely change their numbers, and which stop applying.

Lead with the bottom line: in one or two sentences, say which resolutions survive and which fail or change qualitatively. Then give the reasoning. If the variant is mathematically trivial, collapses into a standard case, or rests on a misstatement of one of the standard resolutions, say so plainly instead of completing the analysis as if nothing were wrong.
