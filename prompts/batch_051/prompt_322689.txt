I'm trying to figure out what principle my cousin Daniel actually uses when he makes ethical judgments about individual contributions to big collective problems, because he is impressively consistent but refuses to state the rule behind his verdicts. Please act as a professional moral philosopher who is especially skilled at reverse-engineering implicit principles from a person's judgments about particular cases. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming candidate rules before you commit; then give me your analysis in the format specified below.

Here is his track record, collected from arguments over the past year.

1. When our grandmother serves an ordinary supermarket roasted chicken, Daniel eats only the side dishes and says the chicken is wrong to buy. He holds this even after I pointed out that the bird is already dead and his one portion will not change how many chickens the supermarket orders.
2. He says it is perfectly fine to skip voting in our congressional district, which one party wins by a huge margin every election.
3. He thinks buying a cheap t-shirt from a brand that has been repeatedly caught using child labor is wrong, even if the shirt is on clearance.
4. He thinks our family's one round-trip flight a year to visit our grandparents is not something we owe it to the world to give up, even though he admits flying is very bad for the climate.
5. He says throwing a single plastic bottle in the trash instead of a recycling bin is not worth feeling guilty about.
6. Last spring he insisted I come with him to a climate march, even while agreeing that one extra marcher would not change any policy.

Next weekend our uncle is roasting a whole pig for a family reunion. The pig has already been bought from a local farm and will be slaughtered and cooked no matter what Daniel does; everyone else at the reunion will eat the pork, and any leftovers will be frozen. I want to know what Daniel would say about eating the pork, if he judges this case in keeping with his six verdicts above.

Your job: (a) give your bottom-line prediction about whether Daniel would call it wrong to eat the pork; (b) identify the principle he seems to be running; (c) name at least two other principles that fit all six verdicts just as well, and say whether each predicts differently on the pig roast; and (d) propose the single follow-up case that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles, and explain why it would.

Format: Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
