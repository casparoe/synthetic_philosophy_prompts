I've been trying to pin down how one of my colleagues, Naomi, who works on bounded rationality and satisficing, actually draws the line between rational and irrational choice. I've collected a bunch of her verdicts over the past year, partly from seminars and partly from arguements at lunch, and I'd like you to predict what she'd say about a new case and, more importantly, to tell me what rule she's implicitly running.

Here are the verdicts. She said a graduate student hunting for an apartment in a tight market was clearly rational to take the first place that met her pre-set criteria—budget, commute, light—even though she'd only seen four places and a better one might well have shown up if she'd kept looking. She defended a diner who orders the first few dishes that sound good from a huge menu without scanning the rest, on the grounds that deliberating further isn't worth it. She called it rational for an overworked ER nurse to follow a quick screening checklist rather than compute posterior probabilities, because speed matters more than marginal accuracy. She said a job candidate was rational to accept the first offer that cleared her stated threshold instead of waiting on other flyouts, since there is always a possible better job around the corner. She thought a friend who spent two years waiting for the perfect laptop was being irrational, because at some point the expected gain from more waiting had fallen below the costs of living with a dying computer. And when I gave her a truly simple choice—two toasters, same price, one strictly better on every feature—she said anyone rational takes the better one; choosing the dominated one is just a mistake.

Now the new case. A friend is choosing among five health insurance plans. The plans are all printed on one page, with premiums, deductibles, and networks equally easy to compare, and she has a whole evening and no pressure. Her only firm requirements are a premium under $400 and a deductible under $2,000. The first plan she reads meets those requirements, and she stops and enrolls, even though a quick look further down the same page would reveal a plan with a $320 premium and a $1,400 deductible that she would have picked if she had compared them. Does Naomi call her rational or irrational?

I can see at least two rules that fit the earlier verdicts. One is that satisficing is just optimizing under search-and-deliberation costs, so when the costs are effectively zero you should maximize. The other is that meeting a reasonable aspiration is itself a standard of rationality, and looking further is optional, not required. Are there other rules that fit her verdicts just as well? Give me your best prediction about the new case, the rule you think she is running, and the further case you would use to settle it if the earlier ones don't.

I don't need references or citations, just careful reasoning. And please write it in continuous prose, like an email or a letter—no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
