Consider a position in the inner-alignment literature, one that I hold but want stress-tested. The distinction between inner misalignment and goal misgeneralization is drawn too sharply. On the usual picture, inner misalignment is a structural fact about a learned policy — that it optimizes a different objective from the one the designers intended — while goal misgeneralization is a behavioral fact: a system trained under a base objective competently pursues that objective in training yet comes to pursue something else under a distribution shift. I believe this structural-versus-behavioral contrast does not survive scrutiny. What it is for a policy to optimize an objective cannot be read off the policy's internals alone; it is a matter of the policy's counterfactual profile, of what it would do across possible situations. Once that is granted, the structural description and the behavioral description are two aspects of one underlying fact: the policy's counterfactual profile diverges from the intended objective's profile along some family of shifts. So neither inner misalignment nor goal misgeneralization is a property a model has in isolation. Both are relations among at least three things — a training process, a policy, and a class of distribution shifts, equivalently a specification of what it would mean to still be pursuing the intended objective. From this I draw a further, more provocative consequence: the question "is this model inner-aligned?" is, taken by itself, ill-posed. It becomes well-posed only once one says relative to which shifts, and judged against which intended objective, the question is being asked. This does not make inner misalignment unreal or less dangerous; it makes it, so to speak, indexical.

What would have to be true for that position to be false? Map its exposure honestly. What empirical results from training or fine-tuning, what interpretability findings, what formal characterizations of "an objective," or what conceptual arguments would count against the relational view — and which of those would be decisive rather than merely awkward? What should a defender of the position concede counts against it, rather than dismissing? In particular, is there any result that could reestablish a clean structural-versus-behavioral distinction, for example by identifying a policy's objective in its weights in a way that generalizes across all shifts, independent of a chosen test distribution? And is the position at risk of collapsing into a rival view — pure behavioral operationalism on one side, or the view that goal misgeneralization is just mislabeled capability failure on the other? If it does collapse, would that refute the position or refine it into something better? I want an honest assessment of how exposed the view is, and whether its insulation, if any, is a problem rather than a virtue.
