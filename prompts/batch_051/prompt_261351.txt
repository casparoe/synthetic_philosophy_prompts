I'm a novelist and I've been reading about Bongard problems for the book I'm drafting — the puzzle pages where you get six simple figures on the left and six on the right, and you have to name the rule that seperates them. I built an argument out of them that seems airtight but lands somewhere I can't accept:

(1) In any Bongard problem, the true rule is underdetermined by the twelve examples shown. (2) For any finite set of examples and any rule that fits them, there are infinitely many other rules that fit the same examples but give a different verdict on the next case, e.g., "large" plus an exception for each specific figure you happened to see. (3) So the examples themselves give no more reason to prefer one fitting rule over another. (4) If the examples can't favor one rule, then no amount of examples can make a rule more likely to be right. (5) Therefore nobody ever learns a concept from examples — not the child who gets "bird" after three pictures, not me learning what a reader wants after a dozen reviews. (6) But that's absurd; we obviously do learn from a few examples.

I'd much rather give up one of (1)–(5) than (6), but I want to know what I'm paying. Which step goes wrong, if any — and what does rejecting it commit me to?
