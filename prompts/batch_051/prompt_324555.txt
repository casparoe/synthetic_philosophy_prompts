The move, in my own words. Someone wants to infer causal structure from observational data. They first discover that certain variables become independent when conditioned on certain other variables. They notice that many different directed acyclic graphs could have produced exactly those independence facts. They then choose the graph with the fewest arrows that fits all the facts, and they go on to read each remaining arrow as a direct causal connection, and each absent arrow as evidence that no direct causal connection exists.

What licenses that second step? The independence facts alone only tell you which graphs are compatible with the data, not which graph generated them. There could be a true graph with more arrows in which the extra arrows cancel. For example, suppose A directly causes C and also causes C through B, but the direct effect and the indirect effect are in perfect balance, so A and C end up independent conditional on B. If the world were full of such cancellations, the sparsest graph would be not just a simplification but causally false.

So the step from "sparsest compatible graph" to "this graph's arrows are the real causes" seems to depend on a substantive assumption, something like: the population's independence facts are only those the causal graph forces; there are no accidental cancellations or perfectly compensating pathways. I have two questions. First, does that assumption have a name — and is it one assumption or two that are often run together? Second, how exactly does it relate to the weaker rule that one should simply prefer the graph with the fewest arrows: is the stronger assumption just a sharpened version of that rule, or is it a different kind of claim — a metaphysical claim about how causes are arranged, or a fallible default adopted for practical reasons? And what would a world or dataset have to be like for the assumption to fail, and would the failure be detectable from the data alone?

I'm not asking for papers or citations; I just want the distinctions and the labels that attach to them.
