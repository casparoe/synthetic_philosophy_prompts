I am a high school student reading Wittgenstein's private language argument for the first time, and I keep finding that the phrase "private language" is used in several different senses. I want a working definition of a private language in the sense the argument is actually about, the sense in which such a language is supposed to be impossible, rather than a definition of the loose everyday idea of a language that only one person happens to understand.

Here are the cases I would like the definition to classify:

1. A diary written in a substitution cipher of English, where each letter is replaced by another according to a key that only the author knows.
2. The last surviving speaker of a natural language, who can no longer converse with anyone in it.
3. A language invented from scratch by one person, with its own grammar and vocabulary, spoken only by that person, but designed so that it could in principle be taught to anyone willing to learn it.
4. A person who writes down a word such as "glimmer" each time a certain recurring inner sensation appears, where the only standard for using the word correctly is the person's own memory that this is the same sensation as before.
5. A code in which words are introduced by pointing to objects that only the speaker has actually seen, for example names for rooms in a hidden building that only the speaker has entered, even though the objects themselves are public in the sense that anyone could in principle enter and look.
6. Idiosyncratic private meanings attached to ordinary English words during silent self-talk, so that the speaker uses the word "done" to refer to a particular feeling of relief, though no one else knows this.
7. A speech system developed by two young siblings that no one outside the pair can understand.

I think cases 1 and 2 are clearly not private languages in the philosophically interesting sense, and case 4 clearly is. I have no confident view about cases 3, 5, 6, and 7. Propose a definition, run it through all seven cases, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. In particular, I would like to know whether the right definition turns on "only one person actually understands it," "could not in principle be understood by anyone else," or something else entirely. I am not looking for exegesis of particular passages or citations, just a definition that does the classificatory work.
