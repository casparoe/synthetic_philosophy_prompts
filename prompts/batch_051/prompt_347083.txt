I'm on the "student success" committee at my university, and we're allocating a new recurring fund (about $900k/year) earmarked for supporting students from low-income backgrounds. We've split into three camps and basicaly can't move.

One camp wants to put everything into direct cash grants to students below an adjusted-gross-income threshold, on the theory that what students need is more resources and they know best how to spend them; they keep saying services are patronizing and half the money will disappear into administration. A second camp wants to fund a well-being package—expanded counseling, emergency food support, a subsidized health plan—targeted at students with the lowest self-reported well-being scores; their line is that the point isn't the money, it's how students are actually doing, and that's what we should target. A third camp wants a "capability menu": laptop loans, childcare, metro passes, paid summer internships—things that expand what students can actually do and be. Their argument is that two students can have the same cash and still not be able to convert it into the same opportunities (disability, a kid, a long commute), and that neither income nor reported happiness captures that.

The chair asked me to draft a short decision memo for the committee recommending one of the three, or a specific mix—but it has to be a real, defensible policy rather than "do a little of everything." It should say which considerations to weigh most when we review proposals, engage the strongest objection from the camps we don't pick rather than strawmanning them, and end with a concrete recommendation the committee could vote on.

There's also a deeper thing the committee keeps tripping over. Everyone says the money should go to whoever is "worst off" or "most in need," but the three camps mean three different things by that, and the definitions point at different students. If you could make the memo partly about that—which idea of "need" or "disadvantage" we're implicitly signing up for—that would help. My co-chair thinks this all resolves to an empirical question about which program has the best outcomes, but I suspect that's wishful thinking, since we cant even agree on which outcome is the right one to measure. Say a little about whether he's right or whether we're stuck in a disagreement about value, not facts, and how to proceed given that.
