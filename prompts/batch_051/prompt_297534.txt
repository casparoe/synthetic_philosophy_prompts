It's a version of Quinn's self-torturer with the numbers changed, and it seems to show rational preferences can be intransitive, which has to be wrong—or at least it cant be that cheap to show. Setup: a medically safe device has a dial running from 0 to 1,000. Each time you turn it up one notch you are immediately paid $100, and the current goes up by an amount too small to feel on its own; you cannot tell setting n from setting n+1, though you can certainly tell 0 from 1,000. Now the argument:

(1) For any n between 0 and 999, setting n+1 feels the same as setting n, and turning it up one notch leaves you $100 richer than not turning it up. So turning it up one notch is rational.

(2) If each step of a sequence is rational when you take it, then taking the whole sequence is rational: a chain of rational choices cant sum to an irrational one.

(3) If taking the whole sequence is rational, then given the choice between taking the path (ending at 1,000, with $100,000) and not taking it (staying at 0), you rationally prefer taking the path. So you rationally prefer ending up at 1,000 with the money to staying at 0.

(4) But offered a one-off choice right now between staying at 0 and moving straight to 1,000 in exchange for the same $100,000, you'd turn it down; the pain isnt worth the money. So you rationally prefer staying at 0 to ending up at 1,000 with the money.

(5) From (3) and (4), you rationally prefer 1,000 to 0 and also 0 to 1,000. Rational preference is intransitive.

(6) But intransitive preferences can be money-pumped: someone can offer you swaps around the cycle and take your money, and it cant be rational to be a sitting duck like that.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
