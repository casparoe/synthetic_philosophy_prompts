Over the past few months I have been trying, with mixed success, to form a costly but rationally attractive intention — to leave a stable job and start an uncertain venture — and the struggle has made me think hard about what an intention is. The account that keeps attracting me treats intentions as a species of plan: forming an intention is not a shadowy inner event but the adoption of a settled plan that guides later deliberation and action, and the rational question is simply which intention would organize my future behavior to best effect. That account is elegant, it makes intention formation continuous with ordinary decision-making, and it explains why I use commitment devices. I make promises and sign contracts because those acts change what it costs to hold or abandon an intention, and therefore make it rational for me to hold one.

Then someone showed me the following puzzle, and it has left me in a genuine standoff. A benefactor offers to make me a large payment tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, I form the sincere intention to drink a vial of something foul but harmless tomorrow afternoon; the money is owed as soon as I have the intention tonight, and whether I later drink the vial makes no difference. I am certain now that tomorrow afternoon I will not drink it, because by then I will have no reason to. And precisely because I am certain of that, it seems to me that I cannot now intend, in any honest sense, to drink it. The instrumental account replies that this is simply an error: the intention is the high-expected-value option, so I should form it. But my firm intuition is that intention is not the sort of state one can produce at will merely because producing it would pay, and that an intention to do what one already knows one will not do is not an intention at all — at best it is a wish, a pretense, or self-deception about one's future actions.

I am less interested in being told which side wins than in understanding what a concession on either side would require. Specifically, I would like you to state the strongest version of the instrumental account and the strongest version of the intuition, then say what it would take to give up each, what it would take to amend each, and whether there is a reading on which both are preserved — for example, by distinguishing intending from trying to intend, or by distinguishing the rational grounds for an intention from its sincerity conditions. I would still like your considered view, at the end, about which should yield and why.
