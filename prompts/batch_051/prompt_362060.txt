Hey, I'm putting together a handout for an intro political philosophy class, and I want a "which theory of rights do you actually hold?" quiz for it. The reading is a chunk of Bentham's Anarchical Fallacies — you know, the bit where he's attacking the French Declaration of the Rights of Man. Just to jog memory, he says something like this: real rights are the children of real laws, and the only laws that exist are the ones made by actual legislators. When people talk about natural rights they talk as if there could be a son without a father, or a law before any lawmaker. He says natural rights are simple nonsense, and natural and imprescriptible rights are rhetorical nonsense — nonsense upon stilts, as he puts it.

So I want the quiz built around that passage, but I don't want it to be a reading comprehension check. I want a decision tree that sorts a student by how they respond to concrete cases, not by which label they like. Each branch should be a question they can answer without knowing they're being sorted into Benthamite legal positivism, Lockean natural rights, whatever.

Some cases I'd like you to work in or near: a slave-holding legal system where the law says the enslaved person has no right to leave their owner; a claim that everyone has a right to adequate food even in a country with no such statute and no court that would enforce it; a custom among a group of people with no formal legal system where everyone treats someone as owning a particular plot of land; a newly passed statute that grants a right that nobody thought existed before and that most people think is unjust. And maybe a case where two people disagree about whether a right exists and there's no court to appeal to.

The leaves I'd like to see at minimum are: Benthamite legal positivism (rights are creatures of law, full stop), Lockean natural rights (there are pre-legal rights that law ought to recognize), a sort of two-tier view where moral rights exist but legal rights are the only ones that carry institutional force, and a deflationary view where rights talk is just a useful way of wrapping up interests or preferences. If there's an unoccupied combination of answers, don't just leave it empty — say what position would occupy it and why nobody plausibly does (either it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Also flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate, like if the questions can't distinguish someone who thinks rights are real but unenforced from someone who thinks they're just useful fictions.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, and I'd like the scoring key to map answer patterns onto the leaves, not just one question per leaf. It's for students who've done the reading but aren't into jurisprudence, so keep the language plain.
