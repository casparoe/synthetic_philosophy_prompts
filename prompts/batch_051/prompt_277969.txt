Since a graduate seminar last autumn, I have been unable to put down the problem of old evidence, and the more I read the more the debate seems deadlocked. The standard Bayesian account says evidence confirms a theory only if it raises the theory's probability, which requires the evidence's own probability to be below one; but old evidence — Mercury's anomalous perihelion, known for decades before general relativity — already has probability one for anyone who knows it, so it cannot raise anything. The responses have hardened into camps: some say we should evaluate the theory against a counterfactual prior from which the old evidence is subtracted, though that subtraction is never unique; others say the genuinely new evidence is the logical discovery that the theory entails the observation, not the observation itself; still others say the difficulty is an artifact of mixing two questions, namely what it is to learn from new information and what it is for a theory to be supported by a fact already known. The camps trade the same objections year after year, and I have begun to suspect they are not really disagreeing about a technical flaw in the formalism but about what 'confirmation' means and what the Bayesian apparatus is for — a model of learning from new evidence over time, or an atemporal relation of support between propositions. Diagnose this impasse: is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or several groups answering different questions under one name? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a technical result, a sharper distinction, a reformulated question — or should I expect none?
