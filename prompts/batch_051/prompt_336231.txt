I'm a philosophy professor by day and the faculty rep on our campus Recreational Sports Council, and we vote Friday on whether to grant club-sport status to a new student group calling itself the Competitive Reasoning League. Their format is this: two-person teams draw a proposition from a hat and get a one-page shared fact sheet, then have an hour to prepare. Judges score on a forty-point rubric. Ten points for restating the opponent's argument before attacking it, and the restatement has to pass a steelman check or the category zeroes out. Ten points for concession discipline: conceding a subpoint when the evidence in the fact sheet really does go against you, without conceding the main thesis. Ten points for responsiveness to the strongest objection the other side actually raised. And ten points for audience uptake: after closings, judges poll the audience to see who can explain each side's argument, and the side better understood gets the points. The group has a faculty sponsor, a draft rulebook, even a playoff bracket. Their case is that this is a sport in every way that matters—coached, scored, officiated, with a clear winner—and that the physical skill requirement is a historical accident; we already fund esports and drone racing, wich makes that line wobbly.

The council is split. Half says the esports and drone-racing precedents settle the is-this-a-sport question, so the real issue is whether competitive scoring would improve argument or corrupt it. Their worry is that the rubric is gameable: a team could farm concession points by conceding trivial subpoints while never moving on the thesis, and the audience-uptake metric rewards making a bad argument crisp rather than making a good argument well. The other half says that's exactly what judges are for, and that a scored league with a public record of winners gives students practice being wrong, conceding in public, and changing their mind. If we deny club-sport status they can still register as an ordinary student organization, but they'd lose the practice-room priority and the travel stipend. I keep going back and forth, and I'm the one who has to write the memo. Draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. Give us a concrete recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions (specify them)—with the reasons, and treat the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than just leaning on the sport definition.
