I've been a software engineer for about a decade, no formal philosophy background. Recently I read Eugene Wigner's essay "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences" (the 1960 one). The bit that stuck with me wasn't the usual slogan people quote, but the part early on where he talks about two miracles: one, that the physical world is law-like and predictable at all, and two, that the human mind has the capacity to come up with mathematical structures that anticipate those laws, even when the maths was developed for its own sake without any obvious physical application in mind. He seems to build the essay around the idea that this is something genuinely strange, not just a nice coincidence.

Here's my issue. Day to day, I use linear algebra, probability, graph theory, whatever, as tools. My working intuition as an engineer is that maths is something we invented. We define axioms and rules to solve local problems, and when a piece of abstract maths lines up with physics, it's because we selected the cases that line up, or because humans only publish and celebrate the successes. If you generate enough formal structures and physics has enough constraints, some matches are bound to appear. That feels obviously right to me. So my intuition treats Wigner's "miracle" as mostly survivorship bias.

But I also can't fully get rid of the feeling Wigner is onto something. The standard examples — complex numbers showing up all over quantum mechanics, or non-Euclidean geometry becoming the language of general relativity — don't feel like random hits. The maths wasn't cooked up to describe those phenomena, and yet it fits astonishingly well. So the theory I'm drawn to is something like: the applicability of mathematics reveals a structural match between our abstract symbol systems and the world, and that match calls for more explanation than "we made it up." That directly clashes with my tool-user intuition.

What should yield here? Walk me through the strongest version of each side — including the best attempts to debunk the intuition as a cognitive bias or selection effect, and the best arguments that Wigner's sense of mystery survives those debunking moves. Is there a reading on which my engineer's tool intuition and something like Wigner's point are compatible, or is one of them just wrong? And what specifically would justify dropping the intuition, versus amending or abandoning the theory? Please write in British English, and please separate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material — I don't want to mistake a minority view for the consensus.
