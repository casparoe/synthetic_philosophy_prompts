I write for a general-audience magazine covering science and ideas, and this spring I have been asked to serve as a preliminary grader for its annual essay prize for early-career researchers; this year's theme is "arguments from nature in social and political thought." The editors set the question below, and below it is one anonymized submission. I would like you to act as the expert grader: a historian of nineteenth-century political thought with a specialty in John Stuart Mill and the utilitarian tradition. Please think carefully and work through the material before grading. Begin by briefly stating the grading criteria you consider appropriate and what a fully correct answer would need to establish, then assess the submission point by point, assign it a grade of fail, pass, merit, or distinction, and explain where the answer is right, where it falls short or goes subtly wrong, and what a stronger answer would have said. At the end, tell me how confident you are in this assessment and single out the point you are least sure about.

Question: "Reconstruct John Stuart Mill's argument in The Subjection of Women against appeals to the supposed nature of women as a justification for their legal subordination. In particular, distinguish the epistemic claim that women's nature cannot be reliably known under conditions of inequality from the normative conclusion that the legal subordination of women ought to be abolished. Does Mill's argument require the premise that whatever is artificial is bad?"

Submission: "Mill observes that the traits defenders of subordination cite as nature are themselves cultivated by the very institutions they are used to justify, so the appeal is effectively circular: women are observed under an artificial regime of restricted education, legal incapacity, and expected submission, and the resulting behavior is then read back as their innate nature. He also claims, more cautiously, that nobody is in a position to know what women would be like under free and equal conditions, because the experiment of allowing that development has never been run. This blocks the appeal to nature as a justification: a law cannot rest on a factual premise about natural difference that the defender cannot establish. Mill then concludes that the legal subordination of women is unjustified and should be replaced by perfect equality in the family as well as in public life. He is not relying on the premise that whatever is artificial is bad, since his normative commitment is to individual liberty and to the principle that restrictions of liberty require a positive justification. Once the natural-difference premise collapses, such a positive justification is absent, and therefore the existing order ought to be abolished."
