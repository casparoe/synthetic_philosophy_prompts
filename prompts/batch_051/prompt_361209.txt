I'm hoping you can act as a philosopher who actually works on just war theory and the moral equality of combatants, because I need a slogan taken apart carefully. My uncle keeps ending arguments with "soldiers aren't murderers." Last week I said that a soldier who voluntarily enlists in a war he has every reason to believe is an unjust war of aggression is still morally on the hook, in some real way, for the enemy soldiers he kills. My uncle shut me down with: no, soldiers aren't murderers — once the shooting starts, both sides are morally equal, end of story.

I can tell there are several distinct claims welded together in that move. Lay out, as a menu I can take back to him, the different precise theses "soldiers aren't murderers" could be expressing: the boring definitional one (murder is a legal category, and lawful combatants killing lawful combatants isn't murder), the institutional-role one, the coercion one, the jus ad bellum / jus in bello one, and whatever more radical readings you can find. For each reading say whether it's trivial, plausible, or just false, and — crucially — whether it actually gets my uncle to his conclusion that I had nothing to complain about. Start by brainstorming every possible reading, including the weird ones, and then sort them. Dont just pick the most defensible reading and argue for it.
