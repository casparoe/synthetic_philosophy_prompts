You are the staff philosopher for the public-interest governance board of a large AI lab. The lab will soon deploy a general-purpose assistant that acts for many users at once in shared settings: group scheduling, jointly edited documents, allocation of pooled compute credits, and moderation of a shared discussion space. In these settings most candidate actions distribute benefits unevenly across users. The engineering team proposes a single cross-user objective: elicit each user's preferences over relevant outcomes, then have the assistant maximize aggregate preference satisfaction subject to a leximin floor that gives strict priority to improving the worst-off. A dissenting group argues for a user-sovereignty policy: the assistant should be liberal-neutral across users' conceptions of the good, never weighing one user's values against another's, and where no Pareto improvement is available it should decline to choose and require the affected users to negotiate. A third faction favors a hybrid that aggregates only over generic resources (time, attention, money, access) while remaining neutral about the ends those resources serve.

I sit on this board, and we need a memo we can vote on. Before drafting, work through the strongest version of the case for each of the three policies. Then draft the decision memo: recommend one policy, with an operationally clear implementation rule the engineering team could put into production, reasons a non-philosopher on the board could act on, an honest treatment of the strongest consideration against your recommendation and why it does not prevail, and a statement of which evidence from pilot deployments would lead you to reconsider.
