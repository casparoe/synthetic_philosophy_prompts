I'm working through Goodman's grue problem for the first time and I've realized I already live by a rule, I just never noticed it. When a student in my intro class proposes a sweeping generalization like "all emeralds are grue" (green before 2030, blue after), I dismiss it before looking at the evidence. My actual policy is: I only project predicates that don't single out a particular time, place, or person, and that my community has already used successfully in predictions. The embarassing part is that I apply this to grue even though, prior to 2030, the evidence for "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" is exactly the same. So I can't tell whether I'm tracking something about the world or just protecting the vocabulary I grew up with.

Is this habit defensible? What would have to be true about the world—or about how languages get built—for my policy to be reliable rather than parochial? And is there a version of the rule I could keep without the circular-sounding appeal to "those are the predicates we happen to use"? I'd also genuinly like to know how I could catch myself out if my language were full of grue-like predicates that had simply become entrenched.
