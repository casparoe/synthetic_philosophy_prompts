In my feminist philosophy course we spent last week on Beauvoir, and the line everyone already knew, "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman," came up in discussion. His move was basically to quote the line and say Beauvoir already settled that question, which felt too quick to me. I dont doubt she was saying something important, but "becomes" is doing an awful lot of work there, and i'm not sure which of the several possible claims the line could be making he actually needs for his shutdown to work. Some readings of it seem almost trivially true, others seem like a much stronger anti-essentialist or existentialist thesis, and I suspect the line only supports his conclusion on some of them, not all.

Could you walk me, in plain continuous prose (no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just the way you'd write an email), through the different precise claims that slogan might be expressing, from the weakest to the strongest? For each reading, say whether it's plausible, whether it's really a claim about becoming versus being born versus the social category of "woman," and whether it would actually license his reply. I'd especially like to know which readings are compatible with there being a bodily or biological basis to womanhood and which aren't, since that was the actual dispute. American English, please.
