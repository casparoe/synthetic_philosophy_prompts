ok so im taking a semantics/philosophy of language class and generics are kind of destroying me. We're doing bare plural generics — sentences like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" — and I can't find a truth condition that gets all the obvious cases right without becoming circular or ad hoc. Like:

- "Ducks lay eggs" is true even though most actual ducks (males, ducklings, non-laying females) don't lay eggs.
- "Lions have manes" is true even though only male lions have manes.
- "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is true even though only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes carry it.
- "Dogs have four legs" is true but not universal; "ravens are black" is true but albino ravens exist; "birds fly" is true but penguins, ostriches, and some other birds don't.
- "Ravens are white" is false even though white ravens exist, and "dogs have three legs" is false even though some dogs do. And "tigers are male" and "ducks are female" are just false even though roughly half of each are.
- "Sharks attack bathers" feels true to me even though attacks are statistically super rare, but I honestly don't know how to defend that.

So a pure majority rule can't handle ducks/mosquitoes/sharks; an "all normal members" rule feels circular; and "the sex distribution doesn't matter for the rule" is definately not right either, because laying eggs and having manes are somehow different from being female or male. My current hunch is that the rule needs something like a property being characteristic of the kind rather than just statistically common, but when I try to spell out "characteristic" I end up back at "normal" and I'm stuck.
