I've been going around in circles on public reason and religious arguments in politics, and I think alot of the fights come from people never saying what they're actually demanding of a theory before they attack it. One person says a religious reason can only count if it can be restated in terms anyone could accept; another says it just has to be sincere and the rest is handled by a prior commitment to fair cooperation; a third says any rule that burdens religious citizens more than secular ones is dead on arrival. Before I read one more defense or critique, I want the spec first.

So: write the desiderata for an adequate theory of public reason — a theory good enough to tell citizens what sorts of reasons (especially religious ones) they may legitimately appeal to when justifying coercive laws to each other. For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration, and say whether it's non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. I want you to cover at least: the difference between a reason being shareable and its merely being intelligible or accessible to others; whether sincerity can genuinely be a requirement given how hard it is to tell apart from strategic restraint; the worry that any restraint test treats religious citizens asymmetrically; and whether mutual assurance/stability between citizens is part of the theory's job or just a nice side effect. Most of all I want to know which pairs of these demands are likely jointly unsatisfiable, and which single demand does the most to rule out the standard views — please flag those. Keep it to exactly seven items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
