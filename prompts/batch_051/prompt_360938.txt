Two colleagues of mine found themselves in a running argument about the convention that 1 BCE is followed directly by 1 CE. (1) The first, who maintains date-handling software, insisted this is a defect: the sequence of years skips the integer zero, so as soon as BCE years are encoded as negative coordinates, 1 BCE must be −1 while 1 CE is +1, making two adjacent years look two years apart and forcing a special correction on every interval that crosses the boundary. He said astronomers solved this long ago by numbering 1 BCE as year 0, 2 BCE as −1, and so on, and the rest of us should adopt the same convention.

(2) The second, an archivist, replied that year numbers are ordinal labels, not coordinates on a number line. There is no zeroth year, just as there is no zeroth monarch among those who bear the same name: the first year of an era is 1 by definition. The interval from January 1 of 1 BCE to January 1 of 1 CE is one year, visible on any timeline; subtracting the labels is simply the wrong operation.

(3) The first answered that this is not how the labels are actually used. Dates are constantly subtracted to calculate ages, terms of office, and accrued interest, and the rule "subtract one more when the interval crosses the epoch" is an admission that the official scheme is a number line with a gap. Astronomical numbering does nothing but close the gap.

(4) The second responded that the fix only raises the deeper question: which year should be year zero? Keeping the zero-point anchored to a disputed birth date shows that an arithmetic tidy-up is not the same as a principled choice of epoch, and any epoch is ultimately a value-laden convention. The public calendar, she said, is an inherited institution whose continuity with documents and ordinary expectations matters more than avoiding a one-line correction.

Adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say what, if anything, the two parties are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
