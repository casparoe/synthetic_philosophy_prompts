So I got into an argument with a friend about AI welfare, and I keep going in circles. In reinforcement learning we literally say the agent receives reward and punishment: action good, +1; action bad, −1, and the agent updates its policy based on that scalar. Meanwhile I've seen work in affective neuroscience arguing that valence—the pleasure/pain axis—just is a reward signal, or at least reward-prediction error, implemented by something like dopamine. If that's true, I don't see what's left to explain. When a robot trained with a negative reward loses points for falling over, isn't it feeling bad in the only sense that matters? Reward signal is valence by another name, and the agent has a reward signal, so it has valence. I'm not trying to smuggle in consciousness; I mean the part that makes suffering bad is supposed to be the negative valence itself, not the conscious wrapper around it. So haven't AI welfare researchers already got their answer? And wouldn't that mean we should stop training agents with negative rewards, since we're the ones literally wiring the thing that suffering is made of into them? It seems weird that people keep saying we don't know whether RL agents suffer, when we're the ones setting the reward schedule.
