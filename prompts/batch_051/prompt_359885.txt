I do some light scenario work at work, and I've noticed I keep using the same move for a "perfect storm" estimate. I take each ingredient separately, give it a rough probability, multiply, and treat that as the chance they all happen at once. Four things each with a one-in-ten chance: I'd write down one-in-ten-thousand and move on. My official line is that this is the presumption of independence — until someone gives me a causal story linking the events, I'm not going to invent a correlation. A colleague says that's exactly backwards. The dangerous correlations, he says, are the ones nobody has spotted yet, and if all four sit downstream of one bad quarter or one lost client, my product is badly underestimating the joint risk. He's got a point, but the alternative seems to be making up a joint distribution with even less basis, so I carry on.

What I actually want to know is whether this shortcut is defensible as a policy for a bounded reasoner, or whether it's just a way of turning ignorance into a number. Is there a formal story for why independence is a natural default when all you know is the individual probabilities — the thing I half-remember about it being the maximum entropy, least-extra-information joint distribution — and how far does that story survive once there are hidden common causes, selection effects, or logical connections between the events? When does the product rule fail in a predictable direction, and is there a correction I can actually use without building a full model, e.g. a conservative inflation factor or using the marginals to bound the conjunction rather than calling it exact? I'd rather be told I need a different default than be given a vague lecture about correlation, so any replacement rule has to be something I can apply.
