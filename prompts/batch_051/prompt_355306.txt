I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, and I've been trying to make sense of a tension I see in my company's people practices.

On one side, our hiring process is deliberately engineered to be impartial. We use structured interviews, anonymized coding tests, and fixed rubrics; interviewers have to recuse themselves when a candidate is a friend or former colleague; and the stated reason is that weighing personal connections or loyalty when evaluating a candidate is exactly the kind of bias the process exists to remove.

On the other side, the same company pays employees bonuses for successful referrals, encourages teams to source candidates from their own networks, and gives referred candidates a faster path through the pipeline. Promotion decisions similarly favor people who have built up long tenure and a reputation as reliable team members, sometimes over external candidates who score higher on the same rubrics we use for hiring. Managers defend this by saying that a known quantity who has earned trust is worth more than a higher-scoring unknown.

I want to know what each of these practices presupposes about loyalty and impartiality. Does one half of the process treat loyalty to one's own group as a bias to be suppressed, while the other half treats it as a value—or even a virtue—to be rewarded? Or does the apparent inconsistency dissolve because loyalty is being valued not as loyalty, but only as a signal of something else, like reliability or lower search cost? Some of the practices might strictly require one view of partiality, while others merely fit well with it; I'd like that distinction drawn carefully. And if the company had to adopt one coherent position—for example, that partiality toward your own people is always a contaminant, or instead that it is a legitimate virtue—what would its hiring and promotion practices have to look like under each?
