I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a long essay about whether consequentialist moral thinking can survive the fact that the long-run consequences of almost any action are, in practice, unknowable. The piece is partly reported and partly a defense of my own position, and before I print that position I want it stress-tested by someone who knows the underlying debate. My commitments, as I would state them in the essay: (1) I'm a maximizing consequentialist about rightness — an act is right just in case no alternative available to the agent would produce better aggregate consequences. (2) I hold that the right way to choose under uncertainty is expected-value reasoning: take the option with the highest expected moral value, using the most defensible credences and value estimates available. (3) I accept the core of the cluelessness objection, understood structurally rather than as a vague worry: the consequences that dominate the moral ledger of an ordinary choice — effects on which people will come to exist, on institutions, on long-run social trajectories — are mostly beyond our ability to estimate, so any calculation that tries to sum them is guesswork dressed up as arithmetic. (4) Nevertheless, I do not think this forces quietism or the abandonment of consequentialism; I remain confident that some moral comparisons are knowable, for instance that donating to a well-evaluated global-health charity does more good than spending the same amount on a personal luxury. (5) Where distant effects are too opaque to quantify, I think the correct response is to prefer robust options — interventions that look good across a wide range of plausible futures — and to treat imprecise or interval-valued estimates as an honest feature of reasoning under deep uncertainty, rather than insisting on precise probabilities or ignoring far-future effects on principle.

I'd like an audit rather than a survey of the literature: do these five commitments actually hang together, and if not, where is the strain? Go through them and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each genuine tension is. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each say what would have to give and what the fix would cost elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would leave me with the most defensible overall position. Say how confident you are in the diagnosis as a whole, and single out the specific element of the audit you are least sure of. End with a machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing the label of that single recommended revision.
