I've somehow argued myself into the position that Scanlon-style contractualism either smuggles aggregation back in or goes silent exactly where it's meant to be useful, and I can't tell wether that's a real result or a mistake in the argument. The culprit is the individualist restriction, roughly as follows: you can't justify a principle to someone by pointing out how much good it does for other people, because each person's objections have to be met one at a time. Then we ran the standard rescue case — you can save one stranger from drowning or five strangers from drowning, equal effort, no special obligations, and everyone knows who's who up front, so no ex ante lottery to hide behind — and the argument came out like this.

(1) An act is permissible only if it is permitted by a principle no one could reasonably reject.
(2) A principle that permits saving the one is one each of the five could reasonably reject: it leaves them to die in order to save another, and it offers no reason why their survival should count for less, which seems to give them no weight as individuals.
(3) So the permissible principle seems to have to be something like save the larger group. But it has to be justifiable to the one who gets left behind, not just to the five.
(4) And the one can reasonably reject save the larger group, because under that principle he is left to die on the strength of a consideration — how many lives sit on each side — that the individualist restriction says needn't settle what he, as an individual, can reasonably be asked to accept.
(5) So the restriction pushes both ways at once: if you save the one, each of the five can reject your principle; if you save the five, the one can. The theory yields no permissible act in a case where it seems obvious what to do.
(6) The exits look no better: let the numbers count in this sort of case and you've quietly become utilitarian; run a weighted lottery and you've given the numbers exactly the role the restriction was meant to deny them; pick nearest or youngest and it's arbitrary; say there's no determinate answer and the theory goes mute in the easiest life-and-death case there is.
(7) So the too-much conclusion: a theory built around not aggregating persons can't even account for the fact that we should save the five, or it stops being non-aggregative the moment it tries.

Where do we put the knife? If its up to me I'd rather give up the determinacy assumption in (6) than give up the restriction, but my mate reckons that's just admitting the view has nothing to say in exactly the case a theory of when one may let people die should handle. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? And could you write your answer in British English, spellings and all?
