Suppose that, ten years from now, the world's economies are replaced by a fully working planned economy of the kind communists traditionally projected: money and wages are abolished, production is coordinated without market prices, and the planning system is genuinely allocatively efficient — it matches output to needs and preferences and uses fewer resources than the market did. Nothing else is assumed to change: people retain their actual orientations toward status, leisure, autonomy, variety, and recognition. Give your best reasoned prediction of how social, political, and economic life would develop over the next two generations. In particular, identify which of the long-standing objections to communism — about incentives, freedom, the concentration of power, innovation, status inequality, and the homogenization of ways of life — survive under these assumptions and which were only ever objections to its feasibility. Make the mechanisms visible: for each surviving objection, say what would have to be true of people or institutions for it to persist, how confident you are in that mechanism, and which change to the scenario would reverse your prediction. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
