I am a retired chemist, and after decades of working with quantitative models I have recently begun reading philosophy of probability and rational choice. A popular book on the mathematics of gambling and investing contains the following summary of the Kelly criterion:

"Suppose you face a long sequence of favorable bets with known probabilities, say an even-money wager on a coin known to land heads with probability p. Kelly proved that the fraction of your bankroll you should stake on each bet is 2p − 1. This policy maximizes the expected value of your wealth in the long run, and because it never stakes the whole bankroll it also eliminates the risk of ruin. Since rational agents maximize expected value, Kelly betting is therefore the uniquely rational strategy for anyone facing repeated favorable bets; the only real caveat is that in practice the probabilities are never known exactly."

I can follow the arithmetic, but the philosophical gloss seems to me to move too quickly, and I have learned to distrust popular writing that runs together a mathematical theorem and a claim about what rationality requires. I would be grateful for an accuracy audit of the passage. In particular:

1. Which claims in the summary are accurate as a statement of Kelly's mathematical result, and which are only simplifications or approximations?
2. Which claims are simply wrong or conceptually confused? I am especially suspicious of the move from "maximizes the expected value of your wealth in the long run" to "maximizes expected value" to "uniquely rational," but I would like to know whether that suspicion is well placed.
3. How would a careful defender of Kelly betting, or of the broader view that repeated multiplicative choices should be evaluated by long-run growth rate rather than arithmetic expectation, state the result, and what would they concede the summary gets right?
4. Does the conclusion — that Kelly betting is the uniquely rational strategy for anyone facing repeated favorable bets — survive once the errors are corrected? If not, what is the strongest defensible claim in its neighborhood?

I am not looking for a bibliography or citations; I want a careful conceptual audit that separates the mathematics from the normative interpretation.
