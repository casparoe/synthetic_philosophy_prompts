So I run a book club and next month we're doing Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." I want to frame it with Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem, not the standard "is Omelas a critique of utilitarianism" discussion. The obvious read is that the city is running a utilitarian trade: one child kept locked and miserable so everyone else gets the festivals and the happiness. But Scanlon's objection is more specific than "you can't sacrifice one for many." It's that you can't sum up all those small enjoyments of the citizens into an amount that outweighs the child's misery, because the principle that permits the arrangement is one the child could reasonably reject, no matter how many people gain. I'd like you to read the story through that lens. Where does it dramatize exactly the aggregative move Scanlon says is illegitimate, and where do the actual scenes complicate a Scanlon reading — for example, the ones who walk away dont seem to be offering an alternative shared principle so much as just refusing, and Scanlon's picture is supposed to be about co-justification among persons, not exit. And is the child even being harmed for the citizens' benefit in the way aggregation cases usually assume, or is the story doing something stranger than a trade-off? I want a reading that's faithful to both the story and the philosophy, and that ends by saying what the story shows about the aggregation problem that the papers don't.
