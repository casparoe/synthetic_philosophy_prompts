I'm a journalist working on a long-form piece about why the word genocide does such different work in international law, in historical and social-scientific description, and in ordinary moral and political argument — and why some campaigns of mass violence are almost universally called genocide while structurally similar ones are not. Before I start interviewing legal scholars and social scientists, I want to fix my own criteria. Please write the desiderata: the full specification of what any adequate account of genocide, as a legal category, a moral category, and a term usable in public discourse, would have to get right.

For each requirement, give me the requirement stated precisely; the case or consideration that forces it; and a judgment about whether it is load-bearing or negotiable, and whether it is a genuine desideratum or only the demand of one camp — strict legal formalism, say, or a Lemkin-style conception of group destruction, or a realist politics of naming.

Use concrete cases throughout. I have in mind as fixed points the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, Rwanda in 1994, and Srebrenica; and as contested cases the Soviet famine in Ukraine, Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge, and the treatment of Indigenous peoples under settler colonialism. Part of the deliverable is an account of which of these are genuinely fixed points and which are only apparently so.

I especially want the following pressure points addressed, not just the textbook ones:

- The relation between destroying a group and destroying its individual members: whether an account must treat the destruction of a group as a distinct wrong over and above mass killing, and what that distinct wrong consists in — the group's institutions, its conditions of social reproduction, its presence in a territory, its collective identity, or something else.
- The intent requirement: whether the perpetrator's aim of destroying the group is essential to the wrong, or whether a pattern of foreseeable destruction under mixed or unprovable aims should fall within the category; and what imposing or dropping the requirement does to proof in a courtroom, to historical description, and to moral judgment.
- The list problem: which groups an account protects and why — the traditional national, ethnic, racial, and religious categories and the contested exclusion or inclusion of political, social, and class groups — and whether the group's boundaries are facts an account takes as given or something the account must itself theorize.
- Cultural destruction: whether destroying a group's language, institutions, monuments, or capacity to transmit itself across generations belongs in the same category as physical and biological destruction or in an adjacent one, and whether the line between them is principled or a political compromise.
- The politics of naming: the word carries legal obligations, marks moral extremity, and is deployed selectively under geopolitical pressure. An adequate account should say whether it is trying to describe the world independently of that politics, to explain why the politics attaches to the word, or to discipline the word's use — and it should not silently run those three aims together.
- The neighboring categories — crimes against humanity, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and terms such as politicide (destruction of a group defined politically) and democide (state-directed killing of a subject population): whether these are subcases, rivals, complements, or different axes of classification, and why an account must not treat genocide as merely the top of a single severity scale.

Then answer three questions about the list itself. Which two or three requirements are most likely to be jointly unsatisfiable, and what would that mean for the project of keeping genocide a single coherent category? Which single requirement, if taken seriously, does the most to exclude the going candidates — roughly, a Lemkin-style account, the 1948 Genocide Convention's legal definition, and a broad social-scientific or activist usage that treats genocide largely as the systematic destruction of a people? Finally, give a short scorecard of those three candidates against the full list, while keeping the weight on the criteria rather than on the scorecard.

I want the result to function as an interview instrument: a checklist of commitments I can watch for, and a set of cases and distinctions I can press the experts on.
