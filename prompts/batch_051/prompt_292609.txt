So a grad student in my philosophy of mind seminar gave an argument last week that's been bugging me. He swears it shows computationalism—the view that the brain is a computer and that this is what explains cognition—is either trivially true or explainatorily dead. I'll reconstruct it as best I can:

(1) A physical system implements a computation C iff there is some mapping from the system's physical states to the states of C such that the system's causal transitions mirror C's formal transitions. This is basically the definition we were given.

(2) For basically any physical system with enough parts and states, and for basically any finite computation C, you can find some mapping—usually a wildly gerrymandered one, but a mapping nonetheless—that makes the system mirror C. So a rock, a bathtub of water, the weather: each "computes" whatever you like, depending on how you label its states.

(3) But then the claim "the brain computes" can't be what distinguishes brains from rocks. If the rock computes every bit as much as the brain, then the brain's being a computer tells us nothing about why the brain thinks and the rock doesn't.

(4) So if the computationalist wants "the brain computes" to explain cognition, they have to mean something more than (1). They have to build in something extra: semantics, biological function, representational content, intentionality, design, whatever.

(5) But the minute you build in that extra, you've conceded the point: computation itself isn't doing the explanatory work; the extra thing is.

(6) Therefore computationalism is stuck: either it's vacuous (everything computes), or it quietly abandons computation as the explainer. Either way, it's not a serious explanatory claim about the mind.

Here's my problem: this argument seems to prove too much. It would also show that a pocket calculator doesn't compute addition in any stronger sense than a puddle does, and that Turing machines don't compute anything except by fiat. But surely a calculator computes. So I suspect one of the steps is doing something fishy, probably between (2) and (3), but I can't name the move. If I had to choose, I'd like to keep (1), since it sounds like the standard definition, and I'd rather give up whatever makes (2) so cheap. But maybe (1) is the real problem. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And if the argument does go wrong, is there still a version of the triviality worry that survives, or does it all collapse once you seperate the good version from the bad one?
