I am a retired experimental neuroscientist, and in the past few years I have taken up the philosophy of mind with the enthusiasm of a late convert. The causal exclusion argument is the issue that now keeps me up at night: if the neural events realizing my desire to reach for a glass are already sufficient for the reaching, and the desire as a mental occurrence is distinct from those neural events, then the desire itself appears to be doing no causal work — it is screened off by its own realization. Since I spent a career in the laboratory, I cannot help wondering what would follow if this were not merely an argument but an accepted empirical result. I would like you to reason through that counterfactual with me.

Suppose that within ten years a technique becomes available — call it interlevel causal auditing — that can estimate directed causal influence in datasets containing both fine-grained token neural states and token mental states (desires, intentions, judgments) for the very same episodes of behavior. A large, preregistered, multi-center study examines ordinary intentional actions: reaching for a glass, answering a question, declining an unappetizing food. In the fitted causal model, the directed edge from the token mental state to the behavior has an estimated strength indistinguishable from zero once the token neural state is included; the mental variable contributes no additional causal information at the level of the individual act. The finding replicates across laboratories, and the press reports the result in headlines as science confirming epiphenomenalism. Take the finding as stipulated; I am less interested in whether such a technique is possible than in what follows if it is accepted.

Please predict, with your reasoning made visible, how this plays out over the following twenty years in three domains: criminal law's treatment of mens rea and responsibility, psychiatry's balance between pill-based and talk-based treatment, and ordinary first-person practice of praise, blame, and effort. I am less interested in a yes-or-no verdict than in the chain of consequences. Which changes follow robustly from the result as described, which depend on how courts, clinicians, and the public interpret it, and which would be licensed only if one ignored the standard philosophical responses to exclusion — overdetermination, the idea that higher and lower levels of explanation carve causation at different grains, or the idea that the mental can be causally relevant as a pattern even when it is not an additional force? State explicitly which links in your prediction you are most and least confident about, and what would have to hold for you to be wrong. Conclude by saying whether the stipulated result would actually establish what the headlines claim, or whether the most interesting consequences would flow from a misreading of the finding. Answer in American English.
