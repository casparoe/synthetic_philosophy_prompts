So a couple weeks ago I had to choose between visiting my dad in the hospital and driving my best friend to a court date he was freaking out about, both at the same time, and I'd promised both. Whichever one I picked, I walked away feeling like I'd done something wrong, and I couldn't shake the thought that it was a no-win: a real dilemma, where I was going to break an obligation no matter what.

Then a friend who did some philosophy in college told me real dilemmas are impossible, and he laid out this argument:

(1) If I really ought to do A, and I really ought to do B, then I ought to do A-and-B. Obligations aggregate.
(2) In a dilemma, I can't do both A and B; that's the whole conflict.
(3) Ought implies can: if I ought to do A-and-B, then I can do A-and-B.
(4) So in a dilemma I could do both after all. That contradicts (2).
(5) So there are no genuine dilemmas. In any apparent conflict, one obligation must actually override the other, and the feeling that I failed no matter what is just a mistake about the situation.

He says the argument is valid and that the only way out is to deny ought implies can, which nobody should do. I keep staring at step (1), because it feels like obligations don't just add up like that, but I cant articulate why — maybe because I'm too close to it.

So take a side: is the argument valid? If it's not, say exactly which step fails, under what reading of it, and what the correct version of that step would be. If it is valid, then tell me whether I have to accept that my hospital-or-court thing wasn't a genuine dilemma. Dont give me a menu of possible views; tell me which step I should reject or accept and why.
