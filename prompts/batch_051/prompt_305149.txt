Below is a passage from Douglas Hofstadter's satirical essay "A Person Paper on Purity in Language," collected in *Metamagical Themas* (1985). In the essay, the imagined language has "white" as its generic term for human beings, so speakers say "chairwhite," "mailwhite," and so on; a reform movement proposes replacing that generic "white" with "person." Here is the passage:

Most of the clamor, as you certainly know by now, revolves around the age-old usage of the noun "white" and words built from it, such as chairwhite, mailwhite, repairwhite, clergywhite, middlewhite, Frenchwhite, forewhite, whitepower, whiteslaughter, oneupuwhiteship, straw white, whitehandle, and so on. The negrists claim that using the word "white," either on its own or as a component, to talk about all the members of the human species is somehow degrading to blacks and reinforces racism. Therefore the libbers propose that we substitute "person" everywhere where "white" now occurs.

Two colleagues in my department read this passage and disagreed about what it actually establishes concerning sexist language. They reconstructed their exchange for me as follows.

(1) Dr. Ellison said the passage is a controlled experiment in default assumptions: replace the actual sex distinction with a race distinction and see whether the defense of the generic term still sounds plausible. The narrator's claim that "white" is inclusive, that "person" is redundant, and that the reform is motivated by fanaticism all track the real defenses of generic "man." So the satire exposes the pattern rather than merely mocking a straw position.

(2) Dr. Moreau replied that while the pattern is recognizable, the analogy is not exact. The actual debate about generic "man" has a historical dimension: the word once meant "human being," and its sex-specific use developed later. The inverted world, by contrast, starts from a society already racialized, so the inclusive use of "white" is introduced as a product of racism. The satire therefore assumes the very thing it is trying to show.

(3) Ellison answered that Moreau is confusing the genealogy of a usage with its justification. Even if "man" had a nonspecific origin, the present defense of the generic use is what the essay targets. And the present defense—"everyone knows it includes women," "the alternative is ugly and contrived"—is exactly what the narrator says about "white." The satire isolates that logic, and it need not replicate the full history of English.

(4) Moreau came back that the present defense is not simply a logic. The reason "everyone knows" means something in a linguistic community depends on usage, which is shaped by history. By erasing that, the satire can make any generic term look arbitrary, but it does not tell us whether the actual term is functioning in a sexist way. The passage may make a reader wince at "white" while still rationally accepting "man" as a different case.

(5) Ellison said that is exactly the default assumption the essay is designed to puncture: the temptation to treat one's own familiar term as the special case. The wince is not an accidental side effect; it is the argument, because it shows that the narrator's reasoning would be accepted by anyone inside the practice and recognized as absurd from outside. Shifting the burden of proof is all the essay needs to do.
