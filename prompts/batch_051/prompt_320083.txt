Fearon's rationalist theory of war begins from a puzzle: war is costly and risky, so for any conflict there should be some negotiated settlement both sides would prefer ex ante, and the question is why rational actors fail to reach it. The standard answers are private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibilities. The framework assumes the stakes of the conflict exist independently of the fighting: there is a territory, policy, or other object of dispute, and war is a costly mechanism for allocating it. Now take a technology race between AI labs or states over first-mover control of general-purpose AI. The prize does not pre-exist the race; the frontier capability is produced by the racing itself. The costs of racing, such as duplicated research, corner-cutting on safety, and the deployment of poorly understood systems, are at the same time the inputs that create whatever there is to win. It is tempting to say the indivisibility mechanism handles this, since a decisive lead looks winner-take-all, but the future gains from AGI could in principle be divided through equity, licensing, or treaty, so that may not be the right mechanism. The deeper issue is that there is no fixed stake over which an ex ante bargain can even be defined.

Does Fearon's framework survive this changed assumption? If it can be repaired, give the repaired statement and say what it commits us to; if it is dead, name the residual puzzle and say what kind of explanation replaces it.
