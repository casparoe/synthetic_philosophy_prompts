A curator I know has a settled-sounding track record on copies, forgeries, and originals:

(a) She considers a modern, acoustically indistinguishable replica of a Stradivarius almost worthless as an artwork, however good it sounds, because it was not made by the historical maker.

(b) She approved a newly painted, clearly labeled reconstruction of a destroyed medieval fresco in a chapel still used for worship, on the grounds that the space needs a devotional image and the original is gone.

(c) She thinks van Meegeren's fake Vermeers should be exhibited as forgeries and as documents of 1930s taste, but not in the Dutch Golden Age galleries.

(d) She insists a heavily damaged ancient sculpture should be shown as a ruin with losses visible rather than finished by modern restoration.

(e) She says an honest full-size copy of a famous painting serves a teaching collection just as well as the original would.

A laboratory can now rebuild, by robotic brushwork and period-appropriate pigments from pre-fire scans and technical studies, a visual twin of a painting destroyed in a fire, with no surviving fragments. The museum where it hung wants to set the twin into the original frame in the original gallery, with a discreet label. What should she recommend if she wants to stay consistent with her track record? Identify the candidate principles that fit her earlier verdicts, say what each predicts for the twin, give your best guess with reasons for preferring that reading, name the closest earlier analog, and specify which further case would most cleanly separate the candidate principles.
