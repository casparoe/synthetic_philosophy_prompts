I've been going in circles on this and want someone to check my steps. I read a chunk of Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, and it seams like Schopenhauer thought compassion is the whole basis of morality—that a deed only counts as morally good if it comes from pity, where you feel the other person's pain as your own. Nietzsche comes after this hard. In The Antichrist he writes:

"What is more harmful than any vice?—Practical sympathy for the botched and the weak—Christianity."

So here's the argument I tried to build:

(1) Schopenhauer says an act is morally good only if it's done out of pity, meaning you directly share the other person's suffering and want to relieve it.
(2) If pity is what makes an act good, then the more pity a person feels and acts on, the better they are morally.
(3) But Nietzsche says pity is harmful: it keeps the weak and botched alive and drags the strong down.
(4) Something that is harmful in that way can't be what makes an act good.
(5) So Schopenhauer has it backwards: either pity isn't the real source of moral value, or his use of 'good' is just leftover Christian morality.

I can't tell whether this argument is fair to Schopenhauer or if there's a step where I'm sneaking something in. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and why? If it is valid, does Schopenhauer have a way out?
