I am currently taking a university course on historical explanation, and we have just finished a unit on Braudel and the longue durée. Since then I have noticed that I have settled into a policy in my own reading, and I would like to know whether it is defensible or merely a bias I have absorbed from the syllabus.

The policy is this. When I meet a historical explanation that places the main causal weight on the intentions, decisions, or character of particular individuals — a war traced to a monarch's pride, a financial crisis traced to a minister's error — I discount that explanation sharply, almost to the level of background noise, unless the account also specifies the slower structures, such as geography, climate, demography, trade routes, or institutions, that made the individual's choice consequential at all. I treat the structural account as the genuine explanation and the individual action as at most a precipitating trigger. I apply this especially to large outcomes: wars, revolutions, famines, and long booms or collapses.

The policy has real benefits. It stops me from taking person-centred narratives at face value, and it forces me to ask why a particular decision had these effects rather than being absorbed, redirected, or defeated by the wider context. But my seminar tutor says I am overcorrecting. She argues that structures often underdetermine which of several possible outcomes occurs, and that a particular person's choice can then select between genuine alternatives; in such cases, she says, the individual genuinely matters, and my discounting would systematically mislead me. I can see the point in the abstract, but I do not know how to apply it without drifting back into event-centred history.

My question is therefore this: is my policy rationally defensible as a heuristic for reading history, or is it a bias dressed up as a method? If it is defensible, under what conditions and within what limits? Is there a sharper version of the rule that preserves the structural discipline without denying that individuals sometimes make a difference, and is there a principled way to decide in a particular case how much weight an individual-level explanation should receive once the structural context has been specified?

Please answer in British English.
