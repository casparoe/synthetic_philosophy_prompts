I teach a first-year college seminar called "Why do we keep doing this?" and this week we're reading Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery." I want to teach it through David Lewis's account of convention rather than the usual mob-mentality reading. The students will have been given a one-page gloss of Lewis on convention, but I need material for discussion.

Write me a reading of "The Lottery" through Lewis's idea that a convention is a regularity in a population that solves a coordination problem, could easily have been another regularity, and is held in place by everyone expecting everyone else to go along and preferring to conform given those expectations. I want you to push the theory against the story and be honest about where each one breaks.

Specifically: if I handed you a Lewisian checklist for "is this a convention," which boxes does the town's lottery clearly tick and which does it fail? The villagers don't act like they prefer the lottery, and it seems to have lost whatever coordination function it might once have had (if it ever had one). So does Lewis's account explain the practice, or does the story show us exactly the kind of self-perpetuating norm Lewis has trouble with — one kept alive by mutual expectation after the underlying preferences are gone? Is the lottery a convention that has outlived its reasons, and does Lewis have the resources to say that, or do we have to go beyond him?

Give me something I can walk into the classroom with: two or three scenes or details from the story (the black box, the ritual sayings, the children's game, Tessie's protest at the end, whatever) and for each say what it shows about the convention machinery and whether it supports or troubles Lewis's analysis. Don't quote from the story; just point to the scenes. And one more thing: if the theory says the lottery is a convention that has outlived whatever made it stick, what would it predict about how the town could get unstuck, and does the story show those levers being absent or blocked? Keep the reading fair in both directions — use Lewis to illuminate the story, then use the story to mark the limits of the theory, and say what a smart student who thinks Lewis is irrelevant here would be right about.
