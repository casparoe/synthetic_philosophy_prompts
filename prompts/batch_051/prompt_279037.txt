I keep finding myself teaching a view I'll call the Moloch picture, and I want someone to tell me, as honestly as possible, what would count against it — not the stock objections people list and then wave away, but a map of where the view is genuinely exposed. The view, roughly: in any multipolar setup (a market, an arms race, a bunch of AI labs, an academic job market) where an actor can capture a private gain by externalizing a cost, and where there's no authority able to make the cooperative move stick, the system drifts toward an equilibrium worse for everyone involved. The drift is self-reinforcing because anyone who unilaterally opts out gets outcompeted, outspent, or just ignored in the short run. So races to the bottom — price wars, credential inflation, safety corners cut, clickbait, antibiotic overuse, the arms spiral — are the default, and the nice equilibrium is the fragile one. Appeals to shared long-term interest only work, on this view, when they're already backed by enforceable coordination or a changed payoff structure.

Play it straight. What would have to be true for that to be false, or true only in a much narrower range of cases than I'm claiming? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, including ones that have arguably already happened. Where could the view actually break? A few candidate failure points I can half-see: cases where actors could visibly defect and didn't, for long stretches, without an enforcer; formal results in repeated game theory showing cooperation can be self-sustaining under conditions that actually obtain, not just in the limit; mechanisms by which a bad equilibrium generates its own opposition and collapses; an argument that what I'm calling "the equilibrium" is really a misdescribed bargaining failure, so the problem isn't a trap but a missing focal point or bad information; evidence that the outcome I'm calling worse-for-everyone is only worse by a standard the participants have reason to reject. I want to know which of these a committed Moloch-theorist will concede count against the view — not just what critics say — and which ones they'd shrug off as compatible after a harmless tweak.

Then be blunt about insulation. Has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability, where "bad equilibrium" can always be redefined and "binding enforcer" is always found after the fact? If it has, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just what a structural thesis about social life looks like? For what it's worth, I'm moved less by real-world counterexamples, which always come with confounds, and more by the thought that the whole trap depends on a model of actors I don't strictly need — so if that model goes, the view loses it's engine. I'm not asking you to pick a side. I'm asking what my view risks, and where.
