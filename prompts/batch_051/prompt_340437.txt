I've been going in circles on the "numbers" question in forced-choice rescue cases this term — the one where you can save one stranger or five strangers, no special ties, each person loses just as much if they die, and there's no way to save more than one side. The debate feels like it's been stuck for decades, and I can predict every move now. On one side, the Taurek-style anti-aggregationists argue like this: compare the one against any single member of the five, and the claim are perfectly balanced; if it were A versus B alone, nothing would favor either. The fact that there are five people on one side doesn't create a new claimant called "the five" whose claim is five times stronger, because each of the five only has a claim that they themselves be saved. So flipping a coin isn't disrespectful — the five, as a group, can't complain, because no particular one of them was treated worse than A. On the other side, the aggregationists say the pairwise ties don't do the work the other side thinks: sure, A against any one of them is a tie, but A against five of them is one tie against five ties. Each of the five has a claim equal to A's, and if equal claims can't be summed across people, that's a substantive moral premise you'd have to buy, not something the rescue case hands you. Saving A ends up looking like letting one person's equal claim beat five people's equal claims.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides aren't really disagreeing about the case at all — they're disagreeing about what a person is for the purposes of moral theory: a sealed-off unit whose claims can't be added to anyone else's, versus a location of harm or value that can be totaled across persons. If that's the diagnosis, then no new case is going to move anyone, and progress would have to look more like a formal result showing one picture secretly presupposes the other, or an honest argument that it's bedrock and we should stop expecting the debate to resolve. But maybe I've got the wrong end: maybe the real disagreement is about what "equal claims" means in the first place, or whether giving someone a chance (the coin flip) and actually saving someone are even commensurable. I don't need you to take sides about who wins; I need a diagnosis of the standoff itself. What kind of disagreement is this actually, and what — given that diagnosis — would count as progress, or why should no progress be expected?
