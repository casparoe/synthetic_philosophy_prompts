I'm a science and ideas journalist, and a big part of my beat is psychiatry and mental health. A few times a month I have to decide how to cover a claim like: a new diagnosis is being proposed, or a research foundation says a common condition is massively underdiagnosed now that a better screening tool exists, or a clinic reports that dozens of people they tested turned out to have condition X all along. I've read enough philosophy of psychiatry to know the rough idea that diagnostic categories don't just label pre-existing kinds — the label changes how people understand and conduct themselves, which changes what the label picks out, which feeds back into the category, and so on. But I know the idea as a idea, not as a thing I can use on deadline.

What I want is a reusable procedure I can run before I file. The stakes are real: if I write "thousands of people have X and don't know it," I'm not just reporting a fact, I'm helping make that category available as a way of being a person. The constraints: I usually have about a day, I can call two or three researchers, I can read the papers they point me to, but I can't run my own analysis, and my editor, who signs off, is smart but not philosophically trained and will not read a essay on the metaphysics of kinds.

So give me a procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with "don't run this as a discovery," and where it stops and says "this is now a values/editorial judgment, not a factual one." Then apply it to two or three worked cases. Please make at least one a hypothetical new or expanded diagnosis being argued for the next manual revision, and one a claim that a well-known condition is hugely underdiagnosed by current screening. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me not to run the story I was excited to run, and say explicitly what the procedure can't settle — for example, whether a category is on balance good for the people who get it, as distinct from whether it's being discovered or made.
