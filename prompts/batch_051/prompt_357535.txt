Suppose I've got funding for a small group — three people, two years — to work on how humans pull a concept out of a handful of examples, using classic Bongard problems as the whole testbed: six panels on the left, six on the right, and the task is to find the rule that separates all the lefts from all the rights. I know the default view is these are just cute puzzles, but I think they're the cleanest setting for studying concept induction without natural-language gloss sneaking in. Rather than surveying the area, I want a defended agenda. The candidate questions I keep going back and forth about are: (a) what makes one rule 'simpler' or 'more natural' than another when the examples underdetermine the rule, and where that bias comes from; (b) how much solving is driven by comparing left vs right panels directly, as opposed to first noticing what the lefts have in common among themselves; (c) whether people are learning a categorical rule or a graded similarity that tolerates borderline cases; (d) how people compose new concepts out of a stock of familiar primitives rather than just matching features. Rank these — or say what's missing — with an eye toward which are upstream of the others, which are actually tractable within two years, and which would change how we think about concept induction if we got a solid answer. Also tell me what kind of results in year one would make you reshuffle the ranking.
