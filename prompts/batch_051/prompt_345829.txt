For the last year I have been working out a view in wild animal welfare that I call the Clearing Principle, and I would like it assessed honestly rather than charitably softened. The principle is this: when a habitat is a welfare sink — meaning that the typical lives of the animals it would produce over time are on balance net-negative, because of predation, starvation, parasitism, or disease — then destroying that habitat is an overall welfare improvement whenever the aggregate negative welfare of the future lives it would otherwise have produced outweighs the direct suffering caused by the destruction. The deaths caused by clearing count as bad only through the short-term pain they involve, not through depriving animals of lives that were on average not worth living. This implies that at least some acts we call environmental destruction would be, on that empirical assumption, unusually effective anti-suffering interventions. Consider a hypothetical wetland whose amphibians nearly always die before metamorphosis except in rare wet years: each generation of lives is brief and dominated by desiccation or being eaten. If draining the wetland permanently ends that cycle, my principle says the acute suffering of the draining is a price worth paying.

I recognize how that sounds, and I want the principle stress-tested for what it claims, not for its packaging. Please do three things. First, reconstruct the strongest coherent version of the Clearing Principle, filling in whatever needs to be specified — average versus total welfare, the status of death versus deprivation, the assumption that habitats can be scored at all. Second, say whether that best version is genuinely distinct from positions already familiar in environmental ethics or population ethics, or whether it collapses into one of them under another name. Third, give the strongest objection or counterexample — real or constructed — that the best version still cannot handle, and locate exactly where it breaks.
