I maintain a small open-source library and our contributor policy has a clause, written in 2019 before anyone on the team was using AI code generation. It reads: "By submitting a contribution, you represent that the contribution is your own original work and that you are able to explain the reasoning behind every nontrivial part of it." Here's the situation I keep going back and forth on. I had an LLM generate a subtle patch: I gave it a failing test and some constraints, it spat out code that passes, I read it carefully, I understand what it does and why it works, and I can now write up a clear explanation of the reasoning behind the tricky parts, like the choice of data structure and the edge condition it checks. But I also know that if I had not run the model, I might never of thought of that particular approach. The clause was added to stop people pasting code they found on a forum and then not being able to maintain it, which is not really what happened here. My question is whether the clause reaches my patch. I want each of the usual ways of reading a rule. What do the words alone require? What does the purpose of the clause require, given that I can definitely explain and maintain the code? What would the maintainers who wrote it in 2019 have said if someone asked them about machine-generated patches, and what would a reasonable contributor understand the word "reasoning" to mean in that sentence? Then tell me which of those readings should actually win, and what it would take for the clause not to catch me. I guess the deeper thing I'm poking at is whether code has to originate as something like thought in a human head, or whether being able to give the reasoning afterwards is all that could ever have mattered. Write it as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, like an email reply.
