I have been trying to isolate a failure mode that appears in distributed software organizations, and I would like a constructed case to reason with in a design discussion about which engineering decisions should require shared sign-off. The failure mode is this: several engineers each independently have the authority to take a risky action; if any one of them takes it, the consequences fall on the whole organization or project; the action is not plainly reckless, and on each individual's own evidence it can look like the right call; but if the decision were made once, by the group with access to all the evidence, the group would reject it. I want the case to isolate this structural problem from the usual explanations, such as misaligned incentives, poor communication, or incompetence.

Construct a realistic scenario set in an ordinary software company or open-source project. Pick a single concrete action that an engineer could take unilaterally—for example, disabling a safety check, bypassing a review gate, merging an experimental dependency into a shared codebase, or rolling back a rate limit before a launch—and make the benefit and the low-probability harm specific enough that rough expected-value arithmetic is possible. The scenario must satisfy all of the following.

There must be at least five people, each with the authority to take that same action alone, where any one person's action is sufficient to trigger both the benefit and the harm for the entire organization. On the full information available across the organization, the action's expected value must be negative, so a single joint decision-maker with all the evidence would not act. At the same time, acting must be individually defensible for each person: each one's subjective expected value on the basis of their own information must be positive, and none of them may be negligent, overconfident beyond what their evidence can justify, or motivated by fear, ambition, credit, or internal politics. Finally, the probability that at least one person takes the action must rise substantially as further competent, well-intentioned people with the same decision rule are added.

After laying out the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification. Show the rough expected-value calculation for one named individual that makes acting rational from that person's chair, and the full-information calculation that makes the action collectively undesirable. State exactly which feature of the case does the work—I suspect it is something like an independent noisy estimate combined with a selection effect in which only the most optimistic estimate gets to decide—and test that diagnosis by removing one element at a time and saying whether the effect survives. In particular, show whether replacing the unilateral norm with a simple majority vote among the same people fixes the aggregate error in this case, and what the organization gives up if it adopts that replacement. Finally, put the strongest version of the opposite position: a defender of the norm that an engineer should act whenever their own expected-value analysis is positive would object to this case in some specific way; say what that objection is, and state what extra principle they would need to add to avoid the scenario's conclusion.
