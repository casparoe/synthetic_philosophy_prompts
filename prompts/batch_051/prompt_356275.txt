ok so we did the St. Petersburg paradox in my econ-ish math unit and I got caught on something. we read this bit from Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 paper *Exposition of a New Theory on the Measurement of Risk*:

"The determination of the value of an item must not be based on the price, but rather on the utility it yields ... There is no doubt that a gain of one thousand ducats is more significant to the pauper than to a rich man though both gain the same amount."

I get the basic fix: use expected utility, not expected dollars, and with log utility the infinite sum converges to a finite number. Fine. But I thought of a variation and now I'm not sure the quote is doing as much work as people say.

Suppose a casino builds a machine that doesn't pay out money. Instead, before each toss, the machine measures the player's current utility function and then pays out a prize that directly adds exactly 2^n utils to the player's total utility if the game ends on toss n. So by construction, the marginal utility of the prize is constant — each extra prize unit is worth exactly one extra util no matter how rich or poor or satiated you already are. The thing that made Bernoulli's "pauper vs. rich man" point relevant has been engineered away.

Now what happens? Is the expected utility of playing this game infinite again? If so, does the paradox actually come back in full force, or is there still some reason a reasonable person wouldn't pay an arbitrarily high fee to play? And does this variation show that Bernoulli's solution wasn't really about utility per se, but just a sneaky way of putting a cap on how much any given prize can matter to you? I'm trying to figure out whether diminishing marginal utility is actually resolving the paradox or just replacing it with a different assumption about what can be offered as a prize. Work through what the standard expected-utility analysis says for this version, and say where the real fix has to come from if not from diminishing marginal utility.
