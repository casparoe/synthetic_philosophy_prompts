I've been trying to separate arguing in bad faith from ordinary epistemic vice—sharing things impulsively, tribalism, overconfidence, refusing to update out of embarrassment—in polarized debates. My working principle is that someone argues in bad faith when they either knowingly assert what they believe is false, or argue only to win rather than to understand. But the principle wobbles against my own gut responses to cases, and I'd like it tested and repaired.

Here are the cases.

1. My uncle forwards a story from an outrage site that has been thoroughly debunked. When I show him the debunking, he looks embarrassed, says he didn't know, and never shares it again, though he never actually says he was wrong. Verdict: not bad faith—he was misled, not misleading.

2. A cable-news host says repeatedly for months that a policy caused rising crime. Internal emails later show his own researchers had told him before those broadcasts that the claim was unsupported. Verdict: clearly bad faith.

3. A politician repeats a debunked statistic for months. There is no evidence he saw the fact-checks, though he employs staff who could have told him. I find myself treating him as bad faith, but my principle can't reach him without proof of knowledge. Verdict: bad faith, but possibly unfair.

4. An activist says, "Voters in that community are motivated entirely by resentment." I give a plausible counterexample. He says, "That's just an exception; the point stands," and moves on. Verdict: feels like bad faith, but my principle doesn't catch it, since he may sincerely believe the generalization.

5. In a planning meeting, a colleague keeps raising objections; each time one is answered he produces another. At the end he grins and says he was "just playing devil's advocate." Verdict: infuriating, but not exactly bad faith.

6. After I show a friend the data, he says privately, "Okay, I was wrong about that one." That evening he posts the original claim on social media unchanged. Verdict: clear bad faith—saying what he now disbelieves.

7. My brother tells me he doesn't want to discuss politics with me because it makes him feel like a witness on the stand. Verdict: not bad faith; refusing to argue is different from arguing badly.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts better than my knowing-falsehood-or-points-scoring test? In particular, what should it say about the politician case, where knowledge is unproven but the person is powerful and advised, and why is that different, if it is, from my uncle's case? Which of my verdicts should I give up rather than complicate the principle to save them? And what would the principle say about the case I keep returning to: someone who no longer trusts any source or person from the other side at all—is that person still arguing in good faith, or have they already left the practice of arguing?
