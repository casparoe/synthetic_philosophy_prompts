Here's a thing about criminal law that's bothered me for a while, and I'd like the philosophy underneath it dug into. If you honestly didn't know that the suitcase you were carrying had drugs in it, courts treat that as a real excuse: mistake of fact is a standard defense, and your uncertainty about the facts counts in your favor. But if you honestly didn't know that what you were doing was illegal — or even genuinely hadn't thought it was wrong — courts almost never care: ignorance of the law is no excuse, and being unsure about the moral status of your act gets you nowhere. So the law treats uncertainty about what's true as highly relevant to blame, and uncertainty about what's right or wrong as basically irrelevant. I know there are exceptions, but set those aside. What view of moral knowledge, and of how much moral uncertainty a person can fairly be held to, does that combination presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit picture, say whether its two halves are consistent with each other, and tell me what criminal law would have to look like if it treated uncertainty about right and wrong the way it treats uncertainty about the facts. And say how confident you are in the reconstruction, and what specific point you're least sure of.
