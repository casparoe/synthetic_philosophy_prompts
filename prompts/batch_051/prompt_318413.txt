Consider how the law handles blackmail. In most jurisdictions, a person commits a criminal offense by demanding money or another benefit from someone as the price of not disclosing true, reputation-damaging information about them. This remains an offense even where the disclosure would itself have been lawful, or at least tolerated, if made outright, and even where asking the same person for the same money would have been lawful had no threat been attached. Two acts that are each within a person's ordinary legal power, making a truthful disclosure and requesting money, become a serious crime when combined as a conditional offer of silence. The doctrine sharpens the puzzle: the truth of the threatened disclosure is normally no defense to blackmail, and it usually does not matter that the blackmailer was legally free to publish the information. Yet the same legal systems generally do not recognize any general right to prevent the truthful disclosure of embarrassing facts, and they protect a good deal of truthful speech strongly.

What theory of the underlying wrong must the criminalization of blackmail be presupposing in order to make sense? Reconstruct the implicit account of the interests at stake — reputation, privacy, autonomy, freedom of speech, freedom of contract, or something else — that would explain why the combination is prohibited while its component acts are permitted. Be precise about which parts of the practice strictly presuppose that theory, which parts are compatible with several rival theories, and where the doctrine appears to presuppose inconsistent things, for example about whether a person is entitled to control the flow of true information about themselves. Then say what would have to change in the law if each of the main rival theories of the wrong were adopted instead.
