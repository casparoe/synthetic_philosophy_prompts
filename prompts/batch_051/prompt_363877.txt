I'm prepping a lecture on Cesare Beccaria's chapter on torture from On Crimes and Punishments, and the intro to my teaching edition gives a summary I've always half-distrusted. It says Beccaria's argument is basically that torture is a bad epistemic instrument: it makes the experience of pain, rather than evidence, the test of truth, so a frail innocent will confess to stop the pain while a calloused guilty suspect can withstand it and go free. It then concludes that Beccaria opposed torture because it produces unreliable confessions and therefore undermines the real purpose of punishment, which is deterrence. On that reading his objection is consequentialist through and through, and it would lapse if torture could be shown to be reliable -- which is exactly the ticking bomb student's opening.

But then the actual chapter has that other turn, where in my old translation it runs roughly: a person cannot be considered guilty before the judge has pronounced sentence, and society may not pull back the shield of its protection from someone until it has been established that he broke the terms under which that protection was extended. That sounds like a different kind of argument altogether, something about the accused's standing before the law rather than the reliability of the pain. I want to use this in class to show why the ticking bomb objection to an absolute ban isn't decisive, but I don't want to misread Beccaria either, and I'm grading essays on this next week. Can you separate what the intro gets right from what it's flattening, and whether the passage I just paraphrased actually refutes the consequentialist reading, or whether Beccaria can hold both? And does his position really rule out the ticking bomb, or does it just reset the burden for what we'd have to establish before torture could ever be used?
