I was trained as a historian of the early Cold War and later spent several years working in complex civil litigation, so I live in two vocabularies. My legal side thinks comfortably in terms of cause-in-fact and but-for necessity, proximate cause, intervening and superseding causes, comparative fault, joint and several responsibility, and the standing distinction between causing an outcome and being responsible for it. My historical side has never articulated a worked-out theory of how historiography apportions blame. I want to use the first vocabulary to interrogate the question that anchors the Cold War-origins debate: who started the Cold War?

Take the three broad families of answer the literature offers — with the usual caveat that the labels flatten rich and shifting bodies of scholarship. The orthodox family stresses Soviet expansionism and revolutionary ideology as the prime mover, with Western policy as response; the revisionist family stresses American economic and atomic policy as the active, system-shaping force; the later structural and post-revisionist work stresses mutual misperception, the security dilemma, and a bilateral spiral no single party controlled. Restate each family in my legal vocabulary as if it were being litigated. For each, specify the but-for claim it is committed to, what plays the role of proximate cause (what is foreseeable, direct, or too remote), and how comparative or joint responsibility would be assigned. Be scrupulous about which parts translate faithfully, which have to be bent to fit the legal forms, and which break the forms entirely.

Then give me the residue report. What in these historical accounts has no legal counterpart — the absence of a plaintiff with clean hands, of a discrete wrongful act, of any remedy, of any court with jurisdiction over the relevant agents; structural causes without an intentional subject; the retrospective and discursive role of the question itself in postwar politics? And render a verdict on the framing: does the residue show that "who started it" was always a legal-moral question wearing historical dress, that the legal framework is simply the wrong instrument, or that the question must be split into a causal-narrative question, an explanatory question, and a normative question that the historiography has let run together? I am not asking you to decide the history; I am asking you to show me, through what survives and what does not survive the translation, what the rival schools are actually disagreeing about and what they only appear to disagree about.
