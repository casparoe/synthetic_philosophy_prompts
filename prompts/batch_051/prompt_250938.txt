I host a small philosophy podcast aimed at readers of rationalist blogs and forums, and next episode's guest is a philosopher who has spent much of his career defending a neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics against the situationist critique. His position, in brief, is that the classic social-psychology experiments—Milgram's obedience studies, the Good Samaritan setup, small mood manipulations—do not show that global character traits are rare or incoherent; at most they show that virtues are hard to cultivate and that their expression is situation-sensitive in ways a mature Aristotelian account already predicts. I want the interview to go well beyond restating that view, and I would rather skip biographical warm-ups. Draft eight interview questions, phrased as you would ask them, each with a one- or two-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer you would most expect him to give. Make sure the set presses on at least these pressure points, though you may reorder them or replace one with something sharper: (1) the low cross-situational consistency correlations and whether treating virtue as rare rather than nonexistent handles the data or merely insulates the view from it; (2) whether the practical-wisdom response—that a virtuous person may deliberately act differently across contexts for good reasons—makes the existence of virtue empirically unfalsifiable; (3) the retreat to local or domain-specific traits and what normative content is lost if the theory makes it; (4) whether an ethical theory centered on an ideal character type needs a descriptive psychology of actual agents at all, and whether avoiding that need concedes the situationist's central point; (5) what virtue ethics should say about moral education and situation design if environments have the power the experiments suggest. End with a question about which concrete experimental result would most shift his confidence in his own position. Keep the full set under 800 words.
