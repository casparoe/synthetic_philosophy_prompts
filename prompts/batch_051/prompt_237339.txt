Ok so I have never taken a class on any of this, I just read blog posts and watch explainer videos, so bear with me. I came across the Bayesian idea that if two perfectly rational people start with different priors but then update correctly on the same evidence forever, their beliefs are supposed to converge in the long run, the priors wash out. That made sense to me at first.

But then I started thinking about how almost nobody I actually know sees the same evidence anymore. My feed gets chosen by an algorithm that is basically trying to maximize what I will click on, and it has learned what I already believe, and my brothers feed does the same for him. We can both spend an hour catching up on the news and come away having seen almost no overlap. It is not that we interpret the same facts differently, it is that different facts got shown to us in the first place. That feels like a genuinely new situation, not just a cleaner example of the old one.

So my question is whether the old convergence argument survives that setup. Once each persons evidence stream is being selected in a way that depends on their current beliefs, through past clicks and watch time, seperately for each of them, is it still true that they have to converge, or can the algorithm keep two priors apart forever by feeding each one whatever it wants? I want to know exactly which premise of the old argument breaks. Is it that they no longer share the same evidence, or that the evidence is no longer independent of their beliefs, or that they are not applying the same likelihoods anymore because the facts in front of them got curated differently? And if the original result is dead under personalization, is there a repaired version that still says something weaker but still worth having, like convergence is only guaranteed when there is enough overlap in the information streams, or when the filter is not allowed to know the persons beliefs? I would like the weakened claim spelled out, not just a yes or no.
