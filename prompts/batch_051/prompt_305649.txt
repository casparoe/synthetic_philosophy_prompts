I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for a piece on moral uncertainty I've been reading around the objection to consequentialism usually called cluelessness. A book for a general audience reconstructs the objection roughly as follows. Act consequentialism says the right act is the one with the best overall consequences. But an ordinary decision, such as spending twenty dollars on a meal rather than giving it to a charity, sets off chains of effects through the economy, the environment, and the lives of everyone who might otherwise have received the money, and those chains fade into an indefinite future we cannot model. Since we cannot trace those chains, the book says, each option is exactly as likely as any other to turn out best overall; consequentialism therefore offers no practical guidance at all, and in practice it collapses into acting on impulse. The author concludes that this is not merely a practical difficulty but a refutation of the theory: a doctrine that tells us to maximize consequences while guaranteeing that we can never know which act does so cannot function as a moral theory at all. I find the conclusion intuitively appealing, but I suspect the argument is being compressed, and I can't tell which parts are the objector's fair statement of a real problem and which are overstatements. Could you audit the summary itself: which parts would a careful consequentialist accept or want to qualify, and does the refutation conclusion survive the corrections? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
