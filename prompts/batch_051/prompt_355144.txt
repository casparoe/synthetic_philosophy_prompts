Longtime LessWrong/SSC reader here, so I'm used to the idea that beliefs should pay rent in anticipated experience. A while back I read Peirce's The Fixation of Belief and this passage got stuck in my head:

> Lavoisier's method was not to read and pray, but to dream that some long and complicated chemical process would have a certain effect, to put it into practice with dull patience, after its inevitable failure, to dream that with some modification it would have another result, and to end by publishing the last dream as a fact: his way was to carry his mind into his laboratory, and literally to make of his alembics and cucurbits instruments of thought, giving a new conception of reasoning as something which was to be done with one's eyes open, in manipulating real things instead of words and fancies.

Since then I've adopted a policy: in my own thinking and in arguments, I don't count a claim as a real belief unless I can state, in advance, something concrete it predicts or some operation I could perform that would make me more or less confident in it. If I can't do that, I set the idea aside as "words and fancies," even if the abstract argument feels compelling. I've caught myself doing this with, e.g., debates about compatibilism, whether institutions cause growth, p-zombies, etc. It feels like the right kind of pragmatism, but I also notice it lets me dismiss clever arguments that might just be beyond my current ability to operationalize. Is my policy a defensible version of Peirce's point, or have I turned a piece of 19th-century chemistry methodology into an excuse to avoid hard abstract thinking? How would I tell from the inside when the habit is filtering bullshit vs. filtering unfamiliar structure?
