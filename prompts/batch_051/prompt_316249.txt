I teach an upper-level ethics seminar — 14 students, 80 minutes. Next week I want to run an in-class exercise on reflective equilibrium, but I dont want the standard "we adjust principles and judgements until they fit" gloss. I want them to feel the more uncomfortable thing: that the method may only guarantee internal coherence, and that people with different starting judgements can end up at different but equally tidy endpoints without any way to settle who is right by the method's own standards.

The shape I have in mind: students first make snap judgements about a bunch of promise-keeping cases. Then each small group has to formulate a single principle that covers their judgements. Then I throw in disruptor cases that are chosen to break whichever simple principle most groups will settle on, and they have to decide what to give up — the principle, an earlier judgement, or both — while recording every change. Then groups compare and (I hope) discover they've built different, internally consistent systems. I want them to run into the underdetermination worry themselves before I name anything.

Can you turn this into a runnable 80-minute plan? I need:

- a case set, maybe 8 starter cases plus 3–4 disruptors, with a clear idea of what simple principle each disruptor is meant to kill;
- materials (cards or a one-page handout) and a shared worksheet that makes revisions visible, crossed-out judgements and all;
- a setup and round structure with timings so the whole thing fits and stays moving;
- a debrief script that maps what happened onto narrow vs wide reflective equilibrium and then onto the underdetermination worry, plus what going wide (bringing in background theories about persons, relationships, etc.) would and wouldn't fix;
- an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce, and what will predictably go wrong — e.g. groups settling on the weakest possible principle to avoid conflict, or students treating their initial judgements as fixed data — and what I should do when it does.

No framing lecture up front, and I'd rather the term reflective equilibrium not be said aloud until the debrief.
