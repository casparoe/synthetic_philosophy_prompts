I am writing a paper on Carl Hempel's Studies in the Logic of Confirmation, and I want to understand the ravens paradox by having a case built to a specific set of constraints. In the article, Hempel isolates two ideas that together produce the paradox. The first is that a positive instance of a universal generalisation confirms that generalisation. The second is that whatever confirms a statement also confirms anything logically equivalent to it. Since the hypothesis that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the hypothesis that all non-black things are non-ravens, a white shoe seems to confirm the ravens hypothesis. A standard Bayesian reply says this is not really paradoxical because a white shoe confirms the hypothesis only by a negligible amount once background knowledge is taken into account.

Please construct a one-paragraph thought experiment in which a Hempelian and a Bayesian assessment of the same observation come apart in a way that is not just a matter of negligible versus non-negligible degree. The specifications are these: the observation should be an ordinary non-black, non-raven object encountered in a realistic field-biology setting; Hempel's account should say that the observation confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black; a Bayesian with a natural, well-motivated prior should say that the same observation reduces the probability of that hypothesis, not merely leaves it unchanged or raises it by a tiny amount; and the reversal must not depend on gerrymandered predicates, on giving the Bayesian information the Hempelian account is not allowed to use, or on making the observation itself off-limits by stipulation. After the scenario, walk through exactly what each account says about the observation and why. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed, and how a defender of Hempel would attack the case. Please write the whole answer in British English.
