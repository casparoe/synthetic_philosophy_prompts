I'm a retired spectroscopist — forty years in the lab, and now I've knocked of to read philosophy, which is how I've gotten myself tied in this particular knot. I'd be grateful if an expert in the philosophy of science, ideally someone comfortable with vagueness and with how science actually credits people, would take a run at it. Please think carefully and brainstorm likely diagnoses before you commit; I'm interested in which step is doing the real damage and what rejecting it costs, not in a quick answer.

The knot is about Stigler's law of eponymy: the semi-joke, with real teeth, that no scientific discovery is named after the person who actually made it first. Lemaître derived Hubble's law two years before Hubble; the Pythagorean theorem was known long before Pythagoras; Halley's comet was watched for centuries before Halley did anything but compute an orbit; Stigler himself named the law after himself and credited the idea to Merton, which rather makes the point. I'm fine with the law as an observation. The trouble is that it seems to run on and swallow its own tail.

(1) For an eponym to count as a misattribution, there must be a fact of the matter about who really made the discovery first.
(2) Stigler's law has held up too widely to be a run of bad luck; it's telling us something structural about how discoveries get credited.
(3) What it tells us, I think, is that making a discovery isn't a single event. There's the vague hunch, the private note, the obscure paper, the polished proof, the moment the community actually takes it up.
(4) The person who ends up with the eponym differs from their neglected precursors only in where they sit on that continuum, not in kind.
(5) If it's only a matter of degree, then for any two candidates there's no non-arbitrary cutoff at which a precursor becomes the discoverer.
(6) If there's no non-arbitrary cutoff, then there isn't really a fact of the matter about who first made the discovery; the attribution is a convention we adopt after the fact.
(7) But if attribution is conventional, misattribution is conventional too — there's no fact for an eponym to get wrong.
(8) So Stigler's law, which needs a real difference between correct and incorrect attribution to say anything at all, ends up erasing that difference. You can't say Halley's comet is misnamed, because there's nothing for the name to miss.

That conclusion strikes me as mad; the law was plainly onto something real, and "nobody discovered natural selection" is not the kind of statement I can live with, but I can't find the step where it goes off the rails. My nose says the trouble is somewhere around (4) to (6), but I can't make the diagnosis sharp. I'd like to keep (2) and the spirit of (3), and also to hang on to the idea that priority disputes aren't just performances. Which step do I reject, and what do I have to pay for the rejection? If you push back on (6), I want to know what that commits me to about vague predicates and scientific credit — and if you push back on (4), I want to know what principled cutoff does the work.
