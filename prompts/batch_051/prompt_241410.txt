I am a high school student who prepares research briefs for my debate team. Next month we are debating the motion "This House believes that Western anti-corruption standards should not be exported to societies with different gift-giving traditions," and our coach has been running practice rounds in which she gives a verdict on hypothetical scenarios about corruption and clientelism. I have kept a record of her verdicts, and I would like to predict how she would rule on a new case that we have not discussed.

Her earlier verdicts were as follows:

1. A mayor awards a no-bid construction contract to a company whose owner had quietly paid for the mayor's daughter's wedding two months before. — Corruption.
2. A week after a clerk approves a routine license, the former applicant sends the clerk a modest box of chocolates as thanks, with no further matters between them. — Not corruption.
3. In a society where it is customary to approach an official with a gift, a family brings the standard goat to a village chief the day before he is to hear their land dispute. — Corruption.
4. In a country where motorists routinely pay police officers a small "dash" at roadside checks, an officer accepts the customary amount from a driver and, in exchange, does not ticket the driver's expired insurance card. — Corruption.
5. A legislator is treated to an expensive holiday by a company while the company's tax break is before the legislature; the legislator later votes for the break and denies being influenced. — Corruption.
6. After a business owner makes heavy donations to an MP's campaign fund, the owner's firm receives a government grant for which it was technically eligible, and the MP's office later admits it urged officials to review the application ahead of others. — Corruption.
7. A councilor spends public funds on identical flood-relief packages in every ward, regardless of how each ward voted. — Not corruption.

The new case we have not discussed is this. In a rural constituency, an MP runs a year-round "relief network" funded by the MP's private resources and donor contributions. It distributes food, school fees, and help obtaining government documents. Records show that recipients are overwhelmingly people who attend the MP's rallies, canvass for the MP, or are otherwise known as loyalists, and the network expands during election campaigns. No one is ever explicitly told that the help is conditional on voting for the MP, but recipients appear to understand that continued help depends on showing political support. The practice is widely regarded in the constituency as how a good patron should behave, rather than as shameful or secret.

If the coach wants to be consistent with her earlier verdicts, what verdict should she give on the relief network — or do her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer? Please (a) state the principle or principles that best explain her earlier verdicts, (b) say what each plausible principle would require in the new case, (c) identify which earlier case is the closest analogy, and (d) propose a further hypothetical whose verdict would most cleanly separate the leading candidate principles. Then give your best prediction of her verdict, being explicit about where the prediction is forced by her precedent and where it depends on which principle she is committed to.
