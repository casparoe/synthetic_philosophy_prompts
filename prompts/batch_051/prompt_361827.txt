Tell me which step here is the first one that's false, because I've spun this out of Gödel and it feels like it proves way too much.

(1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says: any consistent formal system that can express basic arithmetic will contain a true but unprovable sentence.

(2) So take any formal theory T that's supposed to be a complete theory of truth for a domain that includes the natural numbers. By (1), T has a true sentence in its own language that it doesn't prove.

(3) A complete theory of truth for a domain, by definition, proves every true sentence stateable in its own language.

(4) So T is both complete and not complete. Therefore, no formal theory of truth for arithmetic can exist.

(5) And that means giving a formal semantics for natural language is hopeless as soon as the language can talk about numbers: there will always be a truth in the language that the semantics fails to capture.

I suspect the word 'true' is doing seperate jobs in (1) and (3), and maybe (5) is assuming a semantics has to be an axiomatic proof system. But I'd like to know which step actually breaks first, and whether there's a fixed-up version of (1) that makes the argument valid but forces a much more modest conclusion.
