I help run a reading group on scripture, and last week we spent the whole session arguing about where responsible numerical interpretation ends and pattern-hunting begins. We eventually agreed on four tests for calling a numerical observation a genuine symbolic reading rather than a retrospective discovery: (1) the text has to mark or foreground the number itself; (2) any arithmetic used must be conservative — a single, obvious operation on numbers that already function symbolically in the same text; (3) the resulting meaning must fit the plain sense of the passage; and (4) there must be some reason to think the author or original community could plausibly have possessed the count or the computation, rather than its depending on modern notation, numbering systems, or search algorithms.

One case came up repeatedly and split the group. In the Gospel of John's account of the miraculous catch, the haul is specified as 153 large fish. The main proposal was that 153 is a triangular number — the sum of the integers 1 through 17 — and that 17 is itself 10 plus 7, with 7 functioning throughout the text as the number of completeness. On that reading, 153 is a compressed way of saying complete completeness: the overflowing catch gathered in, too full to enumerate without a symbolic total. A skeptic in the group replied that this is no better than the much later observation that 153 equals 1³ + 5³ + 3³, which is just arithmetic cleverness with the digits and could not have been the author's intention. A third person pointed out that if we are not careful we will also be impressed by the fact that the chapter is number 21, and 21 is 3 × 7 — even though the chapter divisions were added long after the text was written.

Run the triangular-number reading of 153 through the four tests, criterion by criterion. Does it qualify as interpretation, as pattern-hunting, or somewhere in between? In particular, I want to know whether criterion (4) is doing the real work here, and whether a rule like "conservative arithmetic only" can actually separate the two activities — or whether the distinction itself starts to blur once the arithmetic is old enough.
