I have spent my career in the natural sciences and have only recently begun reading philosophy systematically, so I ask you to introduce and define any technical vocabulary you use rather than assuming I already have it. I have been puzzled by a pattern in American law and regulation, present across several otherwise unrelated areas, and I would like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments that pattern presupposes.

Consider the following practices:

- A person may donate a kidney but may not sell one. Even a fully informed, healthy adult who locates a willing buyer and a competent surgeon is barred from putting a price on the organ, and offering to buy or sell a kidney is a federal offense. Yet the same society allows people to be paid for blood plasma, while whole blood used for transfusion is almost always collected from unpaid donors.
- During and after disasters, many states make it illegal to charge prices far above pre-disaster levels for necessities such as water, fuel, hotel rooms, and batteries. The prohibition applies even when buyer and seller both agree to the price and the high price would draw new supply into the affected area.
- Buying or selling a vote is a crime, treated as the corruption of a public duty, even though spending large sums on campaigns and lobbying in ways intended to influence political outcomes is generally treated as lawful.
- Paying a woman to carry a pregnancy is an enforceable contract in some American states and a crime in others, although the underlying medical procedure and the parties' relationship are essentially the same; the line is drawn precisely at whether money is exchanged.

In the rest of the economy — groceries, labor, housing, software — the working assumption is that if a transaction is voluntary, mutually beneficial, and harms no third party, there is nothing wrong with putting a price on something and letting the parties agree. My question is not primarily whether the prohibitions above are justified, though your answer should note where a reconstruction has normative consequences. Rather, I want to know: what view or views of value, consent, commodification, and the point of market exchange does this patchwork of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as the practices allow. Then distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely fit well with, and mark the places where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same legal rule. Finally, tell me what would have to change, in law, in institutions, or in our self-understanding, if these practices were replaced by the single principle that informed adult consent plus the absence of direct third-party harm is sufficient for a permissible sale.

I would rather have an answer that tells me the request is confused than one that forces coherence onto an incoherent subject. If the task as posed cannot be done well — if, for example, these practices are too heterogeneous to bear one reconstructed theory, or if the notion of what a practice presupposes is itself the problem — say so plainly and tell me what the better question would be. Please write the answer in American English.
