I'm grading a short assignment in an upper-level bioethics seminar on advance directives. The question I set asked students to work with the section of Ronald Dworkin's Life's Dominion where he discusses Margo, a woman with Alzheimer's who gets obvious pleasure from activities like reading detective novels and painting, even though she cannot remember these pleasures later and can no longer plan her own life. The background I gave them was roughly this: Dworkin distinguishes between critical interests—interests about what makes a life genuinely good as a whole—and experiential interests, which are just interests in enjoying whatever is happening right now.

Here is the question and one student's answer.

Question: Explain how Dworkin uses the distinction between critical and experiential interests to argue that a directive written by Margo's earlier, competent self should generally be honored even if Margo now seems content and resists the treatment. Then say whether his argument actually requires either of the following claims: (a) that Margo's current pleasures have no moral weight at all, or (b) that the later Margo is a different person from the one who signed the directive.

Student answer: "Dworkin says critical interests reflect our values about how our life as a whole should go, while experiential interests are just present enjoyments. In dementia, the patient can still have experiential interests but has lost the capacity to form or revise critical interests. An advance directive is the expression of the earlier self's critical interests. Respecting autonomy therefore means letting the competent earlier self set the terms for the later incompetent stage, because critical interests are what make a life valuable. This shows that Margo's current enjoyment of her painting has no moral weight at all, under Dworkin's view. It also only works if we regard the later Margo as a different person: the earlier person's autonomy is being respected, and the later person has no separate claim because her experiential interests don't count."

Could you grade this? Give a mark out of 10 and the kind of point-by-point comments that justify the mark. I care more about the comments than the number: where does the student get things right, where exactly do they go wrong, and what would a correct version of the questionable parts say?
