I am building a unit for a philosophy of law course on whether it is ever fair to bind people to rules they could not have known. Please write a decision tree — a “which position do you actually hold?” flowchart, not a prose survey — that sorts respondents into positions on this question by what they say about cases and claims, and that takes as its anchor this passage from Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II, q. 90, a. 4:

“I answer that, As stated above (A[1]), a law is imposed on others by way of a rule and measure. Now a rule or measure is imposed by being applied to those who are to be ruled and measured by it. Wherefore, in order that a law obtain the binding force which is proper to a law, it must needs be applied to the men who have to be ruled by it. Such application is made by its being notified to them by promulgation. Wherefore promulgation is necessary for the law to obtain its force.”

In the same article, Aquinas meets the objection that the natural law needs no promulgation by writing: “The natural law is promulgated by the very fact that God instilled it into man’s mind so as to be known by him naturally.”

The tree’s first branch must test a respondent’s reading of the first quoted claim: is failure to make a rule knowable a failure of validity — the rule never becomes a law at all — or a failure of bindingness in the case at hand — the rule is a law but cannot fairly be applied to this person? After that split, build the tree from the logical space of the following diagnostic questions, each phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim, never as “do you accept position X?”: a city council enacts a curfew but an administrative error leaves it unpublished anywhere, and then tries to enforce it; does a rule bind someone only when they actually know it, or also when it has been published and could in the ordinary course reach them, or whenever a competent authority enacted it; does ignorance excuse differently when the act is wrong independently of the rule (*malum in se*) versus wrong only because the rule says so (*malum prohibitum*); should invincible ignorance negate liability outright or merely reduce the penalty; and is the point of excusing ignorance fairness to the offender or improved future rule-following.

The leaves must include these six positions, identified purely by the pattern of answers that reaches them: promulgation validity (an unknowable rule is not a law at all); natural-law dualism (some norms are promulgated through nature and known by reason, so ignorance of them does not excuse, while positive human rules bind only if promulgated); strict no-excuse (a duly enacted rule binds and can ground liability even against someone who could not have known it); fair-notice proceduralism (ignorance excuses only when the legal system failed its own publicity standards, not when a published rule went unread); fault-based (punishment is for culpable choice, so reasonable, unavoidable ignorance excuses even a valid, published rule, while negligent failure to inform oneself does not); and instrumental (ignorance is excused exactly when excusing it produces better compliance or welfare than punishing it).

For every combination of node answers not taken by these six, add an unnamed or undefended leaf and say briefly whether the position is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate and state what further question would be needed. Keep the whole instrument to roughly six to eight leaves.
