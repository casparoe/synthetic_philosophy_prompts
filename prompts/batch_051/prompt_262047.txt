I've basically landed on the view that Lewis's triviality results are close to decisive against the idea that indicative conditionals express ordinary propositions. The intuitive starting point — that the probability of 'if A then C' is just the conditional probability of C given A — feels so unshakeable that for a while I assumed the formal blow-up had to be a trick of the framing. But now I read the results as showing something robust: if the conditional expresses a proposition, probabilities behave classically, and the Equation holds across arbitrary background conditions, the whole thing collapses into triviality. So the moral I've drawn is that indicatives either don't express classical Boolean propositions, or the Equation has to be given a non-propositional reading (Adams-style, dynamic, restrictor, whatever).

The thing is, I worry I've let the result bully me into this view without being able to say exactly what it would take for me to be wrong. So: what would have to be true for the standard moral of Lewis triviality to fail? Lay out the assumptions the proofs actually lean on — not just the Equation, but the stuff doing quiet work, like closure under conditionalization, definability, Boolean closure, import-export, and what counts as a 'proposition' — and tell me which of those could in principle fail while the Equation survives in some recognizable form. Which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible? The non-propositional accounts, three-valued or partial logics, impossible worlds, rejecting import-export, restricting the Equation to factual antecedents, changing what 'probabilities' are? I want a ranked map of defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest verdict on whether the 'death of the Equation' reading of the results runs any real risk or has just been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that should bother me.
