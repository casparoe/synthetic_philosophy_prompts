I am a novelist drafting a novel about two moral philosophers, lifelong rivals, who are forced by the terms of a shared bequest to produce a single public statement on moral worth before either can touch the money. One defends the duty-first view: a right action has moral worth only when the agent performs it from the motive of duty — from a recognition that the action is required, rather than from inclination, self-interest, or an unendorsed feeling. The other defends the right-reasons view: a right action has moral worth when the agent performs it for the reasons that make the action right — the other person's need, the fact that a promise was made, the badness of a harm — whether or not the agent represents those considerations as moral requirements or arrives at them through deliberation about duty. The statement becomes the centerpiece of the novel, so it must read as something two competent, intellectually honest, and somewhat proud philosophers could actually sign.

Please write that statement: the document of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the duty-first and right-reasons positions on moral worth. It should be constructive rather than slanted, exact where exactness is possible, and candid about residual disagreement. Do not include citations or bibliography; this is a working document, not a literature review.

The document should include:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims about moral worth. These should be substantive rather than vacuous — cover, for example, the difference between moral worth and mere rightness; the fact that a right action can be done without moral worth; the relation between an agent's motivating reasons and what makes the action right; and the examples both parties treat as clear at the outset.

2. A disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give the exact duty-first claim, worded so a committed duty-first theorist would sign it; the exact right-reasons claim, worded so a committed right-reasons theorist would sign it; and a note on whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, and why.

3. A crux section: the sub-questions such that, if they were settled, at least one side would change its view. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it — preferably invented or drawn from ordinary life or fiction rather than the professional literature — and record in advance what each side would predict the case shows, and what each side would accept as evidence against its own view.

The crux section should address at least these: whether the mediating thought that an action is required is necessary for moral worth, or whether being directly moved by the right-making features can suffice; whether the counterfactual robustness of a motive — how the agent would have acted had the feeling or inclination been absent — is a reliable test of worth; and whether moral worth is all-or-nothing or admits of degrees.

Do not quietly presuppose that either side has already won. If part of their dispute is merely verbal, say which part and explain why, rather than manufacturing a substantive disagreement.
