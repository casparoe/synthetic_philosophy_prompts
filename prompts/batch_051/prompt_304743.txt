You are a philosopher of language with a working linguist's knowledge of writing systems. Think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming the distinct things a script might in principle be a notation for — sounds, meaning-bearing units, words, etymologies, lexical contrasts, prosody — before settling on your reconstruction.

Consider the following ordinary practice. Standard written English consistently spells the same meaningful unit the same way even where pronunciation shifts across related words: the <g> of *sign* is kept in *signature* though it is pronounced only in the latter; the <c> that ends *electric* is kept in *electricity*, where it is pronounced as /s/; the plural marker is written *-(e)s* in *cats*, *dogs*, and *horses* though it is pronounced /s/, /z/, and /ɪz/. The system lets the eye see that these are the same morpheme even when the ear cannot. At the same time it does not go all the way: *right*, *write*, and *rite* are spelled differently purely to keep distinct morphemes apart, and *knight* fossilizes pronunciations that have long since been lost. Even where the system looks sound-based, it makes a theoretical cut: it writes the aspirated [pʰ] of *pit* and the unaspirated [p] of *spit* with the same <p>, treating that difference as not part of what the language really is, whereas in a language where aspiration distinguishes words the same difference would have to be written with different letters.

The question: a script embodies a theory of what language is, because it must decide which differences in the speech stream are worth preserving and at what grain. Reconstruct the implicit theory of language that the English practice just described presupposes. Be precise about what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it is merely compatible with; say whether its various parts presuppose one consistent theory or several; and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories would license the same conventions. Then take the phonemic principle — roughly, that an alphabet should assign one symbol per phoneme and one phoneme per symbol — and suppose we accepted it as the whole truth and reformed English spelling accordingly. What would the resulting script lose, and what theory of language would that reformed practice itself presuppose? In particular, would a perfectly phonemic script genuinely be only a notation for sound, or would it still have to make the same kind of theoretical decisions at a finer grain?

I am not asking for a history of English orthography or a plan for actual spelling reform; I am asking you to make explicit the theory of language that the existing conventions already commit a writer to.
