In heuristic number theory, a standard move is to treat the prime indicator sequence as if it were produced by independent biased coin flips: the coin at each integer n falls prime with probability about 1/log n, and the outcomes at different n are taken to be independent. Calibrated against the prime number theorem, this model yields the right order of growth for the density of twin primes, for Goldbach representations, and for prime gaps; once corrected for local congruence obstructions, it matches the fine-grained counts to an accuracy that nobody has yet explained rigorously. A number-theory colleague of mine leans on this analogy to defend a broader epistemic habit: he treats well-tested probabilistic heuristics as licensing rational near-certainty in unproved statements such as the twin-prime conjecture. Audit that analogy as an argument for that habit. Give the correspondence table — what stands for the coin, the flip, independence, the probability parameter, the sequence, and the model's empirical tests — and then identify the disanalogies: the ways arithmetic structure, small primes, local congruence obstructions, and possible scale-dependent clustering make the real prime sequence unlike a sequence of coin flips. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from the merely cosmetic ones, and say which features of coin-flip sequences (independence, stationarity, memorylessness, absence of arithmetic constraints) are being quietly imported and are doing the real work. Finally, render a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the random-coin analogy actually support treating an unproved conjecture as something like certain, or was it carrying epistemic weight it cannot bear? If a better analogy would do better, describe it and what it repairs.
