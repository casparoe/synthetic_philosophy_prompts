Okay, so my friend Maya and I spent most of last night arguing about idolatry — what makes valuing something a case of idolatry or misplaced worship, as opposed to just loving it a lot or having an intense hobby. Maya's an atheist, but she takes idolatry really seriously as a category of misdirected love, not just a religious sin. She has this whole track record of verdicts on cases from years of arguments, and I'm trying to figure out if she's actually consistent or whether my line in the fight was right and she's just going on vibes.

If you ask Maya for her principle, she'll say idolatry is about a confusion: you mistake the thing for something it isn't — a statue for a god, a means for an end, a symbol for what it symbolizes. But when she actually judges cases, a lot of her verdicts look like they're tracking whether the thing has taken over someone's life. She says I'm oversimplifying.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on value theory, idolatry, and the ethics of worship. Think carefully: brainstorm at least two candidate principles that could fit her track record, check each one against all the cases, and then give me your best read.

Her verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them:

1. The saint statue. An older woman keeps a bronze statue of a folk saint. She prays to the statue itself, not through it, and insists the metal can hear her because it's been blessed. The rituals have taken over her days and she's stopped going to family gatherings. Maya: idolatry, no hesitation.

2. The widow's photograph. A widow talks to a photo of her dead husband every night, kisses it, and says she knows perfectly well it's just paper, but it helps her remember the sound of his voice. The ritual doesn't crowd out friends or the rest of her life. Maya: not idolatry — grief, not worship.

3. The astronomer. A man says the moon is the only thing that has ever made him feel reverence, and he spends every clear night with his telescope, but he knows it's just rock and his job and marriage are fine. Maya: not idolatry. She says the moon is an appropriate object of awe, and he isn't wrong about what it is.

4. The balance. After a bankruptcy, a man starts treating his checking account like a judging presence — he talks about the number being disappointed in him or pleased with him, checks it hourly, and has let friendships lapse because evenings out feel like betraying it. When pressed, he can't bring himself to say the balance is nothing but a number. Maya: idolatry, and she said the mistake about what the number is is exactly the kind of thing she means.

5. The flag. A man treats the flag as if the actual cloth carries the country's honor, stopped speaking to his daughter after she burned one at a protest, and says the flag has to come before every individual relationship. Maya: idolatry.

6. The fangirl. A teenager has her walls covered with pictures of a singer, knows every b-side, waits at airports; she's clear that the singer is a stranger with flaws, and she still does school and sees her friends. Maya: not idolatry. The intensity isn't the problem, she says.

Now here's the new case that started the fight. My cousin rebuilt a motorcycle over about four years, and it's become the center of his life. He's not confused about it at all — he'll say its metal and rubber, it'll rust, it doesn't care about him, and he doesn't use any sacred or cosmic language about it. But he skipped his daughter's college graduation to go to a rally, he says if he ever had to choose between the bike and staying in touch with his family the bike wins, and he plans his week around riding and maintaining it. When I said the bike was his idol, he laughed and said he's the least idolatrous person he knows, since he doesn't believe the bike is anything more than a bike.

What verdict should Maya give on the cousin if she wants to be consistent with her own track record? Which earlier case is the closest analog? And if her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, be honest about that: say what principle she'd be adopting with each possible verdict, give your best guess about which principle fits her track record better, and describe one further case that would cleanly separate the candidate principles.
