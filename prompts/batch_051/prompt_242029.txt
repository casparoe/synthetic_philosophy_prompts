I'm a novelist, working on a book that spans three generations of a family in a country that did something terrible before most of the current characters were born, and I keep hitting the same wall: I need a vocabulary for guilt that can stick to people who were not there. I've been reading Jaspers's The Question of German Guilt, and he gives four distinctions. As I understand them:

(1) Criminal guilt: breaking a law, something a court can prove and punish.
(2) Political guilt: being co-responsible for what your state does, just by belonging to it and living under its rule; he thinks all citizens bear this, and its forum is the victor or whoever holds the state to account.
(3) Moral guilt: guilt for your own actions, including things you did under orders, judged by your own conscience.
(4) Metaphysical guilt: the solidarity claim — that you should have risked your life to stop an atrocity, and that surviving while others died leaves a kind of debt before God.

I know Jaspers himself mostly resisted saying a whole nation is criminally or morally guilty. So my problem isnt that I want some slogan about collective guilt; it's that I want something he didn't list: a fifth kind of guilt that can pass down to characters born decades later. It shouldn't be a rewording of any of the four, and it shouldn't be just a mixture of political and metaphysical guilt stretched across time. It has to do actual narrative work — it should explain why a granddaughter in the present-day thread feels implicated even though she broke no law, holds no office, didn't personally betray anyone, and wasnt alive to fail to intervene.

So: give me such a fifth category, describe it clearly enough that I could hang it on a character, and then tell me honestly whether it's any good — what it captures that Jaspers's four don't, or whether it collapses back into one of them.
