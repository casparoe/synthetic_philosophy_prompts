I follow a policy in arguments about the long-term future of large systems, and I want to know whether it is defensible or just a bias wearing a quantitative costume. When someone predicts that a state, empire, alliance system, or constitutional order will survive or collapse over the coming century, I refuse to engage with the particular story until a base rate is on the table. I ask what happened to the most comparable cases in the past, treat that rate as my prior, and move off it only for features that can be shown to have distinguished survivors from failures within the reference class. Features that merely make the present case feel unique do not move me.

Last week a friend argued that the current international order is more durable than past great-power orders because its leading state is a democracy, its main rivals are tied into the same trading system, and nuclear weapons have made great-power war too costly. I replied that every declining power had sophisticated people explaining why their case was structurally different, and that the base rate for great-power orders over a two-century horizon is not encouraging. I added that the this-time-is-different move carries no weight unless he can point to a mechanism that actually predicts survival in the chosen reference class, not just a reason the present feels special.

But I can see that this policy might let me sound rigorous while doing no real work. I could gerrymander the reference class to get the base rate I want, ignore the fact that historical cases are not independent draws, or dismiss a genuinely novel mechanism simply because it has no precedent. How should I tell, from the inside, whether I am using the outside view correctly or merely invoking a base rate I have not actually constructed? And is there a defensible version of my rule, stated precisely enough that I could follow it rather than use it to end conversations?
