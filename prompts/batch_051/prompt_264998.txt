I'm trying to work out what I actually think about side effects in agent design, by collecting my gut verdicts on cases before settling on a definition. A delivery robot that smashes a vase while retrieving a package: that is a side effect, and a significant one, even though preserving the vase was never specified. The same robot leaving temporary wheel marks on wet grass: a side effect, but only mildly bad. The same robot passing through a camera's field of view on an unmonitored feed, so that some footage now differs from how it would have been: I don't count that as a side effect at all. A robot that buys electricity from a slightly different supplier, so that the money ends up with a different wholesaler: I don't count that as a side effect either. A robot that switches on a hallway light to navigate and leaves it on: not a side effect. But the same robot nudging a book out of its way and leaving the book on the dining table: a side effect, though trivially reversible. A robot that moves a chair, stands on it, and puts it back: not a side effect; the same robot leaving the chair blocking the doorway: a side effect. And a cleaning robot that, after wiping the counter as asked, also dusts the shelf above it on its own initiative: I count that as a side effect, but not one worth penalizing. Is there a principle that gets all or most of these? Please state the best candidate principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on — a robot that, to plan a delivery route, reads the mail on the counter and reseals it so that no person's beliefs about the mail change. Finally, say whether the resulting principle allows side-effect avoidance to be defined without a full specification of value, or whether it must quietly build in some notion of what matters.
