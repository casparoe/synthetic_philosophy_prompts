Consider a counterfactual world in which Good's theorem fails for one well-defined class of choice problems. In our world, for any Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, a cost-free and perfectly reliable signal cannot lower expected utility, because the agent can always ignore the signal and choose the prior-optimal act. Now suppose that in the counterfactual world there is a class of propositions—call them decision-reflexive propositions—with the following lawlike property: when an agent conditionalizes on such a proposition, the act that would have maximized expected utility under the prior is no longer in the feasible set, and no compensating act is added. The proposition tells the agent, correctly, which option he would have taken had he not received the information, and the very act of entertaining that proposition removes that option from the choice set. For these propositions, free information can lower expected utility in just the way that ordinary information cannot in our world.

Assume that the population consists of otherwise idealized Bayesian decision-makers, that decision-reflexive propositions arise constantly in commercial, legal, scientific, and intimate contexts, and that their occurrence is a stable feature of the world rather than a quirk of limited reasoning. Give a reasoned prediction of how such a society would differ from ours over a century or more. Begin with individual incentives to acquire, avoid, or precommit against information, then trace the effects through institutional design: secrecy and plausible deniability in contracts, rules of evidence and compelled disclosure, experimental practice in science, education in self-knowledge, and the regulation of markets that sell information. Distinguish the consequences that follow robustly from the specified failure of the value-of-information inequality from those that depend on auxiliary assumptions about, for example, whether agents can credibly commit to ignoring information. The answer should be a chain of reasoning about mechanisms, incentives, and constraints, not a story, and it should state explicitly where each link is reliable and where it is speculative.
