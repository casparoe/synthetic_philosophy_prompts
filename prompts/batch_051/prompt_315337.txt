Last night I had a long argument with a friend about how one should choose when probabilities are genuinely unavailable. She defended an uncompromising maximin rule: always pick the act whose worst possible outcome is best, and do not let the best possible outcome move you. I found myself agreeing with her on some cases and resisting her on others, so I want to work out what principle, if any, captures my actual reactions. In each case below, assume I genuinely have no basis for assigning probabilities to the possible outcomes.

1. The startup. I must choose between two job offers. The established firm would pay me $70,000 regardless of how things go. The startup would pay $120,000 if its next funding round succeeds and $40,000 if it fails, and I have no way to estimate which is more likely. I take the startup.

2. The drug. I have a chronic illness. Drug A would leave me for certain at a limited but livable condition that I would rate 6/10. Drug B might restore me to 10/10 or leave me permanently at 1/10, and I have no data on which. I stay on Drug A.

3. The crossing. I am in a country where, if I stay, I will certainly be imprisoned for many years in conditions I would rate about 2/10. If I attempt the border crossing, either I make it and recover a full life, which I would rate 10/10, or I am caught and killed, 0/10. I cannot assign odds to the crossing. I attempt it.

4. The savings. I have $20,000, which is exactly enough to keep me housed and current on my debts. I can keep it in cash, or put all of it into a venture that will either return $100,000 or lose everything. I have no basis for odds. I keep the cash.

5. The two ventures. I must choose between two projects, neither of which offers a guaranteed payoff. Project A will return either $25,000 or $30,000. Project B will return either $5,000 or $100,000. No probabilities. I choose Project B.

My verdicts in (1), (3), and (5) look to me like minimax regret rather than maximin; (2) sits comfortably with both; and (4) looks like maximin reasserting itself against minimax regret. Is there a single rule — a repaired maximin, a version of minimax regret, or something else — that gets all five verdicts without being absurdly ad hoc? Please state the principle precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, test it on at least one further case of your own invention and report its verdict, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have to overturn.

I would also like your sense, if only briefly, of whether this bears on the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute. My maximin friend kept appealing to the thought that, behind the veil of ignorance, one should maximize the position of the worst off. Our other friend replied that, behind the veil, one should give equal probability to each position and maximize expected utility. Does the principle that fits my five cases take a side in that argument, or are my personal verdicts simply the wrong kind of evidence for it?
