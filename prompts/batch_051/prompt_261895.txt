I cover science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about what Twelve Angry Men can show us about the epistemology of peer disagreement. Read the film through that debate: conciliationists hold that when someone who seems as competent and informed as you reaches a different conclusion, you should move substantially toward their view, while steadfasters hold that you may rationally keep your own judgment. The film begins with Juror 8 as the lone not-guilty vote against eleven and ends in unanimous acquittal, which looks like a clean vindication of steadfastness. Yet I suspect it earns that ending only by denying that the other jurors are genuine epistemic peers — several are bigoted, impatient, or deferential, while the holdout is the one actually re-examining the evidence. I want a close reading of the deliberation, anchored in specific scenes as the votes turn, that separates the moments where the film genuinely favors holding fast from those where it rigs the comparison, and that says what the film dramatizes about testimony, memory, and group pressure that the debate's standard examples leave out. Please also be explicit about what is settled in the peer-disagreement literature and what remains contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
