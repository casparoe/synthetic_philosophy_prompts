I'm taking an upper-division course on meaning in life, and we have just moved from Camus and the absurd to the contemporary debate among subjectivists, objectivists, and hybrid views. Rather than adopt a theory and then apply it, I have been trying to work backward from my own reactions to particular lives, and those reactions have turned out messier than I expected. Here they are, roughly as they feel to me:

- The grass counter lives forty years meticulously counting blades of grass in a single field. He is genuinely absorbed, and he reports that the task fills his days. I do not think his life is meaningful, or at most only barely.
- The failed researcher spends twenty-five years pursuing a vaccine that never works, and her notebooks are eventually destroyed. She believed the work mattered, found it gripping, and would do it again. I think her life was meaningful.
- The resentful donor gives away most of a large fortune to an effective malaria charity but feels no engagement or satisfaction in doing so; he acts purely from a duty he resents. I think his life is meaningful, but I also want to say that something is missing from it.
- The devoted parent raises a child with sustained love and attention and finds the whole thing deeply fulfilling. Clearly meaningful.
- The cheerful swindler is a con artist who loves his craft, is superb at it, and feels fulfilled by defrauding vulnerable people. I do not think his life is meaningful at all; if anything, it is the reverse.
- The locked-room painter produces serious, accomplished paintings and then burns each one so that nobody will ever see it, although painting is what she most loves doing. I keep going back and forth on this case, and it is the one I most want the principle to settle.

The pattern I half-see is that subjective engagement and some kind of objective worth both matter, but they do not weigh symmetrically: the grass counter has engagement without worth, and I deny that his life is meaningful; the resentful donor has worth without engagement, and I grant that his life is meaningful. That pushes me toward an objectivist view, yet the researcher makes me hesitate, because her work produced nothing of objective value — the value lived only in the trying. Is there a single principle that captures most of these verdicts, and what does it say about the locked-room painter? And what does it say about the absurdist version of the grass counter — someone who knows the task is pointless, holds no illusion about its worth, and still chooses it each morning?

Please answer as a professional philosopher working in value theory. Think carefully rather than giving the first view that occurs to you; it may help to begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before settling on one. Write in American English and structure the response under the following headings, in this order:

- The landscape (about 250 words): the main candidate principles — subjectivist, objectivist, hybrid, and absurdist — stated precisely enough to test against my cases.
- The best-fit principle (about 400 words): the principle you think captures the largest defensible subset of my verdicts, with each clause motivated by the case or pair of cases that forces it.
- Where my verdicts have to give (about 250 words): which of my reactions the principle cannot accommodate, and which one you would overturn first, with reasons.
- The painter and the Sisyphus (about 250 words): apply the principle to the locked-room painter and to the lucid Sisyphus figure, and say whether the principle distinguishes meaning from dignity or collapses them.
