I want to press on a conflict between a methodological principle I find compelling and a class of material evidence that I cannot persuade myself to explain away.

The principle is one I will call coherence-maximizing reconstruction. When we recover beliefs and institutions from material remains—building layouts, patterned deposits, worked objects, deliberately damaged images—the principle says we should prefer the interpretation that makes the makers and users come out as broadly reasonable and their material record as internally consistent. It counsels us to treat anomalies as invitations to reinterpret rather than as evidence of confusion. Its virtues are the virtues of a good method: it is unified, it supplies a decision procedure for adjudicating between rival readings, and it blocks the lazy conclusion that past people we do not immediately understand were acting irrationally or at random.

But I have a firm intuition that clashes with it. Some of the most charged evidence in the material record is evidence of break and repudiation: a statue whose face has been methodically chipped away before burial, a set of fine vessels smashed and scattered in a pattern that no ordinary use would produce, a name patiently erased from an inscription. It seems to me that the most faithful reading of such acts is that they record the cessation or reversal of something—the rejection of a ruler, a cult, an ancestor, a god, or an institution. The destruction is not a strange way of continuing to venerate the figure; it is the community's way of saying that the figure no longer warrants its former stance. If the method teaches me to reinterpret each such act until it becomes one more harmonious piece of an underlying system, then the method seems to purchase coherence by erasing the very fact the material record preserves so vividly: that communities can be fragmented, that institutions can be overthrown, and that rejection can be as structuring as assent. Would that amendment preserve what is valuable in the principle, or is it merely a softened version of the same error of trying to render the past charitably consistent?
