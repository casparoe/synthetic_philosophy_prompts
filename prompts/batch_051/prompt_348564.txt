I have been reading the chapter “Of Nationality, as connected with Representative Government” in John Stuart Mill’s *Considerations on Representative Government*, and I want to test a principle I take from it against my own reactions to a range of cases. As I understand Mill there, he holds that free institutions depend on the citizens’ willingness to accept being governed together, and that this fellow-feeling normally exists only among people who think of themselves as one nation. He seems to infer that a distinct nation has a prima facie claim to govern itself, and that a state containing several nationalities will find free government all but impossible because the citizens will not sufficiently trust or cooperate with one another across national lines. I want to know whether a principle of that kind captures my own verdicts, which are as follows.

My verdicts on the cases:

Scotland before the 2014 referendum. I think the referendum was legitimate because it was agreed with London and the result was respected, and I think a future clear vote for independence should be honoured. But I do not think Scotland had, or has, a right to walk out unilaterally without such an agreed process.

Catalonia in the 2017 referendum. I think the unilateral referendum was unlawful, the turnout in favour fell well short of a clear majority of the whole electorate, and the independence coalition has since lost its parliamentary majority. But I also think the Spanish state behaved badly in sending police against voters and in prosecuting organizers rather than offering a lawful route for the question to be asked. My verdict is that Catalonia’s claim to independence is not made out, while Madrid’s refusal to negotiate is also indefensible.

Quebec after the 1995 referendum. I think the near-miss made it reasonable for Canada to require a clear question and a clear majority before a future secession, and that a bare majority on an ambiguous question is not enough for an irreversible constitutional rupture. But I think Quebec has a genuine right to put the question again, and to leave if such a clear majority is obtained.

A wealthy region within a larger state that wishes to secede primarily so that it can keep its tax revenues rather than support poorer regions. I think this is not a valid claim; self-determination is not a right of the prosperous to shed obligations to the less prosperous.

A nation distributed among several existing states without a territorial majority in any one of them. I think its members have a real grievance, but the remedy is not necessarily a new state; autonomy, federalism, and minority rights may be the right end.

Switzerland, with its several language communities and no common nationality in the ethnic sense, which has nevertheless sustained free government for a long time. I think this shows Mill’s thesis must be overstated; free institutions do not require a shared nationality.

A city or sub-region inside a territory that is itself seeking independence, where the city’s majority opposes the secession and wants to remain in the original state. I think its self-determination matters, and the would-be seceding region cannot simply define the relevant “people” as its own majority.

These verdicts look inconsistent to me. The first and third cases seem to treat national distinctiveness as a source of a right to leave, while the fourth and seventh cases treat self-determination as something that can cut against the seceding majority. The sixth case seems to reject the very premise the first and third rely on, and the second looks as though it judges a similar national claim more harshly, perhaps only for procedural reasons. Is there a single coherent principle behind these verdicts, or a small set of candidate principles? State each candidate precisely, with the case or cases that motivate each clause, and say what it implies about further cases: a region with a clear, sustained, lawful majority for independence but no distinct language or history; a region with a distinct national character but only a bare or uncertain majority; a multinational state that is stable and free but contains one region with a plausible claim to statehood; a secession that would leave the original state economically unviable; and a region seeking independence after the central state has repeatedly violated an agreed autonomy arrangement. Finally, say exactly which of my verdicts each candidate principle cannot accommodate, and which part of the Mill-inspired principle has to be abandoned or repaired.
