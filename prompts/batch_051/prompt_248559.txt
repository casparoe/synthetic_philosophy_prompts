Okay, I'm on the committee putting together this year's PhD comp in moral philosophy, and I've drawn the question on blame. I want it to be about standing to blame and hypocrisy, but I don't want yet another essay that just rehearse the familiar point that a hypocrite's accusation can be true even though he's the wrong person to make it. The question should force the candidate to commit to an account of what standing actually is: a property of the speaker (their consistency, sincerity, or own track record), a property of the speech act or the blame itself (whether it's apt and directed at a real wrong), or a property of the relationship between blamer and blamed (something about authority or stake in the wrong).

The setup I've been kicking around with a colleague: a factory manager who knowingly dumped waste for years, got caught, served time, paid restitution, and now runs a public campaign blaming a rival firm for doing the identical thing. Does he have standing to blame them? Is it relevant that he's reformed, or that he's been punished, or does the bare fact that he did the very thing he's blaming them for make it impossible for him to blame well? Draft the comp question around some case like that (or a better one you invent), for a 90-minute answer, 20 marks total. Give me a model answer and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how candidates typicaly mess each one up. And please make the marking scheme penalize the mistake of treating hypocrisy as if it made the blame false or the target less blameworthy, rather than changing something about the blamer's position.
