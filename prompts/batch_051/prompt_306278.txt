I am a philosophy professor preparing a paper for next month's law-and-philosophy workshop. The paper defends the claim that, once an employer, insurer, or platform can reliably infer and act on a person's occurrent mental states — attention, fatigue, pain, preference — from brain–computer interface data, the central wrong is not best characterized as a privacy violation but as a forfeiture of first-person authority; cognitive liberty, on my view, should therefore be understood not as a bare negative right against neural interference but as the standing to determine who may treat one's self-reports as defeasible in light of neural data. The audience will be mostly privacy-law scholars who believe the sensible remedy for new sensors is to extend existing privacy and biometrics doctrine, joined by two neuroethicists active in the neurorights debate and one clinical BCI engineer who will press on feasibility and calibration. Please write me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this particular audience would put them, each with a reply I can deliver in under two minutes — indicating what I should concede and what I should hold — and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the one question I should hope nobody asks. Keep the entire prep sheet to no more than 1,300 words.
