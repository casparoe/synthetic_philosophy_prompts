I am taking a graduate seminar in the philosophy of computation, and our last session on the Church-Turing thesis left me more unsettled than anything else in the course. One student argued that the thesis merely provides an explication of the informal notion of an effective procedure, so it cannot be refuted by any discovery about new kinds of machines. Another argued that, read as a claim about what any physically realizable device could compute, it is a substantive empirical proposition, and that exotic proposals such as Malament-Hogarth spacetimes or certain idealized analog computers at least show that the limits of physical computation are not as settled as the standard story suggests. We could not agree on where conceptual stipulation ends and empirical commitment begins.

Write an essay in which you take on the role of a professional philosopher of computation and settle the question I have been turning over. Start by briefly brainstorming the distinctions you need: in particular, the difference between a mathematical claim about effective procedures and a physical claim about what processes in the actual world can compute, plus any further distinctions that seem relevant. Consider, for example, whether the convergence of many independent formalisms—Turing machines, the lambda calculus, general recursive functions—on the same class of functions is itself good evidence for the thesis, or only evidence that a vague informal notion has been successfully precisified. Then present the strongest arguments for treating the Church-Turing thesis as a definitional or conceptual truth, and the strongest arguments for treating it as an empirical hypothesis or as something revisable. Weigh those arguments against one another and defend a definite verdict about the epistemic status of the thesis and about which formulation, if any, deserves to be called true. If you defend a hybrid position, specify exactly which component has which status rather than saying it is partly both and leaving it there.

End the essay by stating how confident you are in your central claims, and by singling out the single point on which you are least confident and explaining why.
