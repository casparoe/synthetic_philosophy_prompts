I am a lecturer in statutory interpretation, and I have been trying to give my students a defensible test for when a court may decline to apply the literal words of a statutory provision because the result would be absurd. The working test I teach has four conditions, all of which I currently tell my students must be satisfied:

1. The literal reading must produce a result that no reasonable legislator, giving the language its natural meaning and knowing the evident purpose of the provision, could have intended.
2. The result must undermine the provision's central purpose; a merely suboptimal, harsh, or partially effective result is not enough.
3. The statutory language must be capable of bearing the proposed corrective reading; the court may not, under cover of avoiding absurdity, amend the text.
4. The departure must be limited and judicial; it must not require the court to choose among open-ended policy alternatives or to revalue a figure, date, or threshold that the legislature fixed.

I have drafted the following fictional case as an exercise for my students, and I now think it is genuinely borderline under my own test.

A common-law jurisdiction has in force the (fictional) Clean Waterways Act 1901. Section 12 makes it an offence to 'cause or permit any polluting matter to enter a waterway' and provides for 'a fine not exceeding one hundred pounds for each day on which the contravention occurs'. The maximum has never been amended or indexed, and the daily figure is now trivial by comparison with the cost of compliance. A manufacturing company allows effluent to enter a waterway on each of 400 consecutive days. It could have prevented the discharge by installing filtration plant at a one-off cost of £2,000,000. Its directors work out that the maximum penalty is £100 per day, and therefore £40,000 for the whole period, decide that paying the maximum is cheaper than complying, and record in an internal minute that the fine will be treated as an operating cost. Assume, for the purposes of the exercise, that no other enforcement power is available to the regulator. The regulator prosecutes and asks the court not to apply the statutory maximum, on the ground that applying it would turn a prohibition backed by a penalty into a price for polluting. The company replies that the words are plain, and that if the fixed amount is now too low the remedy lies with the legislature rather than the court.

Go through the four conditions in turn and say whether the case satisfies each of them. Identify the points at which the conditions underdetermine the answer, and say whether the case shows that any of the four needs to be revised, added to, or abandoned. I want the reasoning, not citations or a survey of the literature.

Please write your answer in British English.
