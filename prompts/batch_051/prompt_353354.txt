Much of game theory and information economics rests on the assumption that certain facts—the rules of the game, the rationality of the players, the public nature of an announcement—are common knowledge: everyone knows them, everyone knows that everyone knows them, and so on without bound. A skeptical worry, however, is that this infinite hierarchy may be observationally empty. Any particular choice problem makes only finite demands on beliefs about beliefs about beliefs; beyond some level, further iterations of *she knows that I know that she knows* appear to be behaviorally inert. If that is right, a group that genuinely satisfied common knowledge and a group that merely shared beliefs out to some sufficiently high but finite level could produce identical behavior in every experiment we could run.

Treat the following as the claim to be investigated.

**Claim.** Common knowledge—of the structure of a game or of the players' rationality—is not a state that actual agents can properly be said to enter; it is an idealization in the modeler's description. No finite body of experimental data could distinguish genuine common knowledge from sufficiently deep finite-order mutual belief, because the infinite hierarchy has no behavioral signature of its own.

Your task is to test this claim as far as testing can go. Choose one concrete interactive setting and specify it precisely enough that a laboratory could implement it. Examples include: a public announcement of the rules followed by a coordination game; a p-beauty contest in which each player submits a number and the winner is closest to two-thirds of the average; a game whose standard solution depends on iterated deletion of dominated strategies; or a betting setting in which two agents who share a prior and have common knowledge of each other's posterior beliefs should decline to take opposite sides of a wager. The design should state the players, payoffs, information, the sequence of public events, and at least one treatment or measurement meant to separate the claim from its denial. You may use choices, incentive-compatible predictions of other players' choices, response times, or subjects' written accounts of their reasoning, but indicate for each measure what it licenses and what it merely suggests.

Then answer the following questions. What pattern of results would favor the claim, and what pattern would favor the denial—the view that common knowledge does have a behavioral signature of its own? What does even the best version of the test fail to settle, and why? Is the residue a practical limit of measurement, or a defect in the notion of common knowledge itself? End by judging whether the claim survives contact with the proposed test.

Structure the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. The claim under test (about 120 words). Restate the claim and distinguish it from nearby claims, such as the claim that people are irrational or that rationality is rare.

2. The test setting and design (about 350 words). Specify the game or situation, the public events meant to induce common knowledge, the conditions varied, and the measurements taken.

3. What would favor the claim (about 180 words). Describe the result that would support the view that common knowledge is a behaviorally inert idealization, and state the inference that result licenses.

4. What would favor the denial (about 180 words). Describe the result that would support the view that common knowledge carries a distinct behavioral signature, and state how far that result would generalize.

5. What the test cannot settle (about 200 words). State the residue that remains even under the best design, and judge whether the untestable part matters for the use of common knowledge in game theory or is harmless idealization.

6. Verdict (about 150 words). Offer your own assessment of the claim in light of the proposed test.
