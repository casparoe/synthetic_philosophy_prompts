I am trying to see whether my settled judgments about immoral artists and their work express a single defensible principle, because on their face some of them conflict. Here are the cases.

1. A formally accomplished and psychologically astute novel is later traced to an author who committed a serious assault years before it was written; nothing in the novel draws on or alludes to that wrongdoing. I judge the novel to be excellent.
2. A painter who is known to have been a domestic abuser produced serene, impersonal landscapes. I judge the paintings themselves to be good, though I understand why someone would not want to hang them.
3. A comedian's act consists almost entirely in mocking members of a vulnerable group, and the comedian is also cruel to staff offstage. I judge this not only morally indefensible but bad as comedy.
4. A stylistically dazzling memoir by a person who caused real harm is written to justify himself and to seduce the reader into forgiving him. I cannot count it good literature, however accomplished the prose.
5. A performance artist stages a piece that inflicts real, non-consensual harm on a participant. I do not count it good art, whatever its conceptual interest.
6. A filmmaker who abused someone during production directs a subtle film about remorse. I find the film powerful, but the knowledge of the abuse sits inside the film for me: the remorse on screen feels hollow, and I doubt the film deserves the aesthetic credit it would otherwise have.

Cases 1 and 6 trouble me most, because both are good works by bad people, yet I judge them differently. Is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or most of them? If not, which of my verdicts does a defensible view force me to revise, and why? Finally, what does that principle say about a case I remain undecided on: a novel whose moral outlook I genuinely admire, written by an author who has done something seriously wrong outside the work, where other readers knew of the wrong but I did not?
