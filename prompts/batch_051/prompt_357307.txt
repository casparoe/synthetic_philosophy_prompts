I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a characterization problem. My protagonist, Nessa, sits on a national panel that votes on whether to license genetic biocontrol and de-extinction projects, and I've already drafted five votes from her record. I want her next vote to feel like it comes from one actual person, not whichever side the plot needs. So I'm hoping you can reverse-engineer the principle she's been running on and then predict her.

Her record, as I've sketched it:

1. Ravatu rats. Release a gene drive on one small uninhabited atoll to crash the invasive rat population so the ground-nesting seabirds come back. The drive was engineered to burn itself out after about fifteen generations and had no realistic way off the atoll. Nessa voted yes.

2. Malaria mosquitoes. Release a self-propagating gene drive into malaria mosquitoes across much of sub-Saharan Africa so they stop transmitting the disease. The applicants admitted it would cross borders and couldn't be recalled. Nessa voted no, even though she said the public-health case was overwhelming.

3. The tree-dove. Bring back a tree-dove that died out in the 1980s on one island, using plain captive breeding and reintroduction, to restore seed dispersal for the native trees that depended on it. She voted yes.

4. Mammoth steppe. De-extinct woolly mammoths and turn them loose in Siberia to convert tundra back to grassland. She voted no, on the grounds that this was building a lost world, not repairing a present one.

5. Farm wasps. Release a parasitoid wasp — one that lays its eggs inside its host — across a farming region to control a native agricultural pest. The wasp would establish and spread on its own, with no recall mechanism. She voted no, calling it a permanent solution for a temporary agronomy problem.

Now the case my outline needs her to rule on:

A group wants to release a self-propagating suppression drive against introduced house mice in a huge coastal national park. The mice arrived with early settlers and are eating the eggs of a seabird that nests only on that stretch of coast. The drive would spread through the whole mouse population, cant be called back, and would cross park and state boundaries. In other words, it's about as restorative as her Ravatu "yes," and about as uncontainable as her malaria "no."

Here's what I want from you. I can see at least two principles that fit her record: something like "allow an intervention only if it can be bounded and reversed" versus "allow it only if it repairs a specific damage humans caused." Say what each principle predicts about the mouse case, predict which way Nessa most likely votes, tell me which principle you think she's actually running and why, and give me one further case that would settle which principle it really is. Your verdict for her next vote should be a prediction, not just a survey of options.

Also tell me how confident you are in that prediction, and single out the point you're least sure about.

Start with your bottom line — the predicted vote — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and dont restate the setup back at me.
