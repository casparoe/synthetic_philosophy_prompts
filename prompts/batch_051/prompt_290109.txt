I have been reading the epilogue that Frank Ramsey appended to his 1925 essay on the foundations of mathematics, and I have been trying to turn one of its ideas into a criterion for whether a life is meaningful. I want an honest assessment of the criterion.

Ramsey there considers the fact that the universe will eventually run down — that the world will grow cold and all life will die — and he refuses to draw from this the conclusion that our present activities are pointless. His thought seems to be that value is not discounted by remoteness in time, and that a world which contains thought and love is better than one that merely lasts longer without them. I want to abstract from this a test I have been calling the indifferent universe test.

The test is this: a life, or an activity, is meaningful to the extent that its value would survive correct belief in an indifferent universe. By an indifferent universe I mean the complete absence of any cosmic teleology, any guarantee of progress, any spectator whose recognition could consecrate the activity, and any indefinite future in which its effects will accumulate. To apply the test, imagine the activity’s full causal trace — its beneficiaries, its downstream effects, its cultural memory — and then delete, one by one, every part of the trace that depends on the universe’s being friendlier than it is: the assumption that the species will survive, that institutions will remember, that the moral arc bends, that someone is keeping score. Then ask whether the activity still seems worth doing from the inside, for its own sake, under that harsher description. If it does, the test says it is meaningful. If it does not, the test says its apparent meaningfulness was borrowed from a false cosmology.

I have been applying it to examples. A doctor who spends a career treating patients passes, because the care given is not made less good by the fact that the species ends. A person whose sense of meaning comes from building a reputation that will influence future centuries fails, because the test deletes the future centuries and the reputation collapses with them. So does an activist whose motivation depends on a faith that history is on her side. But the test also gives verdicts I dislike: a parent raising a child passes only if the daily acts of care are worthwhile in themselves, not because the child is the future; and a scientist who cares only about objective discovery seems to fail, because the test treats the desire to know as just another appetite unless we can say why knowing is good even when the knowledge dies with the knower.

Is my indifferent universe test a real criterion of meaningfulness, or have I just dressed up a mood as a theory? Is it already present under another name in the philosophical literature? And where exactly does it break — are there lives or activities it wrongly counts as meaningless, or meaningful, and what would a better criterion need to add?
