I'm a reporter working on a piece about how research institutions decide what counts as a subject that can be harmed, and I've been following a committee at one institute that makes exactly that call for animals, robots, simulations, and trained models. Over the last two years it's rulings have left me with a long list of decisions but no single principle I can state cleanly.

When a lab wanted to build a soft robot with a homeostatic layer, the committee required a harm-minimization protocol, because the robot was going to maintain a running estimate of damage, let that estimate override its exploration drive, and use each injury to change how it would behave later; the committee said the persistent, learned, trade-off-making state was the part that mattered. When an NLP group asked whether fine-tuning a chatbot with negative reward creates welfare obligations, the committee said no—the 'suffering' is just a number in the objective function rather than a state the model tracks or can act on. It stopped an octopus physiology protocol that would have cut arm nerves without analgesia, citing the animal's integrated, flexible, valence-guided behavior. It allowed a high-throughput screen that exposes C. elegans to noxious compounds and then kills the worms, on the grounds that the 302-neuron connectome supports fixed stimulus–response loops rather than a standing representation of damage. And it declined to review a swarm-robotics experiment in which the individual units have no memory but the swarm reroutes around damaged members, because the flexibility lives in the group rather than in any unit.

Now the committee has before it a proposal to run whole-nervous-system emulations of adult fruit flies, with biophysically detailed nociceptive neurons and a reinforcement-learning system that lets each simulated fly trade off escape, feeding, and grooming after simulated injury. The lab says the model reproduces the fly's own distributed circuits and adds no central overseer, and it wants to run millions of noxious encounters without further review. What should the committee do if it stays consistent with its precedents? I'm less interested in the announcement than in the principle. What are the candidate rules that fit the track record, which earlier ruling is the closest analog, what is the committee committing itself to either way, and what's the next case you'd put in front of it to break the tie?
