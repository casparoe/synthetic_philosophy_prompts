I have been asked to lead a session of our interdisciplinary decision-theory group on what common knowledge and rationality actually commit us to in game-theoretic reasoning, and in preparing my notes I realized that my own views on these questions had accumulated piecemeal over the years and had never been tested against one another. I would rather find the cracks now than in front of the group. As best I can state them, here are my current commitments.

(1) I accept the standard agreeing-to-disagree result: if two Bayesian agents share a prior and their posterior probabilities for an event are common knowledge between them, those posteriors must be equal. (2) I believe that persistent disagreement in the real world is often fully rational, and that its usual source is a difference in priors rather than any failure of conditionalization or information-sharing. (3) In my own modeling work I treat the common prior assumption as a largely innocuous convention, in something like Harsanyi's sense that differences in subjective beliefs can always be redescribed as differences in information without much loss of modeling generality. (4) I accept the standard backward-induction result for the centipede game: common knowledge of rationality implies that the first player ends the game immediately, since any later node could be reached only after someone passed when a rational player would have taken. (5) Yet I also think that if I were actually seated across from someone playing the centipede game, I would probably continue past the first node, and that doing so would be rational given my uncertainty about the other player's rationality and future play.

I would like an audit of this set. Go through the pairs and distinguish the genuine tensions from the merely apparent ones; rank the genuine tensions by severity and identify the cheapest repair for each; and finally tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set cohere.
