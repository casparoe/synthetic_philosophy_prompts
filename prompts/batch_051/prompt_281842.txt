I've found myself defending a position in our philosophy of mind reading group and I want to stress-test it properly. The position is roughly this: sentience and moral status aren't a binary, they come in degrees, and the gradient runs all the way down. So a dog has more moral status than a mouse, a mouse more than a bee, a bee more than a flatworm, and at the very bottom there's a kind of flicker of something that makes me a little uneasy saying a thermostat has literally zero. I don't mean a thermostat feels pain — I mean that once you cash out 'sentience' as a set of information-processing, feedback, and valenced-response capacities, there's no principled line you can draw that keeps every insect and neural-net and simple feedback system on the zero side. The view is meant to be a scalar realism: moral status is a matter of more-or-less, not yes-or-no. I'm aware it sounds a bit barmy to some people.

What I'd like is a serious map of what would count against this view. Not just the obvious objections — I've heard 'thermostats don't feel anything, that's the end of it' — but the actual evidence or arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. For instance, if a particular theory of consciousness implies a sharp boundary (say, only creatures with global workspace dynamics can have any valence at all), that would be a strong counter if I accepted the theory. Or if gradationism turns out to make moral demands that nobody, including me, can actually take seriously, that's a different kind of cost. I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the view is in danger of being qualified into unfalsifiability — whether I've set it up so that anything a thermostat does can be reinterpreted as a 'low degree of sentience' and nothing could ever count against it. If it is that insulated, is that a fatal problem for a view in this area, or is some insulation normal for claims about consciousness?
