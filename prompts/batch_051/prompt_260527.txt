I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been working on a feature about how societies abandon deeply entrenched practices — the duel, footbinding, and Atlantic slavery — and whether such changes are driven by moral argument, material interest, or shifting codes of honor, a debate that runs from the interest-first Williams thesis about abolition to the honor-based account associated with Appiah. Drafting the piece has forced me to set down my own positions, and I now have six commitments. Before I file, I would like a consistency audit rather than a summary of the debate: where do these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs merely look as though they conflict, how should the genuine tensions be ranked by severity, and what is the least costly revision in each case?

I lay them out as follows.

First, my default picture of moral change is interest-first. Entrenched institutions generally end not because critics win the argument but because the institutions stop serving the material interests of the groups that defend them; the explicit moral case against an institution is usually applied after the underlying shift has already happened.

Second, British abolition of the slave trade nonetheless looks to me like a counterexample to that default. The movement reached its height while the trade was still central to British prosperity, and it was powered by religious and humanitarian conviction of a kind that an interest-first account has to explain away. In that case I take moral argument and mass agitation to have been causally load-bearing, not decorative.

Third, for the end of dueling I find the honor account plainly stronger than either rival. Dueling in Britain did not collapse because its practitioners were persuaded that it was wrong or because it had become economically costly to them; it collapsed because the code of gentlemanliness shifted, so that refusing a duel ceased to be dishonorable and the duel itself came to look faintly ridiculous. The engine there was cultural, a redefinition of what counted as honorable, not a change in material interests.

Fourth, footbinding fits the same honor pattern. A practice that had lasted for centuries collapsed with surprising speed once bound feet were redefined from a status symbol into a mark of national backwardness. Persuasion played a part, but it worked by changing what was shameful rather than by substituting reasoned conviction for shame.

Fifth, I do not want the differences among the cases to be arbitrary. The variable I would emphasize is the structure of esteem. Where the practitioners and defenders of a practice share a single hierarchy of esteem, and the practice functions as a status marker inside it, honor can do the work on its own, as with dueling and footbinding. Where the beneficiaries do not seek the esteem of the people harmed, as with Atlantic slavery, honor among the defenders is too weak a lever, and ending the practice requires a wider mobilization that alters either its moral meaning or its material costs for a broader public.

Sixth, I also believe that the honor shift in the dueling case was itself made possible by a prior shift in material power, namely the rise of a commercial and professional class large enough and confident enough to impose its own standard of respectability on the older aristocracy. That suggests honor may be only the proximate mechanism and material transformation the deeper cause, though I am unsure whether that distinction can be maintained without collapsing the honor account back into the interest account.

Which of these six are actually in tension with one another, which only appear to be, how should the tensions be ranked, and what is the cheapest way to restore consistency without merely relabeling the commitments?
