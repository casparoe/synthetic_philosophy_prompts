I'm in a historiography seminar that's been going on about hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, and I've realized I don't actually know where the line is between a narrative that explains a past event and a narrative that just gets imposed backwards onto it. So I went and collected my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know whether a single principle is behind them, or close. An engineer warned before the Challenger launch that the O-rings could fail in the cold; after the disaster the explanation that the cold made the O-rings fail gets accepted, and I think that's a real explanation rather than hindsight, because the causal story was already on the table before the event. A journalist explains a startup's collapse by saying it scaled too fast and lost its culture, but a year earlier that same journalist praised the aggressive scaling as bold; this one I distrust as a story told backwards. A historian explains the fall of the Roman Republic as a slow unraveling of unwritten norms—the murder of Tiberius Gracchus, then Sulla's march on Rome, then Caesar crossing the Rubicon—where each violation made the next one easier, and he leans on letters and speeches from the period to show the actors themselves treated precedent as live; I accept this one, even though I can see the same facts could be narrated differently. A book claims empires collapse from imperial overreach, expanding until the costs hold of holding territory outrun the benefits; I think this is nearly unfalsifiable, since if an empire doesn't expand it stagnates, if it does expand it overreaches, and the theory pockets either outcome. An explanation of D-Day says the invasion succeeded because the weather cleared enough on June 6 and would likely have failed otherwise; I find this legitimate even though it rests on a counterfactual, because the dependence on weather is concrete and the planners' records show it. A biographer says a trader made a ruinous bet because he was overconfident after a string of wins; I find that seductive but not explanatory unless there's evidence about what he actually thought at the time, since 'overconfidence' postdicts too easily. A social scientist explains a rise in crime purely with demographics and unemployment rates, no story about anyone's choices; I accept it, but I notice I find it less satisfying then a good narrative even when its evidence is better.

Is there one principle that gets all or nearly all of those? What does it predict about the case I can never settle, that the First World War was caused by the assassination of Franz Ferdinand? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to give up? Please lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of the question.
