I write about science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I have noticed a recurring maneuver in causal explanations that I do not have a word for. In reporting on an outbreak, an epidemiologist will move between two kinds of sentence without marking the shift: "the pathogen caused the illness" and "the ministry's failure to act early caused the outbreak to spread." In reporting on an infrastructure collapse, an engineer will say both that fatigue cracking caused the failure and that the inspection program's failure to flag the cracks caused it. In each case, something that did not happen — an absence, a non-event — is treated as a cause just like an event. But only certain absences are admitted. No one writes that a bridge collapsed because of the absence of a passing structural engineer who might have noticed the crack; no one writes that a patient died because of the non-donation of a kidney by each of the thousands of people who could have offered one. The admitted absences seem to track roles, duties, expectations, or institutional mandates rather than anything physical. I want to know whether this pattern has an established name and how it relates to the broader question of whether an absence can be a cause at all. I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: what distinguishes the inspector's failure from the passerby's absence, and is that difference metaphysical, moral, or merely a convention of explanatory style? If no standard term fits exactly, I would welcome a definition and a name of your own, with boundary cases.
