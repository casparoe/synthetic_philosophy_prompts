Two friends of mine got into it at dinner over whether Malthus was wrong or just early. I'm a novelist, not an economist, so I mostly listened, but now I'm writing a scene around the argument and I want to know who actualy had the better of it.

(1) Dana said Malthus was just refuted: he predicted food could only grow arithmetically while population grows geometrically, so living standards would always be pushed back to subsistence. What happened instead is that food production and population both exploded while living standards rose, so the prediction failed. A failed long-run prediction means the theory is false, not 'early.'

(2) Jerry said she was being too quick. Malthus had picked out a real pressure: populations tend to grow into available resources. That pressure didn't vanish; we kept enlarging the resource base with technology and cheap energy. So he was right about the mechanism but early about the timing.

(3) Dana said that's an unfalsifiable rescue. Any failed long-run prediction can be protected by saying the bad outcome was merely postponed. If a theory can survive any refutation by pushing the date back, it's not doing scientific work.

(4) Jerry said it's only unfalsifiable if the postponing factor is vague. Here it's independently identifiable: synthetic fertilizer, fossil fuels, the demographic transition. You can test whether those explain the escape. Long-run predictions carry implicit ceteris paribus clauses; when the background conditions changed, the prediction wasn't refuted, it was conditional.

(5) Dana answered that an open-ended ceteris paribus clause is exactly the problem—what observation would count against Malthus? And if the saved version only says 'population can't outgrow the resources available to it,' then it's a tautology, not a prediction.

(6) Jerry came back that the original specific claim, geometric vs. arithmetic growth, was tested and died; the softened claim is a different theory. So the real question isn't whether the original was falsified, but whether a descendant theory is still live.

By then everyone wanted to talk about something else. Can you adjudicate the exchange step by step: who's right about which specific point, who's more right overall, and what are both missing?
