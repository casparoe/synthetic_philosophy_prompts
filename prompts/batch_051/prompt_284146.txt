I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I serve on the editorial advisory board of a magazine that sits at the intersection of science, history, and politics. The editor-in-chief is planning a special issue built around the idea of non-human historical actors: germs, climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions treated as forces that have brought down empires, triggered famines and revolts, and remodeled political institutions. Her pitch is that this reframing corrects histories in which only human intentions count as causes, and that it would give familiar material new urgency for our readers. Several of us on the board are uneasy, myself included. We worry that the word "actor" smuggles in agency where there is only causal power, and that without strict guardrails the issue will slide into a determinism in which political choices and institutions become mere downstream effects of microbes and weather.

I have been asked to draft a short policy memo for the board setting out how the issue should handle this framing. Specifically: should we adopt the language of non-human historical agency at all, and if so, under what conditions is it defensible to describe a pathogen, a climate anomaly, or an eruption as a historical actor? What distinctions — between causal salience and agency, between a triggering event and an enabling condition, and between a shock and the societal vulnerabilities it exposes — should contributors be required to observe? And what editorial guardrails would keep the issue from reproducing the old environmental-determinist mistake of reading social and political outcomes straight off the physical environment?

The memo should close with a clear, actionable recommendation the board can vote on, and it should give an honest, non-caricatured treatment of the strongest argument for the editor's proposed framing, even if the recommendation ends up constraining or rejecting it.
