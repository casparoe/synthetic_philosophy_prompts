Write the joint statement that a committed defender of the physiological-integration criterion of biological individuality and a committed defender of the evolutionary criterion could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The question before them is under what conditions, if any, a holobiont—a host together with its persistent microbial symbionts—counts as a single biological individual. Their running cases are a coral with its photosynthetic dinoflagellate symbionts (zooxanthellae), an aphid with its obligate bacterial symbionts (Buchnera), and a human with her gut microbiome; a Portuguese man o' war is available as a boundary reference case. By stipulation, the two sides accept the following characterizations of their starting positions. The integrationist holds that an organism is best identified by the strength and topology of functional integration among parts—shared boundaries, metabolic coupling, coordinated development, repair, and homeostatic regulation—and that at least the coral and aphid holobionts meet this standard, while the human case remains an open question. The evolutionary theorist holds that an organism is best identified by whether the parts constitute an evolutionary individual with sufficiently aligned fitness interests and shared reproductive fates to face natural selection as a unit; on that criterion holobionts generally fail because host and symbiont reproduce largely independently, so the relevant evolutionary individual is usually the host alone (or, in other systems, a colony or superorganism) rather than the host-plus-microbiome.

Draft the document such a collaboration would produce. It must include:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section whose entries are substantive: each claim should constrain at least one plausible view, and none should be acceptable merely because it is ambiguous or trivially hedged.
2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is formulated twice—once in wording the integrationist can affirm ungrudgingly and once in wording the evolutionary theorist can affirm ungrudgingly—so that neither side recognizes a caricature of its own position.
3. A list of genuine cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Separate these from disagreements that, on examination, are merely verbal, and say explicitly when a remaining dispute is about which criterion gets to govern the word organism rather than about any fact of the matter.
4. For each crux, the argument, observation, or experiment both sides would accept as bearing on it, described concretely enough that the two sides could record predictions in advance. State what each side predicts and what pattern of results would make each side revise or qualify its position on the central case.

Close with a joint paragraph both can sign, stating the conditions under which each would concede the central case and the conditions under which they would instead classify their disagreement as purely terminological.
