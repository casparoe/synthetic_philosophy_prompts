Consider the following practices from music publishing and copyright law, as they operate in the United States. A songwriter owns something before any performance or recording exists: the musical work, the composition itself. That work is legally treated as the same object across changes of key, tempo, instrumentation, and even significant rearrangement. When a performer records it, a second owned object comes into existence—the sound recording—usually owned not by the performer but by a label. If another artist records and releases a cover, that artist must pay the owner of the musical work a mechanical royalty for the right to reproduce the composition, while the new sound recording becomes a fresh owned object; the owner of the original sound recording receives nothing from the cover. When a song is played on over-the-air radio, owners of the musical work are paid a performance royalty, but owners of the sound recording generally are not; when the same song is streamed digitally, both are typically paid. An orchestra may freely perform a public-domain symphony but may not copy a particular recording of it, and its new recording is copyrighted even though no one owns the composition any longer.

The practice thus distinguishes a composition, its performances, and its recordings as different kinds of thing, with different owners, rights, and identity conditions. It also seems to hold both that a musical work stays the same through radical sonic differences and that a recording is a distinct protected object partly because of its specific sonic character.

What ontology of musical works does this scheme presuppose? Does it commit itself to the view that a musical work is an abstract type or structure of which performances are tokens—and if so, what exactly is a recording: a token of the work, a token of the performance, a type whose instances are copies, or a second work in its own right? Does the practice require a score-based or structural account of work identity, or is it compatible with sound-based or historically individuated work accounts? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the scheme strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where the practice is ontologically underdetermined. Finally, explain which parts of the legal and commercial scheme would have to change if the presupposed ontology were replaced by a nominalism that admits only performances and recordings, or by a purely acoustic view on which each sounding is itself the work.
