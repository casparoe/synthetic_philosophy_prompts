Something I've been chewing on. The classic republican argument for a citizen army, the version you get from Machiavelli and the anti-standing-army tradition, goes roughly like this: a republic should defend itself with its own citizens rather than mercenaries or a permanent professional caste, both because voters who have to fight and die in a war won't start reckless ones, and because citizen-soldiers with a stake in the state won't become the instrument of a tyrant the way hired or career soldiers might. The argument quietly assumes, among other things, that wars require large numbers of ordinary citizens to do the actual fighting, so the physical costs of war land on the very people who consent to it.

Here's the development that seems to mess with it. A rich modern democracy can now fight a war, or an open-ended campaign, mostly with an all-volunteer professional military plus contractors, drones, cyberattacks, and precision strikes. The public pays with taxes and maybe with loosed civil liberties, but almost nobody who voted for the war has to show up and get shot, and mass mobilization is held in reserve for true existential emergencies. At the same time the modern professional military isn't Machiavelli's mercenary: its members are citizens, it answers to civilian government, and it shows no obvious interest in seizing the state. Yet the insulation between the voting public and the bodily costs of war looks even thicker than what the anti-standing-army writers worried about.

So my actual question: does the Machiavellian citizen-army argument survive this development, does it need repair, or is it just dead? I want to know which specific premise the development bears on, whether the argument can be restated in some form that still works without mass conscription, and whether at that point it's really still an argument for conscription rather than just an argument for making war costly to the people who decide on it. And if the empirical bet has simply failed, since rich democracies do seem to keep starting or joining wars without a draft, does the normative core about decisions being made by the people who'll bear the costs survive on its own?
