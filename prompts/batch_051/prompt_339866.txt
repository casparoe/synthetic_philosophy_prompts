For a school club on artificial intelligence and society, I worked out the following argument and have not been able to find where it goes wrong. It seems to prove that no AI can ever genuinely interpret another AI, yet the conclusion is strong enough that it would also rule out much of the understanding we ordinarily take ourselves to have.

(1) Suppose one AI system attempts to interpret another. The interpreter must be either at least as complex as the target or strictly simpler than it.
(2) If the interpreter is at least as complex as the target, then its own internal computation is at least as opaque as the computation it is trying to explain; trusting its explanation would require a further interpreter, and that further interpreter raises the same problem, so we are left with an infinite regress and no certified interpretation.
(3) If the interpreter is strictly simpler than the target, then it lacks the representational capacity to track the target's actual computation; its explanation is at best a lossy summary or a behavioral prediction, not a genuine interpretation.
(4) Therefore no interpreter can produce a genuine, certified interpretation of an AI system.
(5) But this proves too much: by the same reasoning, no human could genuinely understand a machine simpler than a human brain, and no small program could be debugged, because the debugger's model would be either too simple to be faithful or too complex to be trusted without yet another debugger.

Where does this argument go wrong? I would prefer to keep the idea that finite, genuine understanding of a complicated system is possible, but I want to know which premise that forces me to give up and what else I lose by giving it up. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
