I am a high school student, and next month I will give a short talk to my school's Global Affairs and Economics Society defending the claim that rich countries should shift a substantial share of their foreign aid away from in-kind goods and donor-run institution-building projects and toward unconditional cash transfers delivered directly to poor households, including in countries with high levels of government corruption. My case will rest on four claims: first, the right comparison is not between cash and a flawless anti-poverty program but between cash and real-world aid that often disappears into contractors, overhead, and failed projects; second, cash respects recipients as agents who know their own needs better than distant donors do; third, the empirical record of unconditional cash transfers is at least as strong as that of more paternalistic interventions; and fourth, the corruption objection cuts both ways, since corruption also distorts in-kind and project aid, and cash may give poor people resources to demand more from their own governments. The audience will be roughly two-thirds economics students who care mostly about incentives and measurable outcomes, and one-third students interested in human rights and global justice, some of whom worry that cash transfers quietly abandon the longer-term project of building just institutions. I would like a question-and-answer prep sheet: the objections I should genuinely expect from these two groups, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased the way each group would actually put them, with what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, where each reply is softest, and at the end the one question I should most hope nobody asks.
