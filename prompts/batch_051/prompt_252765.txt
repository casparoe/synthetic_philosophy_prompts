I sit on my hospital's patient-safety review committee, and we're stuck on an adverse-event review. In July, our chest-pain AI triage tool flagged a 31-year-old man as low risk for aortic dissection. The ED attending, who was covering eleven other patients at the time, had a normal ECG and a negative high-sensitivity troponin, so she discharged him with a muscle-strain diagnosis. He died of a dissection eight hours later. The vendor's contract calls the tool "advisory only" and says the clinician keeps final authority; our own policy says the same. The attending says she made a reasonable call given the model's score and the test results, and that no one actually knows what features the model weighted down. The vendor says the model performed as specified and it's on the human to override it.

Sorry for the wall of text, but the committee really is split three ways. One group wants to treat this like a bad lab instrument and assign responsibility to the treating physician for failing to override. A second wants to name the vendor, because the hospital has become dependent on a model whose internal reasoning no one can explain. A third says this is exactly the kind of responsibility gap people have been warning about, and that we'd be lying if we assigned blame to any single person. We need a formal written opinion for the medical director. Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday. It should recommend a concrete course of action — for example, a medical error report naming the physician, a formal notice to the vendor, changes to our algorithm-governance rules, or no fault finding — and it should engage the strongest version of the side you reject, not just restate hospital policy.
