I've been trying to assemble the full set of structurally distinct objections to the doomsday argument — the anthropic argument that one's observed birth rank is evidence that the total number of humans will turn out not to be vastly larger than it is now — and I suspect I now have all the standard ones. The objections I already know are these. (1) The reference-class objection: the crucial likelihood depends on an arbitrary choice among equally defensible reference classes, such as all humans, all sentient beings, or only observers capable of formulating the argument. (2) The no-random-sample objection: one's birth rank is not produced by any actual randomizing process, so treating it as uniformly distributed over all births is unsupported. (3) The prior-dependence objection: the argument supplies only a likelihood ratio, and with a suitably different prior over possible total populations the doom-soon conclusion can be reversed or made to vanish. (4) The self-indication objection: the fact that one exists at all is itself evidence favoring hypotheses with more total observers, and in the simplest setup this cancels the doomsday shift. (5) The unbounded-future objection: if possible total populations can be arbitrarily large or infinite, the needed priors and likelihood ratios fail to be well defined, so the argument loses its quantitative force. (6) The everyone-in-history objection: the same reasoning would have produced a confident doom-soon update for observers at nearly every earlier time, so it appears to prove too much to be a reliable guide now. Give me one more objection that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of these — ideally one that attacks a different step of the argument than the sampling model, the reference class, or the prior. Then say honestly whether you think it works and what it adds that the listed objections do not.
