A friend of mine runs a reading group on nationalism, and last week they spent the meeting on Ernest Renan's 1882 lecture "What is a Nation?" I wasn't there, but afterward two members, Lena and Owen, rehashed the argument in front of me and asked me to settle it, which I couldn't. The fight started over this passage from Renan: "Where national memories are concerned, griefs are of more value than triumphs, for they impose duties, and require a common effort."

Lena argued Renan was right: a country's identity is held together less by victories than by shared griefs, because mourning imposes a duty to carry forward what was lost. She pointed to Masada and Dunkirk as national stories in which defeat or near-catastrophe did more binding work than any straightforward win; the shared experience of suffering creates an obligation to the dead and to one another that triumph doesn't. Owen said that was too generous. He said the American "Lost Cause" after the Confederacy is exactly a national memory organized around grief, and it bound people together largely through a false, self-exculpating story that imposed the wrong duties—fidelity to racial hierarchy and resentment against the federal government. If Renan's criterion ranks griefs above triumphs, it seems to license all griefs equally, including those built on defeats that deserved to be defeats. Lena replied that Renan is not saying every grief is morally admirable; he is saying that suffering in common unifies more than joy does, and that the duty it creates is real as a social fact, not as a moral endorsement of the original cause. The Lost Cause is an objection only if you assume the duty must be a just one. Owen came back: but then the passage does no evaluative work at all; it just says shared pain is stronger glue, which is true of a criminal gang too. Unless Renan gives us a way to distinguish grieving a defeat from making a cult of the defeated cause, he can't explain why some national griefs are the healthy kind. Lena said the distinction comes from whether the memory produces a "will to live together" in the present rather than a fantasy of reversing the past; Masada, as told, is more about collective endurance than about restoring a kingdom. Owen answered that this is exactly how Lost Cause commemoration presents itself to outsiders—as heritage, endurance, and honor, not as revanchism—so the test may be too easy to satisfy.
