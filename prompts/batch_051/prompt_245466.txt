I'm grading a question in an intro semantics course and want a second pair of eyes. The question was:

A dictionary writer says that a word doesn't actually have one meaning but a whole range of meanings, and therefore that it's simply false that words have determinate meanings. Critically assess that claim, making sure to distinguish polysemy, homonymy, and vagueness.

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The writer is mostly right that words don't have a single fixed meaning. In 'healthy food' and 'healthy patient,' 'healthy' carries related but different senses (promoting health vs. having health), and that's polysemy: one lexical item with several related senses. Homonymy is like 'bat' the animal and 'bat' the sports equipment, where two separate words happen to share a form; there the meanings are unrelated, though honestly the line between polysemy and homonymy is fuzzy. Vagueness is different: it's not about multiple senses but borderline cases inside one sense, the way 'red' shades off into 'orange' with no sharp cutoff. So the writer is right overall. Since a word like 'bank' is vague between its financial sense and its riverside sense, and 'run' has dozens of senses, the word itself doesn't fix its meaning — context has to select one."
