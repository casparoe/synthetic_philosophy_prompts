I'm putting together a unit for an undergrad machine ethics class, and I need a classification handout. The debate is whether an AI that can only issue judgments counts as a moral agent. Picture a system that takes in a scenario, outputs an assessment like "the right thing is to do X", but has no actuators, no write access to anything that matters, and its outputs go to a logfile nobody reads until after the relevant decisions have already been made. I want a decision tree where each branch is a yes/no question students can answer before knowing any theory labels, so the tree sorts them by what they actually think about cases rather than which name they like.

Please make these exactly the leaves (plus whatever unnamed positions the logical space forces on you): (a) issuing a judgment is already a kind of action, so the system is a moral agent; (b) moral agency requires the capacity to act on your judgment, so the system is not an agent at all; (c) the system can be an agent in a thin sense but cannot be responsible, because ought implies can and it cannot change the situations it evaluates; (d) the system is a moral agent but never responsible, because responsibility requires genuinely alternative possibilities and the outputs are just a function of weights and inputs; (e) the system is not even a moral patient, since moral status requires interests or a welfare, not just correct evaluative output.

Build the tree from the logical space first, not from the list of names. Lay out the load-bearing questions — does agency require affecting the outside world, is forming and emitting a judgment a move in the space of actions, must the thing you are responsible for be something you could have prevented, do you need alternative possibilities or is authorship of the output enough, and does being an agent come apart from being a patient — then locate the positions among the resulting combinations. Flag any answer-cells nobody occupies, and say in a sentence per empty cell whether it is incoherent, just deeply unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag the positions that your tree cannot seperate.

You must use these three cases as diagnostic questions somewhere: (1) a human advisor whose written recommendations are always ignored by the people with power — is she assessable for what she recommends? (2) a vending machine with a broken robot arm that computes "stock B3" but never dispenses — anything to assess? (3) a deterministic AI that would have issued a different judgment only if its weights or inputs had been different, and neither of those is under its control — does that count as could-have-done-otherwise? Aim for seven to nine branch points, each concrete enough that a student who has never read a paper on free will or moral responsibility can answer it.
