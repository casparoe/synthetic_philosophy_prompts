I've been tending toward a pretty austere view about culture: languages, traditions, art forms, festivals are valuable only because particular people value them, and once no one is around whose life they shape, there's nothing left to be lost. I like the view because its tidy: it gives one decision procedure for every preservation question (find the affected people and ask how much it matters to them), it doesn't posit spooky group-level goods, and it explains why we shouldn't spend public money embalming a dialect nobody wants to speak. But there's a firm intuition I can't shake. If the last fluent speaker of a language dies before she's recorded it, something has gone out of the world above and beyond the fact that a person died, and I'd call it a loss even if no surviving person's preferences were frustrated and no one will ever miss it. I can't tell whether that intuition is tracking a real feature of value or is just sentimentality about my own attachment to cultures. Which should yield? Give me the strongest versions of both, what it would cost to debunk the intuition, what it would cost to amend the theory, and whether there's a reading on which they're both right.
