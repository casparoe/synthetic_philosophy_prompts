I am a PhD student in the history of science, and I am trying to reverse-engineer the standard my supervisor applies when she calls a piece of historical writing Whiggish, meaning that it treats the past as if its point were to produce the present. She has never stated her criterion explicitly, but over the past year I have recorded her verdicts on the following works. She called Whiggish a draft chapter that retold the Copernican controversy as a story of how astronomy freed itself from Aristotelian error and advanced toward modern cosmology, treating Galileo as a hero and the defenders of the old system as obstacles. She also called Whiggish a paper on nineteenth-century work on the absorption of radiant heat by gases, which presented those investigators as if they had been reaching toward contemporary climate science and traced a continuous line from their experiments to present-day climate models. She did not find Whiggish a study of early modern alchemy that reconstructed the discipline's practices in its own terms and explicitly declined to measure them against modern chemistry. She also did not find Whiggish a history of the eugenics movement within early genetics that stressed contingency and discontinuity rather than progress, even though its author roundly condemned the protagonists by present-day moral standards. Finally, she called Whiggish a history of statistics that described a steady, cumulative improvement in technique from early probability theory to contemporary data science, each generation building directly on the last, with almost no dead ends and little overt praise or blame.

A new case has come up. A proposed article on the history of disease control argues that human health has improved dramatically over the last three centuries, but that the improvement came through a sequence of ruptures — miasma theory, bacteriology, antibiotics, molecular medicine — each of which redefined disease rather than built on its predecessor, so that no single concept runs continuously from the past into present-day medicine, and each break was contingent rather than inevitable, even though the present is plainly the beneficiary. I need to predict, if I am to be consistent with my supervisor's track record, whether she will call this article Whiggish. Please identify the candidate rule or rules that fit her earlier verdicts, say what each predicts about the new article, give your best guess as to which rule she is actually running and why, and name the further case that would most decisively distinguish the remaining candidates. In your response, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
