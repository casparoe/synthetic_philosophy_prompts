I'm collecting proposed resolutions of the paradox of deontology, and I want to test whether the standard list is complete. By "paradox of deontology" I mean this: a deontological constraint forbids me to kill an innocent person even when killing one innocent person would prevent five other innocents from being killed by someone else. Complying with the constraint seems to leave the world with more violations of the very kind the constraint condemns, which can look as though the deontologist is forbidden to act in the way that best serves the value underlying the constraint.

Here are the resolutions I already have:

(1) The agent-relative reasons resolution. The reason not to kill is first-personal: it is a reason for me not to kill, not a reason derived from an agent-neutral ranking of outcomes. Since no one's reason is simply "minimize killings overall," there is no demand that I produce the outcome with fewer killings.

(2) The agency or clean-hands resolution. There is a decisive moral difference between what I do and what I allow or what others do. I have a special responsibility not to be the author of a killing, and that responsibility is not to be traded off against preventing other agents from killing.

(3) The victim's-complaint resolution. The constraint is owed to the particular person I would kill, and her complaint against me is not answered by the fact that others would otherwise be killed by someone else. The complaints of the five run against their killers, not against me.

(4) The doing/allowing and intending/foreseeing articulation. The forbidden act would be an intentional killing by me, while the five deaths would be ones I merely fail to prevent and which are intended by another; because this structure matters morally, the case is not one in which the same sort of moral wrong is simply present five times rather than once.

(5) The axiological diagnosis. The paradox presupposes a consequentializing picture on which the wrongness of killing amounts to the badness of killings, where badness in outcomes can be added up. The deontologist denies this: the consideration protected by the constraint — the inviolability or moral status of persons — is not the kind of consideration that aggregates into larger and smaller quantities across states of affairs.

I believe this list covers the standard moves, so I am looking for a sixth resolution that is genuinely new. It should not be a rewording, a special case, or a conjunction of items already listed. If you can supply one, explain how it differs from the five above and whether it actually dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it.
