I am preparing a graduate seminar in metaethics and was recently sent the following paragraph, which a colleague tells me is from a draft reference work on the wrong kind of reasons problem. I am considering assigning the passage to my students as an orientation to the topic, but I want an independent assessment of how much of it I can rely on before I do so.

"The wrong kind of reasons problem is the problem of separating, among the considerations that bear on whether to have an attitude, those that genuinely count in favor of the attitude from those that merely count in favor of being in it. The distinction is usually introduced by means of a case such as the following: an evil demon threatens to harm you unless you admire it, though it possesses no admirable feature whatsoever. Plainly, the threat gives you a reason to admire the demon. But the buck-passing account of value holds that for a thing to be good just is for there to be reason to value it, so the account appears committed to calling the demon good. The buck-passer's standard reply is to restrict the account to the right kind of reasons, identified with object-given reasons, which are supplied by features of the object itself; wrong kind, or state-given, reasons are those that reflect the beneficial consequences of having the attitude rather than anything about its object. The main difficulty that remains for this reply, and the focus of much of the recent literature, is epistemic: a deliberator confronting some consideration must be able to tell reliably whether it belongs to the object-given or the state-given class."

My own suspicion is that the paragraph is broadly competent but that one or two of its formulations are subtly too strong, and I want to know exactly where it is sound rather than merely plausible. Please give me a careful accuracy audit of the passage: separate the claims into what is correct and standard, what is a common but potentially misleading simplification, and what a careful defender of the buck-passing or fitting-attitude accounts would reject or correct. In particular, I would appreciate sustained attention to two matters: whether the closing characterization of the remaining difficulty as an "epistemic" one is accurate, and whether the contrast between object-given and state-given reasons is drawn too narrowly or in a way that depends on a stronger claim than the literature warrants. Do not simply deliver a verdict; show the reasoning a specialist would use in deciding what to trust.
