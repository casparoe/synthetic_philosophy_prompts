What's your actual credence that you can have both of these at once: (i) indicative conditionals like "if the switch is flipped, the light comes on" have real truth conditions, and (ii) the probability of such a conditional just is the conditional probability P(light comes on | switch flipped)? I know the Lewis triviality results are meant to show that, under standard probability theory, accepting the second as a thesis about propositions with truth values collapses the probability function into something degenerate. But I keep meeting people who say the proof only goes through because of right-nested conditionals, or because he assumed conditionals are closed under Boolean operations, so a principled restriction can save the whole thing. Give me a number, not a survey: how likely is it that the truth-conditional reading of Adams's/Stalnaker's thesis survives in some form? Then decompose it—how much hangs on what "the probability of a conditional" even measures, how much on which assumptions of the proof you're allowed to drop, how much on whether ordinary language conditionals have truth values at all, and which subquestion would move your credence most.
