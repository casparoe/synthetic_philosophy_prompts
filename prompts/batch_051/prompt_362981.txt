So there's this proposal a friend's think tank keeps pushing for making pre-AGI commitments stickier, and I can't tell if it's brilliant or a self-referential trap. The mechanism is a public registry. Anyone — a lab, a company, a country — can put down a natural-language promise about what they'll do after AGI exists, like 'I won't use the first AGI to build autonomus weapons until at least two other parties have AGI too.' To make the promise credible, you put up a serious amount of money in escrow — say, 10% of your annual budget or 1% of GDP, whichever's bigger. The money gets paid back to you later with interest, but only if a judge decides you kept the promise. Here's the part that bugs me: the contract doesn't name a human judge. It says that when the first system that passes a specified capability threshold actually gets built, that system itself will be handed the registry and asked to decide whether each signatory kept their promise, by interpreting the text and applying it to whatever the post-transition world looks like. The escrow automatically executes the verdict, and if the verdict is 'no,' the forfeited money goes into a post-AGI compensation fund that the same system administers. Until the trigger, a signatory can amend their promise freely, but each amendment costs a revision fee, and the fee gets split among all the other signatories. After the trigger, promises are frozen. So we're asking people to bet money they can only get back if the AGI, reading a text they wrote years earlier, says they were the good guy — and the same AGI controls whatever they lose. Walk me through how this actually plays out. Who signs, and what kinds of promises get written? What happens to the revision-fee market as the trigger gets near? What is the first AGI incentivized to do with it's power to award or deny all this money? How would a lab that fully plans to defect game the mechanism before and after the trigger? Where does it snap, and is there any patch that survives without gutting it?
