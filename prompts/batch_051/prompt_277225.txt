In a debate about how historians ought to judge decisions, the phrase 'hindsight is 20/20' keeps being invoked as though it settled the matter. It arose most recently in an argument about the 2008 financial crisis: one speaker claimed the build-up of risk was evident at the time and that the failure to act was culpable; another replied that 'hindsight is 20/20,' which appeared to mean that a judgement formed only after the outcome cannot support the claim that the crisis was foreseeable. I want that sentence dismantled. What distinct precise claims could 'hindsight is 20/20' be used to assert? For each reading, state whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical thesis, or false, and explain how it bears on the retrospective charge that someone 'should have seen it coming' and on any wider claim about historical inevitability. Where relevant, distinguish the slogan from the psychological phenomenon of hindsight bias, from outcome bias, and from the narrative fallacy in historical explanation. Say which reading, if any, would actually underwrite using the phrase to dismiss a retrospective judgement of culpability, and which reading people who deploy it in such arguments most likely intend. Finally, indicate how confident you are in each main claim you make and single out the point you are least sure of. Please answer in British English.
