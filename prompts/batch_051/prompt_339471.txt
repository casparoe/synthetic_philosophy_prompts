You are a philosopher of language who specializes in vagueness and the sorites paradox. Please think carefully, and before you settle on a definition, brainstorm three or four candidate accounts of what makes a predicate vague, briefly explaining why you reject or revise the ones that get the cases below wrong. Do not merely label the cases; show the definition doing the work.

I want a working definition of a vague predicate — or, if you think the right object of definition is the notion of a borderline case, say so and define that instead — that correctly classifies each of the following items. For each item, I want two things: first, the verdict — does the predicate clearly apply, clearly fail to apply, or apply only borderline? Second, the diagnosis — is the predicate there functioning as a paradigm vague predicate, a precise predicate, or is some other phenomenon, such as ambiguity, context-sensitivity, open texture, or an unresolved empirical question, the better explanation?

1. "Heap" applied to 100,000 grains of sand in a conical pile.
2. "Heap" applied to exactly one grain of sand.
3. "Heap" applied to 250 grains of sand in a neat pyramid.
4. "Bald" applied to a man with zero hairs on his scalp.
5. "Bald" applied to a man with a full head of hair and no visible thinning.
6. "Bald" applied to a man with no receding hairline but uniformly sparse hair, roughly 3,000 hairs spread over his scalp.
7. "Green" applied to a paint chip halfway between the last chip an ordinary observer calls blue and the first one they call green in a hardware-store paint strip.
8. "Is an even number" applied to 10^100.
9. "Is a prime number" applied to 101.
10. "Is a child" applied to a person who is 17 years and 11 months old in a jurisdiction where the legal age of majority is the person's 18th birthday.
11. "Is a game" applied to a solitary puzzle app with no scoring, no win condition, and no failure state.
12. "Is alive" applied to a virus particle outside a host cell.
13. "Is a planet" applied to Pluto after the International Astronomical Union adopted a definition under which Pluto no longer qualifies.

I am confident about items 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, and 9: the first four are paradigm cases of a vague predicate applying or failing, and the last two are cases of a precise predicate applying or failing. I lean toward calling items 3, 6, and 7 genuine borderline cases, though I cannot say exactly what distinguishes them from items 10 through 13. For items 10 through 13, force a verdict — for each, say whether it is vagueness or something else — and then tell me which of your verdicts across the whole list is least secure.

Before you close, test your definition against higher-order vagueness. Suppose a person shown the 250-grain pyramid in item 3 also hesitates over whether "borderline heap" is true of it. Does your account allow for borderline borderline cases, or does it simply redraw the sharp boundary one level up? If it redraws the boundary, say whether that is a price you are willing to pay. A good definition should also explain why the step-by-step argument from 100,000 grains down to 1 grain produces a swarm of borderline cases rather than a single clean cut-off.

Throughout, keep separate what is settled among philosophers of language from what is actively contested. If your definition rests on a view such as semantic indecision, truth-value gaps, or epistemicism, mark it as a view and not as the consensus; if you rely on any contrary view, mark that too. Close with a single-sentence definition you would use in an undergraduate class, and identify the hardest case for it.
