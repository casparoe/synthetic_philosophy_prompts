You are a philosopher of science who works on the metaphysics and epistemology of classification—natural kinds, realism about scientific categories, and the difference between kinds that are discovered and kinds that are made. Approach the problem as you would a careful review of a research proposal.

A colleague of mine defends the following claim: the mature sciences progressively carve nature at its own joints. The periodic table's elements and biological species, he says, are classifications that track divisions already present in the world, whereas categories like weed, vegetable, and tree are projections of human interests and practical convenience onto a world that is indifferent to them. Progress in science, on his view, consists partly in replacing the second kind of classification with the first.

I am skeptical, but I cannot tell whether my skepticism is well-founded or just a vague preference for deflationary talk. So I want to know: is there any observation, experiment, or measurement that could actually bear on whether a classification carves at a real joint of nature rather than merely being useful, projectable, or familiar? Please design the strongest concrete test you can of the colleague's claim, using their own contrast—elements and species on one side, weeds and vegetables on the other. Specify what data you would collect, the result that would support the colleague's realism about joints, and the result that would support one or another deflationary reading. Start by brainstorming several candidate tests and say briefly why you reject the weaker ones before settling on the strongest.

Then be precise about what even a clean positive result would not show. For example, would it show that elements and species are real divisions in some robust sense, or only that some categories are more predictively fecund than others under our actual investigative practices? Would a clean negative result show that weed is just as real as gold, or only that realness admits of degrees and is partly purposes-relative? Is the gap between the best possible evidence and the metaphysical claim a merely practical limitation on testing, or does it reveal something defective in the very idea of the world having joints? Finally, tell me what observation you would actually need to see to change your mind about the colleague's claim.
