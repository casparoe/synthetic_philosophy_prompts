I retired a few years ago from a career in solid-state physics and have been reading philosophy seriously ever since, and lately I've gotten tangled up in Rorty's antirepresentationalism. I used to think of myself as a fairly standard scientific realist, but the more I read him, the less sure I am about what would actually change in how I talk about my old field if he's right. Sometimes it sounds like a huge claim—physics isn't in the business of mirroring nature, it's just one among many vocabularies that happen to work—and sometimes it sounds like nothing much, because I never literally believed that a band structure was a picture of something out there in the first place.

I'm supposed to give a talk to my old department this fall, mostly experimentalists with no patience for philosophy, on what Rorty would and wouldn't change about the way a working physicist describes what she's doing. I've collected a list of candidate implications people draw from his view: (a) scientific truth is just agreement among the relevant researchers; (b) we should stop saying we 'discover' things and say instead that we cope with them; (c) realism about unobservables like quarks or the wavefunction is a mistake; (d) it's a category error to say later theories are 'closer' to the world than earlier ones; (e) the science/pseudoscience distinction does no real work; (f) science has no special epistemic authority over other vocabularies—it's just better at some purposes and worse at others; (g) scientific progress is just the replacement of one vocabulary by another. I'm sure I'm missing some, so add any you think belongs on the list.

Here's what I'd like from you. Rank these from 'would genuinely change how I write and give this talk' down to 'overated or doesn't follow at all.' For the top two or three, explain what makes them real implications of antirepresentationalism rather than just rhetoric, and why they'd actually land with a room of working scientists. For the bottom ones, tell me what hidden premise would have to be smuggled in to make them follow. Finally, tell me which single implication I should build the talk around and what the strongest objection from my old colleagues would be. I'm less interested in whether the position is true than in which parts of it, if taken seriously, would actually move a room of experimentalists.
