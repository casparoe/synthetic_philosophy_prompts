I am drafting a short article on Boethius's answer to the problem of divine foreknowledge, and I would value comments on the argument before I write it up. My working thesis is that the atemporal or eternalist reading of Boethius does not solve the problem it is usually invoked to solve; it relocates it. The problem, as I set it out, starts from temporal foreknowledge: if God knew yesterday what I will choose tomorrow, then yesterday it was already fixed what I would choose, and tomorrow it no longer seems within my power to choose otherwise. The Boethian move is to deny the premise about God's location in time: God does not know things in advance because God is outside time and apprehends every time at once. My argument is that this move does not by itself secure the alternative possibilities that motivated the problem. For any act I perform, one can still ask whether I had it in my power to act so that God's eternal cognition would have been otherwise, and the usual answers either deny that power and thereby end up in a form of necessity by another name, or affirm it by appealing to a compatibilist account of ability that would have worked even without the eternity move. I have three specific questions. First, is this an uncharitable reading of Boethius, given that he may not have been trying to preserve alternative possibilities in a libertarian sense at all, but only to show that divine knowledge does not impose necessity of its own? Second, in the argument as sketched, am I relying too heavily on an unexamined distinction between logical and temporal priority? Third, would the paper be strengthened or muddied by closing with a Frankfurt-style argument that alternative possibilities are not needed for moral responsibility, or does that concede too much? I am not asking for literature or references; I am after the shape of the argument.
