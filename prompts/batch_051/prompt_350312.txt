I'm an undergrad who just spent a semester getting convinced by the case for futarchy—the idea that instead of electing people to make policy by hunch and lobbying, we should run conditional prediction markets: pick a success metric, have markets answer 'if we adopt policy X, what will the metric be?', and do whatever the markets say. The case seems genuinely strong to me. Markets aggregate dispersed information better than a room full of experts; they give a definite, hard-to-fudge answer; and they replace a process where the loudest stakeholder wins with a process where people have to put real money behind what they think. I also find the 'you're choosing to be ignorant' argument pretty devastating: if a policy is going to kill some number of people or not, those numbers exist whether or not anyone bets on them, so refusing to price the outcome just means deciding with less information.

But then a class exercise proposed a binding 'mortality market': a conditional market on 'if we reverse the speed-limit cut, how many road deaths occur next year?', where the policy decision would follow whichever side of the market came in lower. And I have a firm, almost physical conviction that this is wrong—not wrong because the forecast would be bad, but wrong because something important gets corrupted when a government chooses policy by letting traders make or lose money on the number of people who die. My intuition says some outcomes are not the kind of thing a polity should be betting on, even when the information would be useful. It isnt just squeamishness about death; I think I would feel the same, maybe worse, about a binding market on 'if we cut the welfare program, how many children fall into poverty?' where the answer determines the policy. I can feel the futarchy reply already: those are exactly the questions where being information-blind is most costly, so ruling them out just hands them back to lobbyists and gut feeling.

I dont want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there's a reading of futarchy on which both survive—say, prediction is fine but binding decision markets on repugnant outcomes are not; or the market is run by some nonprofit and traders can't cash out on bad outcomes; or the live metric has to be set higher up, like overall wellbeing, rather than deaths directly.

You're a philosopher who works on prediction markets, governance, and moral psychology. Start by brainstorming the strongest steelman of each side, take your time, and then give me a negotiated verdict rather than a one-sided take.
