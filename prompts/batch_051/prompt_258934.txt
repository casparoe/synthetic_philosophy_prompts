Build me a thought experiment where two traders, both in fact fully rational, honest Bayesians with a common prior and no liquidity or hedging needs, still both rationally trade on pure private information. The usual no-trade logic (the Milgrom–Stokey result) says that should be impossible if their rationality is common knowledge: each would read the other's willingness to trade as evidence about what they know and back out. So your scenario has to make rationality true but not common knowledge between them. That is, everyone in the case is actually rational, but at least one party has realistic second-order uncertainty about whether the other is rational, or about whether the other knows that they are. Constraints: ordinary workplace or market setting, one paragraph, no perfect predictors, no one lies or trades on inside information, and the information differences are mundane commercial knowledge. After the scenario, walk through exactly why the theorem would have blocked the trade had rationality been common knowledge, identify the precise feature of the case that breaks common knowledge, and say whether a defender of the no-trade theorem should treat your case as a counterexample or as a case the theorem simply never covered. The point is to get clear on whether the theorem's punch comes from rationality itself or from the common knowledge assumption.
