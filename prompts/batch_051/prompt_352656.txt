I'm a novelist, not a professional philosopher, but my current protagonist has spent three hundred pages asking himself whether he could will his maxim as a universal law, and somewhere around the Hakone subplot I realized I've never actually settled whether that move is doing real conceptual work or just decorating a gut reaction. So I've been poking through the decendants of that line of thought: Hare's idea that moral judgments are universalizable prescriptions, and Singer's way of turning "what if nobody did anything?" into pressure on the rest of us while someone drowns. I've got a claim I want stress-tested. My hunch is that, psychologically, when ordinary people ask "what if everyone did that?", the judgment they produce is mostly rationalization: they already feel the act is wrong (or fine), and the universalization test just drapes a consistency-looking outfit over a feeling that was there all along. In other words, it isn't working as an independent test at all.

Design the best empirical study you can to distinguish that from the rival view: that universalization is a genuine inference people actually perform, and that doing it can move their moral judgment rather than merely decorate it afterward. Tell me what result would convince you of each side, and what result would leave you still torn. Then be honest about the residue. Suppose the data came back clean — say, it's almost always post-hoc rationalization. What would that settle about whether universalizability is still a good moral demand, the way Singer wants to use it? Or is the normative question a seperate one that no experiment could reach, with the psychology just sitting beside it?
