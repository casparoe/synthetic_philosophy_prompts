I've been arguing with my sister about coincidences for years, and I keep reaching for the birthday paradox as a sort of universal solvent for "what are the odds" stories. You know the move: with 23 people in a room it's more likely than not that some pair shares a birthday, even though any particular pair is a 1/365 shot; so when she dreams about a person she hasn't thought of in years and they call ten minutes later, I tell her the space of possible coincidences—dreams matching calls, songs matching exes, strangers who know your hometown—is so enormous that something this striking was basically guaranteed to happen to somebody, and she just happens to be the somebody. I've internalized this enough that I reflexively discount any anecdote that starts with "what are the chances," and I'm fairly convinced this is the correct, debiased stance.

But lately I've been trying to formalize what the birthday calculation actually licenses, and I can't do it. If I treat her dream as a specified-in-advance event, the probability under "no mechanism" looks genuinly tiny, and a Bayesian should update. If I treat it as one member of a gigantic family of possible coincidences, the probability that some coincidence happens to someone is near one, and no update is warranted. Both framings feel principled, and I suspect the whole disagreement is quietly driven by which reference class each of us picks. So: does the birthday paradox actually show personal coincidences are almost never evidence, or does it only debunk a sloppy post-hoc way of being surprised? What would I check in a concrete anecdote to tell which situation I'm in without rigging the reference class? And tell me where the "coincidences are worthless" conclusion is most likely to go wrong—what consideration or argument would flip you the other way, and where your reasoning is most fragile.
