I've followed the debates about the European Union's democratic deficit for years, and they mostly argue past one another: one camp says the European Parliament is too weak, the other says only the member states can keep the Union legitimate. I want to approach the question as a problem in reverse-engineering a piece of institutional design rather than grading it.

Take the EU's ordinary legislative procedure, where the formal right of legislative initiative belongs almost entirely to the European Commission — an appointed body, not an elected one. The directly elected European Parliament can amend and reject legislative proposals and must agree with the Council for most legislation, but it cannot itself introduce a bill; it can formally request that the Commission propose something, and the Commission may decline and explain why. This near-monopoly on initiative coexists with treaty language presenting the Union as a representative democracy in which citizens are directly represented in the Parliament and member states are represented by their governments in the Council, and with the stated trajectory of ever closer union among the peoples of Europe.

What theory of democratic legitimacy does this distribution of agenda-setting authority presuppose? I want a reconstruction of the implicit view, not a verdict on whether it is defensible. In particular: does the design presuppose that there is a European interest distinct from the aggregation of national, ideological, or partisan preferences, and that an insulated appointed body is better placed to identify and advance it than an elected chamber is? Does it presuppose output legitimacy — legitimacy through effective problem-solving for the governed — rather than input legitimacy — legitimacy through elective authorization and responsiveness — or some specific hybrid of the two? Which rival views of democracy and sovereignty does it quietly rule out, and which does it merely appear to rule out: majoritarian parliamentarism on the model of national democracy, intergovernmentalism (the Union as an arrangement among sovereign states), deliberative supranationalism, consociationalism (power-sharing among segments rather than majority rule)?

Finally, be strict about the difference between what this practice necessarily presupposes and what it merely fits well with or permits. Several different theories, with different commitments about where sovereignty ultimately resides, might license the same institutional design; if so, say which features of the design would actually distinguish them, and what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed theory were swapped for one of its rivals.
