I'm in a course on church and state across England, Germany, France and the US, and something keeps bugging me whenever we talk about the German church tax and the Church of England's establishment. The defenders of these arrangements always say stuff like: nobody is forced to stay, you can leave the church, or you can just not participate, so its basically an inherited institutional default rather than something coercive. And that sounds almost exactly like how behavioral economists talk about default effects — the organ donor enrollment kind of finding, where people overwhelmingly stick with whatever option is preselected for them, even when switching is cheap. So now I can't figure out if established churches (and state-collected church taxes) are basically just defaults set by a very old choice architect, or if I'm pattern-matching between things that are only superficially similar.

I'd want the answer to actually check the mapping rather than just vibe with the analogy. What would have to be true on both sides for default effects to genuinely explain the persistence of state churches or church taxes? For example, if it's really a status-quo-bias story, then the key variable should be the cost of switching, and a prediction would be that making church deregistration as easy as ticking a box (which it deliberately isnt in Germany) should cause membership to drop a lot. Does that prediction actually hold up, or does it point somewhere the analogy breaks? Also, does anything from the nudge-ethics debate — like when a default counts as manipulation rather than mere convenience — transfer over to establishment law as a test for when state neutrality is real vs. just a default tilted toward the historically dominant church? And on the other side, where does the mapping clearly fail: legal disabilities for dissenters, the state's expressive endorsement of a religion, and church property held for centuries don't seem to have any real analogue in organ-donor checkboxes.

Also please dont make a contested position sound like the consensus — keep straight what's actually settled about defaults and about establishment, and what's still genuinely fought over by people who work on this.
