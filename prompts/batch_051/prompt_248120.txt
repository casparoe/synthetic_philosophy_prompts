Last weekend a few of us got into an argument about the word went, and I have been turning it over ever since. Elena remarked that it is strange that the past tense of go is went, since the two words share no sound at all. Marcus said that is just what it means for a verb to be irregular: English has a rule that the past tense is formed by adding -ed, and went is one of the stored exceptions to that rule, in the same family as ran and sang. Ravi objected that this is not a harmless way of putting it. An exception, he said, only makes sense relative to a rule that was supposed to cover the case, and the -ed rule was never supposed to cover go. Historically went was the past tense of wend, and English pressed it into service to fill the past-tense slot for go, much as am, is, and are fill in the present tense of be. So he claimed went is not an exception to the -ed rule at all; it is a different word filling a hole in the verb's forms. Calling it an exception to the -ed rule, he said, is like calling a tricycle an exception to the rule that bicycles have two wheels: the rule is true of bicycles, and a tricycle is simply a different kind of thing. Marcus replied that this analogy misses the point, because a tricycle is not a bicycle, but go is a verb, and the rule was stated over verbs. If you redefine the rule as covering only verbs that take -ed, he said, then of course nothing counts as an exception, but that is just defining the problem away. Real descriptions of a language have to be a rule plus a list: the rule does the productive work, and the list holds the memorized leftovers. Children show this by saying goed before they learn went, which proves that the -ed rule really is applying to go in their heads and that went is a violation of it. Ravi answered that goed only shows children must learn which forms go with which verbs, not that the adult grammar treats went as a broken rule; the adult system could equally be described as having a regular class, a vowel-changing class, and a small set of verbs that borrow their forms from elsewhere, with no single rule that go violates. He added that if every non--ed form is called an exception, then the rule is doing so little work that it is not worth calling a rule. By then nobody could referee the argument, least of all me.
