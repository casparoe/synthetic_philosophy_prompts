Someone in a discussion about content moderation was arguing that the people who write and enforce the rules shouldn't be blamed as individuals — they're basically little Eichmanns, thoughtless functionaries inside a system, and the real evil is structural. The analogy was doing a lot of work in that conversation and I couldn't tell if it deserved to. Could you take it apart? Give me the correspondence table: what in the moderation case is supposed to play the role of Eichmann, of the desk, of the trains, of the victims, of the trial itself? Then show where the mapping starts to bend or break. In particular I keep thinking of features of Arendt's actual account that people tend to skip: her emphasis on the absence of thinking and judgment, the fact that the trial made a person answer face-to-face rather than dissolving responsibility into a system, the fact that Eichmann sat inside a totalitarian apparatus rather than a private company with terms of service, and that she still thought he was responsible and should answer for what he did as an individual. Which of those gaps matter for the argument the guy was making and which don't? The conclusion he wanted was: don't blame the individual moderators or policy writers, blame the system. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing from the start? If it's a broken analogy, what would a better one look like?
