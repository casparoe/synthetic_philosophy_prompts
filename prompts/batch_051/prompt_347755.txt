Suppose the leadership of a large psychiatric hospital becomes persuaded by eliminative materialism: the view that beliefs, desires, and the other propositional attitudes are not real states of the brain but posits of a folk theory that a mature neuroscience will discard. The hospital therefore adopts a documentation rule. Case notes, treatment plans, and team meetings may no longer use mental-state verbs such as believes, wants, intends, hopes, or fears, except inside quotation marks, where they count strictly as reports of the patient's own speech. Staff must instead describe behavior, environmental contingencies, and, where available, neural measures. Treat the rule as a mechanism, not as a philosophical position to defend or attack. What happens to clinical communication once staff must predict what a patient will do without saying what the patient wants? How quickly do the forbidden generalizations return disguised as "tends to," "responds to," or "oriented toward," and what does that disguise do to the rule's purpose? Where does the rule break first—risk assessment, insurance forms, informed consent, legal testimony, or informal handover between shifts—and can those breaks be patched without reintroducing folk psychology? Finally, does the quotation-mark exception collapse under pressure, since transcribing and acting on a patient's reported words seems to require attributing meaning to them?
