In my high school's philosophy club, someone recently brought up the doomsday argument, and I have spent the last few weeks reading whatever I could find online about anthropic reasoning. The distinction that keeps tripping me up is between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption. As I understand them, SSA says I should reason as though I were randomly selected from all the observers who actually exist, while SIA says that my own existence is itself evidence in favor of hypotheses on which there are many observers rather than few.

Here is the part I cannot get straight. Any hypothesis on which I never exist is already off the table from my point of view: I could not be sitting here considering the hypothesis unless I existed. That means conditioning on my own existence is not an extra empirical update that one of these principles performs and the other neglects; it is a shared precondition for doing the reasoning at all. So once both SSA and SIA have taken that step, it seems their remaining probabilities should coincide, which would make the dispute between them—including the famous presumptuous philosopher case—a disagreement about notation rather than about how to actually revise one's beliefs. Is that right? If I am missing a real residual difference, could you explain exactly where it enters, and, if possible, walk me through a concrete example like the doomsday argument where the two assumptions assign different numbers after both have conditioned on my existence?
