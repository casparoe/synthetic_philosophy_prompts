William James in The Moral Equivalent of War argues that a peaceful society still needs the martial virtues—obedience, group loyalty, hardihood, willingness to defer to a common cause—and that modern war is too destructive to remain the institution that supplies them. His proposed replacement is a peacetime conscription of young people into an army enlisted against nature: mining, fishing, road-building, dangerous civic work under military discipline but with no external enemy. Rather than asking whether this is a good idea, assume a reasonably wealthy democracy actually passes it: every eighteen-year-old must serve two years in this Civic Labor Corps, organized into units with ranks, uniforms, group competitions, and harsh conditions, but no weapons and no enemy to kill. Walk through how the thing would actually behave. What would recruits and officers optimize for, how would status get allocated, who would try to get out or game the system, and which failure mode shows up first? In particular, would it preserve martial virtues or just produce the peacetime equivalents of a bureaucracy, a hazing hierarchy, or a political jobs program? What second-order effects would it have on the labor market, on ideas about masculinity, and on the state's ability to mobilize people later?
