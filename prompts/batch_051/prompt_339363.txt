I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I'm stuck on a paper about biological individuality. I've been using a fairly standard-looking list of criteria for what makes something an organism, and I want to see whether it survives contact with a case I can't stop thinking about. The criteria are something like: (1) physiological integration — the parts are coupled tightly enough to maintain homeostasis and coordinated responses; (2) genetic unity — all the cells, or nearly all modulo somatic mutation, share one genome; (3) a reproductive bottleneck — each generation starts from a single cell or at least a narrow unified propagule; (4) functional specialization with loss of part-autonomy — the parts do specialized jobs and can't persist as independent units; and (5) a self/non-self boundary, usually cashed out immunologically.

The case: a coral holobiont — the animal polyp, its intracellular photosynthetic algae (zooxanthellae), and its associated bacteria. The algae translocate fixed carbon to the polyp, the polyp supplies nitrogen and shelter, the bacteria mediate nutrient cycling, and bleaching shows how poorly the polyp does without its algae; on paper that's very tightly integrated. But the partners come from different branches of life, have completely separate genomes, reproduce mostly on their own schedules (in many corals the next generation of polyps acquires its algae from the water rather than inheriting them from the parent), and the microbial membership shifts with temperature and depth. So it seems to score high on integration and specialization but probably fails genetic unity and the bottleneck. Then there's the Portuguese man o' war as a comparison, which is the reverse pattern: one zygote buds into a colony of specialized, non-independent zooids, so it sails through the genetic and bottleneck criteria but almost everyone still calls it a colony rather than an organism. That makes me suspect our everyday category and the criteria aren't really tracking the same thing.

Go through the criteria honestly for the coral holobiont, and use the man o' war where it helps. Where the answer comes out no, say whether that shows the criterion is doing real work or whether the criterion is smuggling in something the case reveals. I mostly want to know: is the holobiont an organism under these criteria, and if the criteria say no, is the right move to revise the criteria or bite the bullet and say the holobiont isn't one? Please also separate the settled empirical biology from what's genuinely contested among people working on biological individuality, and don't present one side of the debate — holobionts are obviously organisms or obviously not — as the consensus when it isn't.
