Suppose that, starting tomorrow, a cheap, portable, and reliable medical technology becomes available that can fully revive a person at any point up to 24 hours after their heartbeat and breathing have stopped, restoring them to their previous level of health with no lasting brain injury; after 24 hours, revival is impossible. Assume that the law continues to treat a person whose heartbeat and breathing have stopped as dead, and that physicians keep certifying death on those familiar clinical signs rather than waiting to confirm that the condition is irreversible. The technology becomes widely available within a few years, and its limits are well understood by the public.

In a wealthy liberal democracy, predict how the legal and ethical practice of assisted dying, terminal sedation (keeping a dying patient deeply and continuously sedated until death), and advance directives would look twenty years later. I want the chain of reasoning rather than only a conclusion: name the medical, legal, psychological, institutional, and economic mechanisms through which the change would work, and for each major prediction say how robust you take it to be and what assumptions it rests on — that is, what would have to turn out to be false for the prediction to be wrong. Address in particular:

- How does the objection that euthanasia permanently forecloses the patient's future change, once being dead can be a temporary and curable state within the 24-hour window?
- Does the moral and legal difference between withdrawing life-sustaining treatment and actively causing death shift, given that a patient who dies after withdrawal might now be revived?
- Does the doctrine of double effect — roughly, the idea that giving pain relief that foreseeably hastens death can be permissible when death is not intended — become more or less central to end-of-life law?
- Would assisted-dying statutes keep a terminal-illness requirement and mandatory waiting periods, and how would safeguards change when the operative line is less life/death than reversible death/irreversible death?
- What new inequalities, abuses, or incentives might arise — for instance, around who is monitored during the revivable window, who may refuse revival, or how insurers and hospitals treat the temporarily dead?

End by indicating where your confidence is lowest and what considerations, if they turned out differently, would most change your answer.
