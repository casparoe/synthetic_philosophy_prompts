I am putting together an exercise for an upper-level ethics seminar on the dispute between moral particularism and moral generalism, and I would like a sorting instrument in the form of a decision tree. The tree should take a student from a single opening question about a concrete case — not about theory — to a leaf that names their position. Please build the tree from the logical space first, rather than from a list of labels: fix your branching questions, then locate the familiar positions within the resulting combinations, and, for any combination that is empty, say whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unstable, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Each branch should be labeled by the specific claim or answer that splits it, and every node should be phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to.

The tree should separate three things that textbook treatments routinely run together. The first is invariance of valence: whether a consideration that counts in favor of an act in one context must count the same way in every context, or may count against — or not at all — in another. The second is codifiability: whether a finite, statable set of principles could capture what makes actions right, regardless of whether any single principle is exceptionless. The third is the epistemic question: whether principles are needed for us to discern or justify rightness, rather than being needed only for the action's being right. Your tree should keep these axes distinct and should not make the inference from holism to particularism automatic; leave room for a position that accepts holism about reasons but holds that the context-dependence can itself be codified by principles that specify enablers and disablers.

Please include at least the following leaves: an exceptionless or absolutist generalism; a Ross-style pluralism of prima facie duties, each of which always counts morally even though what one ought to do all-things-considered depends on the whole situation; a contextualist generalism that accommodates holism through codified enabling conditions; a Dancy-style particularism that rejects codification outright and treats moral judgment as a skill of discerning the salient features of a situation; and a weaker, rules-of-thumb particularism that rejects codification of rightness but allows local heuristic generalizations. If a pair of leaves cannot be separated by a clean question, flag that pair and explain where the two views blur into each other. Aim for roughly seven to ten branch nodes and five to seven leaves, and keep the whole instrument teachable in a fifty-minute class.
