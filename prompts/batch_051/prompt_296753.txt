I write about science and ideas for a general audience. Most weeks I publish pieces that turn on a contested empirical or conceptual claim — whether intermittent fasting slows ageing, whether a particular result is causal, whether an AI system understands anything in a substantive sense. I have come to think that my working method for handling expert disagreement — quote the other side and move on — is not intellectually defensible. I want a procedure I can actually run on deadline for deciding how much a piece of disagreement should move my confidence in a claim before I publish.

I know the philosophical territory is roughly the debate between conciliationism and steadfastness: some hold that when someone who seems just as likely as you to be right disagrees, you ought to move substantially toward them; others hold that you may retain your view if you are the one in possession of the first-order evidence. I do not want an abstract adjudication of that debate. I want it translated into an editorial procedure I can use repeatedly.

Build it around these working conditions. I usually have between thirty minutes and half a day to resolve a flagged disagreement. I am not a domain expert in most of the topics I cover. Sometimes the other party is a named scientist with a serious reputation; sometimes it is a fellow journalist with no special expertise. An editor must sign off before publication, and the procedure should therefore output something usable, such as 'publish as written', 'qualify the claim', or 'hold the piece', rather than merely instructing me to reflect further. The error costs run both ways: I do not want to pass on overstated findings, and I do not want to manufacture false balance by treating every fringe objection as a reason to walk back a well-supported claim.

The procedure should tell me, in order: what to establish about the other party and about my own basis for the claim before I adjust at all; how to determine whether I am dealing with an epistemic peer rather than someone whose relevant track record is markedly worse or better than mine; how to treat the case where I have read the underlying study myself and the other person clearly has not, as against the case where we have read exactly the same material; and what to do when I cannot assign numbers to anyone's reliability. It should also distinguish, in practical terms, between disagreement about the evidence itself and disagreement about what weight the evidence deserves, and between a bare judgement from the other party and reasons I can evaluate on their merits.

Apply the procedure to three cases from my actual work.

First, a colleague with a comparable track record is working on the same deadline with the same press release and the same journal article in front of us. The colleague concludes that a 300-person observational study of daily multivitamin use justifies a headline about reduced cardiovascular risk, while I conclude that it does not.

Second, a well-credentialed academic writes to me that the central claim in a piece I have filed — that a particular climate feedback is weaker than is usually assumed — is not supported by the paper I cite. The academic has read the full paper. I have interviewed the paper's lead author and read the abstract, but I have not read the paper itself.

Third, a senior editor with decades of experience disagrees with the way I have characterised a philosophical dispute in a feature. The editor offers no reasons, only a verdict. I have done the interviews and read the primary texts myself.

For at least one of these cases, I suspect a rigorous procedure will tell me to do something I would rather not do. Tell me where that happens and why.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do. It should not pretend that every step can be made mechanical. Mark the points at which editorial judgement re-enters, and say which of those points a checklist can at best discipline but not eliminate.
