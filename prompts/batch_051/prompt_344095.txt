My sister and I recently became jointly responsible for our parents' medical and financial affairs. After we disagreed about one of the decisions, she said that my reserve was not a harmless personal style but a way of shutting her out: if I genuinely regarded her as an equal in a decision we had to make together, I would make the reasons, doubts, and reservations behind my position legible to her instead of asking her to trust me. As she pressed it, the argument looked like this.

(1) Lying to someone is wrong because it substitutes one person's judgment for another's, depriving the other of facts they need in order to reach their own view.
(2) Withholding a mental state that could affect what the other person would think or do accomplishes by omission what lying accomplishes by commission; reticence is only slower deception.
(3) There is no principled place to stop, since any unstated reason, doubt, regret, or mixed motive might matter to someone who has to decide whether to rely on one.
(4) So the wrongness of lying extends to a general duty to make oneself legible to anyone who is a partner to a decision in which one's conduct affects them.
(5) Taken to its full extent, this duty requires something close to full transparency: disclosing passing resentments, private embarrassments, doubts about the relationship itself, and judgments the other person would find wounding.
(6) But that kind of transparency is not merely exhausting; it would corrode the very cooperation and affection it is meant to serve.

I want to keep both (1) and (6), because the wrongness of lying and the unlivability of total transparency each seem to me true and important. That pushes me toward rejecting one of (2) through (5), yet each step looks solid when I inspect it alone. Tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does, or which premise I should give up and what that costs someone who still wants an honest relationship. Please answer in American English.
