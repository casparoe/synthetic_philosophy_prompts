I teach moral philosophy, and I have been turning over a problem about the familiar what-if-everyone-did-that test. In its canonical forms — Kant's Formula of Universal Law, Hare's universal prescriptivism, and the way later writers such as Singer put the test to work in practical ethics — the test asks us to evaluate an individual act by imagining it performed by everyone. The motivating examples, though, all seem to be additive. If everyone stole, property would dissolve; if everyone made false promises, promising would become impossible; if everyone evaded taxes, the institutions taxes support would fail. In each case the universalized world is just the individual act writ large, and that is what makes the contradiction or the bad outcome visible.

But many contemporary cases do not have that additive shape, and I suspect the argument was quietly built for an additive world. Suppose a driver takes a back-road shortcut whose only advantage is that most other drivers are not on it; if everyone took the shortcut, it would be slower than the road it bypasses. Or suppose an investor buys an asset because it is undervalued; if everyone followed the same rule, the undervaluation would disappear and the act would no longer deliver its intended benefit. Here the universalized world cancels the very property the individual act depends on, rather than magnifying the act. Congestion, network effects, algorithmic coordination, and tipping points make this a standing feature of contemporary social and economic life, not a marginal curiosity. I want to know whether the universalizability argument survives those cases. The pressure could fall on a hidden premise — that universal performance yields a coherent, scaled-up version of the individual act — or it could fall only on careless act-description, in which case the test survives once we specify maxims carefully, such as taking the shortcut only when others are not taking it. My question is: does the Kantian/Harean/Singer-style test actually go through in threshold-type cases, and if it needs repair, what exactly does the repair preserve and what does it give up?

Please answer as a moral philosopher who works on Kantian ethics and its prescriptivist and consequentialist heirs. Before committing yourself, sketch the two or three most plausible ways the argument could come out — that the test fails outright, that it survives only with a repair, or that it was never genuinely threatened. Then reconstruct the argument's premises, test each premise against the nonlinear cases, and separate the universalizability question from the act-description question; resist settling for a bare taxonomy. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
