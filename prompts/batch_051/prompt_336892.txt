I am a PhD student in philosophy of science working on projectibility, and I have been collecting my own judgments about which observed regularities may rationally be extended to unobserved cases. I am not raising the global skeptical problem about induction; assume for present purposes that some projections are rationally permissible and others are not. What I want is a principle, or a small set of principles, that sorts them.

Let the present time fall before 2050. Here are four cases and my verdicts.

(1) Every emerald examined so far has been green. I accept the projection that emeralds first examined after 2050 will be green.

(2) Call an object grue just in case it is green if first examined before 2050 and blue if first examined after 2050. Every emerald examined so far is grue, since all have been first examined before 2050 and all are green. I reject the projection that emeralds are grue generally, which would commit me to expecting emeralds first examined after 2050 to be blue. I reject this even though the evidence supporting the grue projection is extensionally identical to the evidence supporting the green projection in (1).

(3) A laboratory manufactures crystals containing a compound that irreversibly changes in 2049. When tested before 2050, a crystal yields a green reading; when tested after 2050, the same test yields a blue reading. Call a crystal chromic just in case it would yield a green reading if first tested before 2050 and a blue reading if first tested after 2050. Every crystal tested so far was first tested before 2050 and yielded green, hence was chromic. I accept the projection that crystals first tested after 2050 will be chromic, that is, will yield blue readings. I accept this even though chromic has the same time-indexed form as grue.

(4) A survey of a lake samples fish with a net that cannot retain fish two centimetres or shorter. Every fish collected is longer than two centimetres. I reject the projection that all fish in the lake are longer than two centimetres, even though the predicate is simple and familiar, because the sampling method alone guarantees the observed regularity.

These four verdicts leave me unable to state a single rationale. They suggest that projectibility is not a matter of sameness of evidence, syntactic simplicity, or familiarity of the predicate, but rather something about whether the regularity is underwritten by a mechanism or a genuine kind. Yet that is only a slogan, and I cannot currently make it precise.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures these four verdicts? State it precisely and show step by step how it delivers each one. Then apply it to a further case on which I have no settled judgment: ornithologists define alboise as white if first observed before 2050 and black if first observed after 2050; every petrel observed so far was first observed before 2050 and was alboise; and there is no known mechanism connecting petrel plumage to the date of observation. Should I project alboise? Finally, say honestly which of my four verdicts, if any, your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the principle or the verdict should give way.
