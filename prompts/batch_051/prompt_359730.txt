A liberal democracy has decided that a single public-service AI will make routine allocation decisions—places in public schools, social housing, policing priorities, triage in public health, and comparable goods—and that this AI's objective function must be set by citizens rather than by engineers or ministers. The following mechanism is proposed.

Each citizen is issued an annual endowment of one hundred value tokens. A standing commission draws up a fixed list of evaluative criteria—welfare, autonomy, equality, dignity, social trust, care, cultural continuity, and the like—revised only by supermajority. At any time during the year, a citizen may permanently burn tokens on one criterion; a criterion's weight in the AI's objective grows with the square root of the tokens burned on it, so the first token buys a full unit of weight and the hundredth buys far less than the first. At year's end the weights are summed, normalized to one, and published, and for the following year the AI maximizes that weighted sum across all citizens in every decision it makes.

Two safeguards are attached. First, no criterion may finish a year with more than forty percent of total weight; any surplus above forty percent is discarded and the remaining weights are renormalized. Second, what matters is only the act of burning—there is no token to sell or transfer—so provenance is irrelevant.

Analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. Walk through the incentives it creates, with attention to: (a) who burns tokens most reliably and on which criteria; (b) whether concave weighting protects minority concerns better than a simply proportional vote, and whether the forty-percent cap protects minorities or merely shifts power among pluralities; (c) where it is gamed first—strategic burning to offset a rival criterion, mobilization campaigns that frame burning as a moral duty, attempts to induce the commission to redefine the criterion list so that one substantive position is split across several criteria or crowded out by relabeling; (d) the standing of citizens whose values appear nowhere on the list, and whether the state has inadvertently endorsed a comprehensive moral doctrine—the problem liberal neutrality is meant to avoid—by optimizing a weighted sum of substantive criteria; and (e) second-order effects, such as the annual publication of the weights becoming a focus of partisan contest or the rise of value entrepreneurs who build careers narrating the weights. Conclude with the failure modes that are fixable without abandoning the mechanism and those that are not.
