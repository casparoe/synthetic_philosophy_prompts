I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and I'm teaching a small undergraduate seminar this term on the martial virtues — courage, valor, honor, discipline — and what becomes of them in a society that doesn't go to war. We've spent a few weeks on the classical picture: Aristotle's idea that courage is a mean concerning fear and confidence, and that its paradigmatic exercise is facing a noble death in battle. The class is split on whether peacetime courage is even the same virtue or just a borrowed label, and we keep running into the worry that in a peaceful society the martial virtues either atrophy or get domesticated into moral courage, which may not be the same virtue at all. I'd like to close the last session with a poem that does real philosophical work rather than just decorating the point.

Could you write a Petrarchan sonnet in the voice of a soldier who has come home to a peaceful country and is trying to figure out what to do with the virtue he was trained for? The octave should render the martial picture of courage — facing death in battle for something worth dying for, the mean between cowardice and rashness — and the volta at line nine should turn to the peacetime problem: if the virtue is defined by its battlefield object, what is its object when there is no battlefield? The sestet shouldn't just relable everyday caution, risk-taking, or stubborness as courage, and I'd rather it leave the problem unresolved than resolve it too neatly. The turn should do the conceptual work — the shift from the old paradigm to the question of whether the paradigm can survive its own success.
