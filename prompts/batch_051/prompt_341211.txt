You are a professional moral and political philosopher who works on the ethics of cooperation and collective action. Think through the issue carefully, and before reaching a verdict, begin by disentangling the distinct claims being made and the different senses of terms like "free rider" and "parasite." Do not cite external sources or provide a bibliography; engage only with the arguments as stated.

At a dinner after a departmental retreat, two colleagues got into an argument about a third colleague, Nadia. Nadia is an active researcher who submits papers, relies on published work, and routinely benefits from the peer-review system, but she has not reviewed a manuscript or sat on a committee in years, despite being asked repeatedly. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Daniel said Nadia is a free rider and arguably a parasite: she takes a steady stream of reviewing and committee labor from others while contributing none of her own, and the system exists only because enough people do their share.

Amara replied that she could not identify a victim. If Nadia stopped existing, no specific person would be worse off in any measurable way; if she started reviewing tomorrow, no specific person would be better off. A wrong without a victim, she said, is not obviously a wrong at all.

Daniel said that was too crude. The wrong is not a harm but an unfairness. Nadia accepts the benefits of a cooperative practice on known terms while exempting herself from its costs; reviewers evaluate her work against the background expectation that working researchers reciprocate.

Amara answered that a benefit one never asked for cannot impose a debt. Nadia did not create the terms of the practice, and the fact that others choose to volunteer cannot conscript her into volunteering.

Daniel responded that Nadia does ask for the benefit: submitting a paper is a request for reviewers' unpaid labor, not a sandwich pressed into her hand. She knowingly goes through the benefit side of the line while dodging the contribution side.

Amara granted that Nadia seeks the benefit, but said this only makes her ingratitude more explicit. Seeking and receiving a benefit can ground gratitude and perhaps a norm of collegiality, but not an enforceable obligation of fairness unless she consented to the scheme. Absent such consent, what remains is that she fails to benefit others, which is different from harming them. She added that every reviewer can decline and no one is under Nadia's control, so the word "parasite" simply replaces the unresolved harm problem with an inflammatory word.

Daniel replied that the parasitism lies not in controlling any particular person but in occupying a social role that presupposes a labor pool she refuses to join. The free rider and the parasite are the same failure at different scales.

Amara closed by saying that Daniel's fairness argument still depends on showing why Nadia's non-contribution wrongs anyone. If the difference her contribution would make is effectively zero, and there is no agreement she entered, then Daniel is left with no victim and no consent—nothing but a judgment that she is ungenerous.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. Who is right about which specific claims? Is there a principled difference between a free rider and a social parasite, or is the latter just the former at a larger scale? What, if anything, are both of them missing? In particular, consider whether the strength of the fairness objection depends on whether the benefit is excludable, whether one person's consumption diminishes the supply, or whether participation in the practice is genuinely voluntary.
