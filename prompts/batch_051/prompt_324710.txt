I've been stuck on a running disagreement with friends about whether the killing/letting-die distinction survives once you taboo the words (that is, once you state the boundary without using “kill,” “let die,” or their synonyms) and ask what non-verbal difference the label is actually tracking. The standard bioethics cases feel too clean to settle anything, and the conversation keeps running into the same two intuitions without producing a criterion. I want a working criterion that separates doing from allowing — or, failing that, a definition of “killing” that draws a defensible line against “letting die” — and that does not break down on messier cases where the relevant “agent” is an institution, a policy, or an algorithm.

Please test your criterion against these cases: (1) a doctor disconnects a ventilator from a patient with no prospect of recovery, and the patient dies; (2) a doctor administers a lethal injection at a consenting terminally ill patient’s request under a permissive law; (3) a pandemic triage protocol reallocates a ventilator from a patient with low survival odds to one with high odds, and the first patient dies; (4) a company keeps selling a drug after learning it causes a rare fatal side effect, while a second company discontinues an unprofitable rescue drug and a dependent patient dies; (5) a person declines to donate a kidney to a sibling who then dies of renal failure; (6) a parent withholds food from an infant, who starves; (7) a self-driving car is programmed to swerve away from five pedestrians and strike one pedestrian, who dies; (8) a government cuts a child-nutrition program during a recession, and child mortality rises by an amount its budget office had predicted; (9) a passerby does nothing while a stranger drowns in a shallow pond.

My suspicion is that the distinction is doing two jobs — a structural one about whether a continuous causal chain from the agent’s body intersects the death, and a moral one about how much blame attaches — and I expect the triage-algorithm and drug-company cases to pull those apart. I’d like you to propose a definition, run it against each numbered case, say explicitly where it yields the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Put your final one-sentence criterion on the last line, beginning with “Verdict:”.
