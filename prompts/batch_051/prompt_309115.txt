I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I run a reading group at work on the concepts we quietly rely on in reliability engineering. I'd like you to build a short sorting quiz—a "which account of dispositions do you actually operate with?" instrument—but one that sorts people by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they like.

The background, as I understand it. In engineering we often talk about a system's dispositions through bare counterfactuals: the service is crash-prone under malformed input just in case, if it received malformed input, it would crash. I gather philosophers call this the conditional analysis of dispositions. I've also run into two problem cases for it. In a fink case, the triggering event itself removes the disposition before it can manifest—a component that senses the test probe and disables itself is the engineering analogue; the counterfactual "if probed, it would fail" comes out false even though the component was disposed to fail before the probe arrived. In a mask case, the disposition is present but outside interference blocks the manifestation: a fragile glass wrapped in packing foam still fails to break when struck, but we don't want to say it wasn't fragile. The interesting question, for me, is how much work we should make the bare counterfactual do and what is "really there" when it gives the wrong answer.

Please write the quiz and its scoring key. Requirements:

- Audience: working software and reliability engineers with no philosophy background. Define "disposition," "fink," and "mask" in a short preamble rather than assuming them.
- Seven questions, each a short concrete case—mix ordinary examples with fault-injection and reliability analogues—with two or three answer options. No question should ask which theoretical view sounds right or mention a label.
- Include at least one case about a disposition that never manifests in any actual run because a permanent mask is always present, asking whether the property is still there. I want that question to push people past verbal formulas.
- The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least five positions, described in plain terms: (a) bare conditional analysis, on which x is disposed to M under S just in case x would M if S; (b) ceteris-paribus conditional analysis, on which the counterfactual holds only under normal, interference-free conditions; (c) intrinsic causal-basis view, on which the disposition is a real property of the object that grounds the counterfactuals; (d) a powers view, on which dispositions are fundamental and directed, and finks and masks are just cases where a power is blocked from manifesting; and (e) an eliminativist view, on which disposition talk is useful shorthand for categorical properties plus laws. If these five don't exhaust the patterns your questions produce, add only positions your questions genuinely separate.
- Build from the logical space rather than from the names. First identify the two or three diagnostic questions doing the work—candidate questions are whether the object keeps the disposition when the manifestation is blocked; whether it keeps the disposition when the stimulus itself would destroy it; and whether the "normal conditions" are fixed by the world or by conversational context. Then locate the positions as combinations of answers, mark the combinations no position occupies, and say briefly whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If two positions fall into the same combination and the quiz cannot tell them apart, flag that rather than forcing a false distinction.
- No citations, bibliography, or reading list; I want the instrument itself.
- Hard limit: 1,000 words for the entire deliverable.
