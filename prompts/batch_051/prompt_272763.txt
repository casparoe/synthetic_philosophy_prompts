I am a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I teach a third-year undergraduate module on determinism and explanation. In two weeks I have a 90-minute seminar with sixteen students, and I want to replace the usual lecture with a structured game. The session is the entry point to our unit on chaos, and the students should come away feeling, before the conceptual vocabulary is handed to them, that a rule can be completely deterministic — each state fixing exactly one successor state — and yet practically unpredictable when the present state is known only to finite precision. I want them to see why predictability is an epistemic matter of how much a measurement reveals, how determinism and predictability come apart, and why none of this amounts to indeterminism.

You are an expert in philosophy of physics and in designing active-learning exercises for philosophy. Please think carefully about the mechanics before settling on a single plan; if it helps, brainstorm two or three candidate game structures first and then commit to the strongest one. Deliver a single runnable activity, with the reasoning left implicit except where I need to understand a design choice.

Design a finite-precision forecasting game along these lines, adapting freely if you can improve on it:

- Participants work in small groups, each using a common deterministic rule that is simple enough to compute by hand or with a basic calculator. Something like a decimal digit-shift rule or a doubling-and-fractional-part rule seems promising; choose the rule and justify the choice.
- Each group is shown its current state only to a fixed number of decimal places. Behind each shown state there is a fuller true state known only to the facilitator, so no amount of cleverness will let a group recover the hidden digits.
- Groups wager points, not real money, on forecasts about the state after various numbers of steps. The set of forecast horizons should straddle the point at which the shown digits run out, so that groups succeed reliably on the near side and cannot outperform chance on the far side. Please specify the betting and payoff scheme so that overconfidence is genuinely costly without being punitive.
- The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the conceptual distinctions: determinism as a unique-successor property of the rule; sensitive dependence on initial conditions and the divergence of near-identical starting states; the resulting predictability horizon; the demand for something like Laplace's demon's infinite precision; and why the observed unpredictability does not show that the rule is random or that the world is indeterministic.
