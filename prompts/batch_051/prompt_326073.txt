I have been reading F. H. Bradley, *Appearance and Reality*, and I would like help unpacking a passage from Chapter III, ‘Relation and Quality’. I do not have the text to hand, so I shall give a close paraphrase rather than a quotation. Bradley asks us to consider two terms, A and B, which stand in a relation C. He then argues as follows. If C is a real constituent of the complex alongside A and B, then C must itself be related to A, and to B, by further relations D and E. But then D and E will in turn require relations to their terms, and the process never ends. He takes this to show that no relation, conceived as an independent real thing, can account for the unity of its terms, and on that basis he concludes that relations, as ordinarily understood, are appearance rather than reality.

My difficulty is in seeing precisely what this regress is supposed to show about the unity of a proposition or judgement. Could you help me with three things? First, distinguish two readings of Bradley’s argument: (a) the weaker methodological reading, on which the regress shows only that we must not treat a relation as if it were another term of the same kind as its relata; and (b) the stronger metaphysical reading, on which the regress shows that any attempt to explain unity by positing a relating constituent is doomed, because the very form of explanation reintroduces the problem. Which reading is better supported by the passage as paraphrased, and why? Second, apply the distinction to a simple proposition such as ‘Socrates is wise’. If we analyse this proposition as a complex of Socrates, the quality wisdom, and an instantiation relation, does Bradley’s regress undermine that analysis? Be precise about where in the analysis the regress would arise, if it does. Third, suppose a philosopher replies that the regress is benign, because a relation can relate its relata without standing in further relations to them. Is that reply adequate against the stronger reading, or does it simply refuse to answer the question of how the relation does its relating? I am especially interested in whether Bradley’s own conclusion—that relations are unreal—follows, or whether one can accept the regress while retaining relational facts as fundamental.
