You are a philosopher of causation and historical explanation who moonlights as an editorial consultant, and you should think carefully before you write anything. I'm a senior editor at a publishing house that produces high-school and first-year-college history textbooks, and every few weeks we have the same fight: an author submits a chapter that explains a major episode, and the reviews come back mutually contradictory. One reviewer says the chapter is monocausal and ignores structural preconditions; another says it's laundry-list history that treats a dozen "factors" as equally important; a third says the author mistook a trigger for a cause and a long-run trend for background. I need a reusable procedure — not a one-off ruling — that my editors can run on any chapter to decide which causes to keep as causes, which to demote to background conditions, which to cut, and which to flag as "legitimate explanatory choice, but state the contrast question." These chapters go into books teachers actually teach from, so a bad causal explanation ends up in a thousand classrooms, and my managing editor won't sign off on anything that smells like editorial taste.

Ground the procedure in whatever the philosophy of causation and historical explanation actually licenses — counterfactual dependence, INUS conditions (a cause is often an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition), overdetermination, preemption, and the idea that "the cause" is relative to a contrast (why war in 1914 rather than 1913? versus why war rather than peace?). But it also has to be runnable by editors with no philosophy training in under, say, fourty-five minutes per chapter, and it has to be honest about where the answer is a judgment call dressed up as a rule.

Please write the procedure as a step-by-step checklist: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "cut" or "demote" or "flag," where it hands the decision to a person, and what the procedure cannot settle. Then run it on three worked examples: (1) a chapter that explains the outbreak of World War I almost entirely through the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, (2) a chapter that explains the 2008 financial crisis as, in effect, caused by subprime mortgage lending, and (3) a chapter that attributes the abolition of the British slave trade to William Wilberforce. Make sure at least one worked example produces an answer the asker won't like — cutting a compelling story or keeping a boring structural cause the editor would rather drop — and don't soften that.
