I'm currently taking a course on mechanistic explanation in the sciences, with particular attention to how researchers argue about whether an anomalous result reveals a different mechanism or merely shows the same mechanism operating under unusual conditions. A parallel from computer science keeps striking me as more than a metaphor, but I cannot tell whether it is a genuine structural identity or just pattern-matching. In the scientific cases, two systems with different physical parts are often said to run the same mechanism so long as the organization of the relevant entities and activities is preserved. This sounds like the familiar CS hierarchy of function, algorithm, and implementation: two programs can compute the same function while using different algorithms, and two programs can instantiate the same algorithm while differing in their primitive operations or concrete realization. I want to know whether "the same mechanism" in philosophy of science maps onto one of those levels, or whether the hierarchy fails to capture what scientists mean. If a mechanism is like an algorithm rather than like an implementation, what plays the role of the primitive operations, and what is the analogue of extensional equivalence—is it that two systems produce the same phenomenon, or that they produce the same pattern of responses across interventions? And where does the analogy come apart? An algorithm is usually thought to be substrate-independent, whereas in biology the material properties of the parts often appear to matter for what the mechanism does. Does that spoil the parallel, or does it only show that biological mechanisms are algorithms tightly coupled to a substrate? I would prefer an account of exactly where the mapping holds and where it fails to one that simply calls the analogy illuminating.
