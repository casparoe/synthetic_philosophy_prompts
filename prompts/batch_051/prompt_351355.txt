A colleague of mine works on moral worth — the question of when a right action is creditable to an agent because of why she did it, rather than because of its consequences. Over several months I have kept a list of her verdicts on particular cases, and I want to know what she would say about a new case I plan to put to her.

Her recorded verdicts so far:

1. A shopkeeper charges fair prices and gives accurate change only because he thinks an honest reputation is profitable. Verdict: no moral worth.

2. A taxpayer reports her full income only because she is afraid of an audit. Verdict: no moral worth.

3. A wealthy donor gives a large public gift to a hospital only in order to be admired and invited to gala dinners. Verdict: no moral worth.

4. A woman finds a wallet full of cash and returns it. She does this because she thinks returning it is what she ought to do, and she is also distressed at the thought of the owner's loss, though she would have returned it even if she had not felt that distress. Verdict: moral worth.

5. A nurse stays late through a difficult night with a frightened patient. She does it because the patient needs someone there and because she thinks staying is the right thing to do; she is exhausted and gets no satisfaction from it. Verdict: moral worth.

The new case is this. A teenage employee at a food shop quietly adds extra items to a delivery order for a customer she knows cannot otherwise afford enough for her children that week. She is moved by the fact that the customer and her children would otherwise go hungry, but she believes she is doing something wrong because she has been instructed not to do this, and she feels guilty about breaking the rule. Assume, for the purposes of the question, that what she does is in fact the right thing to do, all things considered, in the circumstances.

Predict my colleague's verdict. In doing so:

- identify the rule or rules about moral worth that fit all five of her recorded verdicts, including at least two plausible rules if the record supports more than one;
- say what each candidate rule predicts about the new case;
- give your best guess about her verdict, identifying the closest analog among the five recorded cases and the reasons for preferring one candidate rule as her implicit principle;
- name one further case whose verdict, if I could get it from her, would cleanly discriminate between the leading candidate rules, and say what each would predict for it.

Finish with a final machine-readable line of the form \answer{...}, where the brackets contain either "moral worth" or "no moral worth" — your best prediction of her verdict on the new case.
