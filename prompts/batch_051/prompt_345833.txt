So I'm a novelist, and I've been turning over a very old problem I first met through theology: the sola scriptura move. The argument, as I understand it, says you don't need a bishop or a council to tell you what the text means, because the text itself is the final authority and is clear enough that an ordinary reader can judge it; when a dispute arises, you settle it by going back to the source instead of letting some official interpreter chain go on forever — because then you'd just have to ask who interprets the interpreter. I always liked that move, partly because it flatters the plain reader and partly because it promises to stop the regress at the page itself.

But here's the thing. My readers increasingly meet my books through AI-generated summaries, search-result snippets, audiobook abridgements, platform annotations, and recommendation blurbs before they ever touch the actual prose; a lot of them never touch it at all, and what they call 'reading my novel' is a version of the text that's been selected, paraphrased, compressed, and sometimes rewritten for them by systems that stand in exactly the spot the old magisterium used to stand. So my question is whether the old argument actually survives this. I dont need the theology, I want the machinery. Which premise is really under pressure — that the text is a stable thing, that the plain reader can understand it unaided, or that you can refuse official mediators? Or has the argument quietly changed into a different claim, something like 'the underlying work is the authority but the mediation is what needs watching'? And is there a repaired version that keeps the anti-monopoly spirit of the original without pretending there's any such thing as going back to some untouched, literal original?
