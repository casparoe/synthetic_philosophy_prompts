Something's been nagging me about the Meno and I'd like to work through it properly rather than get a summary. Near the end of the dialogue (Stephanus 97-98, the road to Larissa and Daedalus statues stretch), Socrates first says that true opinion is no worse a guide to action than knowledge: the person with a correct hunch gets you to Larissa just as well as the one who knows the way. Then almost immediately after, he says knowledge is prized more than correct opinion because it is tied down by a reasoning out of the cause, the way the statues of Daedalus would run away unless they were tethered. Those two claims look like they are in tension. If right opinion does the job equally well, what exactly does the tether buy, and why should it make knowledge more valuable if the value was meant to show in acting correctly? I want you to (a) lay out the candidate reconciliations — practical versus intrinsic value, stability over time, teachability, anything else you can defend from the text — and then (b) say which of them, if any, is an honest reading rather than special pleading. In particular: is there a reading where the road-to-Larissa point does real work in Plato's overall argument that virtue needs to be more than lucky opinion, or is he just shifting the goalposts once he realises opinion gets the job done fine? Take a position at the end, even if it's a hedged one.
