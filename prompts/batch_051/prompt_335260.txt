A commentator I was reading defends the People's Action Party's style of government through an analogy, and I want to know how much work the analogy can actually do. His move, in compressed form: nobody who boards a long-haul flight insists on electing the captain or voting on the flight plan; one accepts the captain's authority because the airline has a long record of safe and punctual arrivals, and one judges the arrangement by the landing rather than by the procedure that put him in the cockpit. Political rule, he says, is relevantly similar: what justifies a government is delivering sustained prosperity and public order, so a polity with a generation of growth and stability behind it—Singapore on his account, or China in the stronger version—is legitimate in the way a well-flown aircraft is, even though its rulers were not chosen through competitive elections. I find this seductive and suspicious at once. Please audit the analogy rather than just have a view about it. Spell out the mapping between the two cases (passengers, captain, boarding, flight, landing, and the rest); identify where the mapping breaks; isolate which disanalogies are incidental and which matter for the specific conclusion being drawn; and explain what features of the flight case the comparison quietly imports into politics that may not be there. Then say whether the conclusion—that demonstrated prosperity and order can by themselves justify authoritarian rule—survives once those disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy would carry the point better, or none can.
