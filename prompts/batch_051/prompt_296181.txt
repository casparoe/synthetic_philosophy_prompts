Recently I have been trying to design a proof-based agent, one that acts only on what it proves, and I keep running up against the question whether such an agent can ever be justified in trusting its own proofs. I have accumulated a set of commitments and would like a consistency audit before I present them. (1) An agent should act on a conclusion only if it has proved that conclusion from premises it accepts. (2) The agent's system is strong enough to encode arithmetic and to express its own proof predicate, so it can represent claims of the form "P is provable in this system." (3) By Löb's theorem, no consistent system of that kind can prove the reflection schema, if P is provable in this system then P, for arbitrary P; proving every instance would let it prove every sentence. (4) Nevertheless, an agent that has derived P from accepted premises is entitled to add P to its beliefs; otherwise it could not trust any of its own conclusions. (5) Trusting one's conclusions requires a general guarantee that the proof machinery is sound, not merely piecemeal checks of particular derivations. Do (1) through (5) jointly hang together? I cannot tell which of these are in genuine tension and which only look as though they are. Please separate the genuine tensions from the merely apparent ones, order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each genuine tension say what would have to give and what the least costly revision loses.
