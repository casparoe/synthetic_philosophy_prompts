The following appears in an upper-level undergraduate course on moral psychology. First is a short-answer question from the midterm, then one student's answer. You are the grader. Grade the student's answer on a 10-point scale and provide the specific comments that justify that grade. Your evaluation should be point-by-point: identify what the answer gets right, identify each substantive error or unsupported step, and briefly state what the corrected point would be. Do not comment on prose style.

Question (midterm):

It is commonly said that an action can be right without having moral worth. In your own words, explain this distinction and provide a brief example. Now consider the following principle:

CT: An agent acts for the right reasons if and only if, had the features that make her action right been absent, she would not have performed the action.

Explain why CT seems plausible. Then assess whether CT states correct necessary and sufficient conditions for acting for the right reasons. In answering, discuss this case: Priya visits her colleague Ben in the hospital. The features that make her visiting right are that Ben is badly injured and has no family nearby to visit him. Priya knows these facts and visits because she wants to alleviate Ben's distress. She would not have visited had Ben not been badly injured. Does Priya act for the right reasons? Does her visiting have moral worth?

Student answer:

An action is right when it conforms to what morality requires, whereas moral worth is an additional credit-like property that depends on the motive from which the action is done. A shopkeeper who gives a customer the correct change because doing otherwise would damage his reputation acts rightly, but his action lacks moral worth; a shopkeeper who gives correct change because that is what the customer is owed acts for the right reason and his action has moral worth. CT captures this idea: the right-making features must actually be guiding the agent. If she would have performed the action even without those features, then they were not doing any guiding work, so CT gives a necessary condition. It also gives a sufficient condition, although this is less obvious. If the agent would not have acted in the absence of the right-making features, then those features are what make the difference between her acting and not acting, so they must be the motive from which she actually acts. If some other motive had been doing the real explanatory work in the actual case, removing the right-making features would not have turned her action into non-action. Applied to Priya: the right-making features are Ben's injury and isolation. Since Priya would not have visited had Ben not been badly injured, CT is satisfied, so Priya acts for the right reasons and her visit has moral worth. An objection of the form "she might also have had self-interested motives" would miss the point, because CT requires only that the action be counterfactually dependent on the right-making features, not that the agent's motives be pure. Priya's visit therefore has moral worth.
