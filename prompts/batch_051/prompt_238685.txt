Write the joint statement that a committed diachronic-coherence theorist and a committed time-slice rationalist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about money pumps. By "diachronic-coherence theorist" I mean someone who thinks a rational agent is answerable to a standard that applies across time, not just at a time: an agent whose time-indexed preferences form a cycle like the one below is irrational because they cannot keep a stable plan, even if each individual trade maximizes their current preference. By "time-slice rationalist" I mean someone who thinks what an agent rationally ought to do at a time is fixed by the agent's mental state at that time: if at each moment the agent picks the option they prefer most among the feasible ones, the agent has not done anything irrational, and the money pump shows only that someone else can profit from changing tastes.

Use this as the shared test case. On Monday a person holds plan A for the weekend (a long hike) and prefers plan B (visiting family) to A. On Tuesday, after no new information, they prefer plan C (running errands) to B. On Wednesday, again after no new information, they prefer A to C. Each day they pay a one-dollar fee to swap whatever plan they hold for the one they prefer that day. They end up back holding A, three dollars poorer. Both sides should treat this as the canonical case and can add bells and whistles (fees, framing effects, regret) only where those actually matter to a crux.

I want the document to do the real work, not paper the disagreement over. Don't write a dialogue or issue a verdict. Include:

(1) A numbered agreed-claims section. Substantive claims only—nothing like "both sides value rigor." It should cover at least: a neutral description of what the person in the case does and where they end up; which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, if any, and which are not; and a precise statement of what each side would have to show to move the other.

(2) A disagreements table. Use these columns: the diachronic-coherence formulation; the time-slice rationality formulation; the crux (the sub-question that, if settled, would get at least one side to revise); a test, case, or argument both sides agree bears on that crux; and each side's prediction for that test, recorded seperately in advance. Each side's own view should be phrased so that side would accept the wording. Give at least three rows, and make one row about a version of the case where the person can precommit (for example, on Monday they can pay to lock in plan A for Wednesday).

Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by the disagreements table. Then end with a one-sentence summary of the largest remaining disagreement as the final line, beginning "Summary:".
