There is a striking asymmetry in the way scientists and historians describe superseded theories. Phlogiston theory and caloric theory are standardly called false: their central entities are said not to exist, and the theories are treated as mistakes that chemistry and physics eventually corrected. Newtonian mechanics, by contrast, is standardly described as approximately true — a limiting case of relativity and, in a different sense, of quantum mechanics — valid in a restricted domain and still the correct tool for most purposes. On its face, each case involves a theory that enjoyed considerable predictive success, was later replaced by a framework with different fundamental commitments, and is no longer accepted as the full story. State this asymmetry precisely and ask whether anything justifies it. Is there a principle that yields both verdicts — for example, that a superseded theory deserves to be called approximately true only when the successor theory contains it as a mathematical limiting case, or only when its central terms can still be regarded as referring to real quantities — or is the difference an artifact of our historical position: a kind of anachronism that flatters the theories we still use and condescends to the ones we have fully abandoned? Test the candidate principles against intermediate cases: caloric theory, which had quantitative successes but whose central entity was discarded; the luminiferous aether, which was woven into nineteenth-century electrodynamics and is now said not to exist; and the Bohr atom, which textbooks retain as a pedagogical stepping stone without treating it as a limiting case of quantum mechanics. Conclude by saying whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and what that implies about how we ought to describe the theories that will supersede our own.
