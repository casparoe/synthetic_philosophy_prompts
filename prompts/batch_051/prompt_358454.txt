I run a tiny high school podcast about language and philosophy, and after a whole season of interviewing my friends about whether their dogs understand grammar, we finally booked an actual linguist. She defends a words-and-rules picture of morphology: regular past tenses like walk/walked are built on the spot by a default rule, but irregular ones like sing/sang or go/went are stored whole in memory, and suppletion is the extreme case where the past tense gets grabbed from an unrelated root instead of being derived. My co-host thinks this is obviously backwards, because sing/sang/sung and ring/rang/rung cluster like little rules of their own, and little kids say "goed" long before they say "went," which makes the regular rule sound like the thing they learn first, not some fallback. I'm supposed to run the Q&A after her talk, and I dont want it to turn into her just restating the view for ten minutes.

Could you put together eight to ten questions that actually press on the weak spots? Somewhere in there, make her explain why stored irregulars are not a random list—why do they fall into patterns like sing/sang/sung—and what her view even means by a "rule" if the regular past is just the default that fires when memory has nothing special to offer. I'd also like a couple questions on sound change: the usual line is that sound changes are regular and have no exceptions, but real languages are full of words that dodged a change through analogy or borrowing, so I want to know what's left of the idea that grammar is rule-governed when both morphology and sound change seem full of exceptions. For each main question, add the follow-up you'd ask after her standard reply so I don't get stuck nodding. End with the question my co-host keeps yelling about: how can a speaker know "went" is the past tense of "go" without deriving it from "go"—is that a rule, or just a really well-practiced memory that we've gotten used to calling grammar?
