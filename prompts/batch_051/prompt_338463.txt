At a long dinner party, I watched two old friends, Lena and Ravi, argue about Lena's eating practices, and they eventually asked me to referee. Lena has been a strict ethical vegan for years, out of opposition to industrial animal farming. Recently she has begun doing two things that Ravi considers hard to square with that. She occasionally takes meat and dairy that a nearby supermarket has thrown out, after it has passed its sell-by date and before the bins are collected. And she keeps three rescued ex-battery hens in her garden; once she has fed some of their eggs back to the birds (which she says replaces nutrients they lose through excessive laying), she eats the rest rather than let them go to waste. Ravi is not himself a vegan and does not object to using animal products as such; his complaint is narrower: that Lena's behavior contradicts her own stated commitments.

As best I can reconstruct, the exchange went like this. Ravi opened: "You say a chicken's life and body are not ours to use, and then you eat a chicken that died inside the system, just because it was going to be thrown away. Your objection can't only be to the purchase. Treating a body as food is itself the thing you claim to reject." Lena replied: "No, what I reject is paying for animals to be bred, confined, and killed, or doing anything that keeps that system running. Meat from a bin pays no one, appears as no sale, leads to no reorder. If I leave it, it rots out of principle alone. You're confusing causing a wrong with standing near its aftermath." Ravi said: "But you are still making yourself a person who eats animals. You tell people you're vegan, then neighbours see you with a carcass. You weaken the norm you say you are trying to build." Lena answered: "I don't treat veganism as a purity identity; I treat it as a strategy against a particular system of exploitation, and bin meat sits outside that system's incentive loop. If anything, it makes the point sharper: I would rather eat the system's waste than give it money." Ravi pressed: "Then take the eggs. You do not need them. Taking them casts the hen as a producer, which is exactly the role the egg industry assigns her. 'It does no harm' is not the same as 'it respects her.'" Lena said: "The hen has no interest in her unfertilized eggs; left in the coop they attract pests, can break and encourage egg-eating, and the hens do not miss them. The relevant tests are whether she suffers and whether my using the eggs creates demand for more exploited hens. Both answers are no." Ravi came back: "That test is conveniently thin. A person can benefit from the byproducts of an atrocity without creating marginal demand for it, and the benefiting itself is still a moral fact. If a thief dumped a stolen wallet in the rubbish, you would not keep the cash on the grounds that the theft had already happened and returning it would not reduce theft." Lena answered: "No, because the wallet has a living victim with a claim to it, and my keeping it would block that victim's recovery. The chicken is dead, the farmer was paid before I arrived, and no one downstream treats my salvaging as an order. You keep reaching for contagion and taint because the causal story will not do the work."

At that point the argument began to loop and they turned to me. I honestly could not sort out which points were real and which were rhetoric. Please go through their exchange turn by turn and say who is right about which specific claims, where each of them overreaches, and what both are missing. Then say where you think the overall weight falls. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who have thought carefully about this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
