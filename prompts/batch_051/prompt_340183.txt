I do policy work at a small org that comments on AI regulation, and the one argument I keep having to answer is: "corporations are already legal persons and nobody thinks they're conscious, so making an AI a legal person is just the same familiar bit of legal technology." The speaker then concludes we should treat AI legal personhood as nothing new and nothing to be alarmed about. The other side in our meetings says this is a category mistake, because a corporation is really an aggregate of humans with money and shared interests, and an AI isn't. I'd like you, as a philosopher of law, to audit this analogy properly rather than score points for one side. Lay out what maps onto what (a table is fine), where the mapping breaks, and which features of corporate personhood are being quietly imported that an AI system wouldn't have. Then say which of those disanalogies actually do work in the argument and which are just decoration, and whether the "nothing new or threatening" conclusion survives once the real ones are on the table. Start with a brainstorm of candidate disanologies and then trim. If there's a better analogy that does the same work more honestly, say what it is and where it also breaks.
