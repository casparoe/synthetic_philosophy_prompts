I'm a philosophy grad student preparing a workshop paper for next month, and I could use a prep sheet more than a pep talk. The paper is for an interdisciplinary workshop on global labor; most of the room will be development economists and trade-policy people, with a few political theorists, and I'm the only person presenting something with "exploitation" in the title. My thesis is that the standard defense of sweatshops gets one move wrong: showing that a sweatshop job makes a worker better off than her actual alternatives (which it generally does) establishes that the exchange is mutually beneficial in the Pareto sense, but it does not yet establish that the exchange isn't wrongful exploitation. I want to defend the idea of mutually beneficial exploitation: a firm can exploit a worker even when both parties gain, because the worker's outside options are so lousy that her "choice" doesn't sanitize the terms, and what matters is not just whether she gains but whether the terms she got were fair given the background constraints. My rough argument is: (1) the worker's alternatives are so bad that treating her acceptance as meaningful consent is dubious; (2) the firm knowingly takes advantage of that fact to offer terms it couldn't get away with in a more balanced bargaining situation; (3) fairness of terms, not Pareto improvement, is the relevant standard; (4) therefore an exchange can be both genuinely beneficial and wrongfully exploitative, and we can criticize it without saying the worker would be better off if the factory closed.

The economists are going to push back hard, in their vocabulary rather than mine, and I want to be ready. They tend to think "exploitation" only makes sense when someone is made worse of, and they'll probably frame my position as paternalism that would hurt the very people it claims to help. Prepare me for the question period: what will they actually object to, in what order, which objections have real force and which are noise, what I should concede up front to keep credibility, and what the one reply is that has to land for the paper to survive. Also tell me the objection I should be afraid of, the one I probably haven't thought of.
