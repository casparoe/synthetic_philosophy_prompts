The standard objective Bayesian argument says that when a reasoner's information consists of nothing more than a few known expected values, there is a uniquely rational prior: the maximum-entropy distribution, the one that is as uninformative as possible while still satisfying those constraints. Now consider a development that puts pressure on the argument's underlying setup. Increasingly, the parameter spaces used in science and engineering are continuous and high-dimensional — the weights of a large neural network, for instance — and they admit indefinitely many equivalent reparameterizations, with no single natural coordinate system in which to measure how spread out a distribution is. In such cases the maximum-entropy prior is not invariant: the distribution that is flattest under one parameterization is no longer flattest under another, so two reasoners with identical evidence but different coordinate choices will assign different probabilities. Does this development kill the objective Bayesian claim that there is a uniquely rational prior, or can the argument be repaired, perhaps by building invariance under reparameterization into the rule itself? Identify the specific premise the development bears on, and say what would have to be true for the claim to survive.
