ok so I just got back from a three-hour argument with a friend about whether the whole 'AI race is like war' thing actually earns its keep, and I'm less clear than when we started — not about the object-level question, but about what we were even disagreeing on. I want a diagnosis of the arguement itself, not another take on who's right.

my friend's position: Fearon's rationalist explanations for war don't transfer to AI development. firms aren't states, nobody's getting killed, there's no battlefield, and there's no obvious bargain sitting there waiting to be struck and missed. he says AI development is really a coordination problem, or just normal market competition with externalities, and that calling it a 'race' or 'war' is mostly rhetoric that makes safety people think in terms of enemies and national security. plus he says labs can signal capabilities through benchmarks and publishes and safety evals, so the information asymmetry thing is overblown.

my position: Fearon's three mechanisms aren't about violence, they're about why self-interested actors fail to reach a deal they'd all prefer to fighting. and the bargaining structure seems to fit: labs have private information about capabilities and real incentives to misrepresent (overstate for funding and recruiting, understate to hide a lead); there are commitment problems everywhere, like a lab that gets ahead can't credibly promise it won't exploit that lead later, so rivals race to avoid being at its mercy — basically the preventive-war logic; and if AGI, or something like an off-switch, turns out to be winner-take-all you get issue indivisibility. so it's a model, not a literal war, and it transfers fine.

the weird part is we agreed on nearly all the facts about labs, capabilities, incentives, timelines. we both know this literature. and we still went in circles for hours.

so my actual question: what kind of disagreement is this? my hunch is that I'm making a modeling claim (the bargaining structure fits) while he's making a pragmatic or normative claim about how we should talk about the thing (the war frame poisons the discourse, so it's not worth invoking even if the structure fits). but I could be wrong — maybe there's a clean empirical question underneath, like whether labs can credibly signal enough to remove the information problem, or whether the outcome is genuinely indivisible, which would settle it. or maybe we just disagree about what counts as 'war' in the social-science sense. diagnose it with reasons, not a survey of Fearon or the AI race literature. and then tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis: which consideration should make you switch to a different account of why the debate stalls, and where in your own reasoning you're least confident.
