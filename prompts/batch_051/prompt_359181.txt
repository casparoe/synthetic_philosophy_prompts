The eighteenth- and nineteenth-century versions of the idea that history is providential — Kant's hidden plan of nature, Hegel's cunning of reason, and the popular phrasing about the arc of history bending toward justice — all seem to quietly assume that history just keeps going long enough for whatever the mechanism is to do its work. Kant, for instance, was happy to let the mechanism run for centuries and didn't really consider that the species might wipe itself out before things reached their proper endpoint. But we now face exactly that possibility within a century or two: serious extinction or permanent collapse, from nuclear war, engineered pandemics, climate tipping points, or misaligned AI. Some people even read the Fermi paradox as evidence that intelligent species tend to kill themselves off before any long arc can complete.

So my question is: does the arc-bends-toward-justice argument survive this, or was it always silently betting on an open-ended future? Which premise actually takes the hit — the claim that there is a mechanism, or the unstated assumption that the mechanism gets an indefinite amount of time to work? Is there a repaired version, for instance turning it into the claim that the mechanism only operates if the species keeps the process from being cut short, so the conclusion becomes a duty to keep the process alive rather than a prediction that justice will win? And would that repaired version still be the same view, or a different one wearing the old clothes? I'm after the structure of the argument, not policy recommendations.
