I am a PhD student in applied ethics, and next month I am presenting a paper at a workshop on food policy and animal ethics. My thesis is that the familiar practice of ranking beef and chicken diets by the number of animal deaths caused per calorie is not robust: the ranking shifts, and can reverse, depending on whether one counts only the direct deaths of farmed animals or also the collateral deaths of wild animals during feed-crop cultivation, and on how one treats insects and very small mammals. I conclude that per-calorie death counts should not carry the weight they currently do in ethical dietary guidance. The audience will include effective altruists who have defended beef over chicken on death-counting grounds, and animal welfare scientists who regard estimates of crop-related wild animal deaths as too speculative to enter moral arithmetic. Prepare me for the question period: the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each group would phrase them, which objections have real force, what I should concede up front, and the weakest point in my argument that I should be ready to defend rather than hope they miss.
