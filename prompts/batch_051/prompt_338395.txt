I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on the ethics of transparency, concealment, and manipulation. Over the past few years I have settled into the following five views, and I would like to know whether they form a stable package or whether some of them are in more tension than I have noticed.

(1) There is no general duty to be transparent about one's inner life. Within broad limits, one is entitled to keep one's beliefs, emotions, and intentions private, and that privacy is a genuine good rather than a useful convention.

(2) Deception is presumptively wrong in a way that cannot be reduced to its bad effects. Its distinctive wrong is that it manipulates the other person: it moves them to act on a picture of your mind or of the facts that you have manufactured, bypassing their capacity to decide for themselves rather than engaging it. This includes not only lying but also misleading omissions where the other party has a reasonable expectation of disclosure.

(3) In close relationships — friendships, romantic partnerships, long-term collaborations — there is a duty of legibility that goes beyond the duty not to deceive. One should make one's important needs, grievances, and intentions reasonably interpretable to the other person, even when they have asked nothing and even where silence would fall short of inducing any false belief. Persistent withdrawal into opacity can wrong an intimate without taking the form of an outright deception.

(4) Transparency has no intrinsic value. Being an open book is not a virtue in its own right; transparency matters only as a means to goods such as trust, autonomous choice, and coordination.

(5) Benevolent withholding is sometimes permissible: one may temporarily withhold information that would seriously upset someone when sharing it now would predictably impair their ability to deliberate or to perform an important task, even if the information is relevant to choices they face.

I have the sense that something in this package pulls against something else, but I cannot tell whether the problem is a real inconsistency or only an apparent one that dissolves once the claims are made precise. Could you audit the set for me? In particular: which pairs are in genuine tension, in the sense that accepting one should rationally push me to revise the other; which apparent conflicts are only apparent; and, if something has to give, which revision is cheapest and what would it cost the rest of the package? Please order any tensions you find by how serious they are, and be specific about what each commitment commits me to. I am not looking for a literature review, just an internal audit of whether these views can be held together.
