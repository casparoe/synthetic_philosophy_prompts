I've been going down a rabbit hole on Merleau-Ponty's idea of the lived body lately — I'm just some guy who got curious, not a student or anything, so I might be butchering the terms — and the bit that stuck with me is motor intentionality: the way your body is already aimed at the world before you think about it. Like how your hand is already the right shape for the door handle before you decide to open it, or how an experienced driver's foot is braking for a hazard before the thought 'brake' is even there. The body has its own kind of know-how.

The other night I rewatched the original Karate Kid and it hit me that the whole Miyagi training method is basically that. Daniel spends days doing chores — painting the fence, sanding the floor, waxing the car — with no idea what they're for, and then Miyagi suddenly throws punches at him and his arms do the blocks on their own. The movements were already in his body before he could say what they were for. I can't tell if the movie is actually showing the lived-body idea or if I'm just dressing up the usual 'practice makes muscle memory' cliche.

So I'd like a reading of the movie through this lens. Which scenes actually dramatize motor intentionality or the lived body, and which scenes pull against it — like Daniel moping because he doesn't see the point, as if a movement only counts once the mind understands it? Does the film end up closer to Merleau-Ponty or closer to the mechanical automatic-reflex picture? Where does the film resist the reading, the parts an honest critic would have to admit don't fit? And what, if anything, does the movie add to the idea that the philosophical writing on its own doesn't?

I know people who actually work on this still argue about how much of the know-how is 'in the body' versus in the brain or in habit. Please keep what's settled and what's genuinely contested clearly separated, and don't present a contested take as the consensus.
