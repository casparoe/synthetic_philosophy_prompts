I'm a philosophy professor, and I've spent years defending a view I shorthand as 'one ought at a time.' The idea is that however messy things look, there is always an all-things-considered answer to what a person should do. Prima facie duties are real inputs, but once you weigh them the loser doesn't leave a remainder: if you do what you ought to do, you have done nothing wrong, and feeling guilt would be a kind of moral confusion — maybe useful as a psychological brake, but not rationally required. I like how tidy it is: it keeps 'ought' from contradicting itself and gives you a clean procedure. Rank the considerations, pick the strongest, and you're clean.

But I can't make it sit with the standard dirty hands case. A prime minister can authorize a strike on a munitions factory that will kill thirty civilians but prevent a massacre of thirty thousand. I do think he ought to authorize it; refusing would be self-indulgent or worse. Yet I also can't shake the intution that those thirty deaths are not just the regrettable price of the right act. It seems to me that he wrongs those civilians, that there is something he owes them which no future good fully cancels, and that a politician who felt no guilt — who walked away thinking 'done and dusted' — would be morally deficient, not merely bad at public relations. I find myself wanting to say both that the act was right and that it was in some sense wrong, which is exactly the contradiction my tidy view was built to avoid.

So here's what I want. Give me the strongest version of each side, then negotiate between them. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — an actual debunking explanation of why decent people have it even though it's mistaken? What would it take for the theory to go, or at least be amended — are we forced into genuine moral remainders, agent-relative wrongs, or two senses of 'ought'? And is there a reading on which both survive, where the act is right but it wrongs the civilians in a way that isn't the same as 'he should not have done it'? Don't just tell me which side wins; tell me what each option costs, and whether that compatibility reading is a real third way or just equivocation dressed up as insight.
