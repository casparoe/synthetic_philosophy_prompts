I keep noticing a parallel between two ways of severing a discipline's inner-state requirement, and I want to know whether the resemblance is structural or merely verbal. On the legal side, strict liability allows a conviction to rest on proof of the proscribed act and its consequences alone: there is no need to prove mens rea, and the defendant's impeccably innocent state of mind is at most a consideration at sentencing rather than a bar to liability. On the epistemological side, process reliabilism allows a belief to count as justified when it is produced by a reliable cognitive process, even if the believer can give no reasons, has no internal confirmation that the process is reliable, and in general lacks access to what makes the belief justified. As I read them, both positions replace a condition on the subject's own perspective — a culpable state of mind in the one case, internally accessible grounds in the other — with a condition on an objective, external fact: the act and its harm, or the reliability of the production process. Is this the same philosophical move made twice, or does the analogy come apart under inspection? In your answer, spell out the mapping you are using and where it strains, identify what would have to be true of both sides for lessons to transfer from one to the other, and give at least one consequence the parallel suggests that you think is false.
