I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending the claim that recognition — acknowledging another person in her particularity, as someone with a specific evaluative outlook, projects, and self-understanding — is an irreducible category of moral requirement, distinct from respect. By "respect" I mean the familiar category of duties that constrain how we may treat a person: duties of non-interference, nondiscrimination, promise-keeping, and the like, which can in principle be specified in terms of actions and omissions. I will argue that respecting someone in that sense is not sufficient for recognizing her, that the failure to recognize someone can be a moral wrong even where no respect-based duty has been violated, and that the wrong cannot be restated as a duty to perform or omit any finite set of actions. My argument, compressed: (1) there are ordinary cases — a colleague fulfills every role-obligation competently but never once registers your particular commitments; a care professional performs every care-task correctly while treating the recipient as a case rather than a person — in which the person on the receiving end complains, and the complaint is not merely about hurt feelings; (2) the best explanation of the complaint is that something was owed in attitude, not only in action; (3) whatever is owed in attitude cannot be paraphrased into action-duties, because any specified set of actions can be performed with the wrong attitude, or with none; (4) therefore a distinct category of requirement is needed, and any moral theory that recognizes only action-guiding duties of respect is missing it.

The audience is a philosophy department that leans heavily Kantian: most of the faculty work in Kant's practical philosophy or contemporary Kantian constructivism, and they share a working assumption that moral requirements must be requirements of practical reason — universalizable, action-guiding, and binding on the will — and that "ought" implies "can." There are two or three Hegelians who are broadly sympathetic but will press me on whether I have actually escaped the Kantian framework, and a virtue ethicist who will want to know why this is not just a description of a neglected virtue. There are no care ethicists in the room. I expect the Kantians to be the loudest, and I expect their objections to worry less about my empirical cases and more about the architecture of obligation.
