For most of my working life I directed a small marine-biology field station; I retired in the same year that it was wound down and folded into a distributed collaboration. By several measures the science improved afterwards: costs fell, the data pipeline widened, and nobody spent an afternoon in a boat merely to swap a sensor. Yet something about the place disappeared—the communal midday meal, the arguments that were possible only because people were in the same room, the unplanned favours. I have since taken up philosophy through a continuing-education programme, and I keep returning to the question of what, if anything, was genuinely lost. One difficulty is that my reading group uses the word 'community' in several incompatible ways, and our arguments about the field station repeatedly bottom out in unstated assumptions rather than in reasons.

I should like to produce an eight-to-ten-question sorting quiz for that group, one that assigns each of us to a position by what we say about concrete cases rather than by which label we prefer. The leaves should include at least: an instrumentalist view, on which the good of a community is exhausted by the goods it delivers to its members; a communitarian or constitutive view, on which membership and belonging are themselves non-instrumental goods; a social-need view, on which community answers a basic human need in something like the way recognition or health does; and a deflationary view, on which what is mourned is mostly familiarity or nostalgia rather than any distinctive good. Add a further leaf if you think the logical space requires it. The questions should sort along the following fault lines: whether an online group that reproduces the same conversations, rituals and services can genuinely replace a face-to-face community; whether the value of a community is fungible across communities, so that losing one can be fully made good by joining another equally fine one; whether unchosen communities—a family, a place, a profession one was born into—can be good for a person in ways that chosen associations cannot; and whether a community's dissolution can matter even when no individual member's stated preferences are frustrated. Each question should be answerable by someone who has never read the literature, and none should simply ask whether belonging is intrinsically valuable. Please include a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the leaves, flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate, and note where an answer combination is logically possible but unoccupied—and whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
