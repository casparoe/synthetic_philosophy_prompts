I've been going down a rabbit hole with the question of whether natural selection is a stupid optimiser, and I've ended up with a homemade idea I'd like poked at hard, because I can't tell whether it's doing real work or whether I've just put a new hat on a familiar point.

I've been calling it the fidelity tax. The core thought: in a non-stationary fitness landscape, any optimiser pays a cost for being good at fitting the current optimum, because the current optimum moves; the cost is paid in the currency of later adaptability. Evolution, on this story, is structurally low-fidelity — selection is weak and noisy, drift pushes lineages around the landscape, and a lineage that matches today's environment too exactly is a lineage that shatter when the environment shifts. Think of a specialist lineage that fits one host perfectly and then that host disappears; the generalist survives. So evolution's apparent stupidity isn't a bug; it's a kind of regulariser. SGD is a much better descender than selection is, so a trained network ends up too faithful to its training distribution and pays a high fidelity tax. And I want to read dropout, label smoothing, mini-batch noise, early stopping, even weight decay, as controlled ways of deliberately paying that tax up front — artificial sloppiness as a stand-in for selection's natural sloppiness.

Tell me honestly: is this a real distinction, or is it just the bias–variance trade-off in an evolutionary costume? If it is earning its keep, what does it predict that ordinary talk about noise helping generalisation doesn't? Where does the analogy actually break — is there a case where the fidelity tax says one thing and evolution or ML practice does the other? And if the idea survived, what's the most non-obvious thing it would tell you to change about how you train a model? No references needed, I'm after the reasoning rather than a bibliography. Answer in British English, please.
