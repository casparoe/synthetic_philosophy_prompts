In retirement I have begun sitting in on an undergraduate philosophy course on personhood and medical decision-making, and the instructor has asked me to help grade a short-answer assignment on advance directives. The question set to the students was this: "John Locke, in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, 'Of Identity and Diversity,' writes: 'In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else. For in them the variation of great parcels of matter alters not the identity : an oak growing from a plant to a great tree, and then lopped, is still the same oak ; and a colt grown up to a horse, sometimes fat, sometimes lean, is all the while the same horse.' Explain what the 'something else' is for human persons in Locke's account, and discuss whether this passage supports or undermines the claim that an advance directive made by one's earlier self should bind one's later self."

One student answered as follows: "Locke's point is that identity for living things is not about the continuing same material particles, because an oak or a colt remains the same through material changes. For persons, the something else is the same consciousness. So if a later self no longer shares the earlier self's memories and beliefs, there is a break in consciousness and the person is different. That would mean the earlier self cannot bind the later self, so advance directives lack authority when the later self has genuinely changed. But Locke also distinguishes the person from the man, and the man is the forensic bearer of responsibility; because the body is continuous, a directive can still bind the man even if not the person. That is how society can hold someone responsible for a debt they do not remember undertaking. So the passage ultimately supports binding advance directives only through the continuity of the man."

I will keep my own view of the material out of the grading. Please give this answer a grade and the specific comments that justify it: identify which parts of the student's exegesis of the passage are accurate, which parts go beyond or against the text, and whether the final conclusion about the man as forensic bearer is properly attributed to Locke. Be committed in your assessment rather than laying out interpretive possibilities.
