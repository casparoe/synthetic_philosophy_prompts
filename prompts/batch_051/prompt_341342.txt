Two friends of mine fell into an argument over a proposed city ordinance that would require homeowners to clear snow and ice from the sidewalk in front of their house within 24 hours of a storm, subject to a fine. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Here is how it went. (1) Jordan called the ordinance a plain loss of liberty: the city would be using the threat of a fine to make him do labor he never chose, and that is exactly the kind of interference with negative liberty that a free society may not impose casually. (2) Nia replied that the 82-year-old two doors down, who walks with a cane, cannot safely reach the bus stop when the sidewalk is iced over; if liberty means actually being able to move about and conduct your life, then the ordinance expands the real freedom of pedestrians, especially the elderly and disabled, so it protects freedom rather than destroying it. (3) Jordan said she was confusing freedom with ability: by that reasoning he would be unfree whenever he could not afford a plane ticket, and the concept of liberty would lose its special link to coercion. (4) Nia answered that positive liberty is not the claim that every inability is unfreedom; it is about being in a position to direct your own life, and a city whose sidewalks are impassable for much of the winter has failed to secure the conditions of self-direction for a whole class of residents. (5) Jordan shifted ground: the decisive problem, he said, was neither the labor itself nor some abstract capacity, but domination. Because the fine would be enforced erratically, often only when a neighbor complains, residents would live at the discretionary say-so of city officials, and that is a threat to liberty even when no fine is actually issued. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, say who is right about which specific point, and identify anything important both of them are missing. Respond in American English.
