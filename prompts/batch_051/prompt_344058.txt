I am a novelist. Nearly every book I write includes scenes in which a character must decide whether to continue an undertaking or abandon it after investing years of work, money, reputation, or love in it. These scenes recur constantly, and they typically carry a large share of the novel's moral and psychological weight, so I want to stop handling each one by feel.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in practical rationality, decision theory, and the philosophy of economics. Help me build a reusable diagnostic procedure for these scenes, grounded in the actual debate about sunk costs rather than in the slogan that sunk costs should simply be ignored. I know that economics teaches the irrelevance of sunk costs, but I also gather that the philosophical question—whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational—is genuinely contested, and I want the procedure to retain that contest rather than flatten it.

By a reusable diagnostic procedure I mean a checklist, a short flowchart in words, or an ordered set of questions I can run while drafting or revising. Given a scene in which a character refuses to quit partly or wholly because of what has already been invested, the procedure should determine which description best fits: (a) the character is committing the sunk cost fallacy; (b) the character's continuing is rationally defensible for reasons other than the sunkness of the costs; (c) the scene mixes fallacy and good reasons, and the procedure shows which one is doing the real work; or (d) the scene cannot be settled by the procedure and must be marked as a judgment call. The procedure should state explicitly which distinctions must survive this simplification and which can be dropped, and it should be honest about where it will misfire.

The constraints are these: I have no training in decision theory; I will use the procedure at drafting and revision speed, not in a study; and my editor, who must sign off on character arcs, needs to be able to follow the procedure well enough to push back on my classification of a scene. Write for someone without the theory at the keyboard, but do not distort the underlying arguments to make them easier to swallow.

Please think carefully. Begin by brainstorming, in a short section, the candidate reasons a person might rationally continue after an unrecoverable investment—reputation, new information, promises to others, identity, the independent value of not wasting, and anything else your analysis turns up. Then build the procedure from that. Afterward, work through the procedure on two or three invented scenes, including at least one where the procedure concludes that the character's staying is rationally defensible rather than fallacious—the kind of answer that would force me to rewrite a scene I had been fond of—and at least one where the procedure shows my original framing of the scene was confused. Conclude by saying clearly what the procedure cannot do, both for my drafting and for the underlying philosophical question.
