I follow a policy for finding out what people really value, and I want to know whether it is a defensible epistemic shortcut or a piece of economic theory pretending to be common sense. The policy is this: when I need to know what a person prefers — whether it is a friend whose stated values I am supposed to accommodate, a negotiating partner, or someone whose professed principles are being offered as a reason for me to act — I set the person's statements aside and look only at the choices they made when those choices cost them something: money, time, an opportunity, social credit, comfort. I call it the choice test. A stated preference gets admitted as evidence only if it is costly to the person to state, in something like the way a choice is costly: for example, when saying it works against their other aims, or when they act on it at a real price. The intellectual backing, as I understand it, is revealed-preference theory: economists say we can ascribe a utility function to someone if their choices satisfy a few consistency conditions, and that the utility is not a hidden feeling but an index that represents the pattern of choices; a person prefers x to y, on this picture, in exactly the sense that they choose x over y when given the chance. I use the test most when I need to predict how someone will act under pressure, or when I have to decide how much weight to give to what they say they care about.

My worry is that I may have taken a modeling convention from economics and converted it into a rule for reading human beings, without noticing the step. The test seems to treat people who have genuine preferences they consistently fail to act on — because of weakness of will, confusion, intimidation, or lack of opportunity — as if their preferences simply did not exist. It may also flatten the difference between what a person wants and what they are willing to pay for, and between utility as a representation of choice and utility as something more substantive like satisfaction, enjoyment, or welfare.

Is the choice test defensible as I apply it? Under what conditions does inferring real preferences from costly choices go wrong, and what would a corrected version of the policy look like — one that keeps the suspicion of cheap talk without simply defining the person's values as whatever they happened to do? And from the inside, how do I tell whether I am tracking what the person actually values or merely projecting a consistent preference ordering onto their behavior and calling the result their utility?
