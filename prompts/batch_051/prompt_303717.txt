I write about physics for a general-interest science magazine, and we're putting together an interactiece on whether the wavefunction is a real thing in the world or more like a weather forecast — a summary of what you happen to know. I want the centerpiece to be a which-camp-are-you-actually-in quiz, and I want the questions to do the sorting, not the labels. So build me an 8-to-10-question quiz with a scoring key. Each question should be a concrete case or a plain-language claim with two or three answer options, phrased so a curious reader who's never heard of any of these positions can answer honestly. No question should offer a list of named positions and ask the reader to pick the one they like. Under the hood, build around the psi-ontic vs psi-epistemic split, and make the questions track things like: whether there's a fact about a particle's position before anyone looks; whether two agents assigning different pure states to the same system are disagreeing or just have different information; whether collapse is something the world does or something an agent does to their betting odds; and whether configuration space should be taken literally. I want the leaves to include at least Everettian/many-worlds, de Broglie–Bohm, spontaneous-collapse realism (the GRW family), wavefunction realism, QBism, and a Copenhagen/instrumentalist leaf. Use Schrödinger's cat and Wigner's friend if you like, but also write one question about two agents assigning different states to the same run of a genuinely random-looking setup, because I've never seen it done well.

After the scoring key, write me a short debrief: which leaves your questions can't cleanly seperate (I suspect QBism and Copenhagen end up on the same side of almost everything unless you push hard on the subjectivity question), and flag the empty cell — a psi-epistemic hidden-variable position — with a note on why it's empty: is it ruled out by a no-go theorem (I think the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem is the one) or just abandoned? Don't manufacture distinctions the questions don't actually draw; say where the instrument breaks.
