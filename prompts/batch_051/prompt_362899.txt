I am putting together the final session of an undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and I would like to close the unit on consciousness with a poem that does genuine conceptual work rather than decorating the ideas with verse. Please write a villanelle about Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his alternative, the multiple drafts model. The form should be more than a container: the villanelle's two recurring refrain lines should themselves enact the dialectic. One refrain should voice the seductive picture of a single mental stage where experience comes together and is presented to an inner spectator; the other should voice the correction that there is no such central place and no privileged, final version of events, only parallel drafts under continuous revision.

The repetitions matter because the Cartesian theater is not an error one refutes once and then sets down; it keeps reasserting itself whenever we describe consciousness, and the poem's circular returns should capture that rhythm of relapse and re-correction. The result should be precise enough that a philosopher of mind familiar with the multiple drafts model would recognize the view and find nothing garbled, yet still work as a poem for a reader who has never encountered either term. Avoid the shortcut of simply repeating the two phrases as refrains while leaving the intervening stanzas empty of development.
