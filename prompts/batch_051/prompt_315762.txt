I'm giving the eulogy at my uncle Daniel's funeral next Saturday, and I want it to be true rather than flattering. Daniel died at seventy-four of a heart attack on the way to inspect a new store. He built a chain of pawnshops and rent-to-own furniture stores across three states, starting from a single storefront at twenty-six. By any honest account he was an acquisitive man: he wanted more stores, more revenue, more of everything until the week he died, and he never treated that hunger as something to apologize for. His view was that the craving to have more kept him disciplined, kept his stores open in towns the banks had abandoned, kept forty years of employees in steady work, and made credit available to people who had nowhere else to get it. In his telling, his private greed was other people's payroll.

Not everyone in that room will accept that telling. His younger sister stopped speaking to him after he called in the loan on her bakery and she lost it. His daughter worked for him for a year, quit, and later helped run a campaign against high-interest lending; she describes the business as legalized extraction. He was not cruel, and he was not secretly softhearted, either. He was exacting: he remembered every debt, collected every dollar he was owed, and believed the profit itself was proof the service was wanted.

The funeral will be a mixed room. Store managers who worked for him for thirty years will be angry if I damn him; family members who consider him a predator will be angry if I canonize him; and a Baptist minister who used to preach against usury is coming anyway. Daniel was not religious, so I can't fall back on heaven or a better place. That would be false comfort, and the task is to comfort without lying.

I'd like a five-minute eulogy that faces the underlying philosophical question rather than stepping around it: was his acquisitiveness a vice that happened to produce good, an engine of prosperity disguised as a private vice, or something else? Please don't write a eulogy that hovers indecisively between verdicts, lists virtues and faults, and lets the room make up its mind. Commit to a defensible reading of his life, build the whole speech around that commitment, and let the philosophy determine what can and can't truthfully be said about him. It should land as comfort, not as a lecture.

After the speech, include a brief note to me explaining the interpretive line you took and where it leans on a contested view of greed.
