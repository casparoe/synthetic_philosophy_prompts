I'm drafting a short piece for a policy blog and I'd like comments before I share it with people who actually know economics. The piece starts from a passage near the start of Arthur Pigou's *The Economics of Welfare* (4th edition, 1932), where he writes: "When we elect to watch the play of human motives that are ordinary—that are sometimes mean and dismal and ignoble—our impulse is not the philosopher’s impulse, knowledge for the sake of knowledge, but rather the physiologist’s, knowledge for the healing that knowledge may help to bring."

My draft argues roughly this: the usual economist's reflex of ranking carbon taxes above regulation, and both above degrowth, claims Pigou as its ancestor. But if you take this passage seriously, Pigou isn't offering a formal efficiency test; he's saying the whole point is to be a doctor for a sick social body. That reframes the standard climate-policy ordering. A carbon tax is a good scalpel, but maybe the patient doesn't need a scalpel; maybe it needs surgery on the capital stock, land-use rules, or an honest conversation about reducing energy throughput. Direct regulation can target paricular forms of lock-in that pricing won't move fast enough. Degrowth looks different under the physiologist metaphor too: instead of being scolded as anti-economic, it might be asking whether the measure of health is the size of the patient's appetite. My worry is I'm overreading a bit of rhetorical stage-setting and turning it into a substantive argument.

Can you give me comments on: (1) whether I'm using the Pigou passage fairly, (2) where the argument is most likely to get attacked by an economist who thinks carbon pricing is still the default, and (3) whether the degrowth section overreaches. Also happy to hear what I should cut to make it sharper. I don't need line edits, just the main weaknesses.
