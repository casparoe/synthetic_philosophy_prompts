im a phd student in chinese philosophy, and next week im teaching an intro section on how the zhuangzi handles death. i want to close with a poem, and i want it to be a pantoum, because the form literally does the thing the text is doing: lines come back in new positions, nothing is added or destroyed, just rearranged. the opening stanzas should sit with the ordinary dread of death and the scene where huizi shows up to mourn zhuangzi's wife and finds him drumming on a tub and singing, and huizi basically says this is too much. but it should also register that zhuangzi did grieve at first — that the equanimity is something he arrives at, not a performance. the middle should turn on his reply: before she was born there was no life, no body, no breath, and then one change after another brought her here, and now shes changed again the way the seasons turn, so to keep wailing would be to miss the pattern. the ending should land on the part of the text that actually keeps me up, which is the genuine uncertainty — how do i know the dead dont regret having clung to life. leave that question open, dont resolve it into comfort. please skip the butterfly dream, every undergrad already knows that one. the repeated lines should be chosen so that when they come back in their new slots they mean something slightly different, because that recurrence-with-a-difference is the whole point of the view and the whole point of using a pantoum. it should be accurate enough that someone who knows the text recognizes the exact moves, and good enough that someone who has never read it still gets the shape of the position.
