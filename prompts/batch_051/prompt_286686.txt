So one of our postdocs has been sitting in on an anomaly-detection lab and came back to the mechanisms group with an argument I cant shake. His claim is that the phrase 'anomaly in a mechanism' is basically incoherent—that every anomaly is a different mechanism, never the same mechanism misbehaving. Here is the argument as he gave it, cleaned up a little.

(1) A mechanism is individuated by its component entities, their activities, and their organization: same mechanism means same components, same activities, same organization.
(2) In an anomalous run, the system's output differs from what the mechanism is supposed to produce.
(3) Output doesn't differ unless something in the full causal story differs.
(4) A difference in the full causal story is either a difference in the inputs/background conditions or a difference in the mechanism itself.
(5) The anomaly-detection people are not trying to flag different inputs; they are trying to flag internal faults—a stuck valve, a shorted sensor, a node that has stopped doing its job. So a genuine mechanism anomaly is a case where the mechanism itself differs from normal.
(6) Therefore, in any genuine mechanism anomaly, we have a different mechanism, by (1).
(7) So the same mechanism is never the thing that malfunctions. A jammed gear isn't the old mechanism failing; it's a new mechanism.

The conclusion can't be right, since half of mechanistic explanation consists in saying that a mechanism persisted while a part failed: the watch still has its movement when the escapement jams, the regulatory network is the same network when one node sticks. But I don't want to just reject (1), because it looks like the standard entities-activities-organization story I teach in the intro philosophy of science course. And if I weaken (1) to let broken parts count as the same mechanism, I need to know what 'same mechanism' then means under perturbation—which is exactly what the anomaly-detection people wanted to ask in the first place. Which premise should I give up, and what does the repair cost? I'd especially like to know what (1) has to become once we allow that a mechanism can survive a component failing to perform its normal activity.
