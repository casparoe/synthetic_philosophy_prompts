I'm a senior currently taking a philosophy of history seminar, and I got asked to give the student commencement speech at our university. One debate from the course keeps coming back to me: the hinge of history hypothesis — roughly, the idea that some stretches of time are wildly more consequential than others, and that ours may be one of them because of technologies like AI, climate change, and the sheer size of the future that our choices might affect. The opposing view says this is mostly present bias with a grand-sounding name: people in 1913, 1517, and 410 also had reasons to think their moment was uniquely pivotal, and the structure of that feeling should make us wary of trusting it now.

I want the idea to actually do work in the speech, not just be decoration. I don't want to stand there and tell my classmates we are the chosen generation — that feels false, and its exactly the self-flattery the course warned about. But I also don't want the cynical version, where I say no age is special, because that would drain the speech of any call to seriousness and its also an overclaim in the other direction. The awkward truth seems to be that we genuinely might be at a hinge, and we might not know which way it swings until afterward. The audience will be mostly graduates and their families, plus my professor and a few philosophy grad students, so it needs to land for both halves without becoming either a TEDx slogan or a seminar paper.

Can you write it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion? Like, the five moves of the speech in order, not five separate possible openings.
