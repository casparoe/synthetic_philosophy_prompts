I have been arguing with a friend about whether ordinary ways of praising size actually track anything in moral reality. We both notice that a larger family tends to be celebrated, a growing city is read as a sign of health, and a species whose numbers are climbing counts as a conservation success; we also agree that the average well-being involved can stay fixed while the population grows. Where we part ways is whether any of this familiar "more is good" attitude reflects a genuine feature of value. I give roughly 0.7 credence to the claim that there is impersonal value in additional good lives: that, holding average well-being constant at a clearly positive level, a world with twice as many such lives is, pro tanto and all else equal, morally better. She gives it about 0.2, on the grounds that what we actually respond to in bigger families and cities is status, narrative, or convenience, and that the approval would evaporate once those confounds were controlled for.

Give me your own number for that underlying claim, but more importantly lay out the reasoning. I would like the question decomposed into at least the following sub-questions, with a rough probability assigned to each: (1) Must all value be value for someone, or can there be impersonal value in a life's being lived well? (2) If impersonal value exists, does the marginal value of an additional good life stay positive and roughly constant, or does it fall toward zero — and does accepting it down to lives only barely worth living commit one to something like the Repugnant Conclusion, and how much should that count against it? (3) How much evidential weight should ordinary "bigger is better" attitudes about families, cities, and species receive, once confounds such as status, narrative, and simple growth bias are subtracted? (4) Is "better" in that ordinary talk even the same concept as impartial moral betterness, or is it closer to "more impressive" or "more secure"? Then say how these sub-questions combine to produce your overall credence, identify the sub-question to which your number is most sensitive, and say which of the two people — at 0.7 and 0.2 — most likely disagree about. End by naming the single piece of evidence or argument that would move your credence the most, and roughly by how much.
