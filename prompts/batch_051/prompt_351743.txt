Next week I am leading a two-hour seminar for philosophy PhD students, and the assigned topic is Lewis's Principal Principle — the thesis that rational credence should, in some precise sense, defer to objective chance. The group is fluent in Bayesian argument and comfortable with conditional probability, but none of them has done serious work on objective chance. My job is not to survey the literature; it is to get them to see why the principle is neither a trivial consequence of the word 'chance' nor a rule for how to update when someone announces a chance value, and why the interesting philosophical work lies in the restrictions on what may be taken as evidence. I do not want an explanation of the material for me; I want a teaching plan for them.

Please give me the sequence of ideas and why that order rather than another: what has to be on the table before the principle can be stated without inviting confusion, and what depends on what. Build the session around a single worked example — a coin, a radioactive atom, or some other idealized chance setup — and tell me exactly what features it must have if it is to carry the whole discussion, including the hard part, and what features would quietly mislead. I expect certain misconceptions to surface, such as 'chance is long-run frequency' and 'the principle just says: set your credence to whatever chance you learn.' Tell me which further misconceptions to anticipate, and at which point in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. The part I am most worried about is the middle turn of the session: the principle's simplicity disappears once the notion of admissible evidence is restricted, and the original formulation is strained by cases in which a chance theory assigns positive chance to a history that would make that very theory false — the undermining problem. Should I present that as a counterexample that forces a distinction, as motivation for Lewis's revised principle, or as something to flag and set aside, and what should the students take away from it? Suggest as well what I should explicitly leave out at this level and say I am leaving out. End by proposing one question I can put to the room that would tell me whether they have grasped the principle as a norm connecting two different kinds of probability, rather than merely memorized its form.
