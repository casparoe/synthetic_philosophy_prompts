I am a novelist, and I have been turning over a parallel between two uses of absence that I normally encounter in entirely separate parts of my life: the philosophical problem of causation by omission, and the craft problem of making a missing act do narrative work in a story.

Take the philosophical case. A gardener does not water your plants while you are away, and the plants die; we say the gardener's omission caused their death. But we do not say that the Queen of England's not watering the plants caused their death, even though a pure counterfactual test would treat the two absences alike, since the plants would have survived had she watered them. A standard response is that an omission becomes a cause only against a norm or expectation: the gardener was supposed to water the plants, the Queen was not. On this view, absences are not causes out there in the world in the way events are; they acquire causal status only when we contrast what happened with what a background of duties, habits, or normal functioning marked out as the default.

Now take the narrative case. A novel can make a missing event—the letter never posted, the warning withheld, the confession that never leaves the throat—bear enormous causal weight in the plot. Yet whether a reader experiences that absence as a cause seems to depend on what the story has led them to expect of the character in the circumstances. The same silence that would be devastating in one scene is inert in another. Fictional omission looks contrastive in the same way: the missing act becomes causally salient only against a norm the text has established, just as the gardener's omission is salient only against a norm of plant-tending.

I am trying to decide whether this is a deep structural identity or merely a loose analogy resting on the shared word absence. Please work through three things. First, state the mapping precisely: what in the philosophical case corresponds to what in the narrative case, and where the two structures come apart. Second, say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one domain to the other—for instance, if fiction works by training readers in norm-relative causal judgment, does that tell us anything about ordinary causal thought, or is storytelling merely exploiting a cognitive tendency that the metaphysics is independently trying to describe? Third, name at least one concrete thing the parallel predicts that turns out false, and say whether that failure dissolves the analogy or only localizes it.
