I have recently started using word embeddings in a project on conceptual drift in political language, and a colleague in my working group has pressed the following argument on me, which I cannot answer to my satisfaction. (1) On the embedding picture, a word's meaning just is its vector in a high-dimensional semantic space, and semantic relations among words are geometric relations—proximity, direction, and vector offsets—in that space. (2) Hence if two word-vectors are close under the standard cosine metric, the words are close in meaning. (3) But in standard embedding spaces, antonym pairs such as hot/cold, love/hate, and buy/sell are reliably among each other's nearest neighbors. (4) So the geometry ranks antonyms as near-synonyms. (5) Antonyms are opposed in meaning, and no representation can rank a pair both as opposed and as near-synonyms while claiming to capture semantic similarity. (6) Therefore either embeddings do not model meaning at all in the sense that their celebrated analogy results require, or those analogy results (for instance, king − man + woman ≈ queen) are mere artifacts rather than semantic structure. I would prefer to keep treating embeddings as at least partly semantic, but I cannot honestly say whether the argument contains a bad premise, a bad step, or a conclusion I must simply live with. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
