Last night a close friend and I argued at length about when a piece of software ought to count as orphaned, in the sense that others may reasonably treat it as effectively unowned. He defended a four-part test, which he believes applies to any creative work. On his account, a work is orphaned when (1) a diligent search cannot identify or locate the rightsholder; (2) the rightsholder is no longer offering or maintaining the work in a way that amounts to ongoing stewardship; (3) the only thing standing between someone and an otherwise-legitimate use is a formal requirement of permission; and (4) there is no credible indication that the rightsholder will resume meaningful control of the work, whether through licensing, distribution, or active maintenance. I was uneasy with this test, but I could not say exactly what it was missing.

To sharpen the question, take the following hypothetical case assembled from several stories I have heard. A software package I will call MammalScope began around 2007 as the doctoral project of a marine biologist, its sole author and rightsholder, and became the de facto standard for detecting beaked whale clicks in long passive-acoustic recordings. Hundreds of papers and several government monitoring pipelines now depend on it. Around 2016 the author left academia for an industry job; he stopped answering substantive email about the project, and the documentation domain eventually lapsed, so new users now learn the workflow from archived pages and third-party tutorials. The code remains publicly downloadable under a license the author wrote himself, which permits academic and noncommercial use but requires his written permission to redistribute modified versions. He has not disappeared entirely: once or twice a year he still merges a trivial pull request, usually a typo correction or a version-string update, with a one-line comment, and his account shows ordinary sporadic activity unrelated to the project. Recently a group of researchers found that the package silently produces wrong annotations for a widely adopted recording format. They have a patch ready, but years of patient emails, a registered letter to his last known address, and a request routed through his former university's technology-transfer office have produced no substantive response.

Does MammalScope qualify as orphaned under my friend's four-part test, such that the group may treat it as effectively unowned and circulate the corrected version? Please go through the criteria honestly. Where the verdict is genuinely unsettled, say whether that is because the criteria are doing meaningful work or because the case exposes a defect in them. In particular, I want to know whether the test conflates being findable with being reachable, or occasional housekeeping with stewardship, and how you would revise the criteria in light of this case. I am less interested in what a particular court would do than in whether the concept of orphanhood, as my friend framed it, can coherently handle this case.
