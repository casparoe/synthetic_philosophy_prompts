I chair a university committee revising our general-education curriculum. Our procedures are formally open: every member may speak, and final decisions are taken by majority vote. In practice, senior faculty dominate the discussions; their length of service is treated as itself a decisive reason, junior members' proposals are taken up only when a senior member sponsors them, the adjunct and student representatives are rarely called upon, and when a disagreement appears likely to delay the vote the chair declares that we are close enough to consensus and proceeds. A colleague has suggested that what troubles me is not ordinary bias but a deeper failure in the conditions under which a deliberative outcome can be legitimate for everyone bound by it. I want a precise account of that defect, and of what my committee would need to change before its decisions could claim that kind of legitimacy. What should such an account say?
