I am a retired natural scientist — by training an earth scientist — and over the last two years I have been reading philosophy of history. I have accumulated a set of verdicts about period labels that I cannot yet reduce to a single principle, and I would like you to help me either find the principle or identify which verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases, with the judgments I actually make.

1. The Middle Ages. I call this an invention. The label was applied by people who came later, to a stretch of time whose inhabitants did not share any sense of being in a 'middle' anything; it unites centuries and regions that strike me as more different than alike. I understand that all boundaries are drawn somewhere, but this one feels like a filing category imposed from outside rather than a feature of the past.

2. The Renaissance. I also call this an invention, though I feel more resistance. There was a real, self-conscious movement in Italian letters and art whose participants believed they were reviving antiquity after a long decay, and genuinely new things were made and thought inside that movement. But I gather that the unified European 'Renaissance' was assembled after the fact by historians and art historians. So: a later construction, but with a real movement inside it.

3. The Scientific Revolution. This is where I start to lose my footing. The term was attached after the fact, just like the previous ones, yet I cannot bring myself to call it an invention. Between Copernicus and Newton the practices of natural philosophy changed — instruments, experiment, quantification, and the standing of experience — and the change was consequential and, as far as I can tell, irreversible. I want to say the Scientific Revolution is real despite its retrospective name. I cannot yet say what separates it from the Renaissance case.

4. The Anthropocene. My professional instincts come in here. A geologist working far in the future would find in sediment and ice a boundary from the middle of the twentieth century: carbon-isotope shifts from fossil-fuel burning, a pulse of fallout radionuclides from open-air nuclear tests, plastics, aluminium, and the like. That is a discontinuity in the rock record recoverable from many independent proxies, quite apart from whatever any commission decides about the name, its rank, or its exact start date. So my verdict is: real as a stratigraphic fact, with the usual conventionality about the boundary.

5. The Cold War. I lived through it and would call it real. The phrase was in use while the thing was going on; the condition organized the behavior of states, alliances, deterrence, and arms races; it had a recognizable beginning and end even if one can argue about exactly where either falls. I am not entirely sure whether this is the same kind of case as the others, and that uncertainty is part of what I want you to sort out.

My working rule is something like this: a periodization is real when its boundary marks a change in the rate or character of an underlying process — a cut that can be recovered from independent evidence rather than one that exists only in a story — and it is invented when the label does the work the date does not. But I can see that this rule borders on circularity, and it says too little about why the Middle Ages should count as a single period at all, even an invented one.

What I would like from you is the best principle you can state, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. Then please test it against two cases I have not settled. The first is the Industrial Revolution, which I am pulled to call real but which has no agreed start and hides a slow transformation inside the word 'revolution'. The second is the Enlightenment, where I have the opposite problem from the Middle Ages: many eighteenth-century writers thought their own moment was peculiarly rational, yet I still suspect the period is partly a later extrapolation from a few cities to a whole continent. Finally, tell me honestly which of my five verdicts cannot be accommodated under the principle you judge best, and what it would cost me to keep that verdict instead. I am prepared to give one of them up if the result is a cleaner rule.
