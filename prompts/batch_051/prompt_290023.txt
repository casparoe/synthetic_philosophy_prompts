In legal systems with a void-for-vagueness doctrine, a penal statute is supposed to fail when it neither gives ordinary people fair notice of what it prohibits nor supplies officials with standards sufficiently determinate to prevent arbitrary enforcement. Yet the same systems routinely impose serious criminal liability through terms as open-textured as any struck-down prohibition: reasonable belief, gross negligence, recklessness, depraved heart, extreme emotional disturbance. The asymmetry is visible in a matched pair. An ordinance that makes it an offense to act in a public place in a manner likely to annoy others is treated as a paradigm of unconstitutional vagueness. An ordinance that makes it an offense to act in a public place in a manner that recklessly creates a serious risk of death is treated, by the same legal culture, as an ordinary and unproblematic penal provision. In both cases the prohibited conduct is picked out only through an evaluative phrase whose extension competent speakers dispute, and in both cases a finder of fact must supply the missing content after the alleged violation. What principle, if any, justifies the differential treatment? Give the strongest principle you can construct, show how it classifies the two ordinances, then stress-test it against one or two boundary cases of your own invention and state whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
