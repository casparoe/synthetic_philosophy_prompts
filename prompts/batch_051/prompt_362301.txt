I spent forty years doing field ecology and animal behavior, mostly waterfowl surveys, and I've only recently started reading philosphy of language. Something has been nagging me alot, and it concerns the bare general claims philosophers call generics, as I understand it. I've always been comfortable saying that ducks lay eggs and that mosquitoes carry malaria. I know the biology well enough: at any given moment only a minority of ducks are laying, drakes and juveniles never do, and only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes ever carries malaria. Yet I would not say that ducks are female, even though about half of ducks are female, which is a much larger share than the share currently laying eggs. If I were counting individuals, the claim ducks are female covers far more ducks than the claim ducks lay eggs does, but the first sounds obviously wrong to me and the second obviously right. So I have an asymmetry in my own judgments: some properties held by a small minority of a kind get stated as if they were facts about the kind, while other properties held by an even larger minority don't.

It plainly isn't just a matter of how many, so what draws the line? I keep groping toward something like, the property has to be characteristic of the kind, or grounded in what the creature's biology is for, but those feel vague and I'd like them pinned down or replaced. Give me the strongest candidate principle you can find — dispositional, causal, salience-based, whatever — and test it against nearby cases, such as sharks attack people, which I still accept even though vanishingly few sharks ever have, versus ducks are brown, which I reject even though plenty of ducks are brown. Then tell me: is my asymmetry defensible, or should I give up one of these judgments?
