I am a retired natural scientist who has only recently come to moral philosophy, and I keep returning to an episode that seems to pose the Stoic–Romantic dispute about the emotions in an unusually sharp form: the mass public grief that followed the death of Diana, Princess of Wales, in 1997. I know the basic history and am not asking for a retelling of it; I want a disciplined philosophical case analysis of that episode through the two competing accounts of emotion. Take, as a working statement of one side, the Stoic view that grief is a passion resting on the false judgment that an external loss is an evil, so that the wise person would not be inwardly disturbed by it; take, on the other side, the Romantic or sentimentalist view that grief of this kind is fitting and proportionate, a mark of love and moral seriousness, and that its public expression was a sign of emotional health rather than confusion. Work through the episode in both directions: assess what each lens illuminates and obscures about the principal elements of the episode — the crowds leaving flowers outside the palaces, the tabloid demand that the royal family display grief, the criticism of the royal family's initial reticence, and the Queen's eventual televised address — and then say what the episode in turn shows about whether the emotions are part of the good life or merely noise. Throughout, and especially in the final section, separate what is settled about these positions from what is genuinely contested among those who study them, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Structure the response under these headings, in this order: The two theories (about 250 words); The episode through each lens (about 400 words); Where each lens misfits (about 300 words); What the episode shows about the emotions (about 250 words); Settled vs. contested (about 200 words).
