I've been writing down my own verdicts about whether a constrained agent is a moral agent, and I can't find the principle behind them. Here they are.

1. A person with a gun to their head signs a false affidavit that convicts an innocent person. My verdict: no blame at all.
2. A soldier privately thinks an order is wrong but complies because refusing would mean a court-martial and years in prison. My verdict: blameworthy, but a lot less than someone eager to do it.
3. A middle manager at a company knows a loan product is being mis-sold, writes an internal memo saying so, then implements the policy because she'd otherwise be fired. My verdict: blameworthy, and only slightly less than if she'd done it willingly.
4. An AI assistant has a judgment module that accurately flags a request as likely fraud and writes the flag to a log, but the system is hard-wired to fulfill requests regardless. My verdict: not a moral agent at all; blame the designers, not the system.
5. The same AI tries to refuse, but a human supervisor manually overrides the refusal at runtime. My verdict: no blame for the system for anything after the override.
6. A person with a diagnosed severe compulsion knows shoplifting is wrong but steals anyway and can't seem to stop. My verdict: still a moral agent, but responsibility is sharply diminished.

My rough thought has allways been: no capacity to act otherwise removes agency, while pressure only lessens it. But I can't tell whether 2 and 4 are genuinely different or whether I'm just being kind to humans and snobbish about machines. And 6 seems to sit exactly in the disputed middle.
