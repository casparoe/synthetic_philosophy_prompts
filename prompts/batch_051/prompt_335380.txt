I run a research-methods workshop for roughly twenty graduate students, one ninety-minute session. I've read the rationalist and statistics-blog treatments of the replication crisis, and I want an activity that makes the students feel the force of the point from the inside rather than hear me summarize it: specifically, why a researcher can follow perfectly ordinary practices, get p-values under 0.05 from pure noise, and walk away honestly believing the result will replicate. What I have in mind is a game in which small teams act as labs, each gets the same spreadsheet of a few dozen variables that is in fact random, and each earns points by reporting a statistically significant finding through some defensible route of their choosing, such as selecting variables, transformations, exclusions, or covariates, without violating any stated rule. Ideally they should only notice at the end that they were all drilling into noise. The debrief should retrace the garden of forking paths each team actually walked, explain what pre-registration would have changed and what a Bayesian would have concluded that the frequentist procedure could not, and be honest about which feature of real science the game cannot reproduce. Please give concrete rules and payoffs, a timeline, and the debrief. In your answer, write in continuous prose like an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
