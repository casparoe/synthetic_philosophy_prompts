Give me a working definition of "trust in government" that seperates trusting the government from just approving of it, going along out of fear, or being too checked-out to care. I want the definition to handle these cases: a Swede who assumes the tax authority is competent and honest and then doesn't think about it again; an American who thinks their own party's president is doing great but assumes the other party's appointees are lying whenever they open their mouths; a person who follows a public-health order only because they're scared of the fine; someone who reads the minutes of every city council meeting because they expect officials to quietly self-deal unless someone is watching; a whistleblower who leaks a classified report hoping the public will punish the abuse; and a person who votes and then ignores politics for four years. Then run the definition through all six and tell me where it gives a verdict you didn't expect or where it refuses to give one. I suspect the high-trust Scandinavian and the low-trust American aren't actually trusting (or distrusting) the same thing, and I want the definition to show what the difference is. Finally, use that definition to settle the question behind all this: as a default attitude, which is more reasonable, Scandinavian-style trust or American-style suspicion? I want a definite verdict, and I want you to say which of those six cases most embarrasses the side you pick — no both-have-merits waffling.
