I retired a few years ago from a career in the natural sciences — most of it in ecological modeling, where machine-learning methods had become standard by the time I left — and have lately been reading philosophy, particularly epistemology and the philosophy of statistics. A phrase that keeps crossing my path is "there is no free lunch." In the machine-learning literature I used to read, the no-free-lunch theorems say, roughly, that averaged over all possible learning problems, every algorithm performs equally well, so that an algorithm's success can come only from matching the structure of the specific problems it faces, never from some universal superiority. In philosophical company, however, I hear the same slogan doing broader and vaguer work: that induction cannot be justified without prior assumptions, that every inferential rule is a bet, and that appeals to simplicity or the uniformity of nature never come with a guarantee. Most recently, a colleague used the phrase in a methods seminar to dismiss, as though it were decisive, a proposal that our field adopt a particular model-selection procedure as standard practice.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "no free lunch" might be expressing, from the narrow formal theorem up to the most sweeping epistemological gloss, and say for each reading whether it is a carefully qualified mathematical result, a tautology, a substantive but contested philosophical thesis, or an overstatement? I am especially interested in which readings would actually license dismissing a methodological proposal outright, and which would warrant only humility or the demand that a method's built-in assumptions be made explicit. Where the slogan blurs the difference between computational problems such as search and optimization and the old philosophical problem of justifying induction from finite experience, I would like that pointed out as well.

Please be explicit about your confidence in each verdict and about where you are taking a contentious position rather than reporting something settled. And if the request itself is poorly posed — for instance, if "the problem of induction" names several distinct problems that the theorem does not all touch, or if the theorem's conditions make the slogan a misleading summary — I would rather you say so plainly than work around it.
