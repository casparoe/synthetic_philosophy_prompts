Suppose a large philanthropic foundation has become convinced that its grantmaking suffers from cluelessness: it can roughly estimate the first-order effects of an intervention but cannot see how those effects ramify over decades, and it suspects that the unquantifiable downstream consequences dominate the expected value of almost everything it funds. In response, the foundation adopts a single rule for all grants above a modest threshold. Every application must include a causal model running at least one hundred years into the future, represented as a directed graph of events connected by labeled causal links, each link carrying a probability and a magnitude; applications must also include an uncertainty budget stating how much of the intervention's total expected value the applicant counts as modeled and how much as unmodeled. Grants are sized in proportion to modeled expected value, then discounted by a rate that rises with the share of value the applicant leaves unmodeled. A separate internal office may veto any application whose graph contains a node the applicant marks simply as unknown, meaning no probability distribution is supplied. Analyze how this scheme would actually operate, not whether it is well intentioned. What sorts of interventions and applicants would it favor, and how would applicants learn to comply with the formal requirements while leaving the underlying uncertainty untouched? Does the hundred-year graph take on a bureaucratic meaning that drifts from its epistemic one? Does the discount rule genuinely penalize cluelessness, or does it reward the appearance of tractability at the expense of honest uncertainty? Where are the chief failure modes, and could any of them be fixed without undoing the rule's purpose? Answer in continuous prose, as you would write a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
