Bertrand Russell writes in his 1905 article "On Denoting": "All thinking has to start from acquaintance; but it succeeds in thinking about many things with which we have no acquaintance." I am a novelist, and this sentence has unsettled the way I think about my own work. When I write that a fictional character did something, I seem to be thinking about that character, and my readers take themselves to be thinking about her too; yet neither of us is acquainted with any such entity. I want an essay that makes this passage do real work in settling a controversy about fiction. The controversy is this: does a name like "Sherlock Holmes" refer, and what is true in the stories? Some hold that sentences like "Sherlock Holmes lived in Baker Street" are straightforwardly true within the fiction but false without; some hold that they are not really true at all, but shorthand for an implicit "in the fiction" prefix; some hold that the name refers to an abstract object, an artifact of Conan Doyle's writing. Weigh these positions together with the Russellian view that the name does not function as a genuine referring expression but as something like a disguised description or a quantified phrase. I would like you to reach and defend a definite verdict rather than ending in a draw, though a carefully qualified conclusion is acceptable. And if the way I have framed the problem already confuses something—whether about acquaintance, reference, or the notion of truth in fiction—please say so plainly and correct the premise instead of writing around it. Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; write in continuous prose, like an essay in a letter.
