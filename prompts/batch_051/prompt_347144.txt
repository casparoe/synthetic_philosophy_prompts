Please comment, in detail, on the following notes for an essay on convention and the evolution of norms. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations; I want substantive engagement with the argument as sketched.

The essay starts from Lewis's analysis of convention as a commonly known regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, sustained mainly by mutual expectations and shared interests. I want to press on a limitation in that picture. Many norms that get discussed under the heading of convention seem to persist not because each person conforms in order to coordinate with like-minded others, but because a small minority is willing to punish violations, and punishment is effective only when bystanders or third parties share an underlying regularity about when punishment is appropriate. The stability of the first-order norm therefore depends on a further norm about punishment, and that further norm looks itself like a convention of the sort it was meant to explain. I want to argue that this is not a vicious circle but a layered structure: some base-level regularities are held in place by plain preference alignment, while higher-level norms are held in place by enforcement that is itself coordinated.

Tentative thesis: what mainly evolves in social and moral norms is not the first-order regularities of conduct but the second-order regularities specifying who may punish, how severely, and in front of whom. Norm evolution is most consequential when punishment rights migrate from diffuse bilateral retaliation to specialized third-party enforcement, because that is what makes the underlying higher-order convention explicit and thus open to deliberate revision.

I would be especially grateful for comments on four things. First, is the contrast with Lewis fair to his actual view, or does it import a use of convention he would not have endorsed? Second, how serious is the circularity worry, and can the layered-structure reply discharge it without an infinite regress? Third, is the thesis about second-order regularities genuinely interesting, or does it reduce to familiar game-theoretic points about higher-order beliefs and common knowledge? Fourth, what is the strongest objection from someone who says sanctions simply change the payoff structure of the original coordination game, and how should the essay answer it?
