I spent forty years running dose-response experiments, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy, and I've got a homemade view about thought experiments I'd like someone to check. I think of a thought experiment as a single-point titration: the author picks an extreme dose of one variable—push the fat man off the bridge, carve up the healthy patient, flip the trolley switch—and then asks our moral judgment for a read-out. But in the lab you would never infer an entire response curve from one near-saturating dose. A compound can activate at low concentration and kill at high concentration; a response can plateau or turn back down. I suspect moral judgment is the same. At extreme doses the response saturates on the fact that a trade-off is being forced on us, and the intuition tells you little about the ordinary range where the principle is supposed to do its work. Two rival principles can saturate in the same direction in a dramatic case, so the case can't separate them. I call this the saturated-probe worry. It isn't quite the complaint that the cases are unrealistic; it's that a measurement taken outside a method's working range can't tell you much about the method. I'm sure this is already somewhere in the literature, so I don't need a citation—I want a verdict. Reconstruct my idea in its strongest form, tell me whether its more than just saying the cases are unrealistic, and give me the clearest counterexample: a thought experiment that uses an extreme dose yet remains a reliable probe. And say where my picture breaks down.
