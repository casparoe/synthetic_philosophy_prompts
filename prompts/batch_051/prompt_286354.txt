Okay so I just spent the whole evening arguing with a friend about deplatforming, and I realized the argument kept collapsing because we couldn't decide what we were even disagreeing about. He kept saying deplatforming isn't punishment at all—it's just a private company chosing who can use its service, like a bar kicking out a drunk. I kept saying that can't be the whole story, because the practice doesn't feel like a neutral terms-of-service decision. The platform singles out a person, publicizes the removal, points at something they said or did as the reason, and the effect is that their income, audience, and reputation get hit hard. Other people use it as a warning: don't be like X. That's punishment-shaped, whatever you call it.

Then I started noticing how uneven it is. A creator gets removed for "harassment" while another account running an obvious pump-and-dump scam, or posting slurs under a pseudonym, stays up for months. The severity doesn't track the apparent wrong very precisely; it tracks how loud the outrage is, what advertisers think, and how bad the platform looks. And the same company will say both "we don't judge people, we just enforce our rules" and "we're holding people accountable." Those can't both be the innocent just-business line.

So here's what I want. I need someone who actually knows punishment theory and social norm enforcement to tell me what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes, whether the people running it admit them or not. Reconstruct the implicit theory of punishment—or of social sanction, if you think "punishment" is the wrong frame—that deplatforming and the adjacent practices are committed to: employer firings over old tweets, universities disinviting speakers, payment processors cutting off customers. I want it stated precisely, not just "it's complicated." Does the practice strictly presuppose something retributive—that the wrongdoer deserves the harm, and the removal is done as a message that they deserve it? Can it all be explained by deterrence (harm the one to scare the many), incapacitation (take away the microphone), or a pure contract view (not punishment at all)? And what does the inconsistency—severity tracking public pressure rather than the wrong—tell me about which of these is really doing the work?

Act as a professional philosopher who works on punishment, blame, and the difference between legal and social sanction. Before you commit to a view, do a quick brainstorm where you list the candidate principles and run each through two cases: (a) a big platform removing a loud political figure for posts trying to undermine confidence in elections, and (b) a payment processor refusing service to a small business owner because an out-of-context old text leaked and went viral. Then please structure your answer exactly like this:

1. The practice nailed down (~150 words). Restate what deplatforming and the adjacent sanctions consist in, and seperate the contractual removal from the public, shaming, livelihood-destroying part.
2. What the practice strictly presupposes (~250 words). Lay out the implicit theory of punishment or social sanction that the practice only makes sense under, stated as precisely as you can.
3. What it merely fits with (~200 words). Additional commitments that sit comfortably with the practice but aren't forced by it.
4. Where the practice is underdetermined (~150 words). Where several rival theories would license the exact same set of cases, so the practice by itself doesn't decide between them.
5. What would have to change (~150 words). Suppose the real underlying theory were only harm-reduction: no desert, no standing to blame, just minimizing future bad effects on the service. What would the practice have to stop doing if it were being honest?
6. Bottom line (~100 words). What's the cleanest thing I can say to my friend next time, and which of us was less wrong?
