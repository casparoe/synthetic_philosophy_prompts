In his essay Of Money (from the Political Discourses), Hume says the amount of gold and silver a country has is, in the long run, pretty much a non-issue: money is just a way of rating labour and commodities, and if every pound in the kingdom were magically doubled overnight, prices would eventually catch up and nothing real would change — you'd have more coins but no more wealth. But a few pages later in the same essay he seems to run in the other direction: he says that when money begins to flow into a kingdom in greater abundance than before, the whole economy picks up, the merchant gets more enterprising, the manufacturer grows more industrious, even the farmer works with more energy; and he adds that a good government should keep money slowly increasing, becuase that keeps the nation's industrious spirit alive and adds to the stock of labour, which is the real source of its power and riches.

So here's the puzzle: are those two claims in conflict, or is the second just a short-run qualification of the first? If it is a short-run qualification, what exactly is the mechanism — why wouldn't people notice the money coming in and raise their prices right away, so that nothing real changes even in the short run? Lay out the candidate reconciliations (a level-versus-change distinction, different senses of 'money', a difference in time horizon, a change of mind on Hume's part, or anything else you think is live), and then say which reading is honest to the text and which ones strike you as special pleading. If you think the tension is real and can't be fully dissolved, say where the residual problem is and why it matters for his overall view of trade.
