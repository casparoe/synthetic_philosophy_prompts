You are a senior strategic analyst with deep expertise in Thomas Schelling's theory of conflict, credible commitment, and brinkmanship, and with practical experience in nuclear crisis management. I work for the Strategic Risk Analysis Unit of a defense ministry. During an emerging crisis with a nuclear-armed adversary, my team fields a continuous stream of signals: open-source statements, diplomatic notes, movements of dual-capable forces, changes in alert posture, exercises, and fragmentary intelligence about delegations of launch authority. The analysts on the floor are not game theorists. They have roughly 45 minutes to produce an initial read on each significant signal, and above a specified threshold their assessment must be signed off by a legal advisor and then by political authorities before any response is authorized.

I need a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — that an ordinary analyst can run on each significant signal. It should translate Schelling's central ideas into concrete questions asked in order: credibility; costly signals versus cheap talk; the difference between a warning and a threat; brinksmanship as the competitive manipulation of shared risk; and the deterrence paradox that a threat can be made credible not by promising to carry it out, but by arranging that it may be carried out without anyone's further decision, so that part of the choice is taken out of the leadership's hands. The procedure should say what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where it stops with a result such as monitor or no appreciable escalation risk, where it stops with immediate escalation to leadership, and where it must hand the judgment to a person because the question has become one of tolerable risk rather than analysis of the other side.

Then run the procedure on three invented but realistic cases: (1) a public ultimatum accompanied by a large conventional mobilization but no observable change in the adversary's nuclear posture; (2) quiet delegation of firing authority to local air-defense commanders coupled with nuclear-capable bomber flights along a frontier; and (3) a series of threats that most analysts privately judge to be cheap talk, in a context where our own intelligence admits it cannot exclude small, non-observable autonomous triggers. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation the user will not like. Then say what the procedure cannot do — what it cannot settle about the other side's true intentions, and what it cannot settle about which risks are acceptable.

Be self-critical throughout. For each worked case and for the procedure as a whole, say which new evidence or which counterargument would change your mind about the classification or recommendation, and identify where the reasoning is most likely to fail. Before drafting, think through which Schellingian distinctions are genuinely load-bearing in a fast triage setting and which you can deliberately simplify or drop, and say briefly which you dropped and why.
