I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether honesty and professional integrity are valuable only because they tend to produce good outcomes, or whether they carry authority even when the ledger of consequences points the other way; the argument stayed abstract, and I came away wanting something a non-philosopher could actually run. I work in the ethics-and-compliance office of a large engineering firm. Several times a month, a review lands on my desk in which a project manager or engineer made a call that departs from the cost-benefit case — refusing to sign off on a design, disclosing a fault to a client before the firm would prefer, or raising a concern internally — and the person defends the call by appealing to honesty, professional integrity, safety, or loyalty. My office must write a recommendation on whether that appeal is a legitimate ground for the decision or a rationalization; the general counsel or a senior vice president signs off, and project schedules, careers, and regulatory exposure ride on the recommendation.

I would like you to build a reusable decision procedure for these reviews, grounded in the philosophical debate over whether virtues and duties are derivative of good outcomes, merely instrumental to producing them, or independently authoritative. The reviewers who run it will not be philosophers, so the procedure should translate the distinctions rather than parade them. Give me an ordered sequence of questions, a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with the conclusion that the duty or virtue gets no independent weight, the points where it must hand the decision to a human because the question is evaluative rather than factual, and the points where the duty or virtue carries weight all the way up. It must be runnable in under an hour by someone without the theory.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a senior engineer refuses to sign off on a cost-saving change that meets the letter of the safety code but not its intent, citing professional integrity; (2) a project manager discloses a likely legal defect to a client early, weakening the firm's negotiating position but probably preventing a larger dispute, citing honesty; and (3) an employee reports widespread but small expense-account padding inside their own team, citing honesty, knowing the investigation will disrupt the team and that the sums involved are small. At least one of these should come out in a way my office would find unwelcome. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Please lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — the one-sentence decision rule the procedure amounts to — and then give the reasoning and the full procedure, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
