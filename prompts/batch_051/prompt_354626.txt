i keep tripping over the same thing in population ethics and i've started calling it "denominator drift." ordinary moral thinking doesn't seem to use one consistent aggregation. when we improve an existing life, people run a total: +10 welfare is +10 welfare, nobody asks what it does to the average. when we add a life, people suddenly run an average: they ask whether the new kid would be at least as well off as the existing kids, whether the country would "decline," whether a species still counts if there are only a few of them. same underlying quantity, different denominator.

here is a toy version. a family has two kids, both at 8 welfare. improving one of them to 10 is treated as unambiguously good — total and average both go up. now consider adding a third kid at 6. total goes from 16 to 22, so on total logic it is better; but average goes from 8 to 7.3, and people really do seem to treat the addition as a serious question or a loss in a way they never would for the improvement case. (bracket resources and attention.) i think this is most of why the repugnant conclusion feels repugnant: if you are inside the world already, adding a billion lives barely worth living feels like it lowers the average; but if you are choosing between two worlds from nowhere, the total view can suddenly feel obviously right.

so my half-baked theory: ordinary moral judgment has a hidden indexical denominator, "the people already in the circle." it totals changes that keep the denomintor fixed, and averages changes that expand or shrink it. this is supposed to explain why adding a life feels stranger and more contested than improving one, and why we talk about families/cities/species with average-loaded words like "thriving" while we talk about helping actual individuals with total-loaded words like "relief."

i want this checked hard: steelman the denominator view, tell me if it already exists under a less embarrassed name, and then give me the strongest counterexample you can — a case where ordinary moral thinking clearly does not follow my rule — and say whether that kills it or just forces a patch.
