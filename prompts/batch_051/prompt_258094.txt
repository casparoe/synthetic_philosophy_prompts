I'm putting together a bioethics module where students read fiction alongside the philosophy, and I want to pair Flowers for Algernon with the debate about advance directives and whether your earlier self has authority over what happens to you later. The standard cases in that debate involve a competent adult who writes instructions for their future demented self, and the argument is over how much the earlier person should get to bind the later one. Flowers for Algernon seems to scramble this in ways I find interesting but maybe problematic. The Charlie who agrees to the surgery is intellectually disabled and doesn't fully understand the experiment; the Charlie who comes out of it is a genius who feels he's been treated as a lab subject rather than a person, and who understands what was traded away better than anyone around him; then he declines again, and the book ends with him deciding to go to a state home. So the earlier self isn't the obviously authoritative one, and the later self isnt the obviously diminished one either.

What I'd like is a careful reading of the novel through that lens. Does the early Charlie's consent bind the later Charlies, and if it does, which version of him gets to speak for the whole arc? Where does the novel support or resist the usual moves in the advance-directive debate: the authority of the earlier competent self, the claim that the present self's interests can override old instructions, the idea that enough psychological change means the later self is just a different person? Point me to specific scenes worth lingering on in class, and say what the novel shows that the journals-style arguments tend to miss. And be honest where the lens breaks—if the book is really about exploitation or care or disability and the advance-directive analogy only half-works, say that rather than forcing it.
