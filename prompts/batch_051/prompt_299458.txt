You are a moral philosopher who specializes in distributive ethics and in turning normative theories into practical guidance. I run the distributional analysis team at a national health technology assessment agency. We review about ten funding proposals a month, on a one-week turnaround, and my analysts are health economists without formal philosophy training. I want a reusable, one-page screening procedure that operationalizes prioritarianism: the idea that a fixed health gain matters more the worse off the recipient is in absolute terms, but that inequality itself has no independent value. Before you give the procedure, think through the possible interpretations of worse off an agency could use, such as current disease severity, lifetime health disadvantage, or expected future health without the intervention, and explain which the procedure should use and why. The procedure must tell us when a distributional weight should alter a funding decision, how to set its size without inventing arbitrary numbers, and how to avoid smuggling in leveling-down reasoning under the cover of priority weights. It should also say what to do when we have only QALYs, group averages, and cost data rather than individual utility functions. Work through at least three hypothetical cases, including one where the procedure tells us to reject an intervention that would narrow a health inequality without making the worse-off group absolutely better off, and one where it tells us to deprioritize a more cost-effective intervention for a moderately ill group in favor of a less cost-effective intervention for a much worse-off group. Be explicit about which steps are irreducibly judgment calls, where the checklist will misfire, and what the procedure cannot do.
