I spent most of my career around enzymes and cell cultures, so when I retired and started reading philosophy, Montaigne's essays were the first thing that felt like bench work to me — you try things, you hedge, you double back. In "Of Cannibals" he makes a methodological point very early, and it has lodged in my head: "By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report."

Here's what I can't shake. A biotech startup now sells what it calls self-steaks: you send in a small biopsy, they culture your own muscle tissue in a bioreactor, and you cook and eat it. Nobody is killed, nobody is hurt, the cells would have sloughed off your body anyway, and the whole thing is consensual. Everyone I mention this to recoils and calls it barbaric, or at least monstrous. But Montaigne's line seems to say the recoil itself is exactly what we are supposed to distrust, since it comes from vulgar opinion rather than from the eye of reason. The thing is, he doesn't conclude that nothing can be condemned. Later in the same essay he appears to draw a distinction between cruelty to the living and strange customs about the dead and is absolutely willing to call torture barbaric while defending the people he writes about. So treat that quoted sentence as the rule for judging a practice Montaigne never imagined. What does it say, read literally, about self-steaks? What does it say read against the purpose of the whole essay? What would Montaigne have said if you could have described the bioreactor to him, and what would a reasonable modern reader take him to mean? I want the words-first reading, the purpose reading, and the what-he-would-have-said reading laid out seperately, and then a verdict on which one should govern. My suspicion is that the literal reading prohibits condemning the practice by common report but then goes dead silent, and I can't tell whether that's the point or a hole in the rule.
