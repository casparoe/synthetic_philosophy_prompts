im an urban economist, so the vocabulary i actually reach for is bid-rent curves, amenity capitalization, externalities, tiebout sorting, supply elasticities, and zoning as a binding cap on density. i keep running into housing debates that use a more political and moral idiom—the right to the city, neighborhoods as self-determining communities, housing as a home rather than an asset, a tenants' right not to be priced out—and i have a nagging feeling that when i restate these positions in my own terms, something drops out, but i also suspect some of it is less precise than it sounds. heres the assignment. take these three positions and restate each one as faithfully as you can in urban-economics vocabulary: (1) the right-to-the-city position: the city belongs to the people who inhabit and make it, not to landowners or capital, and its residents are entitled to collective control over urban space; (2) the neighborhood-preservationist position (what critics call NIMBYism): long-time residents have a legitimate claim to keep their neighborhood's character and block changes that would alter it, even when newcomers would pay to move in; (3) the supply-side/YIMBY position: housing is kept artificially scarce by restrictions, and building more is the main way to make cities cheaper and less exclusionary. for each one, tell me what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. then give the residue report: is the stuff that doesnt survive a sign that my framework is missing something real—norms, distribution, power—or that the political positions are smuggling in unexamined premises? which of the three is doing the most smuggling? and dont hedge: give me your actual verdict, with reasons. if your verdict is that theres a right to the city that people like me keep missing, say exactly what concept we'd have to add to our models to see it.
