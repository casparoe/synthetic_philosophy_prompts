I am a PhD student in philosophy working on moral psychology and institutional design, and I have been trying to make sense of an apparent inconsistency in how institutions treat partiality. On one side, journal referee forms instruct reviewers to decline when they have a close personal or professional tie to the author, and judges are expected to recuse themselves when a friend, relative, or former client is involved, as though such attachments contaminate judgment. On the other side, the same institutions are built around partial advocates—board members elected by constituencies, attorneys obligated to represent their clients zealously, officials expected to look after their own jurisdiction—and we typically praise loyalty to family and community rather than treat it as a defect. I would like a reconstruction of the implicit theory of partiality that makes this pattern coherent rather than merely contradictory. Specifically, does the practice strictly presuppose that partiality is a bias to be eliminated wherever feasible, or is it better read as a division-of-labor view in which partial motives are permitted or even cultivated in some roles and suppressed in others? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of partiality would license it, and what in the practice would actually have to change if one replaced the operative presupposition with the view that partiality is a virtue?
