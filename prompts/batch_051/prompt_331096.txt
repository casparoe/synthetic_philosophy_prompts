I have been trying to get clear about the boundary problem in democratic theory: the problem that a people cannot legitimately decide who belongs to the people, because every procedure for making that decision already presupposes an answer, and any proposed answer looks either circular or arbitrary. I would like to use two recent British referendums as a way of testing how deep the problem really is. In the 2016 referendum on the United Kingdom's membership of the European Union, the vote was open to British, Irish, and qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK or Gibraltar, and to UK citizens living abroad who had been registered to vote in the UK within the previous fifteen years. Citizens of other EU member states resident in the UK were for the most part excluded; Irish citizens and some Commonwealth citizens who also held EU nationality were included because of overlapping legal statuses. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds could not vote. In the 2014 Scottish independence referendum, by contrast, all EU and Commonwealth citizens resident in Scotland aged sixteen or over could vote, while people born in Scotland but resident elsewhere could not.

Treat this pair of referendums as a case study in the boundary problem. Map each franchise onto the main candidate principles for constituting a demos — the all-affected-interests principle, the all-subjected-to-the-laws principle, a citizenship or nationality criterion, and any hybrid you think is more defensible. For each principle, say what franchise it would have produced in 2014 and in 2016, and where that franchise departs from the one actually used. Then turn the cases back on the theory: does the fact that two referendums on the boundaries of political communities, held two years apart, drew the boundary in such different ways show that the boundary problem has no principled solution, or does it show only that the relevant institutions failed to apply the principles that were available? I am deliberately not asking for a verdict on Scottish independence or on Leave and Remain; I want an account of whether a theory of the demos can discipline its own first step, and, if it cannot, what kind of task we are left with.
