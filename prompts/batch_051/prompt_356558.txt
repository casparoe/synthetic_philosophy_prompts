I'm a novelist, not a planner, but I've been on my town's planning commission for two years, and a vote coming up next month has the whole board quietly dreading the meeting. A developer wants to rezone an old textile mill site and put up 1,400 apartments. The people who already live here have been showing up to the public comment sessions to oppose it, and their worries are real: the elementary school is already full, traffic on River Road would get worse, and the neighborhood would loose the low afternoon light it has now. I don't think their making it up. But the people who'd live in those apartments aren't in the room. Most of them are currently renting in the next town over and driving an hour each way to the same jobs, or doubling up with relatives. If we approve, about 3,000 of them get a substantially better life. If we reject, the 77,000 people already here keep the city they have, slightly nicer than if we'd built.

Our code says the commission should approve what serves the public interest, which is vague enough that our last discussion slid into a genuinely strange argument. One commissioner — a retired math teacher — keeps saying the question is how good the average resident's life is, and by that test the project fails, because the average dips a little. Another says that's obviously wrong-headed, because the total good living in the city goes up by a lot, and ignoring the 3,000 people who'd move here is like treating them as if they don't count unless they're already here. We went in circles on whether a city of 80,000 where each person is a touch worse off is a better or worse city than a city of 77,000 where each is a touch better off. As a novelist I keep reaching for a bad analogy: is a long novel with some uneven chapters a greater thing than a tight short novel? My spouse and I also keep half-having the same argument about a third kid, which probably means I'm not objective.

But I have to draft the actual decision memo for the meeting, and I don't want a both-sides document. Write the memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a committed recommendation and the reasoning behind it, including the strongest point the losing side has. A skeptical resident at the hearing should be able to see their argument taken seriously before the memo explains why we're deciding as we do.
