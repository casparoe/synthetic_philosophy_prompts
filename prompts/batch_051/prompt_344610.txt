Consider the following five features as the standard candidate grounds for treating someone as a compatriot to whom one has special claims — the kind of claim that would justify weighting that person's interests more heavily than a foreigner's when their interests conflict, in decisions about taxation, conscription, disaster relief, or social spending. Treat them as a weighted list of criteria: the more fully a person satisfies them, the stronger the case for a compatriot's special claim, though some features may matter more than others.

(1) Shared legal citizenship in the same state.
(2) Joint subjection to the same coercive legal and political institutions: its laws, courts, police, and tax system.
(3) Reciprocity within a common scheme of social and economic cooperation: contributing to and benefiting from the society's economy and public services over an extended period.
(4) Shared civic identity: participation in public life and mutual recognition as part of the same community, however thin.
(5) Democratic standing: a voice, or at least a realistic possibility of acquiring one, in making the laws one must obey.

Now consider Liora. She was born stateless and has lived in country R for thirty-four years as a permanent resident, with no realistic path to citizenship: naturalization in R is discretionary and has never been granted to anyone in her category. She is fully subject to R's civil and criminal law; she pays income, property, and sales taxes; she is liable to conscription; her children attend R's schools; and she receives the same public services and emergency relief as citizens. She speaks R's language natively, is married to a citizen, helps run a neighborhood civic association, and identifies as being from R. Most of her neighbors treat her as one of them, though in national political discourse permanent residents are sometimes described as outsiders. But she cannot vote in national elections, cannot serve on a jury, cannot hold public office, and carries a permanent-resident card rather than an R passport.

Does Liora have a compatriot's special claim on R's institutions and on its citizens — for example, a claim that her interests should be weighted above those of a foreigner with no connection to R when R sets its tax, conscription, education, or disaster-relief policies? Work through features (1)–(5) one by one, say how far she satisfies each, and say how much weight each feature should carry in the overall verdict. Then give your verdict, and say whether the case shows that the list itself needs revision: is shared legal citizenship doing the real normative work, or has the case exposed it as a proxy for something else? If the criteria underdetermine the verdict, say exactly where they run out and what should be added, dropped, or reweighted.
