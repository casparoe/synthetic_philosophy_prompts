You are a professional metaphysician with a background in philosophy of physics. Think carefully, and begin by briefly settling what you take the position to be before drawing out its consequences.

Consider the following position. The concept of causation is not a candidate for inclusion in any fundamental physical theory. Ordinary causal vocabulary is best understood as a pragmatic, interest-relative device: causal claims pick out coarse-grained, intervention-stable dependency relations that matter to agents, not a mind-independent something that cements events together. On this view, the question Is causation real? is almost as badly posed as Russell suggested when he treated causation as a relic of a bygone age; the serious remainder is not a single relation but a combination of structural facts about the world and facts about our epistemic and practical situation.

What are the most important implications of this position when we are choosing between rival accounts of special-science explanations, such as the claim that smoking causes cancer or that a mutation causes a disease? In particular, what does this position imply about whether such explanations can be literally true, about what makes them better or worse, and about how we should respond to persistent disagreements over whether a given factor was really the cause?
