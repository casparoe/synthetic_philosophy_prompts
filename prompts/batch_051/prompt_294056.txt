Act as a philosopher who works on the epistemology of higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and think through the question carefully before answering; the goal is a usable procedure, not a summary of the literature.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend that went in circles. I described a decision I had made at work: I read a scan as showing a subtle abnormality, while knowing I was sleep-deprived and that our department's own audit shows my error rate on subtle findings rises sharply after night shifts. My friend insisted that once I have that kind of evidence about my own reliability, I should simply stop trusting my first read. I replied that if I refused to trust my judgment every time I knew something about my own fallibility, I could never sign off on anything. We ended up unable to agree about whether a person can rationally think "the evidence in front of me supports X" while also thinking "I am probably not a reliable judge of this evidence right now."

I am a clinician in a busy practice, and I face this several times a shift, where the stakes are a missed diagnosis or an unnecessary workup. I form a first-order judgment about a case — a judgment based on the clinical signs in front of me — and I also possess higher-order evidence: evidence about my own error rates, fatigue, base rates, or known biases of clinicians in my situation, rather than about the patient. I want a reusable decision procedure I can run in about five minutes, without formal training in epistemology, before I sign off on a judgment. It should tell me, in order: what questions to ask; when higher-order evidence should make me merely lower my confidence; when it should make me defer to a colleague, a decision-support tool, or a base rate; and when I should set the higher-order evidence aside and act on my first-order judgment as the least-bad option. Mark explicitly the steps where the underlying theory is contested or where a number must be set by human judgment rather than derived from principle, and say what the procedure cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on these three cases: (1) a subtle lung nodule seen on a chest X-ray after 20 hours awake, with audit data saying my miss rate for subtle findings triples under sleep deprivation; (2) a judgment that a patient with nonspecific abdominal symptoms has ovarian cancer, where the base rate in her age group is extremely low and I know clinicians overestimate rare, salient diagnoses; and (3) a judgment about a common infection where the only higher-order evidence is the generic fact that all clinicians are sometimes influenced by biases they cannot introspect from the inside. In at least one of these cases, the procedure should return an answer I will not like. Begin by briefly laying out the distinctions you are relying on, then give the steps, then run the cases.
