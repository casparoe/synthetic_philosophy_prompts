I am drafting the final section of a paper for a course on value theory, and I have been defending the claim that effort is valuable in itself, over and above the value of its results. A friend in the seminar has pressed the following argument on me, which he thinks is a reductio of my whole position. I find the conclusion unacceptable, but I cannot locate the exact point at which the reasoning goes wrong.

(1) If effort is valuable in itself, then, for any fixed valuable outcome, a version of that outcome reached through greater effort is, other things equal, better than a version reached through less. (2) So if the same discovery is made by one researcher through decades of labor and by another through effortless insight, the laboriously achieved discovery is the greater achievement, and the world containing it is, other things equal, the better world. (3) It follows that we have a prima facie reason to prefer that discoveries be made through struggle rather than ease. (4) But if the good is held fixed and the only variable is the effort poured into obtaining it, this generalizes: for every worthwhile end, we have a prima facie reason to prefer that it be more difficult to reach. (5) We would then have a prima facie reason to welcome slower cures, more arduous recoveries, and needless obstacles placed before every good, since each increment of struggle adds value. (6) Yet it seems plainly false that a world in which basic goods must be wrested through gratuitous toil is better for containing that toil. (7) Therefore effort is not valuable in itself; at most it is instrumentally valuable, and our admiration for those who struggle must be explained without attributing value to effort as such.

My instinct is to hold on to the thought behind premise (1), that effort can contribute value, but I cannot see how to block the slide from (3) to (5). Which step should be rejected, or which premise needs to be refined, and what would that refinement cost? If the argument is valid as stated, then tell me which premise is the least costly to give up, and how much of the ordinary admiration for striving can survive once it is given up.
