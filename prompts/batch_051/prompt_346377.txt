I am a PhD student in moral psychology, and I am trying to pin down a distinction that quietly does a great deal of argumentative work in my field: the difference between a moral preference and a moral bias. When someone opposes a reform and we call their opposition status quo bias, or when a judge gives likable defendants lighter sentences and we call it leniency bias, we seem to be saying more than that we disagree with the judgment. We are saying the judgment was produced in a way that does not track the considerations the person would themselves endorse. But other partialities—a parent's devotion to their own child, or a greater concern for the present than the distant future—can be treated either as legitimate moral preferences or as biases, and it is not obvious what licenses the diagnosis in one case and not the other. My own first pass was that a bias is a judgment the person would disavow under ideal reflection, while a preference is one they would retain; but that threatens to turn every moral disagreement into a diagnosis of bias and gives no independent way to settle what the person would accept under reflection.

I would like a working definition of bias in moral judgment that handles the following cases. (1) A parent spends scarce resources on advantages for their own child rather than on equally pressing needs of a stranger's child. (2) A policymaker discounts the welfare of people two hundred years from now at a steep rate simply because their lives are far in the future. (3) A judge gives a polished, likable defendant a lighter sentence than a surly defendant convicted of the same crime. (4) A person judges harm done by failing to warn someone as less blameworthy than the same harm inflicted directly. (5) A hospital board refuses an organ allocation system that would save measurably more lives because it would overturn longstanding arrangements. (6) A donor is willing to spend the same amount to save two thousand songbirds as to save twenty thousand. (7) A voter supports harsh punishment mainly so that offenders suffer, rather than to deter further crime.

I am confident that (1) is a legitimate preference and that (3) is a bias. I am less able to defend my suspicion that (2) and (7) should also count as biases, and I have no stable view about (4), (5), and (6). Please develop the definition of bias you would commit to, explain the principle behind it, and run it through all seven cases, saying for each whether it classifies the case as a bias, a legitimate moral preference, or neither. Where the definition forces a surprising verdict, say so and either defend that verdict or revise the definition. I am asking for a committed answer, not a survey of candidate definitions.
