For a seminar in philosophy of action I regularly present my students with the following pair of cases, and their answers form a pattern I would like you to assess.

A chairman considers a new manufacturing process. He is told that the process will increase profits and that, as a side effect, it will pollute a nearby river. He says he does not care about the river and approves the plan only for the money. The plan goes through; the river is damaged. Almost every student says he damaged the river intentionally. Change one feature: the side effect is that the river is cleaned. Same chairman, same indifference, same pure profit motive. Now almost every student says he did not improve the river intentionally; it happened merely as a side effect. The asymmetry persists even when the same plan has both side effects at once: the harm is judged intentional and the benefit is not, by the same people and at the same moment.

I am not asking for a summary of the experimental literature on this so-called Knobe effect, and I do not want references or a reading list. What I want is a philosophical audit of the asymmetry itself. Is there a single principle that counts a foreseen harmful side effect as intentional while refusing the label to an equally foreseen helpful one? Which distinction is doing the work: the valence of the outcome, the violation of a norm, the agent's indifference, anticipated blame, something else? Test the principle you propose against these variants and tell me which verdict it yields in each, and whether ordinary judgment ought to follow it:

(i) The side effect injures no one but violates a trivial internal company rule.

(ii) The side effect helps a rival firm the chairman actively wants to fail, so the outcome is unwelcome to him.

(iii) The side effect harms the company's own shareholders, the very people the chairman wants to enrich.

Then give your verdict: should we keep this asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it as an illicit intrusion of moral assessment into the concept of intentional action? If you defend it, defend it against the sharpest version — a single act with a harmful and a helpful side effect, both foreseen, neither wanted, with the agent entirely indifferent to both.

Finally, I want the answer to be self-critical rather than performatively certain. Tell me what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding would lead you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
