I'm moderating the Q&A at a small early modern philosophy colloquium next week, and the speaker is giving a paper on Hume's essay "Of Suicide" — published posthumously in 1777 but probably written in the 1750s. Her line is that modern debates about euthanasia and suicide have got Hume wrong: they treat him as a proto-libertarian about personal autonomy, when his actual argument is embedded in his picture of divine governance and general laws. I want the Q&A to force her to defend that reading, not just restate it, and I'd like the questions to turn on one passage.

"In order to govern the material world, the almighty creator has established general and immutable laws, by which all bodies, from the greatest planet to the smallest particle of matter, are maintained in their proper sphere and function. To govern the animal world, he has endowed all living creatures with bodily and mental powers; with senses, passions, appetites, memory, and judgment; by which they are impelled or regulated in that course of life, to which they are destined. These two distinct principles of the material and animal world continually encroach upon each other, and mutually retard or forward each other's operation. The powers of men and of all other animals are restrained and directed by the nature and qualities of the surrounding bodies; and the modifications and actions of these bodies are incessantly altered by the operation of all animals."

Write me eight to ten interview questions that take this passage as their anchor and escalate in difficulty. I want at least one question that gets her to state, in her own words, exactly how the general-laws argument is supposed to remove the special sinfulness of suicide. Then I want questions that pressure the analogy: whether on Hume's view murder or arson would be just as licensed as suicide once they proceed from the powers God has endowed us with; whether "all events may be pronounced the action of the almighty" empties the idea of acting against divine will of any content; and whether the argument works better against deism than against the more particular providence his opponents might actually hold. For each question, include the reply you'd most expect from someone with her textual-reconstruction commitments, and a follow-up that makes that reply harder to maintain. No generic questions about Hume's life or the essay's reception.

For the answer, I'd like continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just write it like an email. And please write in British English (e.g. 'analyse' not 'analyze').
