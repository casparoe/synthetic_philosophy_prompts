I have adopted an epistemic rule modeled on the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda and the syādvāda method of sevenfold predication. I refuse to assert any proposition outright. Where the subject allows, I answer in the seven modes: in some respect P is; in some respect P is not; in some respect P both is and is not; in some respect P is inexpressible; in some respect P is and is inexpressible; in some respect P is not and is inexpressible; in some respect P is, is not, and is inexpressible. In practice I compress this into a standing hedge, and I apply it not only to philosophical claims but to mundane ones: for example, whether a train will leave on time, which I now qualify by the timetable, the weather, what the station staff know, and what on time means for different passengers. The habit has real benefits. It curbs my tendency to overclaim, and it lets me treat opponents as capturing one genuine respect in which the thing is as they describe. But it also seems to cost me the capacity to make the plain, unqualified assertions that action, testimony, and commitment require, and I notice that no particular failure could now refute what I said. I want to know whether this is a defensible epistemic policy or a way of making error impossible by never going out on a limb. Assess it as a policy. When is the rule reliable and when does it fail? Where is the line between legitimate context-sensitivity and unfalsifiable evasion? Is there a restated version that keeps the insight that every judgment is made from some partial standpoint without losing the capacity to commit? And how could I tell from the inside whether I am practicing many-sidedness or protecting myself from being wrong?
