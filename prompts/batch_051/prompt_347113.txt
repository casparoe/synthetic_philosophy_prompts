My friend and I are both in a philosophy of action class, and we've been arguing about weakness of will — akrasia — between classes for weeks now. I can't figure out the rule behind her verdicts. She says Leah is akratic: Leah had resolved to stop buying expensive handbags, but at the cash register she thinks 'I really shouldn't be doing this' and buys one anyway. She says Marcus is not akratic: his doctor told him to cut back on milkshakes, but he never actually accepted that as a goal, so when he orders one there's no inner conflict — the advice just isn't his. She says Priya is not akratic either: Priya washes her hands forty times a day and says 'I know this is pointless, I wish I could stop,' which my friend calls compulsion rather than weakness. And she says Jon is not akratic: Jon had resolved to quit smoking, but during withdrawal he lights up saying 'I don't have a choice,' and again she calls it compulsion. A guy in our class keeps insisting that these are all just preference reversals from hyperbolic discounting, which she waves off, but it's started to nag at me.

Anyway, the part I can't predict is how she'd handle two new cases. Sam has been sticking to an actual diet he cares about for months; at his birthday dinner he tells himself — and for those ten minutes really believes — that special ocasions don't count, and he eats a huge slice of cake with no felt conflict at all. Tess, by contrast, has never dieted and has no standing plan to eat healthy, but at lunch she deliberates, concludes that the fish is the better choice, then says 'I know I'll regret the burger, but I want it' and orders the burger. What would you bet my friend says about Sam and Tess? I'm less interested in the bare yes/no than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her four earlier verdicts just as well, and which of Sam or Tess — or maybe some further case — best tells those rules apart.
