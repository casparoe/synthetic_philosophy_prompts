Define "privacy" in a way that correctly classifies each of the following cases. Some of them I take to be clear positives, some clear negatives, and some I cannot settle; the definition should earn its keep by forcing the hard ones one way or the other rather than by stipulating around them.

(1) A person alone in a flat with the curtains drawn, thinking through an embarrassing decision.
(2) A stranger briefly glancing at you as you cross a station concourse.
(3) A partner reading your diary without asking and telling no one.
(4) An ex-partner hiding a camera in your bedroom but losing the memory card before ever watching it.
(5) A hospital passing de-identified patient records to a research lab, which promptly re-identifies several patients by linking the records to a public voter file.
(6) A nurse opening your chart without authorization but learning nothing she did not already know and never disclosing anything.
(7) A police drone hovering over an eight-foot fence to record what is happening in an enclosed garden.

I am confident that (1) is private and (2) is not, and I have no settled view about (4) and (6). I suspect the ordinary idea runs two functions together: privacy as a condition in which others lack access, and privacy as a power to grant or refuse access; (4) is where I expect them to come apart.

Propose a definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, say briefly whether what your definition picks out has its value intrinsically, or only derivatively through goods such as autonomy, intimacy, dignity, or the freedom to make mistakes unobserved.
