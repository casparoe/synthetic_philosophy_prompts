I run a small molecular biology lab, and we have an unofficial house rule: we do not argue about methodology in the abstract. If a protocol keeps giving us results that are reproducable across runs and across people, we keep using it even if it is, honestly, a bit jury-rigged and the stats are improvised. If a textbook-approved method keeps flaking, we drop it no matter how much hand-wringing there is about proper controls or falsifiability. Nobody here reads philosophy of science, but our postdoc once told us cheerfully that this is just Feyerabend's anything goes, and he meant it as a compliment. i have started to worry that we are treating a description of science as if it were a permission slip. So: is this a defensible policy for running a lab? Or is anything goes only supposed to be a warning against methodological tyranny and a description of how science has actually worked, not a day-to-day decision rule? When does a policy like ours stop being pragmatic and start being a shield for sloppy, self-serving, or just lazy choices? And is there a restated version that keeps the flexibility but still lets us tell the difference between a weird method that works and a bad method we have gotten attached to? Also, please give your answer in American English.
