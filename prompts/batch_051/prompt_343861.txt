I am designing a unit for a seminar on the philosophy of computation, and I want to anchor it in a real case rather than the usual toy examples—the shared bank account, the incrementing counter—that motivate why mutable state is hard to reason about. I would like you to analyze the Therac-25 radiation therapy accidents through the lens of state and identity in programming. For context, since I want the analysis to focus on concepts rather than forensic minutiae: the Therac-25 was a computer-controlled radiation therapy machine introduced in the early 1980s, produced by Atomic Energy of Canada Limited (AECL). Its control software evolved from code written for earlier machines, but several safety mechanisms that had been enforced in hardware in those earlier machines were, in the Therac-25, moved into software. Between 1985 and 1987, at least six patients received massive radiation overdoses, some fatal; the incidents were later attributed substantially to race conditions in the concurrent control code—situations in which two tasks could interleave so that the program's internal state no longer corresponded to the machine's actual physical configuration.

What I want is not the familiar lesson that safety-critical software demands care. Treat the episode instead as a case study in why mutation is hard to think about. Drawing on concepts such as shared mutable state, interleaving of concurrent processes, and the identity of a program object as a history rather than a snapshot, do the following: (a) reconstruct, in conceptual terms, where the control software made the accidents possible in a way that would not be visible to a line-by-line reading of the code; (b) explain what the episode reveals about the mismatch between an intuitive, sequential, agent-centered mental model of state and what actually occurs when multiple processes mutate the same state; and (c) state candidly where this conceptual lens stops being explanatory—which features of the real episode, such as organizational, regulatory, interface, or incident-reporting factors, the state-and-identity framework is silent about. Conclude with a judgment: is Therac-25 best understood as a failure of programmers' mental models of mutable state, a failure of engineering process, or a case in which the two cannot be cleanly separated—and what follows from that for how state and mutation ought to be taught?
