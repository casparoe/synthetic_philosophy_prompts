I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I find myself pulled in two directions on a question about what makes a model or a description good. I have been reading, in an amateur way, about Rorty's neopragmatism and antirepresentationalism, and the view attracts me more than I expected. As I understand it, the antirepresentationalist holds that our vocabularies, concepts, and models are tools for coping with the world, not mirrors that reflect the world as it is in itself. There is no neutral standpoint outside all vocabularies from which to compare a vocabulary with unconceptualized reality and judge that one is closer to it. A statement or model earns the title true or accurate in the only way that matters: by proving useful for prediction, intervention, coordination, and the avoidance of surprise. One vocabulary displaces another in physics or engineering not because it converges on nature's own categories, but because it solves the problems a community currently has, and the standards for what counts as a solution are set within some vocabulary or another. The appeal, for me, is that this picture is economical. It dissolves the skeptical problem of how a representation could ever be compared with the thing it represents, it abandons a God's-eye vantage point that no human in fact occupies, and it matches how I actually see technical frameworks evolve: a database schema or an API abstraction replaces its predecessor for reasons that look pragmatic rather than because one of them finally mirrors the structure of the domain.

But I cannot shake an intuition from my own work, and it cuts the other way. A topographic map is not merely more useful for hiking than a transit diagram because it has worked better so far; it is useful because it preserves real spatial relations in the terrain. A finite-element model of stress in a bridge predicts cracks because it shares structure with the physical bridge, and that shared structure explains the predictive success. A cycle-accurate CPU emulator passes every test because its state transitions correspond to the state transitions of the emulated circuit. When I debug a model, I do not experience the correction as a mere change of vocabulary; I experience it as replacing a misrepresentation with a more faithful one. The antirepresentationalist tells me that all I am entitled to say is that the corrected model works better going forward, and that the demand for something more—for genuine correspondence—is exactly the habit the view is asking me to abandon. But that reply seems to omit the very thing that explains why the model works better.

I am not asking for a winner. I want to know what it would take for each side to give. Walk me through, as strongly as possible, the case for treating my engineering intuition as an artifact of successful practice that I should debunk and drop, and the case for treating that intuition as evidence that antirepresentationalism owes me a minimal account of structure preservation or correspondence. Is there a stable middle position—a deflated notion of representational accuracy that keeps the explanatory role of shared structure while conceding that there is no vocabulary-independent way to verify it—or is that middle just antirepresentationalism with the word correspondence attached as decoration? I want an account of which components of the theory and which components of the intuition can survive together, and which must go.
