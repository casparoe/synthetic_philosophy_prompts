A friend has been defending the following argument, and I find each step hard to deny, but the conclusion would leave the inside/outside distinction with no useful role in decisions under uncertainty, which I don't want to accept.

(1) For an uncertain forecast—when a project will finish, whether a startup survives, how long a paper will take—the correct procedure is to set aside the particularities of the present case and take the outside view: select a reference class of similar past cases and anchor on its base rate.

(2) But a case never supplies its own reference class. It falls under many at once (all comparable projects; projects of this type in this field; projects by people with this kind of track record; projects that looked equally promising at the start), and their base rates diverge sharply.

(3) Choosing among these classes demands the very case-specific judgment the outside view was supposed to displace: judgment about which features are predictive, how much similarity is enough, and which past cases are too unlike the present one to include.

(4) Such judgment is inside-view reasoning, applied to the selection of the reference class.

(5) So the outside view cannot be applied without depending on the inside view at the decisive step; in any conflict between the two views, the outside view's authority is borrowed from the inside judgment that picked the class.

(6) Hence either the outside view is inapplicable precisely where it was meant to help, or it collapses into the inside view one level up, and the distinction gives no independent guidance.

Which premise or step should I give up? Explain why that step looked innocent, and say what a defender of the inside/outside distinction is committed to once they reject it—in particular, what the outside view could be for if it is not a way of avoiding case-specific judgment altogether.
