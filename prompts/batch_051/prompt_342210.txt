I am a novelist drafting a scene in which a scholar attacks a famous skeptical argument from the pulpit, and I want an unfamiliar objection to David Hume’s essay on miracles. The passage I keep returning to is from An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section X, Part I:

“Though experience be our only guide in reasoning concerning matters of fact; it must be acknowledged, that this guide is not altogether infallible, but in some cases is apt to lead us into errors.”

The objections to Hume’s essay that I already know and do not need repeated are: the Bayesian complaint that he ignores how independent testimonies can overwhelm a low prior; the objection that his principle would also make rational belief in unexpected scientific discoveries impossible; the charge that he begs the question by defining miracles as violations of a law built from experience that is assumed to exclude violations; the worry that his claims about human testimony in Part II are empirical and culturally parochial; and the objection that his own threshold—that the falsehood of the testimony would have to be more miraculous than the event—cannot be computed and cuts both ways. I want an objection that is not in that list and not a variant of anything in it—not a rewording, special case, or combination. It can be an internal tension with the quoted passage, a structural flaw, or a previously unnoticed consequence. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good.
