I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I'd like someone to stress-test a parallel I keep noticing between Douglas Hofstadter's superrationality thought experiments and ordinary problems in distributed systems. I'm prepared to be told it's just pattern-matching, but I want to know exactly where the structure holds and where it gives out.

Here is the parallel as I've worked it out.

Take the Platonia dilemma. N people are separated and cannot communicate; each must independently decide whether to send a telegram by a deadline. If exactly one person sends, that person wins a billion dollars; if none or more than one send, no one wins anything. Hofstadter's superrational move is for each person to randomize: send with probability 1/N. The rationale is that the players are identical reasoners, so an argument that is correct for one is correct for all, and one then optimizes the single shared parameter. Under that rule the probability of a unique sender is (1 - 1/N)^(N-1), which converges to 1/e.

That is exactly slotted ALOHA. N stations share a channel, time is slotted, and in a given slot each station transmits with probability p. A slot succeeds if and only if exactly one station transmits. A given station's probability of getting its own packet through is p(1 - p)^(N-1), maximized at p = 1/N, and the useful-slot probability is (1 - 1/N)^(N-1), converging to 1/e. The way we actually build the protocol, we hard-code the same p into every station. So the engineer seems to be doing Hofstadter's move on behalf of the nodes: impose symmetry across all agents first, then optimize the one common parameter, instead of letting each station choose an independent p against its peers. And the failure mode on the philosophy side, where each person reasons their way into sending with certainty and everyone loses, matches the network failure mode where greedy endpoints move p toward 1 and the channel collapses into collisions.

The second experiment points the same way. In the Luring Lottery that Scientific American actually ran in the 1980s, a reader could enter any number of times by writing a number on a postcard. The prize was one million dollars divided by the total number of entries, awarded to the submitter of a single randomly chosen entry. If the other entrants have submitted S entries combined, your expected winnings are maximized by submitting S entries yourself; if everyone reasons that way, the totals chase each other upward without bound and the prize becomes worthless. The superrational fix was for each participant to submit a single entry with probability 1/E, where E is the expected number of participants, the same Poisson-style damping as in the Platonia case: one shared randomization parameter replaces each agent's unconstrained optimization against the aggregate. In my world this is the same shape as a thundering herd or congestion collapse, where every actor optimizes against the behavior of all the others, drives the shared resource to a state where nobody gets anything useful, and the only robust fix is to impose the same randomized rule on everyone rather than let each endpoint set its own rate.

I can argue both sides. On one hand, the mathematics is identical, and the defining superrationalist gesture, symmetry before optimization, seems to be precisely what a protocol designer does when we set one contention parameter identically across nodes. If that is right, distributed systems has been quietly implementing superrationality for decades under names like contention resolution and backoff, and the two fields might have something real to exchange. On the other hand, stations do not deliberate, do not hold preferences, do not treat their own choice as evidence about what identical others will choose, and cannot be tempted. ALOHA's p = 1/N is chosen by a designer maximizing aggregate throughput, not by a node maximizing its own expected utility under a theory that the others reason identically. That gap could be the entire philosophical point, and I cannot tell whether it is a gap in the analogy or the analogy.

What I want is a verdict that does not just split the difference. State the mapping precisely; identify where the structures coincide and where they come apart; say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer; and name at least one concrete prediction the parallel makes that fails.
