I'm a PhD student in information law, and I'm writing a chapter on whether personal data should be treated as property. The philosophical side of the debate — whether information about you can be owned at all — is well covered, and the policy side is stuck on consent banners and fines. The level I most need help with is institutional: what would actually happen if we built a working property regime for personal data, as opposed to defending one in principle. So I'd like you to analyze a concrete proposal as a mechanism, not as a moral position.

The proposal, which I'll call a data-royalty registry, is this. Every natural person receives a negotiable but partially inalienable property interest in data "about" them, held in an individual account at a public registry. Commercial use of a data type drawn from a person's account is lawful without consent, but only if the using firm notifies the registry and pays a monthly use-fee into the account, set by a formula tied to the volume of uses and to the measured contribution of the data to the firm's revenue from products that depend on it. Individuals cannot veto uses or demand deletion, so firms do not face holdout. Individuals may sell up to half of their future fee streams to third parties, but the other half stays with them and cannot be alienated. Walk me through how this behaves in practice: the incentives it creates for firms, for individuals, and for intermediaries such as data brokers; how each group would try to game the definitions of "about" a person and of a distinct "use"; where the first scandals would break out; and how the redistributive effects are likely to land. Tell me whether the failure modes could be fixed by tightening definitions and auditing without gutting the scheme, or whether the whole design pushes toward a different equilibrium than its proponents intend.

Please answer as an institutional economist who works on property, commons, and information markets. Start by brainstorming the stakeholders and the levers available to each before you settle on a verdict, and think hard about second-order effects — for example, incentives to generate low-value data about many people, to restructure products so the fee base shrinks, or to pass fees to consumers through pricing. End by telling me which empirical assumption in your analysis is doing the most work, and what finding would most change your overall assessment of the proposal.
