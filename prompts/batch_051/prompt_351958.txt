Consider the classic argument that self-deception is impossible: to deceive yourself you would have to get yourself to accept something you know to be false, which seems to require being simultaneously the deceiver who possesses the truth and the deceived who lacks it. Deflationary accounts avoid the paradox by treating self-deception as motivated but non-intentional distortion of belief; intentionalist accounts keep the intentionality and then have to explain why the paradox is not fatal.

Now introduce a specific development. People can now outsource much of their information environment to algorithms: a person can configure her news feed, search results, and LLM-based assistant to systematically omit, down-rank, or reframe evidence about a topic she finds threatening. Suppose someone sets up such a configuration in advance, knowing the evidence points a certain way, precisely so that her future self—who will not remember the details of the configuration and will take the curated environment more or less at face value—ends up believing what the earlier self wanted her to believe. The earlier self knows the truth and intentionally arranges for the later self's false belief; the later self acquires the belief without intending to do so at the time.

Does the classic impossibility argument survive this development? Say explicitly which premise of the argument the development puts pressure on, and whether the case is best described as self-deception, as being deceived by someone else, or as a third category. Then give the strongest version of the impossibility thesis that remains true, or, if you think none survives, state what distinction should replace the old one between self-deception and being deceived by others.
