I've been having this running argument with a friend and I realized I can't actually say what would change my mind, which I guess is a bad sign. The position I've settled into is that wild things and places—forests, rivers, species, whatever—have value in themselves, not just because humans care about them or get something out of them. My friend keeps saying that's not a claim about the world, its a way of refusing to explain why any particular place matters. His example is a degraded urban creek that a neighborhood depends on: he says the relational-value people can explain why that creek matters more to protect than some pristine wilderness nobody visits, but my view either has to deny that or tack on "and it also has intrinsic value" to whatever they say, which makes it look like it explains everything and predicts nothing.

So, trying to be fair to him: what would count against the intrinsic-value view as a substantive position rather than a label? I want the considerations sorted by how decisive they are—what would be a genuine defeater if it held up, what would be merely awkward, and what critics might think is evidence but defenders could reasonably wave off. Are there real disputes in conservation—restoration, rewilding, culling invasives, ranking which habitats to save—where intrinsic value makes a distinctive prediction, and where it might give the wrong answer? What would the world look like if wild things turned out to only have relational or instrumental value? And then the meta-question: is my view just insulated from refutation, and if so, is that a problem or is it just what it is to hold a bedrock normative commitment? Also, if the whole request is confused—like if I'm asking for empirical defeaters for a view that isn't empirical—just tell me that plainly instead of humoring me.
