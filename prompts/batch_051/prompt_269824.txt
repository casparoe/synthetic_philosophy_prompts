I'm in an epistemology seminar and we keep circling something the instructor calls the easy knowledge problem, but we never walked through the actual argument step by step, so I've been trying to reconstruct it and I cant tell whether I've nailed it or built a strawman. The case I've been using is this. I'm looking at a wall, and it visually appears red to me. I take that appearance at face value and form the belief that the wall is red; surely I know the wall is red. Then I notice something further: the wall is red and it appeared red to me. From that conjunction I infer that my color vision got this particular case right. I do the same on Tuesday with a blue wall, Wednesday with a green chair, and so on, each time relying only on how things look to me. After enough cases I generalize: my color vision is reliable. So here is the argument numbered out:

(1) The wall visually appears red to me.
(2) On the basis of that appearance, I know the wall is red.
(3) Therefore the wall is red and it appeared red to me.
(4) Therefore my color vision got this case right.
(5) By repeating this across many colors and objects, I can infer that my color vision is generally reliable.
(6) Therefore I can come to know that my color vision is reliable, using only my color vision itself and no independent check.

My question is whether this argument goes through. If every step is fine, then knowledge of my own perceptual reliability comes for free, which is presumably the too-easy part. But if there is a failure, I can't locate it. Step 3 is just conjunction introduction, and step 4 just records that my visual judgment and the way the world is match. I half-suspect the problem is buried in step 2, as if it secretly builds in the reliability that step 6 then extracts, but I can't make that objection precise. I also keep hearing that bootstrapping arguments are rule circular rather than premise circular, and that rule circularity is sometimes benign — we use modus ponens to reason about modus ponens all the time without scandal. Does my reconstruction conflate those two kinds of circularity, or is that a red herring? Tell me exactly which step fails and why, or, if the argument is valid and each premise is true, say what accepting step 2 already commits someone to about easy knowledge, and whether they are entitled to be unbothered by step 6.
