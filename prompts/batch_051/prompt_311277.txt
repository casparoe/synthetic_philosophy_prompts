I’m teaching an advanced undergraduate seminar on formal models of rationality and risk. We spent a session on Ole Peters and Murray Gell-Mann’s paper Evaluating gambles using dynamics, which argues that expected-value reasoning is often a bad null model for individual choice because an individual lives along a single time trajectory, not across an ensemble of parallel realizations. I tried to extract the central argument as a numbered sequence, gluing together what I took to be the load-bearing claims, and I want a step-level verdict on it.

Here is the argument. (1) A decision-maker realizes only one path of wealth through time; they cannot average across an ensemble of alternative histories. (2) Therefore, for an individual, the quantity that matters is what happens to wealth over time—the time average—rather than the ensemble average. (3) For a multiplicative wealth process, the long-run time-average growth rate is the expected logarithm of the per-period growth factor, not the arithmetic mean of the per-period growth factors. (4) The arithmetic mean systematically overstates time-average growth whenever outcomes are variable, and the gap grows with variance. (5) Therefore an individual facing a repeated multiplicative gamble should evaluate it by expected log growth, not expected wealth. (6) So, for such an individual, expected-utility theory in its standard form either rests on the wrong averages or adds an unnecessary corrective layer.

My question is whether this argument is valid. I am particularly suspicious that somewhere between (2) and (5) a descriptive fact about stochastic dynamics turns into a prescription about rational choice, but I can’t isolate exactly where the modal slip would occur. So: is the inference sound, or does a specific step fail? If it fails, name the step, state the reading under which it fails, and say whether a weaker version would go through.
