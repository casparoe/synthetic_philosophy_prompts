Here's something I keep going back and forth on. A colleague in archaeology insists that whenever we say a pattern of objects or a burial practice shows a belief — say, that a body was laid out with red ochre because the community believed in an afterlife — we are doing interpretation, not inference. His line is that the exact same physical record could be produced by people who just thought the practice was decent, customary, or hygienic, with no afterlife beliefs at all. I think he's too pessimistic, but I cant quite say why.

So suppose we excavate a prehistoric cemetery: bodies consistently placed on their left side, heads to the east, red ochre on the skull, and a small pot at the feet in most but not all graves (children lack the pot). The excavators announce this is evidence of a belief in an afterlife. Treat that as the claim to test. Design the best test you can — comparative, statistical, ethnographic, whatever — that would actually seperate the afterlife-belief reading from at least one deflationary alternative (custom, hygiene, status display, grief without metaphysics). Say what result would count in favor of each reading. Then say what even the best test cannot settle: in particular, whether a positive result licenses the word belief rather than something thinner like 'they would have said yes if asked', and whether cross-cultural analogy can ever do more than make a story plausible.

Keep it concrete; don't wave at 'more evidence'. I want the actual design and the actual limits.
