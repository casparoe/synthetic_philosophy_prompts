I've been reading about contextual integrity and I want to test it on a case where I think it should either shine or get exposed: the Snowden-era NSA bulk metadata collection. Here's the version of the theory I have in mind (Nissenbaum's, roughly): a privacy violation isn't fundamentally about some information being inherently sensitive, or about whether you "agreed" to give it to someone; it's about whether an information flow violates the norms appropriate to the context in which it moves. The phone company gets my call records in a billing/fraud context, and that's mostly fine; the same records flowing to an intelligence agency in bulk is a different transfer between different parties with different norms, and that's where the question lives. The episode for the mapping: in June 2013 the Guardian and the Washington Post reported, from Snowden's documents, that the NSA had been collecting telephone metadata at massive scale — numbers, times, durations, not call contents — under court orders, including a now-famous standing order to Verizon Business for all its call records. The government's defense leaned partly on the content/metadata distinction and on the third-party doctrine: roughly, that you have no reasonable expectation of privacy in records you voluntarily shared with a third party. I already know the timeline; don't rehearse it unless it's doing analytic work. What I want is the mapping contextual integrity demands. Walk me through which contexts the call records live in (the provider's business records, the subscriber's life, the government's security/intelligence context, and the person on the other end of the call), which transfers happened between contexts, and what the relevant context-specific informational norms say about each transfer. Then bring in the relational wrinkle: my metadata encodes information about you too, so the "subscriber" is not the only party whose context-norms are in play. Now compare: where does contextual integrity handle the bulk metadata case better than control-based and access-based accounts of privacy, and where does it do as badly or worse? And be blunt about where the theory itself gets strained — for instance, wether it can actually read "existing norms" off a context when the context is new or contested, and whether it makes privacy too hostage to whatever people happen to expect. End with one sentence, as the very last line, beginning "Bottom line:" that states the single most important thing the episode shows about contextual integrity as a theory of privacy.
