I’ve been reading the Stoic distinction between what is in our power and what is not, and I keep finding modern writers treat it as a kind of psychological boundary: my choices and judgments are mine; my health, reputation, and success are not. That reading seemed clear enough until I came to Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 6, in George Long’s translation:

"Don't be prideful with any excellence that is not your own. If a horse should be prideful and say, 'I am handsome,' it would be supportable. But when you are prideful, and say, 'I have a handsome horse,' know that you are proud of what is, in fact, only the good of the horse. What, then, is your own? Only your reaction to the appearances of things. Thus, when you behave conformably to nature in reaction to how things appear, you will be proud with reason; for you will take pride in some good of your own."

I need help understanding the last two sentences. Epictetus does not say that only your choice or only your act of judgment is your own; he says that only your reaction to the appearances of things is your own. Is that just another way of saying that your own good is the correct use of impressions, or does it point to something broader — for example, to the quality of your inner response, including the way you handle the first movements of emotion before full assent? And why is pride appropriate for this, when earlier in the *Enchiridion* he identifies one’s own good with being free, unhindered, and conformable to nature, not with having produced a particular kind of reaction? I want to know whether this passage fits comfortably with the modern self-help reading of the dichotomy of control, or whether it quietly complicates that reading.
