In his Theodicy (1710), Leibniz describes God as having surveyed the infinite range of possible arrangements of things and as having willed to instantiate the one arrangement that yields the greatest overall perfection, much as a rational designer selects the best plan from a space of alternatives. In casual discussion this often gets flattened into a single image: God choosing among possible worlds is like an architect choosing the best blueprint, with the actual world as the building that gets built. But Leibniz's own machinery — the distinction between God's intellect, will, and power, and the doctrine that not every possible is compossible with every other — suggests the blueprint analogy may distort more than it illuminates.

Audit this architect/blueprint analogy for Leibniz's best-of-all-possible-worlds theodicy. Give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the architect, the blueprint, the raw materials, the constraints, the executed structure, and the moment of choice. Then isolate the places where the analogy breaks. Does it quietly import a temporal sequence of first designing and then executing? Does it suggest that the constraints are given independently of the designer? Does it make the standards of success look like a finite, statable list when Leibniz may not think of the good that way? Finally, judge whether the conclusion the analogy is usually meant to support — that God's goodness is rendered intelligible by his selecting the optimal design — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the architect/blueprint analogy is not doing real work, say what a better analogy for Leibniz's relation among possibles would be.
