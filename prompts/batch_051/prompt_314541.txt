I help run a reading group on the intentional stance as a framework for talking about AI systems, and two members of the group have spent months arguing past each other about whether a large language model literally has beliefs. One of them, call the first the stance instrumentalist, holds that adopting the intentional stance toward an LLM — describing it as believing, intending, planning, or trying to be helpful — is justified only as a predictive device: it buys us compression and reliable expectations about the text the model will produce, but the ascriptions are not literally true of anything inside the system. The second, call him the stance realist, holds that when stance-based ascriptions hold up robustly and projectibly across a wide range of novel tasks and contexts, that robustness is what it is for a belief to be real, or at least makes the ascriptions literally true in the only sense that matters; on his view, the contrast between a merely useful manner of speaking and a real belief is not doing the work his colleague thinks it is. They began from the same evidence — the same observed behavior of the same model, including fluent self-correction and plausible-sounding self-reports about what it was "trying" to do — and have drawn opposite conclusions from it.

They have now agreed to run a proper adversarial collaboration. Write the document that this collaboration should produce. It must contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign, stated bluntly enough to be informative and covering at least their shared evidence about LLM behavior, the role of predictive success, the risk of anthropomorphism, and any conditions under which one side would concede that the other had gained ground; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated once in the instrumentalist's preferred wording and once in the realist's preferred wording, so that each side can sign its own formulation and can recognize the other formulation as a fair, non-caricatured statement of the opposing view; (3) a list of the cruxes — the sub-questions such that a settled answer to them would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, at least one concrete observation, experiment, or training intervention that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about the result written down in advance. Throughout, separate substantive disagreements from merely verbal ones — for example, a disagreement that dissolves once one distinguishes a thin reading of "believes" as a coarse behavioral-role ascription from a thicker reading that requires inner states with the right causal and inferential structure. The document should be precise enough that neither party can sign it while quietly changing the subject.
