I've been teaching the argument from marginal cases in my applied ethics seminar, and a student pushed on something I can't quite shake. The argument, as I usually present it, runs roughly like this: whatever capacities give a "marginal" human — an infant, someone with advanced dementia, a severely cognitively disabled adult — moral standing, some nonhuman animals appear to have those capacities at least as much. So if the marginal human counts, consistency seems to demand that the animal counts too, and withholding that status just because the animal isn't human looks like speciesism.

Here's what's been bugging me. The argument quietly assumes there's a property you either have or lack — sentience, say — and that the only question is whether you drew the line in the right place. But the more I read about invertebrate cognition, and the more I think about artificial systems, the less plausible that binary picture seems. Suppose we accept instead that sentience is a matter of degree: a smooth continuum from zero (a rock, a thermostat) through vague cases (a bee? a lobster?) up to full human experience, with no natural threshold anywhere along it. Maybe add a science-fictional wrinkle: suppose we eventually had a reliable continuous measure of valenced experience, and it confirmed there is no clean line.

Does the argument from marginal cases survive that? Which premise in the argument does the graded picture actually put pressure on? Is there a repaired version that still does something important, or does the whole thing just deflate into a weaker point once the threshold assumption is gone? I don't so much want a verdict on lobsters as a verdict on the argument.
