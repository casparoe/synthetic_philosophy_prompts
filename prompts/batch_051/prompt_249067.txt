I am drafting a paper that sits between a seminar on the foundations of machine learning and a seminar on Hume's problem of induction, and the argument below fell out of it. Each step seemed forced on me individually, but the conclusion is one I cannot accept, and I can no longer see where the joint gave way. I need a diagnosis, not reassurance.

Here is the argument as it currently stands.

(1) The no-free-lunch results in learning theory show that, averaging over all possible input-output mappings with equal weight, every learning algorithm has exactly the same expected out-of-sample error. One algorithm can outperform another on some class of problems only by underperforming it on another class of exactly the same size: every advantage is offset elsewhere.

(2) Thus there is no problem-independent sense in which one learning rule is better than another. Each rule has worlds in which it succeeds and worlds in which it fails, and no rule dominates any other.

(3) Ordinary induction is a learning rule in exactly this formal sense: from a finite sample it selects a generalization — future instances will resemble past ones, simpler hypotheses are to be preferred, observed frequencies are to be projected. Anti-induction — expect the trends to reverse after some finite horizon, or project the least simple hypothesis consistent with the data — is another such rule.

(4) So induction has no advantage over anti-induction except the parochial ones that arise within particular problems already taken for granted. There is no rational basis, independent of already taking our world for granted, for choosing induction over its rival.

(5) Any basis for that choice must be either empirical or a priori. The empirical route is circular: induction's track record is a reason to trust induction only if induction is already the right way to read a track record. The a priori route would have to show induction superior to anti-induction without borrowing anything from the character of our particular world — and that is exactly what (2) forecloses.

(6) Therefore induction cannot be justified. We use it, but the use is arbitrary from a rational point of view.

(7) If our use of induction is arbitrary, then every empirical belief — that experiments replicate, that the sun will rise tomorrow, that the premises of this very argument hold in cases we have not yet checked — is arbitrary in the same sense.

(8) That is too much to accept. So at least one step of (1)-(7) must be rejected.

Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked compelling, what exactly rejecting it commits me to, and which later claims I can still keep. For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (1), because I am convinced the learning-theory results survive careful statement, and I am certain I cannot accept (8). Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis you give and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
