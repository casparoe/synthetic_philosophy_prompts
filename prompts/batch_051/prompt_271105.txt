After retiring from a career in physical chemistry, I began tutoring first-year university students, and I have been struck by a pattern I do not yet have adequate vocabulary for. Their wrong answers are seldom scattered. A student who insists that electric current is used up by a light bulb will, the following week, predict that in a series circuit the bulbs grow dimmer the farther they sit from the battery; when shown an ammeter reading the same current on both sides of the bulb, she does not give up the idea but adds an auxiliary assumption — that the meter itself draws current, that the wires must be unusual, that the battery somehow replenishes what was spent. The same student, in a mechanics unit, will explain why a thrown ball keeps moving by saying the throw deposited a quantity of force in the ball which gradually runs out, and she will treat frictionless motion as a limiting case that would eventually decay to rest anyway. These errors look to me less like gaps in knowledge than like small, generative systems: each one lets its holder predict novel cases, organizes several otherwise unrelated phenomena, and survives counterevidence by local amendment rather than being abandoned.

I have started reading the philosophy of science alongside my tutoring, and the resemblance to what I was trained to call a research program is hard to shake, though I do not want to overwork the analogy. My question is whether this way of seeing students' errors — as theory-like structures rather than as the mere absence of correct knowledge — is an established position in the study of learning, what it is called, and how it has been defended and criticized. In particular, I would like to know where the line falls between a systematic, generative error of this sort and an ordinary false belief or a one-off slip, and whether the self-protecting, hypothesis-saving behavior I keep witnessing is part of the established concept or a feature I am illicitly importing from the philosophy of science.

If no single established concept fits, I would welcome a proposal: a definition, a name, and one or two boundary cases that would help me see what would count as a non-theory-like instance of the same broad phenomenon.
