I've been turning over a version of Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles that seems to get wrecked by indistinguishable quantum particles, and I want someone to tell me exactly where the argument goes wrong or whether it's actually fine. Here is the argument I keep making when I talk to people in philosophy of physics:

(1) If two objects share every property, then they are not two objects at all; they are one. That's the principle.
(2) Quantum mechanics treats particles of the same kind as indistinguishable: swapping any two of them in the mathematical state changes nothing measurable.
(3) Take a many-body state, like two bosons in the same single-particle mode — they have exactly the same spin, energy, spatial wavefunction, and every other one-particle property.
(4) Since swapping them leaves the state identical, there is no fact about which boson is which. So they differ in no property at all, not even 'being to the left of,' 'being the first in the list,' or any other relation.
(5) But the system still has two particles. If it weren't two particles, the probability distribution for detecting two would be different.
(6) So there are two objects with exactly the same properties, and the identity of indiscernibles is false.

I have a feeling the weak spot is either (3) or (4) — maybe "there is no fact about which is which" doesn't automatically mean "there is no relational difference" — but my friend says (5) is the real problem because you can't infer individual objects from the state without already deciding whether particles are individuals. My question: is this argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and what would a repair look like? Could the principle be saved by saying only qualitative properties count, or by giving up the idea that particles are objects at all? I'm fine with the conclusion; I just want the logic to be airtight or at least know why it isn't.
