I'm a PhD student in philosophical theology, and a running argument with my flatmate about the regulative principle of worship has turned into an argument about arguments. For background: the regulative principle says that in the public worship of God, whatever is not commanded is forbidden; the normative principle says whatever is not forbidden is allowed. We tried to reconstruct a defense of the regulative principle from a seminar I sat in on, and I keep loosing the thread in the middle. Here's the reconstruction:

(1) God commands the church to worship him (we're talking about the gathered, public worship of the assembly, not private devotion). (2) When a finite person obeys a command, obedience consists in doing exactly what the command requires — nothing more and nothing less; anything more is a self-chosen addition, and a self-chosen addition is no longer an act of obedience. (3) So if public worship includes an element God has not commanded, that element is an addition to what the command requires. (4) Adding a self-chosen element to a divine command is forbidden: it goes beyond God's revealed will, and that's presumption. (5) Therefore every element of public worship that God has not commanded is forbidden.

I half-suspect the trouble is in (2), because 'what the command requires' can mean the core act (sing) or can mean every detail of its performance (sing, but not necessarily with accompaniment, at a particular hour, in particular clothes), and the argument seems to need the second reading to reach (3) but can only defend the first. My flatmate thinks (4) is the real weakness, since some uncommanded things might just be indifferent additions rather than rebellion. Can you go through it step by step? Is the argument valid as it stands, and if not, exactly which step fails? If (2) is what fails, give me the strongest reading of (2) on which it's true, and check whether the argument still goes through when read that way. And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff — don't present a contested view as the consensus.
