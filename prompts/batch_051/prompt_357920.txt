I'm a philosophy professor who teaches a bit of social epistemology, and I read a magazine piece recently on what financial markets know that gave a quick gloss of the no-trade theorem. I can't tell whether the gloss is fair or whether it quietly slides from a precise result to a much stronger claim. Here's the gist of what the article said (my paraphrase, not a quote):

Suppose Alice wants to sell a stock and Bob wants to buy it. If Alice honestly thought the stock was worth more than her asking price, she wouldn't be selling—so her very willingness to sell is itself bad news about the stock, and Bob should mark his estimate down. But the same reasoning cuts the other way: Bob's willingness to buy at that price is good news, and Alice should wonder what he knows. Since each knows the other is rational, and each knows the other knows, they'll treat willingness to trade as information, updating until the private information is priced in—at which point no one is willing to trade anymore. According to the article, this is what the no-trade theorem says: fully rational agents with different information cannot transact on the basis of that information, because any offer to trade at a proposed price reveals what the other side knows. The only way you get trade, the article says, is if someone is failing to draw the obvious inference, or if the trade isn't really an information bet but a matter of liquidity or hedging instead. Yet real markets churn through trades constantly, so the piece ends by claiming that an enormous amount of actual trading can only be explained by irrationality—or at least by traders systematically failing to update as the theorem says they should.

I'd like an accuracy audit. Tell me which parts of this are faithful, where it slides from the theorem to something stronger, and whether the final claim about real markets survives once the details are corrected. I have a nagging sence that the theorem leans heavily on a common-prior assumption—I'm also thinking of Aumann's can't-agree-to-disagree result—and that it's specifically about speculative trade rather than trade, period, but I don't know the economics well enough to be sure how much those caveats matter. What would a careful defender of the theorem claim, and what would they insist the article overstates?
