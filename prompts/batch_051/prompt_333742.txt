In the Poetics, Aristotle draws a distinction that has long seemed to me more useful than most modern debates about biographical storytelling. He argues, particularly in chapter 9, that the historian states what actually happened, while the poet states what could happen in accordance with probability or necessity, and that poetry is therefore more philosophical than history. He adds that a poet may attach well-known proper names to characters, because the familiar names make the possible pattern credible without turning the work into history. I want to test this argument against a specific new development. Streaming platforms are now commissioning dramatic biopics in which a dead public figure is reconstructed through deepfaked voice and likeness, and invented private conversations are made visually indistinguishable from archival footage. If the particular record and the probable pattern can no longer be separated by the viewer, does Aristotle's argument still hold? Which premise does this development put pressure on, and is there a repaired version of the argument that still does meaningful work? Please separate what is settled in the scholarship from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
