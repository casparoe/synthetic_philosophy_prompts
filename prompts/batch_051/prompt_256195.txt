I am a novelist, and I am drafting the scene that the rest of my book depends on. Two characters are walking home after the funeral of a man they both loved: one is a painter in her late sixties, the other a poet of frankly romantic temperament, roughly half her age. The painter has spent decades training herself in a Stoic discipline. She does not claim to feel nothing. She distinguishes the first involuntary movements of a passion—the jolt, the tears, the heat—from the judgment that follows. It is only the judgment she refuses. On her account, ordinary grief quietly asserts that something outside her own character is good or bad, and that assertion is almost always false. What remains worth feeling is confined to what she can control: the joy of having acted well, the caution of not yet having acted well, the wish for another person's virtue. The poet finds this unbearable. On his view, some emotions are not judgments at all. Grief is not the thought that a loss is bad; it is the direct perception of its badness, the way eyesight registers the world rather than inferring it. To withhold assent from grief is therefore not to correct a false belief; it is to shut one's eyes. Certain values—this particular dead man, this particular body of work, this particular love—are available only to the unsettled mind, and the very disturbance the painter calls noise is the price of seeing them. Their disagreement, in other words, is not just a difference of temperament; it is a live philosophical disagreement about whether an emotion is itself a kind of evaluative judgment or a direct awareness of value.

I ask you to write the dialogue between them. Each speaker should be as intelligent and as committed as the other; neither is a strawman. They should respond to each other's actual arguments and not deliver alternating position statements. Let the argument move through several turns. The painter should press the poet on whether he has any standard for distinguishing grief that reveals something from grief that merely flatters his own capacity for feeling; the poet should press the painter on whether her discipline could have loved this particular man at all, or only some replaceable instance of a general good. Let them arrive at the point where the disagreement reduces to a question about what an emotion is—a judgment, a perception of value, or something else—and let them work there for a while. Neither should win. They may make partial concessions, but the scene should end at a real impasse or with a costly change in one of them, not in a middle position that grants both sides are right. Above all, the dialogue should be speakable: interruptions, regrets, figures drawn from their lives, forward motion from one rejoinder to the next, but no line that is only texture—every move should do philosophical work.
