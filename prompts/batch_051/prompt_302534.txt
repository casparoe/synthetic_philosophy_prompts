I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep tripping over two concepts that sound like they might be the same idea wearing different hats: iterated amplification (HCH) and factored cognition. My rough picture is this. HCH is the recursive-delegation ideal: a human faces a hard question, breaks it into subquestions, hands those to copies or assistants who can break their questions down further, and the whole tree is supposed to stay human-legible and answer-assembling rather than hiding the reasoning in one inaccessible place. Factored cognition is the transparency-by-decomposition ideal: instead of one big model solving a task in a single inscrutable forward pass, you demand that the cognitive work be split into small, seperately checkable operations, each simple enough to evaluate by itself, with the final answer composed out in the open.

Compare these two as idealizations of deliberation. Specifically: is factored cognition the general phenomenon and HCH one particular way of trying to get it, or are they genuinely different idealizations with different failure modes and different places where they stop being honest? And what does each one quietly wave away about actual deliberation—like the fact that some thoughts only form when a big tangle of context stays in one process, or that a decomposition can be lossy because the subquestion itself was the wrong cut? Where would the two actually pull apart in practice?

If I've set the comparison up wrong—say HCH is just a proposal for implementing factored cognition, and contrasting them as rival idealizations is a category mistake—tell me that straight instead of politely playing along with a muddled premise. I'd rather my framing get corrected than get a forced compare-and-contrast.
