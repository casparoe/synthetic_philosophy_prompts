I have adopted a private epistemic rule that I use whenever I assign a degree of confidence to some concrete question—whether a new drug will pass its trial, whether a colleague will meet a deadline, whether a historical claim I read online is true. Before I let the number settle, I run a kind of audit. I imagine a bookie who is allowed to offer me bets at exactly the odds my intended degree of belief implies, with small stakes, negligible transaction costs, and none of the usual psychological distortions about losing actual money. I ask whether that bookie could assemble a package of bets, each one of which I would willingly accept in isolation, such that I would come out a net loser regardless of which way the world turns. If such a package exists, I treat that as a decisive sign that my credences are inconsistent and I revise them until the package disappears.

The habit traces back to Frank Ramsey's essay *Truth and Probability*. As I understand the relevant passage, Ramsey argues that if a person's partial beliefs fail to obey the axioms of probability, then it is possible to exploit that person through a set of bets, each acceptable on its own, whose combined outcomes guarantee a loss. Ramsey presents this as a reason to regard probabilistic coherence as a condition of rationality. But I have started to wonder whether my use of the argument is defensible. Is susceptibility to a Dutch book really a reliable test for which of my everyday uncertain beliefs to revise? Might there be propositions whose acceptance is perfectly reasonable even though their associated credences are Dutch-bookable—for example, because the relevant bets could never actually be offered, or because the propositions are not the kinds of things about which stable numerical confidence makes sense? And from the inside, how would I distinguish between using the Dutch book check as a genuine consistency constraint and using it as a way to launder my intuitions into something that merely looks formally precise? I would like a careful assessment of the policy itself: when it is reliable, when it fails, and whether it can be restated so as to keep its benefits without its costs.
