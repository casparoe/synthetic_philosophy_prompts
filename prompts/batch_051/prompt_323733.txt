A colleague sent round the following argument, and our reading group can't agree where it goes wrong, or even wether it does. It looks as if it proves too much: if you take the way biologists talk about functions at face value, you get Aristotle's final causes back, and then final causes everywhere—so at least one of the steps has to give.

(1) Biologists say the heart's function is to pump blood, and they are right; this is not metaphor but a genuine explanatory claim.
(2) A function claim says what something is for, and being for an end is just what a final cause is: the end explains the thing.
(3) If a claim is literally true in a science, then what it posits is real—or at least we should not be forced to choose between saying biology is false and accepting something real.
(4) Therefore final causes are real.
(5) If final causes are real, then wherever something has a function, that end can explain the thing.
(6) But function-like arrangements turn up well beyond kidneys and hearts: courtship dances are for mating, perhaps ecosystems are for stability, species for persistence.
(7) So the same pattern licenses teleological explanation all the way up, which is precisely the picture modern science thought it had driven out.
(8) Since that conclusion is unacceptable, at least one of (1)–(5) must go.

Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked fine, and what the main options have to pay. In particular, does a selected-effects or etiological account of function block the slide, or does it just relocate the teleology into natural selection? I would like to keep (1) if I can, and I'm not sure which of the others I'd rather lose. Answer in British English, please.
