I run the planning unit at a regional water authority. Four or five times a year we have to pick among climate-adaptation investments — raising a levee, building a reservoir, buying out a few streets, paying farmers to fallow land — where the payoff of each option depends on which future climate scenario actually arrives. Our hydrologists will give us a small set of scenarios (say, a 20-year flood regime, a 100-year one, a 500-year one), but they refuse to put probabilities on them; the models disagree by a lot and they say it would be fake precision. Everything still has to go to a board of laypeople for sign-off. We usually have about a day to run the analysis, and my two analysts are not decision theorists.

I know the rough menu from decision theory under ignorance: maximin (pick the option whose worst case is least bad), minimax regret (pick the option that minimizes how much you'll kick yourself later), maybe the Hurwicz criterion, and I know Rawls and Harsanyi had a big argument about whether the veil of ignorance licenses equal probabilities or something more worst-case-ish. But I can't hand the board a philosophy paper. I need a procedure my team can actually run in a day, and that the board can follow when they sign off.

Please write me that procedure as a step-by-step worksheet. I want the tests laid out in the order they should be applied — starting with a dominance screen (if one option is at least as good as another in every scenario, kill the dominated one) — and then the criteria, with clear rules for what counts as passing a step. In particular:

- mark every step where a "choice" is really a judgment call (how bad the worst case has to be, how to carve up the scenarios, how much we should care about regret) rather than just arithmetic;
- handle the ugly fact that maximin and minimax regret both depend on how finely we slice the scenarios — a "severe flood" can be one state or five, and that changes the answer — with some kind of sensitivity check or a rule for when slicing is gamesmanship;
- say where the procedure stops and hands the decision to the board because it's values rather than method, and what exactly the board is being asked to decide at that point;
- say what the procedure cannot do — for instance, when maximin and minimax regret disagree and the board won't pick a tiebreaker, the procedure should say so rather than pretend.

Then run the procedure on at least these two decisions, showing the worksheet at each step. The numbers are annualized costs in millions, including damages, so lower is better.

Decision 1, levee for a river town:

| Option | 20-yr state | 100-yr state | 500-yr state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do nothing | 1 | 10 | 40 |
| Raise levee 1m | 4 | 4 | 14 |
| Buyouts plus restored floodplain | 8 | 7 | 9 |

Decision 2, drought planning: do nothing (0, 5, 35), build a new reservoir that mostly helps in moderate droughts (2, 3, 20), or a permanent demand-management/rationing scheme that caps the worst case but annoys farmers every single year (6, 7, 9).

And say explicitly: in decision 2, does the procedure give the board an answer it probably won't like, and is that answer what they should do anyway?

Finally, one short paragraph on how the procedure would degrade if we genuinely couldn't enumerate the scenarios (true deep uncertainty), rather than just being ignorant of probabilities.
