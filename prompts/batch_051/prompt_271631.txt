I have been developing a framework for thinking about historical inevitability and would welcome a candid assessment. The central notion is what I call counterfactual damping. Take an antecedent perturbation—some small alteration of the world just before a historical episode: a delayed diplomatic telegram, a general's hesitation, a storm arriving a day late—and ask how far its effects propagate as events unfold. My claim is that for any historical outcome, considered under a given description, there is a boundary in the space of possible perturbations: below it, perturbations are damped out by the surrounding system and the outcome still occurs; above it, they redirect the course of events and the outcome no longer occurs. I call this boundary the outcome's damping horizon. Inevitability, on this view, is not a bare property of an event but a feature of an outcome-description's damping horizon: an outcome is inevitable to the extent that its horizon is broad, and fragile to the extent that its horizon is narrow. Thus the outcome 'a great-power war in Europe within a decade of 1914' may have a very broad damping horizon, while 'the war that actually began in August 1914, with exactly its alliances, timing, and opening campaigns,' has a very narrow one; the familiar dispute may simply trade on two different horizons. I also want to make a stronger metaphysical claim: once an outcome-description is fixed, its damping horizon is an objective feature of the causal structure of the period, not merely a feature of our interests or of our chosen grain of description. Please give me the three strongest objections to this framework, indicate whether I am reinventing an existing position, and tell me whether any of the objections is fatal.
