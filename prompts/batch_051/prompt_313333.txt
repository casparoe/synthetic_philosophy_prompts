I have spent the last few years reading LessWrong and adjacent forums, so I am used to having my positions steelmanned rather than dismissed, and I would rather be told where my own argument is soft than have the audience handed to me as a strawman. I'm giving a seminar in a few weeks to a philosophy of science group, and I want you to prepare me for the question period.

The talk defends a qualified version of the enhanced indispensability argument. My claim is that in the two standard showcase cases — the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas and the hexagonal cells of honeycomb — the mathematics is not just a compact redescription of a causal-biological story; it does genuine explanatory work. My less standard move is that the two cases support different, localized commitments. The cicada case, if it works, commits you only to arithmetic facts about divisibility and co-primality; the honeycomb case, if it works, commits you only to a geometric optimality claim about tiling. I call the position localized explanatory indispensability, and my pitch is that it has the virtues of the enhanced indispensability argument without forcing you to be a Platonist about all of mathematics on the strength of two biological examples.

The argument in brief: for periodical cicadas, the explanatory load is carried by the fact that 13 and 17 are prime, because a prime brood cycle minimizes synchronization with periodic hazards, such as shorter predator population cycles and other broods. Composite candidate cycles are ruled out in virtue of their divisibility structure, and that is an arithmetic-explanatory fact rather than a merely causal one. For honeycomb, the explanatory load is carried by the fact that, among regular polygons that tile the plane, the hexagon encloses the most area for a given perimeter. The bees need not compute this; the biological question "why hexagonal rather than triangular or square?" is answered by the geometric optimum, with a lower-level story available about how a local building rule or physical process makes hexagons stable. I do not want to litigate the bees' computational capacities; my claim is that the optimum, not the mechanism, is what answers the contrastive why-question.

The audience: mostly philosophers of biology and philosophers of mathematics, with two working population biologists who treat mathematical models as predictive instruments rather than as windows onto abstract objects. The philosophers are not Quineans by default; several are nominalist-leaning, one is a structuralist who will accept mathematical structure but not mathematical objects, and the philosophers of biology will grant my causal short stories for both cases and then resist the step from "the mathematics represents an optimum or an arithmetic constraint" to "the mathematics explains." I expect the room to run on different priors than mine, and I want their objections in their own vocabulary rather than translated into mine.
