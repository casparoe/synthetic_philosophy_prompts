I keep a record of the verdicts that a colleague of mine, M., has given over the years on whether particular entities are phenomenally conscious — that is, whether there is a felt, subjective point of view associated with them. M. refuses to state a general criterion and insists on judging case by case, but the record contains eight entries, with the grounds M. gave at the time:

1. Dog: yes. Grounds: pain and fear behavior, flexible learning, clear affective states.
2. New Caledonian crow: yes. Grounds: complex problem-solving and aversive learning, supported by a large and highly organized brain.
3. Octopus: yes, with some hesitation. Grounds: a large, centralized nervous system with differentiated lobes, exploratory behavior, and apparent pain responses.
4. Honeybee: no. Grounds: remarkable learning and communication, but all of it implemented in a nervous system M. regarded as far too small to support a felt state.
5. Lobster: no. Grounds: nociceptive withdrawal and nothing more; no integrated affective state.
6. Jellyfish: no. Grounds: a diffuse nerve net with no central nervous system and therefore no candidate subject of experience.
7. C. elegans: no. Grounds: a 302-neuron nervous system that, despite nociceptive avoidance, M. considered a reflex chain.
8. A wheeled robot trained by reinforcement learning to mimic pain responses and avoid stimuli tagged as harmful: no. Grounds: a simulation of pain, not pain.

M. has now been asked to give a verdict on a new case from a neighboring lab. The case is a newly described marine worm whose central nervous system contains roughly forty thousand neurons and has no cortex-like or cephalopod-like structures. The worm has nociceptive sensory neurons that respond specifically to tissue damage. After an injury, it curls around and grooms the damaged segment, suppresses feeding for a day or more, and subsequently avoids the location where it was injured. These responses are weakened by an endogenous opioid-like compound and by a standard analgesic, and the worm can learn to associate a neutral cue with a noxious event so that the cue alone later elicits defensive behavior. It shows no social behavior and no complex navigation or problem-solving.

The lab wants to know how M. should rule if M. is to remain consistent with the recorded track record. In your answer, please do the following: identify the two or three most plausible principles that fit all eight recorded verdicts; state what each principle predicts for the worm and whether the precedents determine the answer or leave it genuinely open; give your best prediction of M.'s verdict and the precedent among the eight that is the closest analog, with reasons for preferring the principle behind it; say what general criterion M. would be adopting by going each way if the precedents underdetermine the case; and specify a further test case that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles.
