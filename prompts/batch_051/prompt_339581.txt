I'm teaching a short unit on whether a life without sadness and grief would actually be better, and I've paired the philosophy with Brave New World. My students keep reading the novel as a straight-up brief for negative emotions: the World State kills off misery with soma and conditioning, John the Savage insists on the right to be unhappy, so Huxley must be saying sadness and grief are good and we'd be worse off without them. But I'm not convinved the novel earns that conclusion, and I'd like a sharper reading than I can give on my own. The World State isn't just a society without sadness; it's also one without strong family bonds, real art, religion, genuine science, or chosen love, and its happiness often looks more like numbed contentment than anything you'd want. So my students may be treating 'this particular flattened life is worse' as if it were the same claim as 'sadness itself is valuable,' and those aren't obviously the same.

Could you walk me through the novel with that distinction in mind — whether Huxley shows sadness and grief to be intrinsically valuable (good in themselves, or part of what makes a life meaningful) or only instrumentally valuable (worth keeping because stamping them out costs too much, or because they come bundled with things we do want, like honesty and depth)? Point to specific elements — soma and the feelies, Lenina, Bernard, Helmholtz's exile, Linda's death and John's reaction, the lighthouse scene with the whip — and be honest about where the novel resists the reading that negative emotions are valuable. Does Brave New World actually give us reason to think a life with grief is better than a life without it, or does it mostly show that you can't stamp out grief without stamping out a lot of other things too — and would the book itself care about the difference?
