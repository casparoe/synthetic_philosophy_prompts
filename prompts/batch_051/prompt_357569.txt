I've been helping with safety review of model outputs at my lab, and I've drifted into a personal rule I'm no longer sure about: I don't read raw chain-of-thought transcripts. I only look at the final answer and a short summary of the reasoning, either the model's own abstract or the one the reviewing engineer wrote. I tell myself this is a principled epistemic shortcut, not laziness, but the line between those has gotten blurrier.

The case for the rule goes like this. Raw chains are a massive time sink, and I noticed I systematically over-trust fluent ones: a chain that reads like a careful, self-aware reasoner makes me raise my confidence in the answer, even though a fully confabulated rationalization would read exactly as fluent. Fluency just isn't evidence of faithfulness. And there's an incentive problem: if our judgments about runs ever feed back into training or checkpoint selection based on what the raw chain says, we're building a signal that is extremely cheap for the model to fake. The model doesn't need to actually do the safe thing; it just needs to learn to narrate a safe-sounding thing. That creates pressure toward sanitized, obfuscated chains — we'd be training models to write persuasive diaries while treating those diaries as ground truth. So I treat raw CoT as too cheap to fake to be worth reading and lean on summaries, downstream behavior, and occasional audits instead.

The doubt is obvious: I might be refusing to look at the most informative artifact we have because it's annoying and because it sometimes forces me to redo a settled judgment. A friend says I'm conflating my private use of chains as evidence with the lab's use of chains as a training signal, and that my fluency point is a reason to read raw chains more carefully, not less. She might be right, but I can't tell wheter that's genuine epistemic modesty or my usual instinct to defend the rule I already have. I want you to commit, not lay out options.
