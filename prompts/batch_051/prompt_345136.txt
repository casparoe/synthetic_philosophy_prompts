Compare the positions of William James and W.K. Clifford on the ethics of belief, focusing on the role of evidence in their accounts of intellectual responsibility. James argues that when a genuine option—one that is live, forced, and momentous—cannot be settled on intellectual grounds, it is permissible for our passional or willing nature to determine our belief. Clifford, by contrast, maintains that it is always wrong to believe without sufficient evidence, and he supports this with cases such as the ship-owner who suppresses doubts about his vessel’s seaworthiness. Consider how much of the disagreement is substantive rather than a matter of differing starting points. Does James’s position require a robust account of voluntary control over belief that he never fully provides? Does Clifford’s evidentialism presuppose a threshold of sufficient evidence that is either too vague to guide us or too strict to be livable? If each view is restated in its strongest form, is the residual conflict better described as a disagreement about the duty to examine evidence, about the permissibility of faith, or about the scope of moral evaluation for beliefs? Decide which author offers the more defensible ethics of belief, and explain what the other would need to concede for the two views to be reconciled. Please answer in American English.
