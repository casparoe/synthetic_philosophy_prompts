There's a pattern I keep running into and I don't know what to call it. When I sign up for a service or install an app, I'm shown a wall of terms and a button that says "I agree." Nobody involved thinks anybody read those terms. I don't read them, and the company that wrote them doesn't act like it expects me to read them either — the important clauses get buried fifty screens down, or the interface makes reading technically possible but practically absurd. Then later the company holds me to one of those buried clauses, and I usualy go along because, well, I clicked the button. What strikes me is that this isn't just one side failing to read. It's two sides both knowing that no shared understanding was formed, while both going through the motions of an agreement anyway, and then treating the motion as if it did the work.

I know there are legal ideas about being bound by what you had the chance to read, and about take-it-or-leave-it contracts where one side has all the power. What I'm after is the thing in between or underneath: the arrangement where the outward form of agreement is preserved even though everyone knows the substance is missing, and the whole thing gets its force from everyone's willingness to treat the form as enough. Does that configuration have an established name? How is it different from just saying the terms were available, or that the buyer was careless? And if there's no exact name, what would you call it, and where do you draw the line between this and a real agreement or a real power imbalance? I'm not looking for a single vocabulary word so much as a clean account of what's actually doing the normative work when a court says the click is binding.
