I have been trying to work out whether my intuitive judgments about economic inequality can be brought under a single principle. The candidate I keep testing is a version of Rawls's difference principle: social and economic inequalities are permissible only if they are part of a system that makes the worst-off group better off than they would be under the relevantly equal alternative. But my case-by-case reactions keep embarrassing that principle.

Here are the cases, with my unrehearsed verdicts.

A city approves a luxury apartment tower after the developer agrees to include twenty percent below-market units and to fund a new clinic in a poor neighborhood. The tower will plainly increase inequality in the neighborhood, but I think the deal is fine.

A hospital introduces a donor priority scheme: people who pay a large annual fee can jump the queue for non-emergency specialist appointments, and the fee income is used to reduce waiting times for everyone else, including the uninsured. I think this is probably fine, provided the data really show that public waiting times fall.

A technology company raises its CEO's pay from eight million to forty million dollars in the same year it gives every employee a one-time two-thousand-dollar bonus. The CEO claims the raise was necessary to retain talent, and the company's profits rose, funding the bonuses. I think this is not fine. The gap is simply too large, even though the lowest-paid employees are better off by two thousand dollars than they would have been without the raise.

A government raises value-added tax by two percentage points but uses the revenue to increase child benefits for low-income families. The net effect is that the poorest decile is slightly better off, while middle-income households are a little worse off. I think this is fine.

A country opens a state-owned lithium mine on land belonging to an Indigenous minority. The government promises fifteen percent of the royalties to the community and uses the rest to fund public schools and health care nationally. The community is materially better off than it would have been without the mine, but it did not consent and would not have chosen the mine. I think this is not fine, but I cannot explain why under the difference principle; the mine seems to make the least advantaged materially better off.

A university pays a few star professors salaries several times the median, explicitly because their grants and prestige attract revenue that funds need-based scholarships. I think this is fine.

I notice that my verdicts on the CEO case and the star professor case are hard to reconcile. Both are high pay at the top with some benefit to the bottom. The difference principle seems to approve both, and yet I want to approve only one. I also cannot tell whether my lithium mine verdict is tracking a non-material good—something like consent or self-determination—that the difference principle ignores.

I am not asking whether these policies violate Rawls's separate principles of basic liberties or fair equality of opportunity. I am asking whether a single principle about economic advantage can capture my verdicts, or whether more than one value is doing the work. If the principle must be amended with a relative inequality condition or a consent condition, I want to know how, and whether the amended principle still resembles the difference principle enough to be called Rawlsian.

There is also a case on which my gut keeps moving. A country legalizes a market for kidneys, with a regulated price and a rule that every transplant from a paid donor is matched by a free transplant for someone on the public waiting list. I can see the least-advantaged argument in favor, but I do not trust myself to have the verdict fixed. Please tell me what the principle that best fits my other verdicts says about this case, and whether my resistance to it should make me revise that principle or my other verdicts.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question.
