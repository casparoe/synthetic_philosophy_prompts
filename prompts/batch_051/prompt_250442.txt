You are a professional philosopher specializing in seventeenth-century political thought, with particular expertise on Spinoza. Approach this as a philosophical reconstruction, not a historical narrative: the task is to test a theory against a case. Begin by brainstorming the components of Spinoza's framework you will need, and if the case forces you to revise your initial mapping, say so explicitly.

Analyze the killing of Johan and Cornelis de Witt in The Hague on 20 August 1672 as a case study for Spinoza's theory of democracy. The salient facts: Johan de Witt had governed the Dutch Republic as Grand Pensionary of Holland for nearly two decades, under a regime that excluded the House of Orange from the stadtholderate; his brother Cornelis was imprisoned in the Gevangenpoort on a charge of plotting against William of Orange; a crowd stirred partly by Orangist loyalties stormed the prison, seized both brothers, and murdered and mutilated them. Spinoza was a contemporary and a supporter of the De Witt regime and is said to have been shaken by the killings. In the Theological-Political Treatise (1670) he had defended democracy as the most natural form of commonwealth and the one most consonant with liberty, arguing that in a democracy the people transfer their natural right not to an outside ruler but to the community itself. In the later, unfinished Political Treatise he distinguishes the forms of commonwealth and analyzes the passions that both constitute the power of a multitude and threaten to destabilize the state; the manuscript breaks off before finishing the treatment of democracy.

Using that framework, give a disciplined mapping. Ask who the sovereign is in 1672 and where legitimate authority sits in Spinozistic terms; how his account of the passions — fear, hope, indignation, devotion to monarchical symbols — explains a crowd turning against a government of which it was in some sense a part; what his theory says the republican authorities faced and what, if anything, it would have prescribed about managing religion and popular passions before they turned lethal; and whether the killings are best classified as a breakdown of democratic institutions, as the reassertion of monarchical passion within a republic, or as something Spinoza's own conception of democracy makes possible. Then run the case in the other direction. Does it refute the optimism of the Theological-Political Treatise, push toward the tougher, passion-centered outlook of the Political Treatise, or show that Spinoza's democratic ideal was never a theory of mass action in the first place? Be explicit about which of his claims the episode damages and which it leaves standing, and say plainly where his framework simply has nothing to say.
