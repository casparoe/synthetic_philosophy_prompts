I'm a science journalist, and I've been tracking the recurring fight over METI — whether humans should actively beam messages at nearby stars instead of just listening. The dark forest hypothesis is the lens I keep coming back to: civilizations stay quiet because a detected neighbour is, by default, a future threat that can be killed off cheaply, so broadcasting is supposedly a self-wipe move. I don't want to write another explainer of what the dark forest is; I want to work out which of its supposed implications for METI actually carry weight for a policy-minded general reader, and which are overrated or don't follow at all. Rank maybe four or five candidate implications — total radio silence, treating METI as an irreversible existensial bet, shifting resources toward finding pre-industrial or pre-radio civilizations before they leak, maybe even continuing to broadcast as a way of proving we aren't hiding and are therefore less suspicious — or add your own. Defend the ordering. For each one I want to know whether it survives if you push on the underlying game theory: cheap preemption, whether peaceful intent can be made credible across light-years, whether the situation is even a one-shot prisonner's dilemma. And tell me where each implication smuggles in assumptions the hypothesis doesn't actually license. Finally, how should the ranking change if you accept that we've been leaking radio and biosignatures for decades, so 'going quiet' might not even be available to us.
