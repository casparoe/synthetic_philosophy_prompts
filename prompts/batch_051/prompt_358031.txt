I am on the steering committee of a religiously mixed civic association that is about to publish a public endorsement of a state bill on debt relief for low-income households, a matter both factions agree falls under questions of basic justice, and after months of meetings I have concluded that our two factions are mostly talking past one another. One faction holds a broadly Rawlsian line on public reason. On their view, because supporting the bill means calling for coercive state action, our statement should ultimately rest on considerations that citizens of any faith or none could reasonably be expected to share, and while distinctively religious arguments may be voiced, they are subject to a proviso: a suitable nonreligious public reason must be offered in due course. The other faction is inclusivist. They think the proviso treats religious citizens as second-class contributors, that a member's sincere religious grounds for supporting or opposing a coercive law are legitimate inputs to democratic justification on their own, and that any demand to translate them into shared secular terms either distorts them or imposes an unfair asymmetry. Both sides care about the same bill and about the health of the association, but they cannot agree on what our statement owes to the broader public.

We have decided to try an adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to draft the document a good-faith collaboration between a committed wide-view Rawlsian and a committed inclusivist should produce. Please act as a professional political philosopher who works on public reason and religious participation in liberal democracy. Start by brainstorming candidate formulations, and be willing to discard ones that are vacuous or subtly slanted. Then write: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps could sign, including claims about what the coercive use of state power needs by way of justification, claims about which of the other side's core cases each side can grant, and an explicit statement of where the genuine fault line lies; (2) a disagreements table in which the residual disagreements are stated precisely, each in wording a proponent of the relevant view would accept as their own; and (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give at least one concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it — for example, an abolitionist whose only ground for opposing slavery was a religious conviction, or a legislator whose sole reason for supporting debt relief is a particular reading of Leviticus — and record in advance what each camp predicts that test will show.

If you think part of the dispute is merely verbal — for instance, about what makes a reason public, or about how demanding the proviso actually is — say exactly which part and why. And if I am wrong that these two positions form a stable, genuine opposition, or if the task as I have set it up already presupposes something one side would contest, I would rather you tell me that plainly than produce a polished document for its own sake.
