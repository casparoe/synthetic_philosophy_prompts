A friend has an argument that deontological constraints are self-defeating, and I can't find the broken step.

(1) If there's a deontological constraint against killing the innocent, then killings of the innocent are bad, and — other things equal — you should act so that there are fewer of them.

(2) A gunman will kill five innocent people unless you yourself kill one innocent person; if you kill the one, he releases the five. Assume he'll keep his word.

(3) From (1), one killing is better than five. So you should kill the one.

(4) But the very same constraint also says you may not kill an innocent person, even to prevent other killings.

(5) So the constraint tells you to minimize killings, then forbids the only way to minimize them. It defeats its own point.

(6) So deontological constraints are self-defeating. You can't say killings are bad while refusing to reduce how many of them happen.

I'd most like to hang on to (4), since that's the only thing keeping the view from collapsing into consequentialism; (2) is just the setup. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want a real diagnosis too — why the bad step looked fine, and what each alternative commits me to. If the whole thing turns on a slip or a presupposition I can't see, say so plainly instead of humoring me.
