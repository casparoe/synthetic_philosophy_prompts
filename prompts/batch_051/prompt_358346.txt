Consider the following two-player, strategic-form game. The row player has strategies T, M, and B; the column player has strategies L and R. Payoffs are written as (row, column):

|       | L        | R         |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| **T** | (0, 0)   | (10, 10)  |
| **M** | (10, 10) | (0, 0)    |
| **B** | (6, 3)   | (6, 3)    |

Now consider this argument that B should be eliminated by iterated removal of strictly dominated strategies, where a mixed strategy strictly dominates a pure strategy when it yields a strictly higher expected payoff against every pure strategy of the opponent:

(1) If the column player plays L, B gives the row player 6, while M gives 10. So B is strictly worse than M against L.

(2) If the column player plays R, B gives the row player 6, while T gives 10. So B is strictly worse than T against R.

(3) Therefore, by mixing between T and M with the right probabilities, the row player obtains a strategy that yields more than 6 in both columns, and hence strictly dominates B.

(4) A strategy strictly dominated in this way cannot be optimal for an expected-utility-maximizing player and should be eliminated before any further dominance reasoning.

(5) So B goes, leaving only T and M as admissible for the row player.

Premises (1) and (2) are obviously correct, so the argument looks forced. Is it? Tell me exactly which step, if any, fails, and give the correct version of that step; if the argument is valid, say why the conclusion, though perhaps surprising, follows.
