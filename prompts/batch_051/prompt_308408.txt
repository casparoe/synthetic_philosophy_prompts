So I'm writing a novel about a logician who is loosely based on Gödel. The plot: he's old and dying in the near future, and a startup offers him two ways to keep going after death — a whole-brain emulation where they scan and simulate his brain on better hardware and call the result him, or cryonic suspension until a scan or revival medicine is good enough. My character has to decide, but I don't want the decision to be my views; I want it to track the real Gödel's likely reasoning. The book is about what kinds of survival people would actually count as survival, so the reasoning matters.

The real Gödel died before either of these was a live option, which is exactly what I want your help with. As I understand him, he took the afterlife seriously and thought there could be rational grounds for believing in it, not just revealed faith. He also seems to have taken his incompleteness results to put real pressure on the idea that the human mind is just a finite machine, and to have leaned toward the view that no such machine could be the whole story about a mind. I want my logician's response to feel like the historical Gödel's would have felt, not like a generic Platonist's.

So give me a careful predicted answer: how would Gödel have reacted to the upload offer? Would he see the simulation as a genuine continuation of his mind, as a very good model that is nevertheless not him, or as a category error — confusing a pattern that can be run with the mind that does the running? And would his belief in an afterlife make him more or less open to it? I can see two readings: he might dismiss the virtual afterworld as a counterfeit of the real one, or he might take it seriously precisely because he thought survival after death was rationally respectable and would want to see wether this machine version satisfied his own standards. Which reading is stronger, and why?

Then do the same for the freezing option. If the mind is not exhausted by the brain's computational structure, then freezing a brain might not preserve the person — but freezing the whole body at least does not replace the body with an abstraction. Would he have found cryonics more sympathetic as a bet on the actual person reanimating, even if nobody can spell out the mechanism? Or would he have thought both options are just materialist misdescriptions of what a person is?

Dont just say he would hate it because he was a Platonist. I want the tension between the afterlife stuff and the anti-mechanism stuff, and I want you to flag the places where the historical record is too thin to go further.
