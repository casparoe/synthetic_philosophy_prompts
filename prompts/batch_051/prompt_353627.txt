Here's an argument I keep reconstructing from the geometric rationality / Kelly stuff I've read, and I can't tell whether the last step is a sleight of hand or whether I'm just refusing the conclusion because it's uncomfortable.

(1) You get an indefinitely repeated sequence of independent bets with a fixed positive edge, and each round you can wager any fixed fraction of you're current bankroll.
(2) After n rounds your bankroll is a product of per-round multipliers, so by the law of large numbers the log of your bankroll grows like n times the expected log multiplier.
(3) Therefore among fixed-fraction strategies, the one that maximizes expected log multiplier comes to dominate every other fixed-fraction strategy almost surely in the long run.
(4) Maximizing expected log is just Kelly betting, and since log is concave, Kelly betting is formally equivalent to expected utility with log utility.
(5) But the dominance fact in step 3 doesn't mention anyone's risk preferences at all — its just arithmetic about repeated multiplication.
(6) So even an expected-value maximizer (someone with linear utility in dollars) should bet full Kelly, and if they bet more than Kelly they're making an objective mistake that the long run will punish almost surely.

I half-suspect the answer is that step 3 only talks about almost-sure paths, not expected wealth, so a genuine EV maximizer just doesn't care about being dominated almost surely — they care about the tiny chance of astronomical wealth. But then I worry I'm the one smuggling in expected-utility reasoning as the arbiter, which is exactly what geometric rationality is supposed to be pushing back on. So: is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and what's the corrected version of it?
