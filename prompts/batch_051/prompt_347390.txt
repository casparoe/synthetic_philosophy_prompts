I teach Hellenistic philosophy, and each year, when we reach the Stoic revival, I have more trouble than I would like deflating a standing objection to Epictetus. Epictetus distinguishes between what is up to us — in his catalogue, judgment, impulse, desire, and aversion — and what is not, such as health, reputation, and property. The objection is that this distinction seems false as he draws it. I can do a great deal about my health through diet and medicine, about my reputation through conduct, about my property through prudence, and influence over such matters is plainly a matter of degree rather than of kind. Is the dichotomy of control best understood, then, as an imprecise version of the modern advice to concentrate on what one can influence, with Epictetus merely drawing too sharp a line through something scalar? Or is there a different function of the distinction that this objection misses? Please explain how you would present the distinction so that the objection no longer seems decisive. End your answer with a single sentence beginning with the words 'In sum:' that states the most important correction to the objection's framing.
