I've been going down a rabbit hole on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument in philosophy of time. As I understand it: special relativity says there's no frame-independent "now," so which events are simultaneous with you depends on your velocity. The argument then says, take any event you'd normally call future. There's some equally valid reference frame where that event is simultaneous with you right now. If being simultaneous with you is enough for something to be real—which is what presentists seem to need—then that future event is real now, and running this for every future event forces you into the block universe where past, present, and future all exist. Philosophers have been going back and forth on this for fifty-plus years and as far as I can tell nobody has moved: eternalists treat it as basically decisive, presentists say it begs the question or just picks a privileged frame, and around it goes. My working theory is that the whole fight isnt really about physics—it's about what a theory of time is supposed to do: give a frame-independent story of what exists, or make sense of our tensed, present-centered talk. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a real hidden disagreement underneath it, maybe about what the formalism of relativity actually commits you to, or about what counts as an acceptable cost in a metaphysical theory? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey, and an honest answer about what would actually count as progress—a conceptual distinction, a theorem, a new experiment, a reframing of the question—or whether I should just stop expecting progress.
