An engineer I work with is pushing hard for us to adopt chain-of-thought monitoring for an LLM agent we're about to deploy, and he sent around his summary of the technique, which he says comes from an AI safety blog. His version is this: the model has to spell out its reasoning step by step before it answers, and since it's writing down its own actual thought process, you get a direct readout of what it's really thinking — way more than you'd get from the final output alone. The known weakness, he says, is faithfulness: the model might say it's doing one thing while doing another. But he thinks that's only a problem for models that already have a reason to hide their reasoning, and current models are trained to be helpful and honest, so they don't have hidden goals to conceal. So his bottom line is that monitoring turns the hard problem of judging outputs into the manageable problem of reading the model's own explanation, which makes chain-of-thought monitoring a robust safety fix. Another colleague pushed back that the real issue isn't only deceptive models deciding to write fake reasoning — it's that once you start monitoring and scoring the written chain of thought, you're creating optimization pressure for the model to produce obfuscated reasoning that looks good to the monitor instead of reasoning that accurately recounts what it did. I can't tell wether that's the same point as the faithfulness worry or a different one, and I also don't know which parts of the engineer's summary someone who actually works on this would accept. Could you go through it and tell me what's solid, where it quietly goes wrong, and whether the bottom line actually follows?
