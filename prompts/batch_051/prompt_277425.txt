A friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about whether a translation of a sacred text can itself be inspired, and we ended the night with our positions half-defined rather than settled. My friend held that only the original autographs can be called inspired, so a translation can at most reliably convey what the originals say. I found myself unable to give up the thought that the Greek Septuagint and the New Testament writers' apparent use of it disrupt that picture, and neither of us knew what to do with the KJV-onlyist, who seems to treat one early seventeenth-century English version as itself the preserved word of God. Rather than continue trading cases, I want the full specification first: what would an adequate theological theory of scriptural inspiration have to get right once translation is treated as a central fact of the tradition rather than as a fallback compromise? I am asking for a requirements list, not a survey of positions. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say why it is a genuine desideratum rather than the prejudice of one camp.

Among the things the list should address: classification of the baseline cases, including a reader treating the Septuagint as Scripture even though it is a translation of the Hebrew, New Testament writers citing Greek renderings as authoritative, and a KJV-onlyist treating a vernacular version as the received and preserved word of God; distinctions the theory may not collapse, such as being revealed or given versus being transmitted faithfully, serving as a witness to an earlier text versus serving as a church's own Scripture, and lexical fidelity versus theological fidelity; and constraints from textual history it cannot wish away, such as the fact that the Greek used by the New Testament sometimes departs from the Hebrew text as it now survives, and the fact that the tradition has sometimes treated words now judged late additions to a manuscript tradition as scriptural precisely because they stood in an influential translation rather than in the earliest reachable text. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular positions—original-autographs-only Protestantism, KJV-onlyism, and the view that every faithful translation is inspired in exactly the same way as the original—and whether any pair of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, since that would itself be a substantive result. A table is fine.
