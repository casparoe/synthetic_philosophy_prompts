Had a long argument last night with a friend about whether "noun" and "verb" are real categories in the language or just labels linguists slap on distributions. She kept saying theyre just convenient fictions, I kept wanting to say that some grammatical categories are more real than others, and now I want to turn the argument into a short essay. I'd love comments on the idea before I actually write the thing up.

The essay would argue that we should stop treating "grammatical category" as one kind of thing. Some categories seem to have the profile of natural kinds in the homeostatic property cluster sense: their members cluster on a bunch of properties, and the clustering is there because of something in the system, not just because we drew the boundaries. Others look more like family-resemblance categories or purely formal labels.

My running examples would be parts of speech, case, and prepositions. The controversial part is that I want to say abstract Case (the government-and-binding-ish notion, not just surface morphological case) is the best candidate for a natural kind, while parts of speech are shakier than people think, and adpositions are basically a wastebasket.

Rough reasons. For nouns vs. verbs: there's a lot of crosslinguistic evidence that the distinction shows up early in acquisition, patterns differently in aphasia, and tracks a loose object/event split. But the exceptions are also huge: languages with very small adjective classes, nouns used predicatively, verbs used nominally, zero-derivation everywhere. So maybe what we're seeing is a strong prototype effect plus a distributional artifact rather than a kind.

For case: I want to separate morphological case from abstract Case. Morphological case is so variable crosslinguistically that it's probably not a natural kind. But abstract Case, if you buy that picture, is a single underlying mechanism about nominal licensing that explains a cluster of otherwise unrelated phenomena: why DPs have to sit in certain structural positions, why passivization moves objects, why nonfinite clauses treat their subjects specially, why some complements show up with dummy case markers. If one mechanism explains all of that, then the category is doing real work and isn't just a label.

For prepositions: I'm suspicious that theyre the worst case. The class seems to cover spatial relators, grammatical function markers, selected particles, and idioms. The properties don't cluster much; they overlap like a chain. So I'd treat prepositions as more of a radial category or a historical accident than a natural kind.

Questions I'd especially want comments on. First, is the natural-kind framework actually doing any work here, or am I just renaming "has interesting generalizations" as "natural kind"? Second, would a Chomskyan and a functionalist both reject the framing for opposite reasons, and does that matter? Third, what would be the most damaging example for the claim that abstract Case is the best candidate? I'm particularly unsure whether the Case phenomena in languages without case morphology are real evidence or just theory-internal artifacts. And fourth, am I being unfair to prepositions by comparing a lexicosemantic-ish class to a syntactic relation?
