Here's a criterion for objecthood I was taught: a bunch of things compose a single object exactlly when their activities constitute a single life. So the cells in my body compose me, but the grains in a sand pile don't compose anything, because a pile isn't alive. Now consider a honeybee colony. Each bee is an organism in its own right, but the colony as a whole has a shared nest, a single queen, a division of labor, it thermoregulates the hive, and it reproduces as a unit when it swarms. Some biologists even call it a superorganism. Does the colony qualify as a single object under that criterion? If you say yes, spell out what the single 'life' is that the bees' activities constitute, since each bee already seems to have its own life. If you say no, is that a reductio of the criterion, or should we just say a colony is many objects even though it acts so unified? Go through the criterion bit by bit and say whether this case makes you want to revise it.
