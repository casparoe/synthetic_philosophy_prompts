I keep going back and forth on Malthus and I could use some help sorting out what I actually believe rather than just picking a side. The version of Malthusianism I'm attracted to isn't the cartoon one. It's just this: any finite planet where population keeps increasing, or even keeps consuming more per person, has to hit some limit eventually, and when it does something has to give—famine, disease, conflict, a crash, or a deliberate decision to restrain growth ahead of time. That core claim feels almost tautologically true, and it gives one tidy, unified way to think about everything from the Black Death to the green revolution.

But then I have this very firm intuition that the actual record since Malthus wrote is just about as close to a refutation as you ever get in social theory. Around 1800 there were something like a billion people living on a couple dollars a day in today's money, and Malthus's point about arithmetic resources versus geometric population was supposed to mean the checks would keep grinding away. Since then population has gone up roughly eightfold and average living standards rose enormously. If a theory predicts a ceiling and the ceiling keeps moving every time we reach for it, I don't see how its different from a horoscope. So my intuition is that calling Malthus right but early is mostly a way of not admitting the theory lost.

Where I get stuck is that I can't tell whether that intuition is aimed at the core claim or at the original story about mechanisms. Maybe the core claim is just a theorem about finite planets and can't be false, while the falsifiable part—the specific prediction that checks would bind on his timescale—was straightforwardly wrong. But then what exactly is the theory? If it comes with no timing it makes no prediction, and if it makes no prediction then calling him right seems empty. I suspect getting clear on this requires pulling apart two jobs the phrase right-but-early does: one is an empirical claim about mechanisms, the other is almost a definitional point about finite planets.

Walk me through what each option costs: dropping the intuition as a scope-of-observation artifact, amending the theory so it makes more specific claims about mechanisms and timing, abandoning it, or finding a reading where my intuition and the attractive core are both right because they're actually about different things. Tell me what would justify each move, not just which one you endorse. And if you think the question as posed is confused—for instance if I'm smuggling in a bad picture of what makes a long-run theory falsifiable in the first place—say that plainly and explain why, instead of pretending the setup works.
