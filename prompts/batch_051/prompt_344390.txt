Below is a draft essay on origin essentialism — specifically, on whether a person could have come from a different sperm and egg. Please comment on it as a rigorous but sympathetic philosophical referee. The question "Could you have come from a different sperm and egg?" is usually treated as a single modal question with a single answer. I want to suggest that it is really three questions, and that running them together makes the debate look both deeper and more settled than it is.

First is the numerical identity question: in a possible world where a different sperm or egg produced someone with your history, would that person be you? Second is the biological origin question: what biological event fixes the particular organism whose life is a continuation of yours — the gametes, the fertilization event, the first cell divisions, or something later? Third is the template question: could a person derived from different gametes but with the same general genetic endowment and life history be qualitatively indistinguishable from you, even if not numerically identical?

The classic origin-essentialist argument is aimed at the first question, but its standard motivation transfers poorly from artefacts to organisms. For a table, we can say: this very table could not have been made from a different block of wood, because that block was the material out of which the table was originally constituted. But a sperm and an egg are not the material out of which a person is constituted. They fuse, cease to exist as cells, and set in train a process of division, differentiation, and turnover; the adult body is not made of the same materials as the zygote, let alone the gametes. The relation between you and the original gametes is causal and informational, not constitutional. This does not refute the necessity-of-origin principle. But it undermines the easiest route from artefact intuitions to the conclusion that the particular sperm and egg are the objects whose necessity matters.

Even if we grant that some biological particular is an essential origin, the sperm-and-egg slogan may misidentify which one. Monozygotic twins arise from the same sperm and the same egg, yet they are distinct persons. A defender can insist that origin essentialism asserts only a necessary condition, so sharing an origin need not distinguish the twins. But that reply concedes the point that matters: the slogan is silent about what else fixes each twin's particularity. Once we ask that further question, the natural candidate is not the gametes but a later event — the twinning itself, or the earlier allocation of cells and fates within the embryo. The more carefully one tracks when a distinct organism appears, the less theoretical work the sperm-and-egg formulation does.

Chimerism sharpens the point. In tetragametic chimerism, two zygotes — each produced by a different sperm and a different egg — fuse into a single organism whose tissues contain two genetically distinct cell populations. Does such a person come from one sperm and one egg, from two of each, or from one pair only in an ancestor-cell sense that would assign one lineage to the germline and the other to the rest of the body? There is no easy answer within the original picture. This is not a refutation of origin essentialism; it shows that the view needs a distinction among gametic origin, zygotic origin, and lineage origin, and an argument for which of these bears the modal weight.

My conclusion is deliberately modest. I am not arguing that you could have come from a different sperm and egg. I am arguing that the question, as posed, is under-specified: its answer depends on which of the three questions is being asked, and the most plausible regimentations of origin essentialism should identify origin more carefully than the slogan does. One could retreat to a version cast in terms of parents rather than gametes, but that would no longer answer the sperm-and-egg question. The residual puzzle is whether there is a defensible principle of organismic origin — perhaps tied to the earliest event that individuates you as a distinct organism — that still delivers the strong modal claims the slogan was meant to deliver.
