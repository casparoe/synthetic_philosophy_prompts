I've been trying to build a little agent that checks its own reasoning for a side project, and a friend pointed me at Löb's theorem and told me the whole idea was hopeless. The way he laid it out, though, the theorem seems to make things just as impossible for humans as for AIs, and I can't figure out were the argument overreaches.

Here's the argument as I now have it. (1) A rationally self-trusting agent has to be justified in believing that its own reasoning is reliable — otherwise it's just running on blind faith. (2) Being justified in believing your reasoning is reliable means being justified in accepting a reflection principle: for any sentence P, if my reasoning endorses P, then P. (3) To even formulate and check that principle, an agent's reasoning has to be strong enough to encode claims about its own reasoning and do basic arithmetic, so at least as strong as Peano arithmetic. (4) Löb's theorem says no consistent formal system of that strength can prove its own reflection principle; if it did, it could prove every sentence, contradictions included. (5) So a consistent agent of that kind cannot prove that its reasoning is reliable. (6) Given (1) and (2), it follows that such an agent cannot rationally trust its own reasoning. (7) This isn't a special fact about AIs: when I explicitly assess my own reliability, I'm doing something at least as strong as the systems in (4), or near enough that the same limitation should carry over. (8) I can't outsource the problem either, because trusting someone else's reasoning or a separate checker program would just inherit it — I'd have to use my own reasoning to vet their output. (9) So nobody can rationally trust any reasoning, including the reasoning in (1) through (8).

But (9) can't be right. I trust modus ponens and single-digit arithmetic way more than I trust a coin flip, and if (9) were true then the argument just given would be no more trustworthy than fortune-cookie advice, which makes it self-undermining. I'd like you to walk me through which premise or step to reject, why it looked fine, and what each live option costs. If I had to choose, I'd want to keep (1) and (4) — (1) because rejecting it seems to let in blind faith, and (4) because it's a theorem — but I don't know whether I can hold both. Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. Answer in American English.
