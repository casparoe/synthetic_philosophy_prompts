Hey—I'm a PhD student in philosophy of math, and I've been asked to teach a one-off undergrad metaphysics seminar (14 students, 75 minutes) on supertasks. I want to do Thomson's lamp in a way that actually lands. You know the setup: the lamp starts on, gets toggled at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ..., so by t=1 infinitely many toggles have occurred, but the sequence of states is 0, 1, 0, 1, ... and has no limit. The students can all recite that the final state is underdetermined, but they don't feel the difference between that and a supertask that does have a well-defined limit, like Zeno's runner finishing the trip. Design me a classroom activity where they run into this with their bodies, not just watch me draw it on the board. It can use props, assigned roles, a timing rule that forces the halving schedule, whatever—just keep materials free or nearly free, and if a scoring or betting scheme makes sense include one (if it doesn't, say so). The debrief should map what actually happened in the room onto the distinction between the limit of a sequence of states and the state at the limit, and onto why the conclusion that the lamp must end up either on or off is a substantive assumption rather than a fact of logic. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—my money's on someone insisting it has to end one way, or trying to settle it by saying the lamp is both on and off—and how to handle that. And be honest about which part of the idea the activity phsyically cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself. I want the point to hit them through what they do, not through me explaining afterwards.
