I am a science and ideas journalist for a general-interest magazine, and a large share of my reporting now concerns institutional failure: drug companies sitting on safety signals, tech platforms whose moderation procedures misfire at scale, hospital systems with patterns of preventable harm, research institutions that tolerate fraud for years. One editing problem recurs in nearly every such piece. When am I justified in writing that the institution itself — "the company," "the hospital," "the lab," "the platform" — knew, misled, ignored, or was negligent, rather than attributing the conduct to named individuals? I need a reusable procedure I can run on the facts of a story, with the same care I would give to attributing a belief to a person, to decide when an entity-level attribution of blame is warranted and when it is false, unfair, or just a convenient shorthand for "some employees we could not identify."

The stakes are real but not primarily legal. I run into this several times a month, usually on deadline; I have an editor who is not trained in philosophy and will sign off on whatever I can justify in a few minutes; and getting it wrong can defame a company, unfairly pin an institution's failure on one mid-level employee, or let executives hide behind the words "the company erred." Please write the procedure for that editor and me.

Ground the procedure in whatever the philosophy of corporate moral agency has actually worked out that a working journalist can use: whether a corporation can be a subject of knowledge, intention, and blame in a sense that is not just shorthand for the states of its members; how an organization's decision structure can combine what different employees know into something no single employee knows; and when a defective routine or culpable ignorance is the organization's fault rather than any individual's. Translate those ideas into a checklist or decision tree in words: the questions to ask in order, what evidence counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "attribute only to individuals," where it clears an institutional attribution, and where it must hand the decision to a human editor because the remaining step is a judgment of values, emphasis, or newsroom risk rather than a matter of fact. In particular, the procedure should keep three things apart: genuine institutional blame, mere shorthand for unnamed or unidentified employees, and the separate legal question of who can be sued.

Then apply the procedure to the three cases below, showing exactly how each question is answered. Do not soften an answer because it would be awkward to print; I need to see where the procedure bites, including at least one case where it tells me to write something I would rather not write.

Case A. A pharmaceutical company's clinical-trial data shows a serious safety signal only when two datasets are combined: one held by the clinical operations group and one by the pharmacovigilance group. No single employee saw the merged numbers, but the company had a standard signal-detection workflow that would have combined them, and that workflow was repeatedly skipped due to understaffing. Several employees knew the workflow existed and knew it was being skipped; none knew what the merged data would reveal. May I write that the company knew of the signal and ignored it?

Case B. A small medical-device startup's CEO personally directed a misleading marketing campaign, and the device later injured people. The copywriters and designers did not know the claims were false. The company had no meaningful board oversight, no review process, and no written policies. May I write that the company knowingly misled customers, or is the only defensible attribution to the CEO as an individual?

Case C. A hospital system experienced an unusual run of wrong-site surgeries across several of its hospitals over several years. No administrator ordered anyone to skip safety checks, but mid-level supervisors tolerated pencil-whipping the preoperative checklist; the system's productivity incentives made completing the checks costly in ways that discouraged them; and two quality-review committees each saw the aggregated numbers but each assumed the other was addressing the problem. May I write that the hospital system was negligent as an institution, and should the blame attach to the system, to the committees, to the supervisors, or to no one in particular?

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle. Flag the fact patterns where a decision procedure will misfire or where a well-designed institution and a bad outcome are going to be confused, and say explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules. Do not provide a bibliography or recommend readings; the procedure should carry its justification internally. Please write the answer in American English.
