Write the document that a committed naturalist and a committed normativist about health and disease could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. For this prompt, take naturalism about disease to be the view that whether something is a disease is settled, at bottom, by facts about biological functioning — paradigmatically, statistically subnormal functioning relative to an appropriate reference class — and that evaluative judgments do no definitional work. Take normativism to be the view that judgments of health and disease are ineliminably evaluative: a condition counts as a disease only if it is also judged to be bad, harmful, or a departure from how an organism ought to be. Do not let either camp caricature the other; each can grant the other's favorite facts (naturalists can care about suffering, normativists can care about biology) while disagreeing about which facts make something a disease.

Produce the document itself, not an essay about the project. It should contain:

(1) a short preamble stating the collaboration's question and ground rules;

(2) a numbered agreed-claims section — claims both sides genuinely accept, including claims about the history and sociology of classification where they are matters of record, claims about the overlap in paradigm cases, and claims about what would have to be shown to settle or dissolve the dispute;

(3) a disagreements table with a column for the issue, one column each for the naturalist's and the normativist's own wording of their position (each phrasing should be one that side would sign), and a column noting whether the disagreement appears substantive or partly verbal;

(4) a cruxes section identifying the sub-questions on which the dispute actually turns, with, for each crux, a concrete case, test, or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Where neither side can specify what would move them, say so and discuss what that immovability indicates.

Work the cruxes in the weeds rather than reiterating that each side can grant the other's favorite facts. Candidate material includes: conditions once classified as diseases and later declassified, or classified only after long advocacy; statistically common conditions of aging that are nonetheless treated as pathological; the selection of the reference class against which subnormal functioning is measured and whether that selection can be made non-evaluatively; whether nonhuman organisms can be diseased in the same sense as humans; whether health includes positive well-being or merely the absence of disease; whether enhancement-adjacent suffering from normal but disabling traits pulls the two definitions apart; and whether hybrid harmful-dysfunction accounts split the difference or inherit the weaknesses of both. Use whichever of these are genuinely useful and add others as needed.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested philosophical position as the consensus. Where the two camps agree on a historical or sociological claim, distinguish that shared claim from the competing interpretations of what it shows.
