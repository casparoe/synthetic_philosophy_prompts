I teach an upper-level undergraduate module on practical rationality — around 16 students, one 80-minute session — and the week on Buridan's ass never quite lands. They get the picture immediately: an ass, equally hungry and thirsty, exactly between a bale of hay and a pail of water, and the question whether it can choose at all. But by the time we get to the interesting version — whether a rational agent with no preference between two options can still act, and whether whatever breaks the tie counts as a reason — the students have already moved on. They keep saying the ass would just pick one, or that only a defective animal would stand there and die, and they don't feel the underyling problem about choice without preference. I want an activity where they themselves are forced to choose between options they genuinely don't prefer, and where they notice what just picking actually involves: is a tie-breaker (a coin, the left-hand one, copy the person next to you) a reason favouring the chosen option, or something else entirely?

The thing I most want them to come away seeing is the gap between being indifferent and being unable to act, and the thought that a rational agent can act without a first-order reason that favours one option over the other — maybe by adopting a second-order preference about how to settle ties. No lecture up front: they shouldn't hear Buridan or indifference as technical terms until the debrief. Please design a runnable classroom activity for me: rules or roles, materials, a timeline for 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto those distinctions. Also say what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the original puzzle the activity can't reproduce — I suspect the deathby indecision part is hard to get into a classroom without real stakes. And could you write the answer in British English?
