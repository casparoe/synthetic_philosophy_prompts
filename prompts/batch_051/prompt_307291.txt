After a long argument with a friend about whether the thermodynamic arrow of time is genuinely explained by statistical mechanics, I tried to reconstruct the strongest version of the skeptical side. I suspect something in it goes wrong, but I cannot identify the exact step. Here is the argument:

(1) Assume the underlying microdynamics are time-reversal invariant: for every lawful trajectory, the exact time-reversed trajectory is equally lawful.

(2) On this assumption, the second law cannot be a strict consequence of the dynamics; it must be a probabilistic claim about how most microstates compatible with a given macrostate evolve.

(3) The standard probabilistic account of entropy increase is the claim that, given only a system's present macrostate, the overwhelming majority of compatible microstates evolve forward toward higher-entropy macrostates.

(4) But time-reversal symmetry implies the mirror claim: by reversing trajectories, one obtains an equally large set of compatible microstates that evolve backward toward higher-entropy macrostates.

(5) So, conditional only on the present macrostate and the dynamics, we should expect entropy to be higher in both temporal directions, not merely toward the future.

(6) Since our records show a lower-entropy past, the standard account, by its own lights, makes the actual arrow — higher entropy toward the future but not toward the past — enormously improbable.

(7) Therefore, the standard probabilistic account does not explain the thermodynamic arrow of time.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what correction to the standard account would block the conclusion?
