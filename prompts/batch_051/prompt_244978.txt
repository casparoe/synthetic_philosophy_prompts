I'm a PhD student in moral and political philosophy, and I keep getting stuck on an asymmetry in our ordinary judgments about loyalty. If a judge rules in favor of her nephew in a contract dispute and says "He's my nephew," we treat that as corruption and say she should be removed from the bench. But if a father can save either his own daughter or three strangers from a fire and he saves his daughter, we don't just excuse it—a lot of people would think a father who didn't prefer his own child was a moral failure. So in public roles we treat loyalty as a bias to be stamped out, but in private life we treat it as a virtue, almost as if the relation itself changes what count as a reason.

William Godwin, in An Enquiry Concerning Political Justice (Book II, Chapter II), pushes this hard. He asks us to imagine a fire where we can save either Archbishop Fenelon, who would benefit the world, or his valet, who happens to be my brother, father, or benefactor. Godwin says the mere fact that the valet is mine—that the pronoun "my" attaches to him—cannot add weight from the standpoint of universal reason, and that any preference for our own relations rests on a kind of moral magic rather than a real reason. I'm not asking for Godwin exegesis. I want to know whether this asymmetry in our ordinary practice is justifiable: is there a coherent account of why partiality to our own is a virtue in private life but a vice in public roles, or should we follow Godwin and say the private cases are just as unjustified? If you defend partiality, I'd like you to answer the Godwinian challenge directly: if the relation "being my brother" doesn't make the brother's welfare count for more from an impartial standpoint, what exactly does it justify, and should that same justification be available to judges and public officials—or if not, why not?
