I am taking a seminar this term on work, collective action, and the law, and I have been turning over a case that seems to sit right at the edge of our course's working definition of a strike. Our definition has four elements. A strike is (1) concerted action by workers, (2) consisting in a temporary stoppage or coordinated withholding of labour, (3) undertaken to press a demand or express a grievance about the terms and conditions under which the work is performed, and (4) directed at whoever effectively controls those terms and conditions — in the standard case, an employer.

Now suppose the following. Several thousand app-based couriers work in a jurisdiction where they are all classified as independent contractors rather than employees. Through a private chat group, they agree to log off the platform for 48 hours and accept no deliveries during that period. They do not picket or obstruct anyone. In public, they describe the action as a coordinated business closure: each courier, they say, is a small business choosing for two days not to enter into contracts. Their demands, however, concern the platform's payment algorithm; they want a higher base rate per delivery and clearer, more stable rules for when the algorithm may reduce pay. They also ask customers to respect the "closure" and avoid placing orders.

Is this a strike under the four elements above? Please work through the elements one by one and say, for each, whether the case satisfies it, violates it, or exposes ambiguity in it. In particular, does it matter that the participants are not employees, and does their self-description as independent businesses settle the question or merely complicate it? Finally, say whether the case shows that any of the four elements — for example, the assumption that there is an employer against whom the action is directed — is doing less real work than it first appears, and whether such an action ought to enjoy the protections usually attached to the right to strike. Where the criteria underdetermine the answer, say so explicitly.
