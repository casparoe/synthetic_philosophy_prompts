In my reading group on self-sufficiency and autarky we've had the same standoff for weeks, and I'm less interested in who's right than in what kind of disagreement it is. We're reading Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics I.7 (1097b), in Rackham's translation. One side reads this passage as evidence that Aristotle's autarky is social through and through: the self-sufficient life includes parents, children, wife, friends, fellow citizens. The other side says the real definition is in the next couple lines, where he defines the self-sufficient as what, standing alone, makes life desirable and lacking in nothing; on that read, the social list is just a concession and Aristotle's ideal is still individual self-completeness. The same split also seems to show up when people argue about national autarky, as if the ambiguity in Aristotle's sentence is doing work far downstream. The passage were stuck on is:

"The term self-sufficient, however, we employ with reference not to oneself alone, living a life of isolation, but also to one's parents and children and wife, and one's friends and fellow citizens in general, since man is by nature a social being."

We keep going round in circles. Diagnose what's actually going on. Is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of flourishing, a verbal disagreement about the word 'self-sufficient', or are the two camps answering different questions—one about Aristotle's text, one about the ideal for persons now? And given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress?
