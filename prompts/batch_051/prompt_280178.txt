You're a philosopher of social science who works on Lewis-style convention and on cultural evolution, so you're exactly the person for this. In my reading group an economist keeps pushing a line that goes roughly like this: social norms evolve the way species do—there's variation, selection, and inheritance, and the norms that survive are the ones that were fit for the social environment, so a norm that has stuck around for a long time is probably doing useful coordination work and we should be slow to scrap it. Shes extended this to norms around queing, tipping, how meetings run, even some gender roles, and a couple of people have started repeating it to shut down any proposal to change how we do things. I want you to take that analogy seriously and audit it rather than just cheer for your own side. First brainstorm the correspondences: what in the norm case is supposed to play the role of the gene, the phenotype, the individual organism, the population, the environment, mutation, drift, inheritance, and fitness? Then go through the places the mapping strains or breaks—for example, where's the genotype/phenotype distinction, what's the unit of selection, can norms get copied with low fidelity and still count as the same norm, what even counts as reproduction for a norm, and how much of norm persistence is just conformism or path dependence rather than fitness. Then tell me how much of the economist's conclusion actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the species analogy is carrying more than it should, say whether there's a better analogy, or whether the thing is better handled by the Lewis-style picture of arbitrary coordination equilibria kept alive by precedent. I'm not after a book report; I want the mapping table and the places it snaps.
