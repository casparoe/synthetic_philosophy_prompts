Next week I have to teach a fifty-minute class on Abelard's ethics to first-year undergrads—specifically his claim that sin is in the consent, not in the act. Don't give me the lecture: I want you to think like a teacher and plan how I should sequence it. You're an expert in medieval ethics. The students will walk in assuming actions and consequences are what matter, and probably that 'sin' is just a religious word for 'bad deed.' I need them to come away able to hold three things at once: a passing urge or inclination isn't yet a sin (what Abelard calls vice); the sin is in consent, where consent is the mind's settling to do the thing, not merely noticing that it's tempting; and once there is consent, the outward act adds nothing to the guilt, while doing the deed without consent isn't what makes it a sin. Before you settle on a sequence, brainstorm the confusions these three moves will generate. Then lay out the explanatory arc: what has to be nailed down before what, the one carrying example for the whole session and what it must and mustn't do, thier objections in the order they'll raise them, and where in the sequence to head each one off. I'm especially worried about 'so he thinks thought-crime is as bad as murder?' and 'why did we just make it all about the mind when people get hurt?' End with one question I'd ask at the very end to see whether they've got why Abelard thinks the moral weight is all in the mind rather than just memorized the slogan, and say explicitly what I should leave out at this level.
