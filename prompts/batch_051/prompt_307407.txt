I have no training in this area, so I will describe in plain terms a pattern I keep noticing while trying to design a fair voting procedure for a cooperative I belong to. I want to know whether this pattern has an established name and, more importantly, where its exact boundaries lie.

The pattern is this. Suppose there are three candidates, and each voter submits a ranking of all three. I have tried several standard ways of turning those rankings into a single winner: the method where the candidate with the fewest first-place rankings is removed and those ballots pass to the next-ranked candidate; the method where each pair of candidates is compared one against another; and a points system in which a first-place ranking is worth more than a second-place ranking. In each of these, I can find cases where one voter obtains a final outcome she prefers by submitting a ranking that does not match her actual preferences. For instance, a voter whose favorite cannot win may put a tolerable candidate first, and the extra support so transferred can make that candidate finish ahead of a candidate the voter dislikes. Or a voter may push a strong but disliked candidate down below candidates she actually cares little about, lowering the strong candidate's score enough to change the result. What unsettles me is not that any one of these rules has a quirk, but that the same opening reappears no matter how I modify the procedure, provided that the method uses rankings, gives no single voter a special role, elects exactly one winner, and leaves every candidate eligible in principle.

My question is whether this phenomenon is a single recognized thing with a name, or merely a loose family of flaws that happen to show up in the methods I have tried. If it has a name, I want more than the name; in particular, I want to know whether there is a difference between the loose fact that a voter can sometimes gain by misrepresenting her preferences and a stronger, unavoidable version of that fact, and how the two are related. I want the phenomenon carved precisely: under exactly what conditions is the problem guaranteed, and under exactly what conditions does it disappear? Is it confined to procedures that use rankings, or does the same or an analogous problem appear when voters assign scores to candidates, or when they may approve as many candidates as they like? Does the problem depend on there being a single winner, or does it survive when the goal is to choose a committee? Does allowing the procedure to use a random element, such as a lottery drawn after the votes are in, change the matter? And are there broad, recognizable conditions under which the problem is known not to arise even with three or more candidates and a single winner—for example, when the voters' views can be arranged along a single line from left to right? I am not asking which voting method to adopt, and I am not asking for a history of the idea. I am asking how to understand the phenomenon as one thing with a definite shape rather than as a pile of accidental counterexamples.
