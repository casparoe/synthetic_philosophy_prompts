I cover political ideas for a magazine and was at a talk on civil disobedience last week. Afterward I hung around and caught two people, a lawyer and a graduate student I think, arguing about one passage in Thoreau's Resistance to Civil Government—the essay now usually called Civil Disobedience. Thoreau there says something like: if an injustice is just part of the necessary friction of the machinery of government, let it be; maybe it will wear smooth, and in any case the machine will wear out. But if the injustice is such that it requires you to be the agent of injustice to another person, then break the law and let your life be a counter-friction to stop the machine.

The arguement went roughly like this:

(1) The lawyer said the passage gives a clear limiting condition: the duty to break the law is triggered when the state tries to make you do the unjust thing yourself, not when the state is unjust in general. Paying a tax that might fund a war is different from being conscripted to fight in it.

(2) The graduate student responded that this distinction is less stable than he thinks. In a modern state, nearly everything you do—working, spending, paying taxes, obeying regulations—supplies the state with resources and legitimacy, so much that a person's normal life is a continuous act of agency in the machine Thoreau describes.

(3) The lawyer shot back that this is exactly the slide Thoreau warns against when he says the machine will wear out if you let it. If every citizen who disapproves of some policy becomes an agent of it merely by paying taxes, then there is no difference between accomplice and bystander, and everyone is permanently faced with a duty to break every law they dislike. That can't be a workable test.

(4) The graduate student said the test doesn't have to be about "every law you dislike": it can be about whether sharing in the ordinary economies of the state makes you complicit in a specific, serious wrong. The real problem with the lawyer's view is that it treats "agent" as only direct formal causation, whereas ordinary language and law recognise being an accessory or an enabler. Thoreau's counter-friction line, she said, is meant to name a stance toward the whole machine, not just the parts that would force your hand.

Maybe I'm misremembering some wording, but that's the shape of it. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both parties are missing.
