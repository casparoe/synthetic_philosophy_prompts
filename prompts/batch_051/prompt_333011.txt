I want to run an asymmetry past you and see whether you'd keep it. My collegue will happily say that Aristotle was wrong about physics, and that in 2400 our physics will be wrong too—no big deal, that's just science. But mention that in 2400 people will be morally better than us, the way we now think we're morally better than a nineteenth-century factory owner who worked ten-year-olds, and he gets defensive. It's apparently fine for the future to correct our factual beliefs, but not our moral beliefs. And he's not unusual; I see the same one-way attitude all over the place. Is there a principle that generates that pattern? I can think of a few candidates—morality isn't aimed at truth, you can't judge one era's moral code by another's, moral change isn't cumulative, the future's morality isn't knowable now—but each one costs something. Take the strongest candidate, test it against a person in 1900 who used the same reasoning to deny we'd be better than them, and tell me whether the asymmetry survives or should be dropped.
