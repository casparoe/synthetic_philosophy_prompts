I have been trying to systematize my own judgments about a small set of cases, and I would like help finding the principle behind them, if there is one. All the cases concern the same proposition: that the university library is open until nine this evening. In every case the relevant person's evidence is exactly the same, namely, she read the sign on the library door this morning, which listed Sunday hours as 9 a.m. to 9 p.m. Nothing in any case indicates that the sign is wrong except where noted.

1. Low stakes, first person. I am meeting a friend at the library at 8:30. Having read the sign this morning, I say, "I know the library is open until nine." Verdict: my claim is true; I know.

2. Low stakes, third person. A student with exactly that evidence tells a visitor that the library is open until nine. I would have no hesitation in saying that the student knows. Verdict: true.

3. High stakes for the subject, first person. I must place the only signed copy of a form in the library's after-hours box by 8:45; if the library is in fact already closed, the form is void and a fellowship is lost. My evidence is still only this morning's sign-reading. Verdict: I know the library is open until nine. It may be rational for me to telephone and double-check before staking the fellowship on it, but that is caution, not the absence of knowledge.

4. High stakes for the attributor, third person. I must tell the provost that tonight's reception can be held at the library; if I am wrong, the event collapses and with it a grant I administer. A staff member with exactly the sign-reading evidence says she knows the library is open until nine. Verdict: I find I cannot truthfully report that she knows it. Her basis is adequate for ordinary purposes, but not in this situation.

5. Low stakes, but a specific error possibility. A visitor with exactly that evidence says she knows the library is open until nine. Nothing practical turns on it for either of us. However, I know that last month the Sunday hours were changed by a taped notice that was sometimes left up after the change had been rescinded; I have no current reason to think that has happened now. Verdict: I still find myself reluctant to say that the visitor knows. Her evidence now seems too thin, even though no practical stakes have changed.

6. High stakes for the subject, low stakes for everyone else. A junior researcher with exactly that evidence says she knows the library is open until nine; her visa renewal depends on handing in a document there by 8:45. I am a disinterested observer with no stake in whether she is right. Verdict: I would say that she knows. Her high stakes rightly make her careful, but they do not by themselves strip her belief of the status of knowledge.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all six verdicts, or close to all six? I am especially struck by the apparent asymmetry between case 4, where the stakes of the person making the knowledge ascription seem to make a difference to whether the subject knows, and cases 3 and 6, where the stakes of the subject seem to make no difference. Case 5 complicates things further by suggesting that stakes are not even necessary for the shift, since a merely noticed possibility of error produces something like the same effect at no practical cost. Is there one account of knowledge and knowledge ascriptions—contextualist, pragmatic-encroachmentist, subject-sensitive invariantist, classical invariantist, or some other view—that gets all six, or close? If no principled account gets all six, tell me which of my verdicts I should give up, and why. State your preferred principle precisely, say which cases motivate each clause of it, and apply it to a further case: I assert in the morning, with only the sign-reading, that I know the library is open until nine; later the practical stakes rise but my evidence does not change. Looking back, was my morning assertion true, and should I now retract it?
