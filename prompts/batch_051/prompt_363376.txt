I have a distinction I keep reaching for whenever the simulation argument comes up and I want someone to tell me whether it's doing real work or just giving my unease a name. I call them lineage sims and sideline sims. A lineage sim is one run by the descendants of the beings being simulated, so the chain nests inside a single base world; a sideline sim is one run by some unrelated civilization, where the beings inside are a side effect rather than the point. My claim is that the strong conclusion 'we are probably simulated' quietly needs both kinds, and they pull apart. Lineage sims give you a usable self-location step—there's a bounded reference class, the base world plus its descendants' sims—but then the conclusion becomes conditional: we're probably simulated if and only if our own descendants are likely to run sims, which is just one of the disjuncts the argument itself leaves open. Sideline sims make the claim unconditional, since any advanced civ can run them, but there the reference class is unbounded and the runners' parameters are free, so the indifference step doesn't pin anything down; 'most observers are in sims' becomes a claim about an ill-defined pile of possible sims rather than a frequency you can sample from. So the argument gets its numbers from sideline sims and its inference rule from lineage sims. I know ancestor simulations are already the standard example, but I think the distinction does more work than that. Tell me the three strongest objections, whether this collapses into the usual ancestor-simulation point, and whether the 'no grip on sideline sims' horn is just the boring unfalsifiability objection everyone already grants.
