In a seminar on the philosophy of biology, we have been stuck on an old objection to the way evolutionary theory gets presented: the claim that evolution favors the fit and eliminates the unfit looks empty if fitness is just defined as whatever tends to survive and reproduce, because then the claim says no more than that organisms that tend to survive and reproduce tend to survive and reproduce. The response that most interests me is the propensity interpretation of fitness, which treats an organism's fitness as an objective probabilistic disposition of its traits in a given environment to produce a certain expected number of viable offspring—a disposition that can fail to be realized on any particular occasion, much as a biased coin can still land against the bias. On this view an organism can correctly be described as fitter than a competitor even if it actually leaves fewer descendants, and fitness stands causally upstream of survival rather than being read off from it. My seminar is split: some hold that this fully breaks the tautology, because fitness now has content independent of actual outcomes; others reply that the cure is verbal, because the only evidence for such propensities is actual reproductive success, which drags us back to vacuity or at least to unfalsifiability.

I would like your actual credence, not a literature survey, and not a refusal of the form that it all depends on how one defines fitness. What is your probability that the propensity interpretation of fitness successfully answers the tautology objection, where success means it gives fitness a non-trivial, empirically contentful meaning rather than a merely verbal repair? Please decompose the answer into the sub-questions that most affect your credence—at least these, plus any others you think belong: (a) How likely is it that the tautology objection identifies a genuine defect in evolutionary explanations as standardly formulated, rather than attacking a caricature of them? (b) Conditional on its being a genuine defect, how likely is it that the propensity interpretation assigns fitness a content genuinely independent of actual survival and reproduction? (c) Conditional on the propensity interpretation, how likely is it that this content can in practice be measured or tested well enough to support empirical claims, rather than remaining a theoretical posit? (d) How likely is it that a different response—for instance, treating fitness as a statistical summary over actual or expected outcomes—is the better repair, so that the proprietary virtues of the propensity view carry little weight? For each term, give a number and a sentence or two of justification; show how the terms combine to yield your overall probability; then identify the term to which your final credence is most sensitive and say what evidence or argument would most plausibly move it.
