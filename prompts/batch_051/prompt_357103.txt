I serve on my university's standing committee on teaching and learning. We are piloting an AI-based tutoring system for first-year mathematics, and the vendor has given us two configurations to choose between. In expert mode, the system arrives with the correct solution procedures fully built in; it diagnoses student errors against that fixed standard and never revises its mathematical competence from the data it sees. In learner mode, the system arrives with only a child-level capacity to acquire mathematical skill, is trained during the pilot on actual student interactions — including their mistakes — gives wrong answers early on, and continuously revises its own procedures as the term goes on. Half the committee insists that a tutor that is still learning its subject has no business teaching, and that only expert mode satisfies our duty of care to students. A colleague replies by invoking Turing's 1950 argument that, rather than trying to program a machine that already has an adult mind, one should build a machine with a child's capacities and then educate it; on her view, learner mode is closer to how human mathematical competence actually develops. Draft the committee's decision memo recommending a concrete course of action — one configuration, or a staged path from one to the other — with an actionable timetable for this term. The memo should reconstruct what Turing's argument does and does not show for the classroom, give an honest treatment of the strongest case for expert mode, and reach a bottom line the committee can vote on next week.
