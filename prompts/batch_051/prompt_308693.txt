Article IV of our academy's 1694 charter reads: 'No problem shall be accounted solved unless its solution can be performed by the ruler and compass alone; and every solution depending upon a line produced by mechanical motion is void.' This week a member has submitted, not a solution, but what he calls a new object: the quadratrix. He defines it exactly as the line traced by the intersection of a radius rotating uniformly about one corner of a square with a segment descending uniformly along one side of the square, and he stresses that no measurement, estimation, or approximation enters the definition. He then asks the academy to register the quadratrix as a legitimate geometrical object in its own right, observing that once the curve is admitted, the quadrature of the circle can be obtained from it. Has he escaped the charter by submitting an object rather than a solution? More precisely: does Article IV permit the academy to register the quadratrix, and would a later solution that uses the curve to square the circle fall under the prohibition? Argue the matter from (a) the words of the clause, (b) its purpose, (c) what the drafters would most plausibly have said if asked directly whether a curve defined entirely by exact, uniform motions could be admitted as an object even if it cannot be drawn by ruler and compass, and (d) what a reasonable member of the academy would take the clause to require. Say which of these approaches should control the decision, and why.
