I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in decision theory for philosophy students — about 16 people, one 75-minute session — and we are about to cover Good's theorem: the result that, for a single expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization, free and reliable information has nonnegative expected value, because the agent can always ignore it. Last year I proved the theorem on the board and the students found it either obvious or trivial, while missing the substantive assumptions doing the work (a fixed action set, a single agent, and a decision that does not itself change the environment or what other agents believe). This year I want the theorem to be felt before it is named, and then undercut by the group's own play. Design a two-stage classroom activity built around a market-entry framing: a firm deciding whether to enter a market after receiving a report about whether demand is good or bad.

In the first stage each student is a solitary firm and may freely draw a private, perfectly reliable demand report before choosing Enter or Wait; this stage should make vivid that the report is weakly valuable in expectation. In the second stage students pair up with a rival who observes the first firm's decision (but not the report) before deciding whether to enter; this stage should be arranged so that an informed first mover is reliably worse off, on average, than a first mover who is denied the report, because using the information leaks it and the rival adapts. The discovery should emerge from the round-by-round scores, not from the facilitator announcing it. Include concrete rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that needs no real money — with numbers chosen so that the two stages cleanly produce their intended results, a demand-state prior of 50/50, and roles rotated halfway so everyone experiences being both the first mover and the rival. Give me a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps the first stage to the proof strategy behind Good's theorem and the second stage to the theorem's conditions — private information, a fixed single-agent decision problem, and no strategic leakage through the action — before letting the students reconstruct the theorem in their own terms.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect at least that students will conflate ex post regret with ex ante value, and that an informed player who draws bad news will try entering anyway to fake confidence — and how to handle each failure without lecturing the insight away. Be frank, too, about which feature of Good's theorem this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself afterwards. Please write the answer in American English.
