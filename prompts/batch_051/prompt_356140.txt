I've been sitting in on a moral psychology seminar and we keep circling the same arguement about the WEIRD problem, and it's starting to feel less like a discussion than a loop. On one side are people who say the cross-cultural record is basically fatal for moral objectivity: if what reads as a clear moral violation in one society is treated as etiquette, ritual, or just personal taste in another, then our own moral judgments start to look like local custom rather than like they're tracking anything real. They lean on examples like fairness violations showing up as strongly moralized in Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic populations but not everywhere, and on how even supposedly obvious norms shift around a lot. The other side says this proves much less than it sounds like. They point out that every culture seems to have some norms around harm, reciprocity, and incest, however differently they're elaborated, and that variation in moral codes no more shows there's no moral fact of the matter than disagreement about cosmology shows there's no fact about the universe. But noone has actually moved in weeks, and each side seems to think the other is making an elementary mistake.

My working theory is that this isn't really an empirical dispute about how much variation there is, but a conceptual dispute about what moral objectivity would have to be: the first side implicitly takes objectivity to require something like broad agreement across actual human societies, while the second side takes it to require only that some moral claims be true independent of anyone's attitudes, with ordinary disagreement dismissed as noise. That would explain why piling up more anthropology wouldn't help.
