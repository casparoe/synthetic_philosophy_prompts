I work in an applied mathematical field, and over the years I have drifted into a policy that now shapes how I read mathematics. When a theorem is proved by classical, non-constructive means—when the proof assumes that no solution exists and derives a contradiction, or applies the law of excluded middle to an infinite family of cases—I find that I do not form the same attitude as my colleagues. I treat the result as showing something like that our formal theory tolerates the supposition, or that the proposition is refutation-stable. What I do not do is count myself as knowing that the object exists. This began after a collaborator built a computational method on an abstract existence-and-uniqueness theorem; the theorem was perfectly valid, but it gave no route to the object, and the practical project stalled. Since then I have split my acceptance into two tiers: I am willing to use non-constructively proved facts in derivations, but I refuse to place them in the same epistemic box as theorems that come with a construction or an algorithm. Lately, colleagues have told me I am simply an intuitionist, and I am not sure they are wrong. But I also suspect the policy may commit me to something less than the full intuitionist picture—the view that mathematical objects are mental constructions and that the law of excluded middle is not generally valid.

I am less interested in being told which of logicism, formalism, and intuitionism is correct than in having the policy itself examined. A logicist might say that existence follows from definitions and logical principles whether or not I can build the object; a formalist might say I have merely switched which formal game I am willing to take seriously. Is a two-tier attitude of this kind defensible as a way of regulating mathematical belief, and could it be held by a logicist or a formalist for their own reasons, or is it intuitionism all the way down? When is the policy reliable, and when does it collapse? In particular, I want to be able to tell from the inside whether the policy tracks a real epistemic distinction—between possessing a route to a mathematical object and merely knowing that its nonexistence would break the system—or whether I have generalized from one disappointing collaboration into a bias against valid mathematics whose conclusions I am nevertheless prepared to use. If the policy is indefensible as it stands, I would like to know whether there is a nearby policy that keeps the caution without treating non-constructive proof as proof of something lesser.
