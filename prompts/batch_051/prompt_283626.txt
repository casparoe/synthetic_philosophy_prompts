Ive been trying to actually write down my considered view on whether generations are real kinds, and I want a consistency audit rather than a summary of positions. Here's the package I've ended up with:

(1) Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z, etc. are real social kinds, not natural kinds — real in roughly the way the working class is real: people identify with them, institutions sort by them, and the categories loop back into how people act.

(2) But the exact cohort boundaries are arbitrary and partly marketing artifacts. I don't think there's a fact of the matter about whether Gen Z starts in 1997 or 2001.

(3) That vagueness doesn't bother me; I don't think a kind needs sharp edges to be real.

(4) Some cohorts do have a real, event-defined core: people who were in high school when COVID hit, or old enough to remember 9/11, seem to share formative experiences that aren't just a function of being young at the time.

(5) Still, my default when I see claims about generational psychology — Gen Z is more anxious, Millennials are narcissistic — is that the effect is mostly age or period, not cohort. Most of the time "they're just young" or "everyone changed" beats "this cohort is special."

(6) And yet I also think the labels are partly self-fulfilling: once the anxious-Gen-Z story is in the water, Gen Z-ers self-ascribe anxiety and institutions treat them as fragile, so the label makes itself true on the ground.

Genuinely don't know which of these is the weak joint. Which pairs actually pull against each other and how badly, which only look like they're in tension, and what's the cheapest fix for each real tension? Order them by severity and say what would have to give in each case.
