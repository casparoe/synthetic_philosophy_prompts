I compete in school debating and have come to hold a specific view: treating argumentation as a sport—codified rules, impartial judges, a scoring system, and winning as the immediate aim—would improve the quality of reasoning itself, not merely the number of people willing to argue or the enjoyment they get from it. The claim under scrutiny is that sport-style competition makes arguments better: more truth-conducive, better calibrated, and more rigorous. What would count against this? I would like the evidence and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, drawn from what is known about competitive formats, from the logic of scoring incentives, or from the psychology of reasoning. I would also like to know what a defender of my position should honestly concede counts against it—for example, if contests reward persuasion over accuracy, or if assigned positions teach debaters to rationalise rather than reason. Please finish with an honest assessment of whether the view risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualifications, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Please write your answer in British English.
