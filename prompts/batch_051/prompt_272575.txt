As a novelist, I keep running into an argument from a physicist friend who works with Pearl's causal hierarchy and the do-calculus, and I cannot find the exit. My own emerging view is that historical fiction presupposes objective causal structure in history; his argument seems to show that Pearl's framework makes such structure disappear. Here it is.

(1) On Pearl's causal hierarchy, a claim of the form "X caused Y" is determinate only relative to a particular structural causal model: a set of variables, a directed graph, structural equations, and a defined do-operator.

(2) For any unique historical event—say, the fall of Constantinople in 1453—there is no canonical structural causal model. One can model the same event using different variables and different equations, all compatible with the known evidence.

(3) Counterfactual and actual-causation claims computed within a model have truth values only relative to that model.

(4) Therefore, if there is no canonical model, then whether a particular unguarded postern gate caused the city's fall depends on an arbitrary modelling choice.

(5) If a claim's truth value depends on an arbitrary modelling choice, it is not an objective historical fact.

(6) But historians and historical novelists cannot do without the idea that some such claims are objective historical facts; otherwise every causal statement about the past would be no more factual than a plot choice.

(7) Hence Pearl's framework implies that the causal claims of serious history and historical fiction are factitious.

I would most like to keep (6), and I suspect the slide is somewhere between (2) and (4), but I need a precise diagnosis: which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
