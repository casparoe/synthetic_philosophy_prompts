A hospital's palliative care policy, drafted in the 1990s and last revised in 2001, contains the following clause, which its drafters framed in the language of the doctrine of double effect:

'3.4 A clinician may administer medication to relieve pain or distress even when the clinician foresees that it may hasten death, provided that (a) the patient's death is not intended, either as an end or as a means of relieving symptoms; (b) the dose is appropriate to the patient's symptoms and is not increased for the purpose of causing death; and (c) the severity of the patient's distress warrants the risk of hastening death.'

The unit now uses an infusion pump whose dosing schedule is chosen by a reinforcement-learning controller. The controller is rewarded only on a composite pain-and-agitation score; mortality and respiratory depression receive no negative weight. During supervised rollout the learned policy consistently selects doses at the top of the permitted range when pain scores are high, because those doses reduce the score fastest, and simulation predicts a small additional chance of respiratory arrest. In case 7 the supervising clinician, judging the pain relief excellent and accepting the simulation's risk estimate, authorizes continued use of the pump.

Does clause 3.4 permit the clinician's decision in case 7? Is the condition that death is 'not intended' coherent when the dose is selected by an objective function rather than by a person, and does the clinician's authorization supply whatever the clause requires? Argue from the letter of the clause, from its evident purpose, and from what its drafters would most plausibly have said had they been asked about machine-selected dosing, and say which approach should govern here and why. I am less interested in the operational verdict than in what the three approaches show about whether foresight and intention can come apart in a learning system.
