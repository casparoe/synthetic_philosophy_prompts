I run a weekly deduction game night at my flat, and because I wrote the group's rulebook I've somehow ended up as the rules judge. Our Rule 4 says: "A player may not ask a question that takes for granted anything that has not already been established and agreed to as true in the game." We added it to stop people asking things like, Since you have the red card, who did you pass it to?, before anyone had actually shown that the person had the red card.

This week a player asked another: "When did you stop signaling to your partner?" At that point the second player having a partner, let alone signaling, was nowhere on the established-facts board, and the question was basically the first time it had come up. The second player objected and pointed at Rule 4. The asker said they were just asking a question, not asserting anything, and that "whether or not you were signaling" is not literally stated by "when did you stop", so the rule doesnt reach it.

What I can't get past is that we drafted Rule 4 while looking at explicit lead-ins like "since you have the red card," not single words. "Stop" and "your partner" were doing a lot of silent work in that question. To make it worse, a third player later added "the second player has been signaling to a partner" to the board, and most of the table just went along with it for the rest of the round. So now I have two disputes in one: did the original question violate Rule 4 at all, and did the table quietly accommodating the claim afterwards cure it or not?

I want a ruling that works through what the plain words of the rule say here, what the purpose of the rule says, and what we would have said if someone had raised this exact case when we wrote it. And if that's different again, what a reasonable player at the table would have understood the rule to allow. Then tell me which of these should actually govern us going forward, and why. Also be honest about whether there's a coherent reading of the rule on which a question can't "take for granted" anything, since questions aren't assertions, or whether that's just clever sophistry. Could the rule reach something like "stop" or "your partner"; and could silence after the fact ever count as agreement?
