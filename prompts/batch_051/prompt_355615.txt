I have been reading rationalist and rationalist-adjacent forums for years, and I have watched Chesterton's fence turn from a vivid metaphor into something close to a badge of affiliation: one side invokes it to block any change it dislikes, and the other side dismisses it as a cover for status-quo bias. The underlying question is Burkean — how much authority should the present generation grant to arrangements it did not choose and cannot fully justify? I would like to see the principle operationalised and stress-tested against a case.

Suppose we say that an institutional practice counts as a genuine Chesterton fence, deserving of presumptive preservation while its rationale remains obscure, just in case it satisfies the following five criteria:

1. Longevity through turnover: the practice has been maintained by people who did not create it, across at least one real changing of the guard.
2. Lost or contestable rationale: after consulting whatever records survive, a competent observer cannot reconstruct the specific reason the practice was introduced; at best, they can infer a generic rationale that would also justify many similar practices.
3. Asymmetric reversibility: if the forgotten reason is real and the practice is dropped, the resulting damage would be severe and difficult to undo, while the cost of keeping the practice is comparatively modest.
4. Non-isolability: the contribution of the practice cannot be cleanly tested apart from the system in which it is embedded.
5. Practitioner deference: the people with direct responsibility continue to act as though the practice is protecting them from something, even though they cannot say exactly what.

Now take this case. A widely used open-source project has followed a rule since its earliest days that no patch may be merged unless the author has run the full automated test suite twice, once in the usual order and once in reverse, and pasted both outputs into the review thread. The reverse run roughly doubles the cost of automated testing, and contributors describe it as the most irritating rule in the project. Every maintainer who remembered the origin of the rule has left; the surviving explanation is only that some predecessor insisted on it after an incident whose details have been lost from the archives. Two contributors once ran a fork for six months without the reverse run and observed no order-dependent failures, but the senior maintainers refused to draw any conclusion from this, arguing that order-dependent failures are exactly the sort that appear rarely and only under conditions a fork does not reproduce.

Does the reverse-order test rule qualify as a genuine Chesterton fence according to the five criteria above? Work through each criterion explicitly, marking it as satisfied, violated, or underdetermined by the description. Then say whether the definition, the case, or both need to be adjusted. Please write your answer in British English.
