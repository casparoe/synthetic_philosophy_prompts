ok so ive been going down a bongard problem rabbit hole recently — the little puzzles where you see two panels of six simple line drawings each, the six on the left all share some hidden concept that the six on the right are supposed to lack, and you have to recover the rule. (That description is the general idea; no need to reproduce any actual published Bongard problem, and since the post will be text-only you'll have to make up and describe any panels verbally.) what's been bugging me is that people seem to solve these in wildly different ways and never notice, because the puzzles are underdetermined: "all the left ones are roughly triangular," "all the left ones have exactly three convex angles," and "all the left ones are closed shapes with fewer than four corners" can all be true of the same panel, and its not obvious any one of them is the right one. so I want to make one of those "which position do you actually hold" quiz posts for a rationalist-adjacent blog audience, on few-example concept induction: what's actually going on when someone generalizes a new concept from a handful of positive and negative cases, not what labels they'd endorse if you asked directly.

The quiz should sort people by what they say about concrete cases, so no question can just ask "do you think concepts are similarity-based" or "are you a Bayesian." The positions you're sorting toward, as the leaves: (a) rule/definition — concepts are necessary-and-sufficient conditions, generalization is just applying the rule; (b) exemplar/prototype similarity — new items get compared to stored examples and feature weights, and membership is graded; (c) Bayesian simplicity/Occam — there's a prior over candidate rules that penalizes complexity, and the license to generalize runs through posterior inference; (d) theory-based — background knowledge (what's a natural kind, which features are likely causal rather than accidental) does most of the work; (e) pragmatic/task-relative — the right generalization depends on what the concept is for, so there's no context-free "the rule"; (f) skeptical/underdetermination — the examples are compatible with too many rules and there's no fact of the matter absent some stipulation about the poser's intent. If you think two of these collapse into each other, merge them and say why.

Cases that have to show up in the questions: at least one made-up Bongard-style case, described in words, where the same panels support non-equivalent rules at different levels of specificity; a novel-word one-shot case in the spirit of the blicket experiments (a made-up object or animal gets a made-up label once, and then we ask what else gets the label); a grue-style case where a gerrymandered or time-indexed rule fits all observed instances but makes crazy predictions about new ones; and a case where surface similarity and background theory point opposite ways (the dull-colored mushroom from a known-deadly species versus the brightly colored lookalike is the classic shape of it).

Format: 7-9 questions, each a concrete case with 2-4 plain-language answer options, then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves. Build the questions from the logical space first — the axes should be genuine forks like whether the hidden rule is determinate, whether membership is crisp or graded, whether simplicity earns you any extra license, whether the concept's purpose changes the generalization, and whether surface features or background theory win. Don't do one question per label. Note any answer combinations that no listed position occupies, and say whether that's because the combo is incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the quiz can't actually seperate even though people talk about them as different positions.

Audience is rationalist blog readers who are okay with ML-flavored examples but may not know the cogsci literature, so define any technical words inline or avoid them in the questions (the scoring key can be a little more jargony). No citations or bibliography anywhere — everything should stand on the cases. American English.
