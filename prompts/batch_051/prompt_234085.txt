Exam question: "Explain what it means to say that two options are incommensurable, and how this differs from their being incomparable. Does incommensurability imply that neither option is better than the other? Does it imply that choosing either is rationally permissible?"

Student answer: "Incommensurability means there is no common cardinal unit in which the values of the two options can both be expressed. That is weaker than incomparability: two options are incomparable when no positive comparative relation holds between them at all, so there is no fact of the matter about whether one is better, worse, or equal. People often conflate the two, but you can reject a common measure while keeping ordinal comparisons. I can think the novel is better than the film without being able to say by how many units. So incommensurability as such does not rule out one option's being better than the other.

Where it bites is in hard choices. In a hard choice, neither option is better, and since there is no common scale, they cannot be strictly equal either; that would require a shared measure. So the two options must be incomparable, and Raz introduces a fourth comparative relation, 'parity', to describe this: options on a par are ones where there is no fact of the matter about which is better. That is why hard choices feel rationally underdetermined. And if a choice is rationally underdetermined, then either option is rationally permissible — that is just what underdetermination means. Chang's small-improvement argument makes the same point from the other side: slightly improving one of the options never makes it better than the other, which shows the options are on a par."
