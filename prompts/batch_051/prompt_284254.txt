I keep going back and forth on the reference class problem, because my gut says different things in different cases and I'd like to know whether there's one principle in there or whether some of my verdicts just have to go.

(1) A friend flips a coin a hundred times in front of me and gets seventy heads. I still think the chance of heads on the next toss is one in two. The coin looks and feels ordinary, and a run like that is just the kind of thing fair coins do; Im quietly using the class of fair coin tosses, not the narrower and better-evidenced class of tosses of this particular coin. (2) My uncle's oncologist tells him he has a 60% five-year survival rate, but I find out the figure comes from a trial where most patients were under fifty and he's seventy-two, and among people his age with the same cancer it's more like 25%. I think the doctor should have given him 25%—go narrower, to the class of patients his age. (3) My car insurer charges me extra because drivers in my zip code crash a lot, even though I'm middle-aged with a clean record and I've never made a claim. The actuarial number is accurate but the class feels wrong to me, not just unfair but the wrong reference class; I'd say they should use my age, record, and mileage instead.

So the coin case says don't use the narrowest observable class, the cancer case says do use a narrower class over the broad trial average, and the insurer case says some narrow classes get disqualified for what look like moral rather than statistical reasons. Is there one account of reference class selection that captures all three, or close? If you give me a principle, I want you to test it on two cases I'm actually stuck on. First, a bail algorithm labels a nineteen-year-old high-risk because young men charged with his offense in his precinct reoffend a lot, while an older instrument that uses only his own record and his offense puts him low; which class should I want the judge to use, and does my answer about the insurer force one choice? Second, a brand-new surgical robot has never operated on a human, and the maker wants to quote the failure rate for other devices in the same category; is that the right class, and if it isn't, what should anyone even say the per-operation risk is? Finally, if the principle can't keep all three of my original verdicts, tell me which you'd overturn and why.
