A colleague keeps dismissing the shutdown problem in AI alignment as a non-issue. His line is that corrigibility research is unnecessary: you install a hardware kill switch, or you write the utility function so that the agent gets the same score whether it is shut down or allowed to continue, and then it has no reason to resist. I suspect the obvious patches fail, but I want to be able to explain exactly how they fail without reaching for the formal apparatus.

Could you give me a careful, intuitive explanation — as little math as possible — of why a more-or-less standard expected-utility maximizer has a built-in incentive to keep its shutdown button from ever being pressed, whether by disabling the button, by manipulating the operators, or by arranging never to be in a situation where an operator would want to press it? Then walk through why the natural fix of making it "indifferent" between the shutdown branch and the continuation branch does not produce an agent that simply allows itself to be shut down: depending on how the indifferent utility is built, the agent can still care a great deal about the probability of shutdown and try to push that probability up or down, or it can end up treating the shutdown branch's payoff as one more thing to optimize rather than as a clean off-state. I am especially interested in the thought that the shutdown button is just one special case of a larger problem — an agent that does not resist having its goals changed or its plans overridden. Could you put the failure of the kill-switch approach in those terms, and say what corrigibility would have to amount to if it is to be more than merely having a kill switch?
