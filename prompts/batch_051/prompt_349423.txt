I've been trying to understand the whole selfish gene thing for a while now, mostly through popular articles, and im starting to think the people who explain it are oversimplifying. A while back I read a long explainer in a science magazine (can't remember the author) that summarized Dawkins and Williams roughly like this. Evolution makes most sense from the gene's point of view: bodies are just throwaway survival machines that genes build to get themselves copied. So when an animal does something that looks selfless, like a meerkat on sentry duty or a bird giving an alarm call, what's actually happening is that its genes are protecting copies of themselves in the animal's relatives, and the warm feeling of concern is just the soft outer layer on top of that. Altruism is therefore always secretly selfish, just at the genetic level. The article also said group selection got demolished here, since selection never works for the good of the species and talk of group-level selection is only a sloppy way of saying whichever genes do best. Then it gave the critics' side: they say the whole picture is too reductionist, that a gene never does anything on its own, that the organism is a coherent whole, and that there is no such thing as a gene for a behaviour. I half-believe both sides, which makes me suspect the article is flattening something, but I don't know enough to say where. Which parts would a careful defender of the gene's-eye view actually sign off on, which parts would they push back on, and which parts are unfair to the critics? And once the details are fixed, does the punchline that altruism is secretly selfish and group selection was just a conceptual muddle still hold up?
