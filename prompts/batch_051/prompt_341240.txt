A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about whether the similarity relations in a static embedding space amount to a theory of meaning, or merely to a model of word co-occurrence. I left the argument needing a single, clean case to make my position precise, and I want your help building it to specification.

You are a semanticist who works at the boundary between distributional semantics and truth-conditional or intensional semantics. Take your time. Begin by brainstorming at least three candidate cases and briefly say why you reject two of them; then present the one you keep and run the verification.

Construct a pair of ordinary English content words, A and B, of the same syntactic category, meeting all of the following specifications:

1. In a standard static embedding space trained distributionally on a large general corpus (the word2vec, GloVe, or fastText family), A and B are very close: each appears in the other's top nearest neighbours, and their cosine similarity is high enough that an embedding-based synonymy test would call them near-synonyms.

2. A truth-conditional semanticist should say A and B are not near-synonyms: there is at least one simple, non-idiomatic declarative sentence in which swapping A for B changes truth conditions, and the difference is not merely one of register, connotation, emphasis, or implicature.

3. The case must not rely on familiar shortcuts. A and B must not be sentiment or scalar antonyms (good and bad, hot and cold, always and never), must not be proper names or jargon drawn from one technical field, and must not be rare or contrived words. The divergence should come from structure in their distribution, not from obvious opposition.

Then demonstrate that the case works, step by step:

- State A and B, the simple test sentence, and exactly what swapping does to its truth conditions.
- Give the distributional reason the two words end up close: the shared collocates, syntactic frames, or topical contexts that pull their vectors into the same region, and explain in qualitative vector-geometric terms why that yields high cosine similarity.
- Identify the single structural feature of distributional semantics that does the work here, the feature that makes distributional similarity and truth-conditional semantic equivalence peel apart, and explain why it drives the result.
- Say how a defender of embedding-based semantics would resist your diagnosis: which standard reply, such as the claim that embedding similarity tracks substitutability rather than synonymy, or that static representations conflate dimensions a downstream classifier could separate, is strongest in this case, and how far it succeeds.
- Finally, explain what a contextual embedding model, using a transformer's contextual token representations, would plausibly do to this pair: separate them again, fuse them further, or nothing stable, and what that tells us about which aspects of meaning are recoverable from which geometries.

End with one honest sentence on what this case shows, and what it does not show, about the claim that meaning is geometric.

If, after a genuine attempt, you conclude the specifications are jointly unsatisfiable, do not stop there. State exactly which constraints conflict, give the nearest satisfiable weakening, and run the demonstration on that variant.
