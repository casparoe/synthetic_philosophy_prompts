A friend and I argued late into last night about the outside view. I defended the standard argument, which I have always taken to be the most workable form of epistemic modesty: whenever a prediction can be placed in a reference class of broadly similar past cases, the distribution of outcomes in that class should dominate the forecast, because the special features of the present case, however vivid, make forecasters systematically overconfident. My friend granted the history but pressed an objection I could not shake. She said the argument was built for, and only earns its keep in, settings where reference classes are plentiful and stable — construction projects, mergers, software releases, military procurement. The cases she actually worries about are ones for which no settled reference class exists: a first mission of its kind, the macro trajectory of advanced AI, a technology with no historical analogue, a novel intervention in a climate system. In those cases, she argued, somebody must first decide which past cases count as similar, and that decision does all the work; the outside view then smuggles the inside view back in under another name. I want to know whether the outside-view argument survives this development. Specifically: which premise of the argument does her objection bear on, and can that premise be repaired rather than abandoned — for example by decomposing a novel problem into precedent-bearing subproblems, by combining several reference classes, or by conditioning on structural features of a case rather than surface similarity? Give an honest bottom line: does the argument survive, need repair, or is it dead for the class of cases she cares about? Do not answer with a survey of the literature; take the argument apart.

End with a final line of exactly this form:
\answer{<survives|needs repair|dead>}
