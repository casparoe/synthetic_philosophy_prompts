A few years ago I watched undercover footage from inside a large industrial egg farm, and I have been trying ever since — on my own, since I have no formal background in philosophy — to build a coherent view about suffering. I have argued it through with friends, read scattered online discussions, and gradually settled into a position close to what some people call a suffering-focused ethic or negative utilitarianism, though I arrived at it by my own lights rather than through any particular book. Recently I have begun to suspect that the pieces of this position do not all agree with one another, and I cannot tell which of the disagreements are genuine and which are only apparent. I would like an audit of the whole set of commitments rather than a sympathetic restatement of each one.

Here are the commitments, stated as plainly as I can. (1) The only thing that is bad in itself is suffering: pain, fear, grief, humiliation, boredom, and the like. Happiness and pleasure are good in themselves, but the absence of happiness in a being who never exists is not bad, because there is no one there to be deprived of it. (2) Because of this, there is never an obligation to bring a new person or animal into existence, no matter how happy that life would be; doing so can be permissible, but it is never required. (3) It is wrong to bring into existence a life that will very likely contain more suffering than happiness. (4) When comparing outcomes, happiness has real positive weight: a world with many happy lives can be better than a world with few, other things equal. (5) I regard factory farming as among the worst things human beings have ever done; the suffering involved is so vast that I suspect it may outweigh the value of everything human civilization has achieved, and reducing it should be one of the highest moral priorities. (6) If I had a button that would annihilate every sentient being at once, painlessly, without warning, and with no one left behind to grieve, I would not press it; I believe pressing it would be wrong and that extinction would be a horror rather than a kindness.

When I lay these side by side, I cannot tell which of them genuinely pull against one another, which only seem to, and where the fault line actually runs. I would be grateful for a careful consistency check: identify the real tensions among these commitments, rank them by how severe they are, say what would have to give in each case and what the revision would cost, and then tell me which single change would do the most to make the whole position hang together. I am asking because I want to live by a view I can actually defend, not because I am writing for anyone else. Please do not simply flag every pair that could be disputed; I care more about which tensions matter and why.
