I am running a year-long reading group on the value of negative emotions, and we have spent the past term arguing about whether a life without sadness and grief would, in principle, be better. Two of our most stubborn participants have settled into opposed positions, and I would like you to write the adversarial-collaboration document they should have produced after a good-faith attempt to stop talking past each other.

One participant, a prudential eliminativist about negative affect, holds that negative emotional episodes—sadness, grief, anxiety, guilt—are intrinsic prudential bads: if a safe intervention could remove or prevent them without damaging health, memory, motivation, or social functioning, the rational choice would be to take it, and the resulting life would be better other things equal. The other participant, a constitutive-value theorist about fitting negative emotions, holds that at least some negative emotions are not merely unpleasant accompaniments to loss and care but partly constitute appropriate responsiveness to what one values; a life engineered so that grief never occurs would therefore be impoverished or emotionally dishonest in ways that cannot be captured by how the life feels from moment to moment.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both positions can sign—covering the distinction between fitting and unfitting negative emotions, the badness of chronic or clinical depression and anxiety, the instrumental value of some negative affect for decision-making and empathy, and any other common ground they would acknowledge; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is worded neutrally, with a formulation each side would accept for its own view. Then, for each crux—for example, whether the value of grief is fully captured by its experienced unpleasantness plus its causal consequences, and whether a person can fully love what they have lost without being disposed to suffer its absence—identify a concrete case or intervention both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side would predict about it. Write the entire document in British English.
