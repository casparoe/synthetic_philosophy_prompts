I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am putting together a long feature on the philosophical undertow of the contemporary evidence-based wellness landscape: continuous glucose monitors, algorithmically personalized diets, GLP-1 appetite-suppressing drugs, biohacking, and the moralized food movements that now surround them. While reporting I keep returning to Nietzsche, less because his dietary advice was medically prescient than because he treated questions of nutrition, digestion, climate, alcohol, and bodily regimen with an intensity most philosophers reserve for epistemology. He took the physiological condition of a thinker to be inseparable from the quality of his thinking, and he tied the discipline of the body to self-overcoming, independence of judgment, and the refusal to inherit one's ideals secondhand. The textual record is untidy: interpreters disagree about how literally to take his recommendations and how much was retrospective self-stylization. What seems hard to dispute is that he would have rejected the idea that health is a merely technical problem of optimization, while also rejecting any morality that demands contempt for the body.

For the feature I need a section that projects his considered outlook forward onto three developments he never lived to see: first, wearable self-monitoring and algorithmically personalized diets, which promise to hand over dietary judgment to data; second, GLP-1 receptor agonists, which suppress appetite chemically and deliver weight loss without the daily effort of ascetic discipline; and third, the moralized wing of contemporary plant-based eating, which treats food choices as obligations to animals, climate, or the planet. Please draft that section as a coherent essay for an educated lay readership. It should argue for the most defensible Nietzsche-like verdict on each of the three, flag honestly where the textual materials could be read to pull in different directions, and avoid reducing him to either a wellness guru or a reactionary. Above all, evaluate each development by the standard that seems most central to him: whether it strengthens or weakens the capacity for self-overcoming, understood as a bodily and spiritual discipline, rather than merely whether it makes people leaner, longer-lived, or morally correct.
