I've been turning over a parallel between the Hart–Fuller debate in legal philosophy and the running fight in philosophy of medicine about whether disease is a value-free concept, and I cant tell if it's a real structural parallel or just me pattern-matching. On the positivist side, legal validity is a social fact: a rule is law if it was produced through the system's rule of recognition, and whether it's wicked is a separate question from whether it's law. Fuller's side says that a legal system has an inner morality — generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, congruence between the rules as announced and as applied — and that a regime which systematically flouts all of this is not just bad law but has stopped being law at all. The medical debate looks homologous to me: naturalists about disease think a condition is a disease if and only if there's a biological dysfunction relative to species-typical functioning, a purely descriptive biological judgment with no values built in; normativists reply that a dysfunction only gets into the medical category when it's harmful, and harm is evaluative, so disease can't be picked out without value judgments. In both debates one side says the concept can be identified by a descriptive core, and the other side says there's an ineliminable evaluative threshold hiding in it.

My question is whether this is a genuine identity of structure. If it is, then a solution to one debate should transfer to the other, and I'd like to know what transfers. If it isn't, I want to know exactly where the mapping breaks — maybe because law is an institutional artifact while biological dysfunction is a natural property, or because the value in medicine attaches to harm to the patient while in Fuller it attaches to the enterprise of governing, or something else I'm missing. Also, name something the parallel predicts that would come out false, so I can see where the analogy starts misleading me. And whatever verdict you reach, tell me which argument would most change your mind and where your reasoning is shakiest.
