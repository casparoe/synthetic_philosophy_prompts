I'm drafting a chapter on the design of small expert review panels, and I want a consistency check on a cluster of procedural commitments I've been holding, because I can no longer tell whether they form one coherent view or a set of independent impulses. I know the judgment-aggregation literature describes something like my worry under the heading of the discursive dilemma, but I don't want a restatement of the theorem; I want an audit of these commitments in particular. I hold all of the following. (i) Each substantive question the panel must answer — every factual or evaluative premise, and the final verdict — should be settled by a simple majority vote, question by question, with no member given the authority to synthesize the others' judgments. (ii) Whatever the panel publishes should be deductively tidy: it may not affirm two premises while denying what they jointly entail. (iii) The final verdict should remain answerable to the panel's direct vote on the verdict itself; the panel ought not hand down a verdict that a majority of its members explicitly voted against. (iv) Individual panelists may be perfectly coherent in their own judgments while disagreeing with one another, and no member should be made to revise a view merely so that the group can speak with one consistent voice. (v) The outcome should not depend on how the chair partitions the question — whether the panel votes first on the sub-questions and then on the verdict, or directly on the verdict, or in some other order — because a legitimate procedure should not let the agenda do the deciding.

Please rank the tensions among these five by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious; say which tension is the core fault line and which apparent tension dissolves once you look at it; and state the least costly revision along with what it sacrifices. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
