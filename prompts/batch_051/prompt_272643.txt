I do freelance work that's mostly prompt engineering, so I end up reading a lot of blog posts about what language models are "really" doing when they explain themselves. The thing I keep circling back to is whether the reasoning a model prints out before an answer is the actual process that produced the answer, or more like a cover story it makes up after the fact. I tried to settle my own gut feeling on this by writing down an argument, and now I half think it proves to much and half think it's right. A friend of mine would normally poke holes in this stuff, but their not around right now, so I'm asking here:

(1) A language model writes its output one token at a time, and each token is generated on the basis of the whole context that came before it.
(2) So when a model writes a chain-of-thought and then an answer, the chain-of-thought tokens are part of the context on the basis of which the answer tokens get generated.
(3) If something is part of the context on the basis of which an output is generated, then it is among the causes of that output.
(4) If a string of text is among the causes of an answer a model gives, then that string is part of the model's reasoning for that answer.
(5) So the chain-of-thought a model prints is part of its actual reasoning for the answer it gives.
(6) Therefore chain-of-thought reports are faithful, at least in a causal sense: the model really did use the steps it wrote out.

I keep thinking the trick has to be in (4), maybe in how "reasoning" is being used, but I can't make the objection precise. Is the argument valid as written? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of the key term does it fail? And if it is valid, does it actually address the faithfulness worry people argue about, or does it just win by redefining the word?
