I work as a software engineer, and for the past year I've been thinking about how responsibility gets distributed—and sometimes seems to disappear—inside large organizations. A colleague and I have been using a term we coined, a responsibility sink, to describe a recurring pattern: a role, committee, or process that is formally the point where a chain of decision-making ends, but that is arranged so that no natural person who actually occupies it can see enough and control enough to be personally at fault for the outcome the chain produces. Take a hypothetical company whose pricing model quietly applies surcharges to certain customers in ways no individual designed end to end. The data scientist tunes a loss function; the product manager sets a target metric; the compliance reviewer checks only one narrow legal condition; the support staff field complaints but have no authority to change the model. Each person's slice of the pipeline is defensible in isolation, yet the organization could have arranged the pipeline differently, and it is in a position, collectively, to see and correct the harm it produces. We think this is what people are gesturing at when they say the company knew even though no single employee knew.

Our tentative thesis is that a company is blameworthy—not merely causally involved or unfortunate—when a harmful outcome flows through a responsibility sink that the organization deliberately installed, or at least maintained, while it had the capacity and the occasion to reconfigure its own decision architecture. The blame would attach to the structure itself rather than to any individual mental state, in roughly the way we blame a person for arranging their habits so they never have to notice what they are doing. The sink need not have been built with the harm in mind; it only needs to be the sort of arrangement whose foreseeable effect is that information and authority stay separated.

I can see that this might be nothing more than a vivid metaphor, or that it might already exist under some proper name I don't know. I'd like an honest assessment. Is a responsibility sink a coherent concept rather than just a figure of speech? Does the idea already exist in roughly this form? What are the three strongest objections to the thesis, and is any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? I'm especially interested in where it breaks: for example, does it over-attribute blame to organizations that are merely using a normal division of labor, or does it pretend a company has a unified mind when it plainly doesn't?
