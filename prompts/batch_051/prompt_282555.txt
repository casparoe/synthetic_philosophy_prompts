I'm a journalist who covers moral psychology and the history of ideas, and I'm preparing a piece on oaths: why we still take them, what they add to a bare promise, and what the archaic institution of swearing by the river Styx was doing when it imagined an oath that even the gods could not break with impunity. The reporting has made me realize that my own verdicts about particular oaths, collected below, do not fall into any pattern I can state. I'd like to know whether a single principle captures them, and if not, which of my verdicts I should give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

(1) The Styx oath. In the mythical picture, the gods themselves swear by the Styx, and the oath binds even a god who swore overhastily or now wishes to undo it; a breaker is shut out of the divine company for a span of years. My verdict: the myth's logic is right. An oath does not become optional merely because the swearer has changed their mind about it.

(2) The coerced signature. A hostage signs a solemn undertaking under threat of violence and is later released. My verdict: void. An oath extracted by force creates no obligation.

(3) The marriage vow. Two people vow lifelong fidelity; years later both wish to end the marriage. My verdict: their joint consent dissolves the vow. Keeping them bound against their wills would no longer be fidelity but something closer to captivity.

(4) The promise to the dying. A journalist promises a dying source to get a sealed letter to her daughter; the source dies that night. No one else knows of the promise. My verdict: the obligation survives. The fact that the only person to whom it was made is dead does not release the promisor.

(5) The oath of office. An official swears to uphold a constitution and later becomes convinced that one of its entrenched provisions is unjust. My verdict: she is still bound by the oath; the honest response is to resign the office rather than unilaterally set the oath aside.

(6) The criminal loyalty oath. An initiate swears loyalty to a brotherhood that turns out to be a serious criminal enterprise. My verdict: void. An oath to a wrongful end has no hold on her.

(7) The fraudulently induced pledge. A donor swears a solemn pledge to an institution described to her as an orphanage; it is in fact a political fundraising front. My verdict: void, because she was deceived about what she was swearing to.

(8) The perfunctory courtroom oath. An atheist witness takes the standard oath before testifying, silently regarding the words as an empty formality. My verdict: the oath still binds her in a way a bare assurance from the stand would not. Perjury is worse than ordinary lying, and the oath itself adds that extra wrong even when no deity is invoked.

(9) The solitary vow. A person alone in her kitchen vows to go a year without alcohol; the next morning she regrets it. My verdict: she is still bound, at least as much as if she had made the promise to a friend.

My rough hypothesis is that an oath binds when, at the moment of swearing, a person voluntarily and knowingly performs a public act that licenses others to count on her and to treat a breach as a special kind of failure, and that it fails to bind when the performance is obtained by force or fraud. I can already see the trouble: this says nothing about private promises given to no one or to the dead, and nothing about why a regretted oath to an office should hold while a regretted oath to a wrongful brotherhood should not.

What principle, if any, captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? Please test my rough hypothesis against the cases rather than bending the cases to save it. For each clause of any principle you propose, tell me which case forces it. Then apply your principle to two cases I have not listed: an oath sworn in a language the swearer speaks but does not understand well, and a promise made while temporarily delirious from fever. Finally, tell me which of my listed verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and must be revised or abandoned.

Please end with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing the label or labels of the verdicts from (1)–(9) that, on your account, I should revise or abandon, in order of priority if more than one.
