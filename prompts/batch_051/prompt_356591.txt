I recently retired from a career in physical chemistry and have started sitting in on a university seminar in the philosophy of physics. The instructor has recruited me as a second reader for a short-answer examination, and I would appreciate a close grading of one of the responses, since I am still calibrating my standards for philosophical work of this kind.

The examination question read as follows. Assume the fundamental dynamical laws are, as our best microphysical theories suggest, very nearly time-reversal invariant. How, then, can there be a lawlike thermodynamic arrow of time—an asymmetry in which entropy increases toward the future—and what, if anything, does that arrow explain about our remembering the past rather than the future?

The student's answer read as follows. The underlying dynamics permit both entropy-increasing and entropy-decreasing processes; for every molecular trajectory in which a gas spreads out, there is a time-reversed trajectory in which it contracts. What breaks the symmetry is statistics rather than dynamics. For a given macrostate, the overwhelming majority of compatible microstates evolve toward higher-entropy macrostates, simply because higher-entropy macrostates are vastly more numerous, so a system that starts in a nonequilibrium, low-entropy macrostate will, with probability near one, move toward higher entropy. Boltzmann's H-theorem accordingly required the assumption of molecular chaos, the Stosszahlansatz; Loschmidt's reversibility objection shows that this assumption cannot be derived from the time-reversal-invariant dynamics alone. The missing ingredient is a boundary condition: we postulate that the early universe began in an extremely low-entropy macrostate, the Past Hypothesis. Given that postulate, entropy is overwhelmingly likely to have been lower in the past and higher in the future. This in turn explains the psychological arrow, since forming a memory is itself an irreversible physical process that produces entropy; the direction in which our memories point, toward the past rather than the future, just is the direction of entropy increase. It also follows that the second law holds strictly for any finite closed system, because averaging over all microstates compatible with the current macrostate yields future macrostates of higher entropy.
