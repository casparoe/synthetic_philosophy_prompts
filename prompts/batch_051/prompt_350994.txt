I have been reading the opening of Thomas Hobbes’s *Leviathan* (1651), and I was struck by this passage from the Introduction:

“For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life?”

A friend who works in machine learning thinks this passage settles the question of why an AI would want anything: if life is just a motion of limbs, and an AI is an automaton that moves itself, then its goals are no more mysterious than an animal’s appetites. I suspect he is conflating artificial life with artificial desire, but I cannot articulate the precise conceptual issue. Could you give an example of the central conceptual issue that arises when one tries to extend Hobbes’s account of appetite and endeavour to an AI, without grounding the AI’s wanting in evolution or natural selection? I would like the issue itself laid out, not a survey of positions.

Please write in British English. In your answer, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on motivation and artificial agency, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
