I keep coming back to one passage in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII, chapter 9 (Rackham translation), where he seperates the continent person from the temperate person: "But whereas the self-restrained man has evil desires, the temperate man has none; he is so constituted as to take no pleasure in things that are contrary to principle, whereas the self-restrained man does feel pleasure in such things, but does not yield to it." I want your actual credence, not a both-are-ideals dodge, on this claim: when people today praise "self-control," the character they're picturing is much closer to Aristotle's self-restrained man than his temperate man, and so the thing being admired is closer to successful repression than to virtue. Decompose it for me: the probability that ordinary praise really tracks effortful overriding of recalcitrant desires rather than the absence of such desires; the probability that effortful overriding carries the psychological costs that would make "repression" the fair label and not just a slur; the probability that the temperate ideal is a live option for most adults rather than a fantasy; and any other term you'd add. Give a rough number for each, show how you combine them, and tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to.
