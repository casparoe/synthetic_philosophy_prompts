I'm on my university's student wellness advisory committee, and we're stuck on a fairly mundane but philosophically loaded decision: whether to make a brief mental-health screening questionnaire opt-out rather than opt-in during orientation. The public-health folks point out opt-out gets higher completion rates and catches students who would never proactively seek help; the civil-liberties side says this is paternalism dressed up as choice architecture. The chair wants a decision memo by Thursday, with a concrete recommendation and conditions. I keep coming back to On Liberty, John Stuart Mill, specifically the passage in the introductory chapter where he argues that the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others, and that a person's own good is not a sufficient warrant. That feels like it should settle things against opt-out, but I'm not so sure: the university isn't formally coercing anyone, no one is forced to complete the questionnaire, they just have to uncheck a box. Is a default itself a form of power? Would the harm principle actually permit this because the institution has a legitimate interest in preventing harm to others, like a depressed student's risk of harming themselves or others? Draft the committee's memo: recommend whether to adopt opt-out screening, explain how the Mill passage bears on the question, and don't strawman the strongest argument on the losing side. Also suggest any conditions that would make the policy easier to defend if we approve it. A few committee members are pretty sceptical of nudging in general, so the memo should take their worry seriously rather than just appealing to downstream outcomes.
