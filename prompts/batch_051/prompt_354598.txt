I am a high school student preparing an independent research paper that draws on both philosophy and economics, and I have hit a claim I do not know how to evaluate. The claim is that "better than" is transitive: if option A is better than option B, and option B is better than option C, then option A must be better than option C. Economists and decision theorists often treat this as a minimal requirement of rationality. But I have also been reading about spectrum arguments, in which a long chain of options differs only slightly between neighbors — a little less waiting time, a little more money, a slightly lower risk — so that each adjacent option seems better than the one before, yet the final option seems plainly worse than the first; people want to accept every small step but reject the overall comparison. I cannot tell what the transitivity claim is asserting here: a psychological generalization about how people in fact choose, a normative rule about how they ought to choose, or a stipulation about what we mean by "better."

How would one test a claim like that? Design the strongest test you can for the proposition that "better than" is transitive. Be concrete: say what comparisons or stimuli you would use, what pattern of results would support each of those three readings, and what would count against them. Then be equally concrete about what even a decisive result would not settle — for example, whether intransitive judgments are errors by the subjects or genuine features of what they value, and whether the transitivity axiom is a discovery about values or a convention for representing them. Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who study this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
