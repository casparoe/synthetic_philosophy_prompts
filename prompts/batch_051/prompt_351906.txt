I am a high school student writing a paper for a class on global economic justice, and I keep running into the claim that low-wage factory jobs in poor countries can be both exploitative and, at the same time, the best available option for the people who take them. I would like to stress-test that claim against a concrete hypothetical rather than just restate the familiar arguments.

Here is the scenario. In early 2026 a large European clothing brand, facing a consumer campaign, signs a five-year agreement with one of its supplier factories in an industrial district on the outskirts of Dhaka. Under the agreement, the brand continues to buy the same volume of shirts but at a price high enough to cover a 40 percent increase in the factory's base wage for all sewing-line workers, including new hires; the factory, in turn, agrees to remain open and to keep producing for the full five years. Automating the affected lines is technically possible but slow and expensive, and any major capital investment would require renegotiating the contract price, so the factory is unlikely to automate within the five-year window. The agreement covers only this one factory. The brand's other suppliers in the same district continue to pay the going wage, and there is no shortage of people from rural areas willing to migrate for factory work. Most of the workers are young women who send remittances home and expect to stay in the industry only a few years.

I would like a careful, mechanism-level prediction of how the next five years play out: for the factory's original workers, for people in the surrounding area who might have taken jobs at the old wage, for neighboring factories and their workers, and for the brand. Be explicit about the chain of reasoning, and separate effects that follow from fairly robust incentives — labor supply, turnover, competition for workers, the brand's long-run cost calculations — from effects that depend on assumptions about productivity, job security, or the strength of the activism. Then briefly consider what happens when the contract ends. For your central judgment about whether the original workers are better off, and whether the intervention is an improvement for the people the campaign aimed to help, please say which argument or evidence would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
