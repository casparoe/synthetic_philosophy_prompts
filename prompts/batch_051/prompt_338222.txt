I'm a metaethics PhD student and I keep getting stuck on a version of the wrong kind of reasons case I'd love another set of eyes on. Standard setup: a demon says he'll torture you unless you admire him. Most people's read is that you now have a reason to admire him, but the wrong kind of reason—admiring him out of fear doesn't make him admirable. Here's the variant I can't classify. The demon makes the threat conditional not just on your admiring him, but on your admiring him authentically: the admiration can't be caused by the threat itself, or by any plan or strategy you adopt in response to the threat (he can somehow check the causal history of your mental states). Assume you don't already admire him and there's nothing independently admirable about him. So any move you make to get yourself to admire him—psyching yourself up, taking a pill, whatever—makes the admiration a means to avoiding torture, and that fails the condition. Work through what the object-given/state-given distinction says about the reason this threat generates. Is it still a state-given reason? Does it disappear because the required state can't be brought about by that reason? Does it stop being a reason at all, or does it show the distinction needs a third kind? Then run the same question through a fittingness account, the kind that says the wrong kind of reason isn't a reason to admire but a reason to want to admire or to bring it about that you admire. I swing between thinking the case is trivial (there's just no reason here) and thinking it exposes something fragile in how the distinction is usually drawn.
