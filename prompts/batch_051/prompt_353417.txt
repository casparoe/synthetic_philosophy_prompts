Build me a case where the felt-quality theory and the attitudinal theory of pleasure come apart in the direction that's actually annoying to construct. Felt-quality theory: an episode counts as a pleasure only if it has a distinctive positive felt quality — a hedonic tone, a pleasant sensation, something it's like. Attitudinal theory: an episode counts as a pleasure if the person has a positive attitude toward the experience while they're in the middle of it — they're pleased about it, they want it to continue, they would endorse the experience as it's happening.

Build a single ordinary scene with two experiences, E1 and E2. E1 is the boring part: a pleasure on both theories, no argument about it. E2 is the contested one: it should come out a pleasure on the attitudinal theory and not on the felt-quality theory. So in E2 the person has the positive attitude toward the ongoing experience, but there's no distinctive positive felt quality to point to — not even a subtle glow of satifsaction. An honest felt-quality theorist has to call E2 something else — a merely wanted episode, a meaningful strain, a useful discomfort — but not a pleasure.

Constraints: all ordinary life. No drugs, no electrodes, no brain stimulation, no exotic neurology. The positive attitude in E2 can't be relief after something stops, and it can't be pride in a result that comes later; it has to be aimed at the experience while it's happening. Don't reach for tragic art either — "beautiful sadness" is exactly where the felt-quality theorist will claim there's a hedonic quality hiding in the aesthetic experience. And the case shouldn't be grim or compulsive; the intuition should genuinely pull toward calling E2 a pleasure, not toward calling it a problem.

This is for a handout on the heterogenity problem for hedonism (what pleasures have in common, if anything), so the case has to generate the verdicts from the two tests rather than just stipulating them.

Then do the check. In one sentence each, state the test each theory applies. Apply both tests to E1 and E2 and give the verdicts. Name the single structural feature of E2 that forces the split. Say what happens if you remove that feature — do the two theories collapse back into agreement, and on what verdict? Finish by saying how a felt-quality theorist would attack the case, and whether you buy the attack.
