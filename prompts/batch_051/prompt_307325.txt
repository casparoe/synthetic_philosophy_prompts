I recently rewatched the Coen brothers' film A Serious Man after a long period of standing outside organized religion, and a friend's offhand description of it as a dark fable about a good man whom God refuses to help bothered me more than it should have. When the film is treated philosophically at all, it tends to be read through the problem of evil: Larry Gopnik, a mid-century Midwestern physics professor, watches his wife leave him for the unctuous Sy Ableman, resists a student's attempt to bribe him for a grade, shelters a brother whose legal and gambling troubles spill into his home, and waits on a tenure case that seems to be quietly slipping away from him, while the three rabbis he consults offer him, in order, a parking-lot parable about the limits of perception, a story about a gentile's teeth that insists some things are simply mysterious, and no audience at all. The film frames all this with a prologue in which a possible dybbuk is invited into a home and then stabbed, and with an ending in which a doctor's call about a chest X-ray arrives just as a tornado gathers on the horizon. I have come to think the problem of evil is the wrong frame for the film — or at least an incomplete one.

What I want to read it through instead is the problem of divine hiddenness, understood not as the question why a loving God permits suffering, but as the question why a perfectly loving God would permit the state of sincere, nonresistant nonbelief: why a person who genuinely seeks God, and is open to belief, can nevertheless find only silence. The film seems to me to render the phenomenology of that search from the inside — the desire for a sign, or even a usable piece of guidance, met with parables, evasions, and closed doors, and the slow temptation to treat the silence itself as the evidence. But I can also see the film resisting the lens: Larry may not be the nonresistant seeker the argument needs, the prologue's folk world may run on a different logic from the philosopher's perfect love, and the ending may collapse hiddenness back into the problem of evil.

I would like a committed interpretation rather than a survey of possible readings. Read the film through the divine-hiddenness lens and argue for a definite thesis with reasons: where does the lens genuinely illuminate what the film is doing, where does it distort or overreach, and where does the film push back on the philosopher by suggesting that the real failure lies in Larry's demand for a certain kind of sign rather than in God's silence? I also want to know what the film contributes to the hiddenness debate that the bare argument cannot — and what the argument prevents a critic from reading into the film. I am preparing a discussion for a film-and-philosophy group, and I want to walk in with a clear position, including its limits and the points where I should honestly concede the work will not cooperate.
