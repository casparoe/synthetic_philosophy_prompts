I'm an undergrad taking a class on modal notions and probability this semester, and I've also got a part-time job about ten hours a week at a small drone startup. What I do there is review the engineering team's failure-mode writeups before they go to the regulator. The engineers constantly write things like "a loss of GPS could lead to a flyaway" or "there is a possible thermal runaway path from a punctured cell," and when I ask for numbers they just say it's "possible" or "physically possible but not statistically realistic." My boss wants me to turn what I'm learning into a reusable review procedure, because we do this maybe twice a week, each writeup gets about twenty minutes from me, and the stakes are either the regulator rejects the submission or, worse, we let something through that later causes a crash. My boss is the one who signs off, but he'll only look at anything the procedure flags.

So the deliverable I need is a checklist or decision tree that an engineer with no logic background can hand to me, for deciding when a bare "possible" claim is acceptable and when we have to demand a probability, a frequency bound, or some other analysis. I want it to keep seperate three things I keep confusing: logical possibility, physical or nomic possibility, and epistemic possibility (we just don't know enough to rule it out). And I want it to say how each of those relates to probability without treating them as the same thing, because I don't think "possible" is just "probability greater than zero," but I can't articulate why that matters in a hazard log.

Work through the procedure on three cases: (1) an event that is physically possible and has a best estimate of once per 10^9 flight hours; (2) an event that is logically possible but physically impossible given known battery chemistry; and (3) an event where "possible" means only that no one has yet shown a mechanism, so it's really an epistemic claim. I also want one case where the procedure gives an answer I'm going to hate, and an honest statement of what the checklist can't do — where the judgment call is unavoidable anyway.
