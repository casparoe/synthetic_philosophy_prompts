I am a philosophy professor whose research lies in egalitarian political philosophy and the jurisprudence of discrimination, and I think most clearly in the vocabulary of that field. Its central distinctions, as I use them, are these: wronging a person through bias or animus versus maintaining a structure of group subordination; disparate treatment versus disparate impact; formal equality of opportunity versus substantive or fair equality of opportunity; the luck-egalitarian contrast between disadvantages that trace to a person's chosen conduct and disadvantages that trace to unchosen circumstances; and a procedural conception of fairness according to which a decision can wrong someone even when it would not leave them materially worse off than some alternative.

I have been asked to assess a family of results in algorithmic fairness, and I can state their formalism well enough to be sure I have the claims right, but it is not the language in which I do my own thinking. In that literature, a decision rule assigns each person a numerical risk score for some binary outcome, with people partitioned by a protected attribute such as race or sex. Three criteria recur. Calibration within groups holds that, within each group, among all people assigned a given score, the probability of the outcome is the same and is equal to the score itself. Equal false-positive rates holds that, at whichever score threshold is used for a decision, the fraction of people who would not have had the outcome but are nonetheless classified as high risk is the same across groups. Equal false-negative rates holds that the fraction of people who would have had the outcome but are classified as low risk is likewise the same across groups. The impossibility result is that, apart from two degenerate cases — the score is already a perfect predictor, or the protected groups have equal base rates — no decision rule can satisfy all three criteria, and at least one must be sacrificed.

Restate this debate in my vocabulary, criterion by criterion and then for the impossibility result itself. For each item, say which parts translate cleanly, which parts can be rendered only with distortion, and which parts have no expression in my framework at all. Then give me the residue report: considering all of what did not survive the translation, tell me whether my vocabulary is missing distinctions it ought to make, or whether the algorithmic-fairness criteria are doing less normative work than their formal dress suggests. Keep the translation honest — do not quietly import the assumptions of my framework into the formalism and call that a translation, and do not report a clean fit where there is only a forced one.
