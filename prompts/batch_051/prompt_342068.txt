Give me a working definition of a law of nature: precise enough to classify each of the following cases as a law or not a law, and explicit enough that someone could apply it to a new case without further instructions. I take two cases to be settled. The inverse-square law of gravitation, as it appears in Newtonian mechanics, is a law. The generalization that all of the coins currently in my left pocket are copper is not a law. Now the harder cases:

1. The claim that no solid sphere of gold has a diameter greater than one mile.
2. Kepler's laws of planetary motion, considered within Newtonian mechanics.
3. The ideal gas law.
4. The claim that a specified radioactive isotope has a half-life of 1600 years.
5. The generalization that smoking causes lung cancer.
6. The second law of thermodynamics.
7. The claim that the fine-structure constant has the value it actually does.

Propose the definition, run it through every case, and say explicitly where it gives a surprising verdict, a wrong verdict, or no verdict. If a case exposes a flaw, revise the definition and run the revised version through all the cases again. If some cases turn out to count as laws only in a derivative, approximate, statistical, or ceteris paribus sense, specify that sense and say whether it is continuous with or different from the core notion. Then tell me which of your verdicts is least secure, and whether the definition you end up with is closer to a Humean account, on which laws are systematized regularities, or an anti-Humean account, on which laws govern or necessitate.
