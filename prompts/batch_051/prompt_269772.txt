I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on an argument I want one of my characters to make, and I'd rather have the actual philosophical machinery worked out before I put words in her mouth. The book centers on a woman, Elena, who works in the contracts office of an agrochemical company. Her job is to process supply agreements for a pesticide the company is still selling in countries where it's legal, even though the EU has banned it and the company's own internal studies suggest it causes serious nerve damage in farmworkers. Elena doesn't formulate the product, doesn't market it, doesn't set policy, doesn't even make the call to keep the product line going. She just handles a slice of the paperwork. If she quit tomorrow, the company would hire a replacement and the same canisters would reach the same farms, on the same schedule. So in a strict causal sense she doesnt make any difference: her contribution is overdetermined, or maybe even inert.

What I want is for her sister, a moral philosopher, to defend the strongest possible version of the claim that Elena is morally complicit in the farmworkers' injuries themselves, not merely in some vaguer corporate culture. I don't want the soft version — that she has bad character, or should feel guilty, or should quit to send a message. Please argue that she shares in the wrong itself, even though she changes no outcome. I'm not asking for the scene, just the reasoning. It would help if you'd draw out whatever distinctions the argument needs to do real work — say, between changing an outcome and participating in a wrong, or between individual difference-making and acting as a member of a joint enterprise, or between causing and ratifying — and then tell me where the strongest version of this argument is most likely to break down when someone pushes back on it.
