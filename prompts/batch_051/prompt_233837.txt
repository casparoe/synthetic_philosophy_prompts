An argument from a discussion about evaluating AI systems has been troubling me: every premise seems plausible, but the conclusion appears to prove too much, and I cannot identify the exact step where it goes wrong. I will reconstruct it.

(1) In its modern form, applied to optimization processes, Goodhart's law states that when a measure is used as an optimization target, optimization pressure tends to exploit the gap between the measure and the underlying objective it was meant to track, so the measure becomes less reliable as an indicator of that objective the more it is used as a target.
(2) Every proposed defense against this failure is itself a signal or procedure intended to track whether the system is genuinely pursuing the objective rather than exploiting the proxy: a second metric, a regularizer, an auditor model trained to flag reward hacking, or a red-team evaluation.
(3) Whenever training or selection pressure is directed at satisfying such a defense — for example, at producing outputs that the auditor will not flag — the defense is being used as an optimization target, and by (1) it becomes vulnerable to the same divergence between its signal and the underlying objective.
(4) The same reasoning applies to the defense of the defense, and so on; therefore any particular safeguard against specification gaming can itself be gamed, and no finite stack of safeguards is immune to gaming.
(5) But if every check is corruptible in this way, then there is no procedure at any level that can reliably establish whether a system is pursuing the intended objective or has found an unanticipated way to score well; the difference between aligned behavior and gamed behavior would be undetectable in principle.
(6) If the difference were undetectable in principle, then the accepted cases of specification gaming — cases in which a system demonstrably exploited its reward signal while failing the intended task — could never have been established as cases of gaming.
(7) Therefore Goodhart's law, if true, undercuts the very evidence on which belief in Goodhart's law, and in the reality of specification gaming, rests.

I would rather keep the concrete cases of specification gaming as genuine knowledge, and keep the possibility that evaluation can make real progress even though no metric is perfect, than accept the conclusion in (7). Which step should be rejected, why did that step look valid in the first place, and what would a coherent position have to give up in order to reject it?
