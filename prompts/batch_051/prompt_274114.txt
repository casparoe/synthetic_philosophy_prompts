After retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have taken up philosophy, and my reading lately has been on proper names and reference. At the same time I sit on the membership committee of an old international learned society, and the two interests have collided over a bylaw. The clause, unchanged since the 1950s, reads: "Members shall be entered on the roll under family name followed by forename and shall be listed accordingly; when a member's family name changes, the roll shall be amended and the former entry cross-referenced." The drafters assumed a European naming system and never imagined our current cases. First, an Icelandic glaciologist—call her Eyrún Jónsdóttir—for whom Jónsdóttir is a patronymic rather than a family name, who is alphabetized by forename at home, and whose children will not share the name. Second, a Chinese seismologist—call him Li Wei—whose family name, Li, precedes the given name; a clerk working left to right has entered him as Wei Li and now insists that Wei must be the relevant "family name" because it sits in the surname field of the form. Third, an Indonesian ecologist—call her Nurhayati—who bears a single name and therefore has no family name to enter at all.

My philosophical worry is this: the committee argues as if "family name" refers to a stable feature a person carries and as if an entry that changes its labels can still be an entry about the same member. From what I have been reading, it is at least as plausible that a family name is a piece of social technology for picking people out and tracking them over time, and that whether two entries refer to the same member should be settled by the purpose of the roll rather than by the marks in a field. I would like a committed ruling, not a menu of alternatives. Please tell me how the rule applies to each of the three entries, what the main interpretive approaches—the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader today would take the rule to mean—each require, and which approach should govern. Give the answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; use the final item to state the overall ruling on all three entries and why the governing approach prevails.
