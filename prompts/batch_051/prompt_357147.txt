I teach a punishment and forgiveness seminar and for the hell unit I want to use The Good Place, all four seasons, as more than just a pop culture hook. The show appears to start from the proportionality objection: people get assigned to eternal torture in the Bad Place on the basis of a points system operating over one finite life, and the show treats that as obviously indefensible once you actually look at it. But it then does something philosophically interesting — it doesn't just deny hell, it rebuilds the afterlife in stages, from Michael's neighborhood to the revelation that noone has got into the Good Place in five hundred years to the final door inside the Good Place. I'd like a reading of that whole arc through the debate between eternal torment, annihilationism, and universal salvation, and through the question whether any finite sin could ever deserve infinite punishment. Where does the show actually land, and is the position stable? Is the door just annihilationism with better lighting, or is it importantly different because it is chosen and comes after a completed process of moral improvement? I cant tell, and my students split on it. Where does the show stack the deck — does it make the retributivist case for hell easier to beat than it actually is by making the Bad Place cartoonishly cruel and the criteria for the Good Place impossible in a way the tradition needn't accept? And at the end, what does the show do for the debate that an argument in a paper couldn't?
