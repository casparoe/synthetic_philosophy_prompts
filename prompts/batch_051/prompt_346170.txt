A pop-science book I'm reading about why people cooperate summarizes Axelrod's prisoner's dilemma tournaments in one paragraph that I've reread enough times to reconstruct from memory. It goes roughly: Robert Axelrod ran a round-robin computer tournament for the iterated prisoner's dilemma, and the winner was Tit-for-Tat—start by cooperating, then do whatever the other player did last round. This suposedly proved that nice guys finish first, because nice-but-retaliatory strategies beat exploitative ones. The book then says this shows self-interest, over repeated interactions, automatically selects for altruism: cooperative strategies are just fitter than selfish ones, in markets and in evolution alike. And it closes by saying the one-shot dilemma is basically a red herring, since real people interact repeatedly, so cooperation isn't actually puzzling once you stop thinking about single encounters.

I'm inclined to believe this, because it makes cooperation sound like a solved problem, but a friend doing economics winced at 'proved' and said the book was glossing over a bunch. I can't tell if he's just being contrarian. Go through the summary piece by piece and tell me which bits someone working on the evolution of cooperation would accept, which they'd quietly object to, and which are just wrong. Then tell me whether the final claim—that repetition dissolves the puzzle and the one-shot case is a red herring—actually follows from the accurate parts, or whether it's doing more work than the tournament can support.
