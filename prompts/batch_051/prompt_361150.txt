Question: State the Bayesian positive-relevance account of confirmation, explain the problem of old evidence for that account, give one standard historical example of the problem, and then explain and assess one proposed solution, including a significant objection to it.

Student answer: On the Bayesian account, E confirms H just in case learning E would raise the probability of H, i.e., Pr(H|E) > Pr(H). The problem of old evidence is that when E is already known, Pr(E) = 1, and then Pr(E|H) must also be 1 for any H with positive prior probability, so Bayes's theorem gives Pr(H|E) = Pr(H). Old evidence can therefore never move the probability of a hypothesis, no matter how well the hypothesis fits it. The standard illustration is the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion: the data were known long before general relativity, yet the theory's accounting for that exact value is treated as one of its strongest confirmations—a verdict the formal framework seems unable to reproduce. The usual fix is counterfactual: assess H against a background in which the old evidence has been deleted and E is assigned some probability below 1, then conditionalize on E; the resulting rise in Pr(H) is meant to measure the confirmation E provides. Since we can always delete the relevant information from the background, this is straightforward, and the problem is essentially solved. Any residual issue is just logical omniscience, which is really the same difficulty in other words.
