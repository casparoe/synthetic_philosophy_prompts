I have been trying to get clear about the demandingness objection to act-consequentialism, and I keep producing the following argument. Tell me whether it is valid and, if not, which step fails. The conclusion strikes me as too strong, but I cannot locate the error in my own reasoning.

(1) If act-consequentialism is true, then an act is morally right just in case it produces at least as much expected impersonal value as any alternative act available to the agent.
(2) The expected impersonal value of a marginal dollar donated to a well-chosen anti-malaria charity is greater than the expected impersonal value of that same dollar spent on a typical evening out.
(3) The same comparison generalizes: for nearly all discretionary spending short of what an agent needs to remain a functioning agent, donating the money does more expected impersonal good than spending it on oneself.
(4) Therefore, if act-consequentialism is true, agents are morally required to give away nearly all of their discretionary income to well-chosen charities.
(5) If an agent is morally required to do something, then failure to do it makes the agent blameworthy.
(6) But ordinary agents who fail to give away nearly all of their discretionary income are not blameworthy.
(7) Therefore act-consequentialism is false.

I am not asking for a survey of consequentialist replies. I want a verdict on this argument as written. Above all, please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would most plausibly lead you to the opposite verdict, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail?
