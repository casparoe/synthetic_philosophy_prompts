I'm a graduate student in political philosophy, and in two weeks I'm giving a dry-run job talk to a mixed workshop, and the thing I'm actually scared of is the Q&A. The paper defends a natural-duty account of civil disobedience. The short version: consent, fair play, and associative-duty accounts of the obligation to obey the law all fail in the familar ways; the only survivor is a natural duty to support reasonably just institutions, understood mostly negatively (don't undermine them, do help sustain them in easy-rescue cases). From there I argue that public, communicative civil disobedience aimed at a serious institutional injustice doesn't violate that duty — the dissenter is doing what the duty actually calls for, namely pressing an institution back toward the standard that made it worth supporting in the first place. I then drop the stock requirement that the disobedient accept legal punishment to demonstrate commitment to the rule of law, on the grounds that the requirement gets the relationship backwards.

The audience is the problem. It's mostly liberal political philosophers — two quite orthodox Rawlsians, one Kantian who thinks I've quietly gone consequentialist — plus two law professors who work on federal courts and rule-of-law doctrine, and a retired federal judge who keeps muttering "but who decides?" under his breath whenever someone says an unjust law isn't binding. They're not radicals and they're not anarchists; they care about stability, the difference between a justified violation and a legal permission, and what letting disobedient people skip punishment signals to everyone else.

Draft the Q&A prep sheet in two sections, in this order. First, The objection lineup: seven or eight objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered from most dangerous to least (taking likelihood into account), each one phrased the way the person in the room would actually put it, using their vocabulary. For each objection give me: what to concede up front, what part of my view I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a one- or two-sentence note on where the reply is soft. Aim for 250–350 words per objection. Second, The question I'm hoping nobody asks: one or two paragraphs on the question I most don't want raised, why it's dangerous, and the best I could do if it comes up. Don't give me objections an anarchist or a radical-democratic audience would raise; ground everything in the argument I actually sketched.
