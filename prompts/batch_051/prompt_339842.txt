I'm a programmer who's been reading some metaphysics on the side (Stanford Encyclopedia articles, mostly, so go easy on me), and I can't shake the feeling that a debate I know from code is the same debate philosophers have about identity through time. In Python, if I do `a = [1, 2]` and `b = a`, then mutate the list through one name, the other name now sees the changed contents: the identity of the object is the reference/memory location, which persists while its contents change. That sounds a lot like what I take endurantism to say — there's one object that persists through time, and change is that object gaining and loosing properties. In Haskell (or any immutable-data style), there is no such underlying thing: 'changing' a value means producing a new value, and the old one still exists. That sounds like perdurantism — an object just is a sequence of stages, and change is replacement of one stage by another rather than an alteration of a single thing.

Here's the part that actually interests me. The reason mutation is hard to reason about in programming is precisely that it creates hidden dependence on time and hidden sharing: aliasing means two names can point at the same thing, side effects mean you can't just read a function's input and output locally, and you can't freely substitute equals for equals. So I want to know: is this parallel deep or am I just pattern-matching on the word 'identity'? Spell out the mapping in both directions and say which parts of one side have no real counterpart on the other. If the analogy were structural, what would it predict about the metaphysics of change that is false, and what would it predict about programming that is false? And does anything from the programming side — like the way referential transparency makes it easier to reason compositionally, or the way concurrency forces you to track who can mutate shared state — actually transfer as a consideration in favour of one of the metaphysical pictures?
