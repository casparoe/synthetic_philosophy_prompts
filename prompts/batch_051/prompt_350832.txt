Okay so in my psych class we've been doing this whole unit on why people make systematic mistakes in science (mostly physics) and we got stuck on this debate about wether misconceptions are like actual theories or not. The teacher showed us the classic example where a kid says a ball thrown straight up still has a force from the hand on it after it leaves, and that you need a constant force to keep something moving at a constant speed. One camp says kids have a coherent intuitive theory, like a little homemade Aristotle physics, and all the mistakes hang together because they're generated by that theory. The other camp says no, it's not a theory at all — it's a loose collection of small habits or rules of thumb (my teacher called them pieces or p-prims, something like that) that fire differently in different situations, which is why the same kid can give a correct answer on one problem and the wrong answer on a nearly identical one.

What bugs me is that these two camps have been going at it for decades and neither one can land the decisive blow. Each side shows the same kind of evidence over and over — kids applying the same wrong idea across contexts vs. kids being totally inconsistent — and they just accuse the other side of cherry-picking. Our class tried to settle it by giving people a bunch of similar problems and checking whether the same wrong idea carried across, and it made everyone argue harder instead of less.

I'm not asking who's right. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it an empirical disagreement that a better experiment could settle? Is it secretly about the word "theory"? Is it a difference in methods, like interviews vs. written tests? Or are they answering two different questions under one name? And if you could diagnose it, what would actually count as progress, or is the whole thing stuck because the question itself is badly posed? My own guess is that its partly about what counts as "coherent" in the first place, but I want to see if that holds up.
