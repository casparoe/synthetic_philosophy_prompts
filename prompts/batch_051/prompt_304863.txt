I am a high school student preparing a position paper for a Model United Nations committee on the prevention of atrocity crimes. A delegate from another school shared with me a background primer on the concept of genocide, and its central paragraph strikes me as subtly inaccurate, though I do not know enough legal history to identify exactly where it goes wrong. The paragraph reads as follows:

"The term genocide was coined in 1944 by the lawyer Raphael Lemkin to name the destruction of a people as a people, whether by mass killing or by attacks on the group's culture, language, religion, and inherited ways of life. The 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide essentially wrote Lemkin's definition into treaty law: genocide is any of five serious acts — killing members of the group, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to destroy the group, imposing measures intended to prevent births, or forcibly transferring children — when committed with the intent to destroy the group, in whole or in part, as a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. Because proving that special intent is difficult, successful genocide prosecutions are far less common than prosecutions for war crimes or crimes against humanity. And once an atrocity is officially recognized as genocide, international law obliges states to stop it, by military intervention if necessary; this is why governments fight so hard against the label, since accepting it means accepting a legal duty to send troops."

Could you audit this paragraph as a careful scholar of the history of the concept or of international criminal law would? Please go through it claim by claim and separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what is misleading or false. My instinct is that the last sentence is too strong, but I cannot tell whether the earlier sentences contain subtler errors of their own. In particular, I would like to know whether Lemkin's original conception and the 1948 Convention's definition fit together as tightly as the paragraph suggests; whether the account of the intent requirement and its prosecutorial consequences is fair; and whether a formal finding of genocide actually imposes a legal duty to intervene militarily. Finally, could you explain what a corrected version of the paragraph would imply about why states so often resist applying the word genocide to their own conduct or to that of their allies?
