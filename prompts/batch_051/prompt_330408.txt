Last week I rewatched *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance* with a couple of friends, and I've been trying to articulate what the ending actually does. You remember the scene: the editor of the Shinbone Star finds out that Ransom Stoddard didn't really shoot Liberty Valance — that Tom Doniphon fired the shot from the shadows — and he destroys the interview notes and says, more or less, that once a legend has become fact, the paper has to print the legend. I'm leading a discussion on the film, and I dont want to do the lazy "myth vs. fact, and the myth is just a lie" reading, because the film is more interesting than that: Stoddard really does build the legal and political order, Doniphon's hidden shot wouldn't have mattered without him, and his whole career seems to depend on the legend holding together. So my question is about when a legend becomes history in the sense of the record people actually live by, rather than just when a false story catches on.

I've been reading Thucydides' first book alongside it, because he's doing a version of exactly this with the Trojan War: trying to sift what actually happened from what the poets inflated. In Book 1 of the *History of the Peloponnesian War* (section 10 in the Crawley translation) he writes:

"For I suppose if Lacedaemon were to become desolate, and the temples and the foundations of the public buildings were left, that as time went on there would be a strong disposition with posterity to refuse to accept her fame as a true exponent of her power. And yet they occupy two-fifths of Peloponnese and lead the whole, not to speak of their numerous allies without. Still, as the city is neither built in a compact form nor adorned with magnificent temples and public edifices, but composed of villages after the old fashion of Hellas, there would be an impression of inadequacy. Whereas, if Athens were to suffer the same misfortune, I suppose that any inference from the appearance presented to the eye would make her power to have been twice as great as it is."

When I put that passage next to Ford's film, I cant decide whether Thucydides is the newspaper man's ally or his opposite. On the one hand, he's warning that visible remains mislead: posterity would undersell Sparta's power from its ruins and oversell Athens' from its monuments, and Shinbone's whole official history is built on the visible remains of a gunfight that didn't happen the way it was witnessed. On the other hand, Thucydides wants the corrected account to replace the legend, while the editor wants the legend printed because it has already become fact. So here's what I'd like: a reading of *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance* through the problem Thucydides raises in that passage — the gap between what posterity can see and what actually was the case — that treats both the film and Thucydides seriously, and says honestly whether the film adds something to the Thucydidean problem that his method can't handle, or whether it's just a prettier version of a confusion he'd already diagnosed. Would a Thucydidean tell the editor to run the true story and let the state take its chances? Or is there a sense in which a legend that builds a working political order has genuinely become history, in a way that Thucydides' standard of "what actually happened" would miss?
