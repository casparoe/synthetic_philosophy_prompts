Consider a canonical one-shot, two-player Prisoner's Dilemma with symmetric payoffs T > R > P > S: a player who defects against a cooperator receives T, mutual cooperation yields R for each, mutual defection yields P for each, and the cooperator against a defector receives S. The standard analysis is that defection strictly dominates cooperation for each player, so the unique rational outcome of the one-shot interaction is mutual defection; sustaining cooperation requires repetition, external enforcement, binding contracts, or some other factor brought in from outside the game itself.

Now vary the setup as follows. The two players are not humans choosing actions directly but two competing firms, each of which must submit a deterministic, guaranteed-to-halt pricing algorithm to a trusted execution environment before the round begins. At run time, the environment gives each algorithm a complete and verified copy of the other algorithm's source code. Each algorithm then returns exactly one move—cooperate or defect—and the ordinary one-shot payoffs are assigned according to the two returned moves. Firms cannot alter their code after submission, and there is no other communication channel between the algorithms: no messages, side payments, or binding contracts.

To block the most trivial route to symmetric collusion, the execution environment adds one further rule: after both algorithms have been submitted, it checks whether the two programs are identical up to whitespace, comments, and the renaming of identifiers. If they are, both firms are treated as having played defect, regardless of what their code would have returned. The firms know this rule before writing their code.

Does the standard analysis of the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma survive in this variant? In particular: (i) Is unconditional defection still a dominant strategy in the meta-game in which firms choose algorithms rather than bare moves? (ii) Do cooperative equilibria appear, and can they survive the anti-identity rule without relying merely on trivial syntactic rearrangement? (iii) If mutual cooperation is sustainable, should it be classified as collusion, or does the ability to condition one's move on the other's source code remove the independence premise on which the original dominance argument silently relied? State precisely which premise of the standard argument, if any, fails, and characterize the kinds of program-level strategies that can support cooperation under full code transparency combined with this anti-collusion rule.
