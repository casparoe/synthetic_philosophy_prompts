Consider the following tension in philosophical methodology. On one picture, the available evidence consists primarily of intuitions about particular cases, and general principles earn their keep only by organizing those case intuitions. On a rival picture, certain formal constraints — such as the transitivity of value or of evidential support, and a preference for theoretically simple principles — stand prior to case intuitions, so that a recalcitrant case intuition is something to be revised rather than treated as counterevidence. The tension becomes acute when a person's pairwise case intuitions are each compelling in isolation yet jointly violate transitivity (A is judged better than B, B better than C, C better than A), or when a body of case intuitions can be captured by a simple principle only if several of them are corrected — as can happen in pairwise trade-offs among values such as welfare, autonomy, and fairness.

Explain the conceptual issue this raises. In particular: what are the live options for locating the error — the particular case intuitions, the transitivity constraint, the assumption that value must be representable by a transitive ranking, or the demand for simplicity? How might one defend treating transitivity and simplicity not as further evidence alongside the case intuitions but as preconditions for those intuitions' counting as evidence at all? And how might a critic reply that this protects the formal constraint from any possible correction by the case intuitions? Your explanation should make clear what each option implies about what is doing the justificatory work when philosophers appeal to intuitions, and why the dispute is not merely verbal.
