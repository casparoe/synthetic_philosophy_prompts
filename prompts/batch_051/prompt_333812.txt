You are a professional moral philosopher. Please think carefully, begin by brainstorming, and do not settle for the first principle that fits; I want the distinct alternatives worked out before you choose.

I am a high school student, and I have been arguing with my friend Maya about whether it can ever be permissible to bring a person into existence. Maya will not state her general principle — she says she does not have one worked out — but she has very firm, unshakeable verdicts about particular procreation cases. I have been trying to reverse-engineer the rule she is actually running, and I want your help.

Here is Maya's track record so far:

1. Tania. Tania would be born with a condition that causes severe, near-constant pain, and she would die before age two with almost no pleasure. Maya says conceiving Tania would be wrong.

2. Felix. Felix would have a long life that is, for all practical purposes, free of pain and full of intense pleasures. Maya says conceiving Felix is permissible.

3. Priya. Priya would have a long life containing many pleasures along with the ordinary pains of a human life — illness, grief, injury, disappointment — but on any straightforward calculation the pleasures would massively outweigh the pains. Maya says conceiving Priya is permissible.

4. The passed-up happy life. A couple decides not to have a child who would have had a life like Felix's. Maya says the couple did nothing wrong, and the possible child is not deprived of anything.

5. The avoided miserable life. A couple decides not to conceive a child who would have had a life like Tania's. Maya says this is not merely acceptable but positively good: the absence of that pain is itself what makes the decision good, even though no child exists to enjoy the absence.

Maya refuses to commit herself on the sixth case, which is the one I actually need answered:

6. Leah. Leah would have a full-length life that is net-positive in the ordinary sense, but only barely. It would contain frequent and significant pains — a recurring chronic pain condition, the grief of losing several people she loves, and episodes of depression — alongside frequent and equally intense pleasures: deep friendships, absorbing and successful work, beauty, and achievement. On a symmetric ledger, the pleasures would slightly outweigh the pains, but the pleasures would not cancel or numb the pains; the two would simply alternate.

Assume the facts in each case are exactly as stated, and assume Maya is consistent. Please:

(1) Identify two or three genuinely different principles that would generate all five of Maya's recorded verdicts. One possibility to include is something along the lines of Benatar's asymmetry: the absence of a person's pain counts as good even when no one exists, while the absence of a person's pleasure does not count as bad unless someone is deprived of it. State each principle in your own words, not just as a label.

(2) For each principle you identify, say exactly what it predicts about the Leah case and why.

(3) Then give your best-grounded prediction of what Maya would say about Leah — wrong, permissible, or something else. Defend it against the rival principles, and be explicit about whether the five recorded verdicts really force this conclusion or merely make it more probable.

(4) Finally, name one further case I could put to Maya, not a re-described version of any case above, that would best discriminate between the principle you favor and the strongest rival. Say what each principle would predict for that further case.
