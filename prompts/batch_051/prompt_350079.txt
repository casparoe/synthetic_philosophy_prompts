Consider two practices that are rarely discussed together. The first is the parental fiction that Santa Claus brings presents — with the tooth fairy in a supporting role — told to young children and then, in the usual course of things, knowingly retired as the child matures. The second is the device in political philosophy often traced to Plato and usually called the noble lie: a founding myth told to citizens on the grounds that they cannot, or should not, be trusted with the unvarnished truth about their polity’s origins and ordering. On their face the two look alike: a deliberate falsehood is told by authorities (parents; rulers) to an audience judged unready for the truth (children; citizens); the falsehood is defended, when it is defended at all, as producing goods — wonder, gentle moral habituation, and a childhood worth having on one side, social cohesion and willing acceptance of one’s social role on the other; and both arrangements create an asymmetry between those who manage the fiction and those who live inside it.

I want a verdict on whether this is a genuine structural parallel or a superficial resemblance. To make the verdict count, do the following. First, give the cleanest mapping between the two cases — narrator, audience, what the content is for, who benefits, how and whether the deception is meant to end — noting that Santa is typically revealed as a rite of passage while a noble lie is usually meant to persist. Second, say what would have to be true of both sides for any lesson to transfer from one to the other. Third, name at least one thing the parallel predicts that is in fact false, and say whether that false prediction is enough to break the analogy or merely limits its reach.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — a clear statement of whether the parallel is deep, superficial, or partly each — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, do not restate the question, and write in British English.
