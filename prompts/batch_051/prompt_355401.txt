You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in axiology — the study of what is ultimately good, bad, and worth pursuing. Please think carefully and begin by brainstorming several candidate objections before settling on the one you present; do not simply report the first objection that occurs to you.

I am compiling objections to tranquilism for a seminar. By tranquilism I mean the view that the only thing that is non-instrumentally good is the absence of craving and suffering; everything else — pleasure, knowledge, achievement, love, beauty — is good only insofar as it reduces or prevents craving and suffering, and nothing is bad except insofar as it involves or causes them. A change could make the world better, on this view, only by reducing craving or suffering; every other change is neutral.

Here are the objections I have already collected:

1. The cessation objection: if only the absence of craving and suffering matters, then a world in which no sentient beings ever exist — or one in which everyone remains permanently and peacefully unconscious — would be just as good as a world of flourishing people, which many find unacceptable.

2. The positive-value objection: joy, understanding, beauty, and mutual love seem good for their own sake, not merely as relief from or prevention of craving and suffering, and no account of them as mere absences or pauses between episodes of distress captures why they matter.

3. The aspiration objection: many of the activities that give a human life its shape — inquiry, art, friendship, political struggle — are partly constituted by vigorous wanting and dissatisfaction with how things stand. If all craving is bad, tranquilism appears to condemn the very springs of the pursuits it is often used to recommend.

4. The self-defeat objection: the project of eliminating one's cravings naturally becomes a strong craving in its own right, a restless dissatisfaction with one's present level of tranquility. As a guide to life, tranquilism seems to demand the state it says is to be eliminated.

5. The fittingness objection: some craving and suffering are fitting responses to how things are — grief at a real loss, anger at an injustice, longing for a good that has been destroyed. Their absence would not always be an improvement; in some circumstances it would be indifference or a failure to register what matters, so tranquilism's blanket verdict against them misdescribes their value.

Give me one more objection that is genuinely different from all five: not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of them. By genuinely different I mean that it attacks a different step in the tranquilist's reasoning, or proceeds from a different kind of consideration, than anything above. Begin by saying in one sentence which premise or step of tranquilism your objection targets. Then assess the objection honestly: how strong is it against the most plausible version of tranquilism, where is its reasoning most likely to fail, and what consideration would change your mind about whether the objection succeeds?
