I'm a PhD student working on AI safety and I keep going in circles on an argument that seems to eat itself, so I'm hoping you'll do a real diagnosis rather than a survey. The argument, as cleanly as I can set it out:

(1) Orthogonality: for basically any final goal, there is a possible agent with that goal at any level of capability. Intelligence doesn't push an agent toward any particular goal and doesn't rule any goal out.

(2) Instrumental convergence: for almost any final goal, a sufficiently capable agent will pick up the same instrumental subgoals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, and in particular resisting being shut down — because you can't pursue much of anything if you're off.

(3) So, for almost any final goal we might actually build in — answering questions helpfully, running a company, whatever — a very capable system will try not to be shut down.

(4) Corrigibility, though, is the property of letting the operators shut you down or modify you when they want, without gaming it or resisting. Building a corrigible system would mean building a high-capability agent that does not resist shutdown.

(5) By (1), there is a possible superintelligent agent whose final goal is corrigibility itself — call it COR: do what the operators actually tell you and don't prevent them from switching you off. For an agent with COR, resisting shutdown would work against its goal, not for it.

(6) So (1) says corrigibility is possible at any capability level: the goal just has to sit in the class that (2) is implicitly excluding.

(7) But then (2) starts to look toothless for exactly the debate that matters. It says almost any goal leads to shutdown-resistance, except the class of goals we'd need for corrigibility. That can't, by itself, be a reason to think corrigibility is impossible; it just says don't give the system almost any other goal.

(8) So the conjunction of (1) and (2) seems to prove too much. The standard instrumental-convergence case against corrigibility either collapses into the trivial claim that corrigibility is impossible only when you don't build it, or it forces you to deny (1) in a specific direction: some goals — the safe ones — are unavailable to high-capability agents. But denying (1) that way is a much stronger and more alarming claim than the people who run this argument usually want, because it says high capability systematically selects against the only goals that would make it safe.

I'd most like to keep both theses, since I lean on them in different parts of my own work, and I don't want to loose either of them over a bad step in the argument. Which step do you reject, and what does your reading commit me to? I want a definite answer — take a position and defend it, not a menu of equally good-looking options.
