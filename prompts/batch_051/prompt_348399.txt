I work in condensed-matter theory, and I have a standing rule about how much weight to give to lower-level explanation. When a system has a clean separation of scales and I have an effective description of it — a continuum field theory, a hydrodynamic model, an order-parameter theory — whose predictions have been confirmed, I treat the fact that nobody has derived the description, or its parameters, from an underlying microscopic theory as carrying almost no evidential weight against it; I also treat requests to fill in that derivation as optional. Recently this rule decided a disagreement. A colleague argued that we should lower our confidence in a continuum model of a magnetic material because its couplings had not yet been matched to the lattice Hamiltonian. I refused to move, citing universality and the renormalization group: many microscopic inputs flow to the same long-distance behavior, and the model's own predictions had repeatedly survived test. The exchange left me uneasy that I may be using a sound observation about the autonomy of scales to immunize my preferred description against a class of challenges. Is my policy defensible as an epistemic rule? Under what conditions does the absence of a lower-level derivation genuinely count against an effective theory, and how could I tell from the inside whether I am applying scale separation correctly or merely protecting a theory I am attached to? Is there a corrected version of the rule that keeps what is right about the autonomy of scales while still acknowledging the cases in which the lower level matters?
