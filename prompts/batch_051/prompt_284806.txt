Consider four families of explanation for why mystical traditions routinely describe their central experiences as ineffable while also treating them as sources of knowledge. (1) Representational exhaustion: the experience has fine-grained, nonconceptual content that outruns the subject's available concepts. (2) Acquaintance: the subject stands in a direct, non-propositional relation to the ultimate or the divine, so the knowledge is of a thing rather than that something is the case. (3) Apophatic misdescription: ordinary affirmations are not merely imprecise but systematically false of the object, so language actively misdescribes what is given. (4) Social role: calling the experience ineffable marks it as authoritative and shields it from public challenge. Rate each family from 0 to 10 on two scales — how well it explains the conjunction of ineffability with knowledge, and how well it takes the mystic's own self-description seriously rather than explaining it away. Then rank the four by combined score, defend the top and bottom entries in a sentence or two, and state the single consideration that would most change your ranking.
