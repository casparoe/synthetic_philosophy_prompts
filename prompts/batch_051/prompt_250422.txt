I work on a small team keeping an e-commerce backend alive. Last week we had an incident that cost us a fair bit of money, and its still bothering me, but not for the reason you'd expect. We have an Order object that gets created when a customer checks out. When a card payment failed, the retry code updated the order in place: it set order.status to 'retrying', appended a charge attempt, and carried on. A reporting job that had picked up the same Order object an hour earlier was still running, and as it read the order it saw the status change underneath it. Customer support sent round a log where the same object, same order_id, same database row, printed different totals at different points in the same job, and they kept asking which one is the actual order. The annoying thing is I couldn't give them a straight answer. My instinct was to say "they're both the order", which sounds mad.

My colleague says this is just poor discipline: don't hand mutable objects around, problem solved. Another says mutation itself is the problem and we should make Order immutable with copy-on-write. I'm writing the post-mortem and I don't think either of them has put their finger on why the bug felt conceptually slippery, not just inconvenient. We all agree what happened mechanically, but we couldn't agree on what "the same order" means when the thing at the same address changes its state. Is my colleague right that its just a plumbing problem, or is there something genuinely hard about reasoning with mutable state that the post-mortem should say honestly? I'm not after coding advice so much as an explanation I can give the rest of the team (some of whom don't code) for why state and identity seem to come apart under mutation. Could you give me that explanation, in at most 400 words?
