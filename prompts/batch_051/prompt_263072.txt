Here is a parallel I have been trying to assess. In Chapter IV of The Descent of Man, Darwin offers an account of the moral sense according to which conscience is not a separate rational faculty but arises from a conflict between differently persistent instincts. As I understand the passage, a person may act on a strong but transient impulse, such as hunger, vengeance, or lust, even though doing so goes against a more persistent social instinct. After the transient desire is satisfied and fades, the enduring social instinct reasserts itself, and the person now regards the action under the influence of that returning feeling, experiencing uneasiness, regret, shame, or repentance. Conscience, on this view, is retrospective in the first instance: it is the later reappearance of the stronger but temporarily overridden social propensity, and it acquires a forward-looking regulatory role because the remembered pain of that retrospective dissatisfaction can alter future conduct.

The parallel I keep noticing is with dual-self or planner–doer models of time-inconsistent choice. In those models, a short-run self acts on present-focused preferences, while a long-run self evaluates the whole sequence and may later wish the earlier action had not occurred. The resulting regret or self-reproach is explained as the later return of a temporally extended preference after a shorter-lived one has been exhausted. This looks to me structurally very close to Darwin's contrast between transient impulses and enduring social instincts.

Is this a genuine structural identity? In particular, is Darwin's enduring social instinct doing the same work as the long-run planner's utility function, and is the transient impulse doing the same work as the doer's present bias? Or does the analogy break because Darwin's social instincts are evolved dispositions rather than explicit future-oriented preferences, and because Darwin's mechanism does not require the kind of inconsistent discounting that dual-self models formalize? What would have to be true of both accounts for results to transfer from one to the other, and what does the comparison reveal about Darwin's moral psychology that a direct reading of the chapter might not?
