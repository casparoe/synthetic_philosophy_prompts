I am a retired population biologist, and for the past few years I have been reading philosophy of biology with a small group that mixes working ecologists, a few philosophers of science, and one or two interested outsiders. We have spent this term on signaling, and we keep talking past one another about the handicap principle. People announce that they accept or reject the handicap principle, but it has become clear that the label hides at least three or four distinct commitments about what costs are doing in honest signaling. I would like you to build a sorting instrument — a decision tree, or if the positions refuse to lie along a single path, a taxonomy whose branch points are labeled with the questions that separate the branches — that will classify a reader into one of the live positions on the role of costs in honest signaling.

The leaves it should be able to reach are these. (a) The strategic-cost handicap view: signals are honest because higher-quality senders pay a lower marginal cost for a given signal intensity, so the signal is worth producing only to senders above some quality threshold. (b) The index view: many honest signals are tied to the trait they advertise by physical or developmental necessity and cannot be faked, whatever they cost. (c) The social-cost or conventional-signal view: honesty is maintained by how receivers respond — retaliation, discounting, lost future cooperation — rather than by the cost of producing the signal itself. (d) The shared-interest or cost-free view: honesty can be stable when sender and receiver mostly want the same outcome, with little or no strategic cost needed. If the logical space forces further leaves, such as a deflationary view holding that the notion of cost is doing too many jobs to explain anything, add it, but do not inflate the number of leaves merely for symmetry.

Build the instrument from diagnostic questions about concrete signal cases, not from doctrine. Use cases such as a gazelle stotting in front of an approaching predator, a peacock's train, a male frog with an exaggeratedly deep call, a threat display that almost never ends in a fight, a nestling begging in the nest, aposematic coloration in a toxic insect, and a honeybee's waggle dance. Phrase each question so that a reader can answer it from what they believe about the case, without knowing which position the answer pushes them toward. The axes to probe should include at least: whether the signal can be faked; whether the relevant cost is borne in producing the signal or in the receiver's response to it; whether higher-quality senders face a lower marginal cost for the same signal; and whether the signal would remain honest if receivers stopped punishing dishonesty.

I want the instrument built from the logical space first: settle the separating axes, turn each axis into a concrete question, and only then locate the named positions among the resulting answer combinations. For each combination of answers that corresponds to no actual position, say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. If two leaves cannot honestly be separated by any realistic question — for instance because they differ only in formal modeling assumptions and never in any observable case — flag that pair explicitly rather than manufacturing a question that pretends to separate them. Assume the instrument will be used by my reading group, who are scientifically literate but not all specialists in signaling theory; eight to ten questions and at most six leaves is about the right size.
