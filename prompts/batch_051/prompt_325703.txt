I am taking a course on personal identity and the prospects of mind uploading, and I have arrived at an uncomfortable standoff between a theory I find compelling and an intuition I cannot seem to shake. The theory is a functional, psychological-continuity view of survival: what matters for my continued existence is that there be a later being whose mental life — memories, beliefs, plans, habits of thought and attention — is appropriately causally descended from mine, and that the relevant causal and functional organization be preserved. The substrate as such seems irrelevant. This view is attractive because it is parsimonious, because it avoids any immaterial soul or vital force, and because it avoids a parochial carbon chauvinism about biological matter. It also gives non-arbitrary answers in a range of cases: general anesthesia, heart transplantation, and the gradual replacement of neurons. Consider that last case. If one of my neurons were replaced by a silicon component that performed exactly the same signaling function, I would notice nothing and presumably survive. If the procedure were iterated over many months, one neuron at a time, until no biological neurons remained, it is hard to see at which step I would die, so the view says I survive the whole process, and I find that compelling.

Yet I also have a firm intuition that I would not survive a different procedure. Suppose a powerful scanner records every connection, synaptic weight, and relevant dynamic property of my brain; the scan is then used to run a point-for-point emulation of my entire neural network on a computer, while the biological brain is destroyed in the scanning process. The emulation wakes up in a virtual environment, remembers my past, has my mannerisms and projects, would pass every test of being me, and would sincerely report that the procedure worked. But my immediate conviction is that this is the death of the original and the creation of a mere copy, and that there is a further fact about which perspective wakes up that all the functional and behavioral facts leave out. The problem is that the gradual replacement and the upload appear to reach the same endpoint, a nonbiological realization of my psychology, and the only differences between them seem to be the speed of the replacement and whether a causally continuous physical process persists throughout. Those differences look like exactly the sort of arbitrary, unprincipled line the functional view was meant to eliminate.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a simple verdict. Must I give up the intuition about the upload as a brute, evolution-shaped prejudice? Must I amend or abandon the functional, psychological-continuity theory, and if so, what should replace it? Or is there a principled way to hold both — for example, by distinguishing the continuation of a particular implemented mind from the creation of a new instance of the same computational pattern, by treating survival as a matter of degree, or by treating uninterrupted causal integration as an independent requirement? What considerations would justify each of these moves, and how would I tell whether the boundary I am drawing is a real difference or only a source of comfort?
