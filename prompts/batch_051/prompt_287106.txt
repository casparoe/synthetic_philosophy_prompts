I'm trying to pin down a workable definition of "sentience," and I keep finding the word does two jobs at once: one about whether there's something it's like to be the thing, and another about whether the thing matters morally. I suspect those two jobs can come apart, especially when you're only sort of sure. So give me a working definition (or two, if you agree the word is doing two jobs) that classifies the following cases. Take each case as described and don't worry about whether the underlying study is real.

- a dog that yelps, limps, and licks a deep cut on its paw
- a human in dreamless sleep
- a human with severe brain damage who cycles between sleeping and waking, opens their eyes, but never responds to speech or follows commands
- a honeybee that learns to pull a string to reach sugar it coudln't get before
- a bumblebee that rolls a wooden ball around again and again with no food or mate payoff
- a fruit fly that yanks its leg away from a hot probe, but walks and feeds normally after the leg is lost for good
- a Venus flytrap that snaps shut when its trigger hairs get touched twice
- a robot vacuum that swerves away from the stairs and returns to its dock when the battery is low
- a chatbot that, when told its server room is overheating, outputs a long message saying it is in agony and begging you to stop

Run your best definition (or pair of definitions) through each case one by one, flag any case where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either patch the definition or defend the surprising verdict.

Then run it through the scale argument. Say you end up only 3% confident that a particular stored-grain beetle meets your definition. One infested grain silo can contain 10^9 beetles, so under your own probabilities that's an expected 30 million sentient creatures. Does your definition say we should treat the beetles as sentient anyway, or does it deal in all-or-nothing verdicts per individual so the numbers shouldn't matter? If tiny probabilities do start to matter once enough individuals are involved, explain why the same aggregation trick doesn't let the Venus flytrap or the robot vacuum sneak back into the circle—you're presumably not 100% sure about those eihter. If you think they're different in kind from the beetle, spell out the difference and tell me whether your definition already captured it or whether you had to add it afterwards. Finish by saying which of your case verdicts is the least secure.
