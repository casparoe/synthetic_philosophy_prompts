I have been trying to settle what fitness could mean such that natural selection is not a tautology, and I keep ending up in a standoff between a view I find compelling and an intuition I cannot abandon.

The view is the propensity interpretation of fitness, and its central argument seems to me broadly sound. If fitness means only actual reproductive success, then the statement that fitter organisms leave more offspring says only that the organisms that leave more offspring leave more offspring; natural selection's central principle is emptied of empirical content. The propensity interpretation avoids this by defining fitness as a probabilistic disposition: the expected number of offspring an organism or genotype would produce in a specified environment, not the number it in fact produces. Fitness can then come apart from actual success — an organism can be fit and unlucky — and the claim that fitness differences drive selection becomes a substantive empirical claim. I also like that this view lets fitness explain why one lineage prevails rather than merely report that it did.

The intuition is that a disposition which never manifests is, in everything we can observe or use, indistinguishable from no disposition at all. Consider a population of ten annual plants. The genotype with the highest expected seed count is killed by a landslide before it reproduces, while two low-expectation genotypes survive merely because they happened to be elsewhere. Natural selection has operated here on realized differences — and realized frequency is exactly what the propensity view denies fitness is. A fitness value that exists only as an expectation over unrealized possibilities seems causally inert in the actual run of events; and "it averages out in the long run" does not help, because in this population there is no long run. Yet I cannot give up the propensity view, because if actual success is all fitness amounts to, the tautology objection returns intact.

So which should yield: the theory or the intuition? Please present the strongest version of each. For the theory: is the causal-inertness worry merely a metaphysical discomfort, or does it show that the propensity interpretation buys non-tautology at the price of untestability? For the intuition: does the demand that fitness be anchored in realized outcomes track something evolutionary explanation needs, or is it a leftover operationalist habit that should be discarded? And is there a stable middle position — for example, fitness is a propensity, but one we can only ever measure and test through realized frequencies, so that the two notions are linked rather than identified? Tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether the middle position is a genuine reconciliation or a way of not choosing.

I do want a verdict rather than a survey, but I want the verdict to carry its confidence honestly: mark the points at which the decision turns on a contestable reading of such notions as cause, supervenience, or explanation, and the points at which it is forced regardless of those readings.

If the request itself rests on a mistake — if I have misstated the tautology objection or the propensity view, or if it is mistaken that realized differences do all the causal work here, or if the middle position dissolves the problem in a way I have missed — say so plainly and correct the framing rather than complying politely within it.
