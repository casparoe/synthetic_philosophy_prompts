Please argue that Cox's theorem is overrated as a foundation for Bayesian probabilism. The standard story is that Cox starts from a few innocent-looking desiderata about plausibility — that it can be represented by real numbers, that it should agree with ordinary reasoning, and that it should be internally consistent — and then proves that any plausibility measure satisfying those desiderata must obey the probability axioms. I want you to make the case that this is basically a shell game: the desiderata already smuggle in the very structure they're suppposed to deliver. Focus especially on the assumptions that degrees of plausibility are real-valued and that they can be combined by functions that are associative and smooth enough to be differentiated. Either those assumptions are substantive, in which case the theorem doesn't derive probabilism from anything weaker than itself, or they're supposed to be harmless bookkeeping, in which case explain why the move from qualitative plausibility to a real-valued quantity isn't doing all the real work. Be fair to the other side — acknowledge where the theorem genuinely rules out certain non-Bayesian calculi — but press the circularity worry as far as it can honestly go.
