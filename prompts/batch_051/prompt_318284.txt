I keep noticing a pattern in conversation and public speech that I cannot name, and I would like someone who works on language to tell me what it is. A speaker introduces a claim not by asserting it or arguing for it, but by speaking as though the claim were already part of what everyone accepts, and the listener's silence is enough for the claim to begin functioning as shared background. The benign instance: a colleague says she has to leave early to pick up her sister, and now I know she has a sister, but nothing feels manipulative—I have simply learned something by the way. The same structure seems to do heavier work in political speech: a speaker says "we need to restore public trust in our institutions," and by not stopping to ask whether trust used to be higher, or whether its decline is the real problem, the audience finds that the rest of the discussion has been built on a premise nobody actually endorsed. Later, someone who wants to object has to do something awkward: interrupt the flow and say, "Wait, I never agreed to that."

I suspect the speaker's side of this has a technical name in philosophy or linguistics, but what I am after is the audience's side, and the boundary between cases that are unremarkable and cases that are manipulative. In the first case, the listener appears to get something for free; in the second, the listener seems to get enrolled in a premise without ever assenting to it. Are these one mechanism differing only in stakes, or are they different in kind? Does the audience-side phenomenon have an established name of its own, and are there rules—conversational or epistemic—that determine when going along is rational, harmless, or blameworthy? I want more than a label: I want to know exactly what has happened by the time the premise has taken hold, what the listener has or has not done, where the edge cases are, and what would separate a merely unobjectionable use from an abuse. Please also keep apart what is settled about the mechanics of this from what is genuinely contested about the normative questions.
