I'm putting together a handout for an undergrad philosophy of language course, and I want a "which theory of metaphor do you actually hold?" quiz — the kind where people get sorted by what they say about specific sentences rather than by which label sounds flattering. The positions I want on the scoring key are roughly: the Davidson-style view (the words only ever carry their literal meaning, and whatever else a metaphor does is just a causal effect the sentence has on us), the Gricean view (metaphor is speaker meaning — an implicature recovered from the literal content), the Lakoff/Johnson conceptual metaphor view (metaphors are systematic cross-domain mappings in thought, and the sentences are just the surface of that), and a relevance-theory-ish view (metaphor isn't a special category at all, just loose talk on a continuum with literal speech). The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete sentence — someone saying a proposal was demolished, a relationship hit a wall, an idea went off track, a fresh poetic line, a dead metaphor — with three or four plain-language options for what is going on (does it assert a definite extra proposition, does it just push you to notice a resemblance, does it bring a whole source-domain frame to bear, does it just express an attitude, etc.). Include at least one question that isn't about understanding the sentence but about what someone is licensed to infer or do next after hearing it, because the positions really come apart on whether metaphors structure reasoning or just decorate it.

Then give a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto those positions, and be honest about which positions the quiz can't cleanly seperate. Also flag any combination of answers that looks logically possible but that nobody actually holds, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. The distinctions I want the questions to actually track are semantic vs pragmatic, propositional vs non-propositional, whether paraphrase exhausts a metaphor, whether dead metaphors are still metaphors, and whether the metaphor shapes thought or just colors it. The students won't have read the literature, so the questions themselves have to do all the work — no citations or reading list needed, just the quiz and the key.
