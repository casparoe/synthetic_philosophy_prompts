I have found that I hold a set of strong but apparently inconsistent views about when negative emotions are worth having, and I would like you to help me extract the principle behind them—or tell me, honestly, which of my views cannot be saved.

Here are my verdicts on particular cases.

1. **Major depression.** If I were in a depressive episode, I would seek treatment without hesitation, and I would regard the despair, anhedonia, and self-contempt as states with no redeeming value. Permanently removing them would be pure gain, not loss.

2. **Grief.** But if someone I deeply loved died, and I were offered a pill that would erase the grief immediately and permanently, I would refuse it. The grief seems to me to be part of loving the person; a version of me who simply moved on, untroubled, would be in some important way a worse or defective person, not a healthier one.

3. **Sadness from tragic art.** I deliberately read novels and watch films that I know will make me sad, and I regard the sadness as part of what I value in them. I would not choose a version of the work engineered so that I felt only pleasure.

4. **Envy and spite.** I would eliminate these from my emotional repertoire if I could. Whatever information they sometimes carry seems to me outweighed by their tendency to distort judgment and corrode relationships.

5. **Remorse.** If I had wronged someone, I would think it a further moral failing to feel nothing afterward. Remorse seems like a correct response to what I did, not merely a painful symptom.

6. **Stage fright.** A moderate amount of anxiety before a difficult performance strikes me as useful; it sharpens attention. But full panic—racing heart, mental blanking, freezing, avoidance—seems worthless and worth extinguishing, even though panic is a response to a genuinely threatening situation.

7. **Objectless melancholy.** A standing, low-grade sadness about mortality and the human condition, not tied to any particular loss, leaves me divided. Sometimes it feels like lucidity; sometimes it feels like a fog that drains color from everything. I would want to keep it when it is the former and lose it when it is the latter, but I cannot say what the difference consists in.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like this: a negative emotion has value when it is a fitting or accurate response to something that matters—loss, wrong, danger, love—and lacks value when it is a distortion or malfunction. But I can already see trouble with that principle. Panic before a real danger is fitting and accurate, yet I called it worthless. Grief that years later still prevents a person from forming any new attachment is still a response to a real loss, but I am reluctant to say all of it deserves protection. And the sadness I get from a novel is not a response to anything real at all, yet I value it. So perhaps fittingness is the wrong axis, or only part of the story.

Can you state, precisely, one principle—or a small set of principles—that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? For each candidate, tell me which of my verdicts it preserves, which it forces me to revise or abandon, and what it predicts about cases I have not settled: anger at a genuine injustice, chronic generalized anxiety, shame after a private failing, jealousy in a relationship, boredom, and homesickness. If the cleanest principle overturns some of my verdicts, say so plainly rather than complicating the principle just to vindicate every one of them. I am more interested in discovering whether my pattern is coherent than in having all of my initial reactions confirmed.
