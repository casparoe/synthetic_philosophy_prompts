I am a novelist. I am drafting a book about a community of people who undergo a longevity treatment and must live with the consequences for several centuries, and I want their relation to lived time to be philosophically exact rather than merely world-weary. In the course of the writing I have developed a concept I call 'repertoire exhaustion,' and I would like it examined honestly before I build a novel around it.

By a mind's experiential repertoire I mean the set of subjectively distinguishable total experiences available to that mind: every way its sensory, affective, and cognitive capacities allow things to seem to it from the inside, where two experiences the mind cannot tell apart count as a single item no matter how different their external causes are. My central claim is the Finite Repertoire Thesis: for any finite mind — one with finite sensory resolution, finite memory, and a finite conceptual vocabulary — the repertoire is finite. It may be astronomically large; it is not inexhaustible. From this it follows that a sufficiently long life must eventually exhaust the repertoire, in this sense: every later experience is, from the inside, a repetition or recombination of experiences already had, even while the world continues to supply new scenes. Objective novelty continues; subjective novelty decays toward zero.

In the novel, my protagonist describes a sunset as 'already counted' — not déjà vu, but the feeling that the space of possible first-person renderings has been fully toured. A new face, a new city, a new grief present themselves as rearrangements of entries in a finished ledger. I need to know whether that image can be earned or whether it is a seductive error.

Please assess the idea candidly and charitably. First, reconstruct the thesis in its strongest defensible form and say whether it is coherent. Second, tell me whether it already exists under another name in the philosophy of mind, and, if so, what in my formulation is or is not distinctive. Third, say what it gets right about the difference between objective and subjective novelty. Fourth, say where it breaks. I can already see three candidate fractures. First, sensory magnitudes are continuous, so the thesis may depend on a finite-resolution premise I have quietly supplied. Second, experiences seem partly individuated by what they are about — their intentional objects — and an indefinitely rich world combined with recursive thought may leave the repertoire open-ended. Third, a mind is altered by having experiences, so the repertoire may not be a fixed inventory to be used up but a system that rebuilds itself at each step, perhaps indefinitely. Are these the strongest objections? Are there worse ones? Which, if any, are fatal rather than repairable? And after all repairs, does the concept still capture something real about the difference between a long life and a new one?
