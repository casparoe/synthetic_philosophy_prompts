I'm a software engineer, and I sit on the governance committee of a large open-source project — a distributed build and task scheduler with roughly forty regular contributors. Last quarter we went through a bruising RFC process over replacing the core task-queuing model, and the retrospective has split the committee. Several contributors argue the discussion was never decided on the merits: senior maintainers posted far more often, stated their positions with more confidence, and effectively buried a proposal from two newer contributors that, in hindsight, now looks better than the option we adopted. A few of us have been reading about Habermas's ideal speech situation as a lens for this — the idea, as I understand it, being that a decision is legitimate only if those affected by it could accept it after a discussion free of coercion and status, in which only the force of the better argument carries the day. We are now considering a six-month policy change: RFCs would be posted anonymously for the first two weeks, each participant would be limited to a small number of comments per thread per day, and a rotating respondent would be responsible for summarizing and defending minority positions before the maintainers vote. The rest of the committee is opposed. They argue that in an engineering project expertise must be allowed to carry weight, that a discussion is not coercive merely because some contributors know more than others, and that a design ultimately gets tested in code rather than in the symmetry of the conversation.

Draft the committee's decision memo. It should reach a concrete recommendation — adopt the policy, reject it, or adopt it in modified form — with reasoning a group of working engineers could actually act on. Take the Habermasian case seriously without turning the memo into a philosophy seminar, and give an honest presentation of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses. I have no formal background in this, so write it for a committee of mostly practicing engineers.
