A linguist friend of mine has spent the past year collecting verdicts about bare plural sentences, the sort like "Ducks lay eggs" that generalize over a kind without saying "all" or "most." Before she will tell me the theory she is testing, she lets me quiz her on her own judgments. So far she has been remarkably consistent:

- "Ducks lay eggs" — true, even though only female ducks ever lay eggs.
- "Ducks are female" — false, even though roughly half of all ducks are female.
- "Ravens are black" — true.
- "Dogs have four legs" — true, even though a dog that loses a leg does not stop being a dog.
- "Mosquitoes carry malaria" — true, even though fewer than one percent of mosquitoes carry it.
- "Sharks attack bathers" — true, even though almost no shark ever attacks a bather.
- "Canadians are right-handed" — false, even though about nine in ten Canadians are right-handed.

A simple majority rule cannot be what she is running: it would make "Canadians are right-handed" true and "Mosquitoes carry malaria" false, reversing two of her verdicts.

Two rules do fit everything she has said so far, and there may be others. One is a characteristicness rule: "Ks are F" is true just in case F belongs to the characteristic nature or normal condition of Ks — the way egg-laying is characteristic of ducks, blackness of ravens, and four legs of dogs — or, failing that, F is rare but striking for Ks, the way attacking bathers is striking for sharks and carrying malaria for mosquitoes. On this rule, a merely statistical fact about actual Ks, such as right-handedness among Canadians, does not make a generic true.

The other is a contrast rule: "Ks are F" is true just in case F is much more likely among Ks than among non-Ks. On this rule, "Ducks lay eggs" is true because laying eggs is far more likely among ducks than among non-ducks, while "Ducks are female" and "Canadians are right-handed" are false because those properties are no more likely among ducks or Canadians than among things that are not ducks or Canadians.

She has now told me the next sentence she will be asked to judge, and has challenged me to predict her answer before she gives it: "Ellery Street residents are left-handed." Ellery Street is an ordinary residential street with no distinctive institutions. It happens that seventy percent of its one hundred and twenty current residents are left-handed, against about ten percent of the surrounding town's population, because a real-estate agent who represented a circle of left-handed violinists steered them there over two decades. The concentration is an accident of that history.

If my friend is being consistent with her earlier verdicts, what should I expect her to say about "Ellery Street residents are left-handed"? Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and does it actually dictate an answer? If her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say clearly which rule each answer would commit her to, give your best prediction and the reasons for it, and name one further sentence whose verdict would settle which rule she is running.
