I'm a software engineer, so maybe that's why I keep seeing this through an overfitting lens. When someone argues from history, they're usally pattern-matching on a training set of one and calling the result a prediction. A friend says the US/China situation is obviously Munich 1938; another says no, it's 1914; a third says you're both wrong, it's the Thucydides trap — meaning a rising power taking on an established one, so war is more or less baked in. Each of them picks the single historical case whose outcome already matches their preferred policy. Same with interventions: whatever you want to do, there's a precedent — if you hesitate you're at Munich, if you escalate you get a Vietnam-style quagmire. So my running rule is: if the main support for a claim is an analogy to a single famous historical episode, I discount it almost to zero. I don't think the analogy is false exactly; I just treat it as no real evidence unless someone can show me the underlying causal dynamics are the same, not just that the story has the same shape.

My brother says I'm throwing away centuries of collected experience, and that analogical reasoning is the only way to reason about big one-off events, since we can't run controlled experiments on countries. He has a point there. But I've also caught myself being selective: if someone on my side says "Vietnam" I nod along, and if the other side says "Munich" I roll my eyes. So I honestly can't tell whether my rule is correcting for a real failure mode or its just a bias dressed up as a rule. Is the policy defensible as a belief-formation habit?
