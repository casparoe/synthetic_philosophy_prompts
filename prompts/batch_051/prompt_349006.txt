His view, very roughly, is that history is made by an enormous swarm of tiny individual impulses, none of them decisive on its own, and that tracing 1812 to Napoleon's orders is a retrospective illusion. He reaches for a calculus analogy: just as no single infinitesimal gives you the integral, no single famous decision gives you the campaign. At the same time he's clearly worried about free will versus necessity—he seems to want history to be determined by a vast web of causes, but he also wants to hang onto the experience of freedom.

Now the anachronism. After Tolstoy died, chaos theory turned the old for-want-of-a-nail intuition into something you could study mathematically: a deterministic system can be so sensitive to initial conditions that two nearly identical starting points blow apart, and long-term prediction becomes impossible in practice even though everything is still law-governed. The popular image is the butterfly effect.
