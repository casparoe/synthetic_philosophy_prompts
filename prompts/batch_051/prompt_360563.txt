After our seminar on collective responsibility on Thursday, two classmates continued arguing in the hallway and I followed the whole exchange. The case concerned a pharmaceutical company that had, over several years, raised the price of an old, widely prescribed generic by roughly fortyfold, where no single person had ever decided on the increase as a whole: the pricing committee set a revenue target, the finance group modeled what the market would bear, the legal team structured the changes to avoid triggering review, and executives approved each step separately, so that each individual contribution was defensible in isolation. Nadia insisted that it would be a category error to say the company itself is to blame. "Blame is owed only to an agent that can understand the wrong, feel the force of criticism, and answer for what it did. A company has no mind—there is nobody home—so 'blaming' the company is either personification or a dramatic way of saying we want to penalize it. And notice who is actually punished by a fine: shareholders, employees, maybe patients. If blame is supposed to track the wrongdoer, punishing the company punishes a lot of people who didn't do the wrong."

Marcus pushed back that the company does deliberate in a real sense, since it has objectives, decision procedures, an internal flow of information, and a memory that survives the replacement of one executive by the next; the price increase was the output of its procedures, and no individual's act counts as the wrongdoing, while the company's act does. "If only individuals can be blamed, then a harm that was deliberately and predictably produced by an organization has no responsible agent at all—which is the most convenient possible result for the people inside it." Nadia replied that this final move was emotional rather than philosophical: if no individual bears full responsibility, the honest conclusion might be institutional failure without a blameworthy subject, the way a poorly drafted statute can be the product of no one's full intent; an avalanche is a causal process too, and we do not call it blameworthy. Marcus answered that an avalanche does not respond to criticism or change its conduct in light of reasons, whereas a company does—it issues findings, replaces policies, pays restitution, and in that way answers as an entity. "Blame may not require the capacity to feel guilty; it may require being the sort of thing that can recognize that a norm applies to it and modify its behavior accordingly. A corporation can do exactly that through its decision structure, so calling it blameworthy is literal, not metaphorical."

I have been turning the exchange over since then and cannot tell where each of them goes wrong, or whether they are partly right about different things. Could you walk through the back-and-forth and adjudicate it point by point? For each stage, say who is right about what, where the argument overreaches, and what both of them are missing—especially because I have a vague sense that the word "blame" is doing more than one job in this argument, but I cannot make that precise.
