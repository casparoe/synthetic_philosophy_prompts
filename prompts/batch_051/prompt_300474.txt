In the picture-language tradition that descends from Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE, a manual for the design of public-information signs states its fundamental rule:

'A sign belongs to the picture language only when its shape and its place among the other signs suffice for it to be read. The sign always stands for a kind or a relation, never for an individual thing or a passing event; a picture that can be read only with a word beside it is not a sign of the language but an illustration.'

The manual was written for printed statistical charts and exhibition posters; its authors never considered images displayed on a phone.

Now a municipal health office in a city of several hundred first languages sends to every phone a push notification consisting of three emoji and nothing else: a face in a medical mask, a red droplet, and two upright figures standing a measured gap apart. The office intends the sequence as the instruction to wear a mask, mind the droplets, and keep the gap. It soon emerges that, on one operating system, the red droplet is read as a teardrop and, on another, as a drop of blood, while some users describe the two figures as dancing. The office will also place the red droplet on printed posters directly beneath the word 'droplets'; on the phones it appears alone. The designers say these are emoji with socially circulating meanings, not the manual's signs, so the rule does not reach them. A critic says the rule was written for exactly this dependence.

Does the rule apply to the phone glyphs? Give, in turn, the reading from the text of the rule, from its purpose, from what the manual's authors would have said had they been asked, and from what a reasonable reader of the manual would take it to mean. Which reading should control here, and what does the gap between the office's intention and the users' reception show about what the rule was always meant to protect?
