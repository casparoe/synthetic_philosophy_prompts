I co-run a monthly philosophy discussion group at my company. The regulars are mostly software engineers and product managers, 12 to 18 people, none with formal philosophy training, and we have 90 minutes. I want to run one session on the Marxian ideas of exploitation and alienation, but I want the group to feel the pull of the ideas themselves before anyone names them. I have read some Marx on my own but have no formal background, so the activity needs to carry the conceptual weight rather than depending on precise lecturing at the end.

Design a runnable production-line game for this group, plus a debrief. The through-line should be that exploitation and alienation are distinct phenomena, and that both can emerge from the structure of a workplace — specifically from who owns the tools, who controls the pace and design of work, and how the product is sold — rather than from anyone's bad character. The participants should construct the sequence themselves and only notice at the end where they have arrived. Do not let the facilitator use the words exploitation or alienation until the debrief.

The game should be playable in one room with ordinary materials. It should have at least three rounds:

1. A round in which each participant owns their own tools and materials, makes a small craft themselves — something simple, like a folded paper object — sells it, and keeps the full sale price.
2. A round in which the tools and materials are owned by one or two participants, everyone else sells labor time for a fixed wage, and the owner sells the output. The owner keeps whatever remains after paying wages and input costs. The wage and price schedule should be set so the arithmetic makes the remainder visible: workers produce more value per hour than they are paid, and the owner's income comes from that gap.
3. A round in which the owner also controls the design and pace of the work, each worker repeatedly performs one narrow subtask without seeing or completing the whole product, and workers can be replaced if they are too slow. The round should be structured so the product becomes someone else's thing, the activity becomes mechanical, and the relation among workers becomes a competition not to be replaced.

Use small real stakes so the reactions are genuine — actual small cash, or tokens exchangeable for something worth having, not points that count for nothing. Include a concrete price, wage, piece-rate, and materials-cost schedule for each round. The person introducing each new round should present it neutrally, as an efficiency improvement or a change in funding, not as a moral setup.

Then write the debrief. It should map specific moments in the activity onto specific concepts: the moment in round 2 when someone notices the owner's take, onto the idea that exploitation is the appropriation of the gap between the value workers create and the wage they receive, even inside a voluntary-looking contract; and the round 3 experience of narrowing, opacity, and replaceability, onto alienation as estrangement from the product, from the activity of working, from other workers, and from one's own human capacities. Show where each step can be resisted and what it costs to resist it there — for example, the contract is voluntary and the owner contributes tools, coordination, and risk; specialization raises total output and may leave everyone materially better off; competition between owners can bid wages up; and workers could refuse to enter rounds 2 and 3. Say which of these resistances the structure of the game actually lets players exercise and which it forecloses.

Be frank about what the game cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply in the debrief: the absence of real subsistence pressure, since no participant actually depends on the wage; the fact that real production involves machinery, materials, ideas, and borrowed capital, so the game's labor-only accounting is an artifact; the historical scale of factory systems and labor markets; and the difference between a 45-minute artificial workplace and a lifetime of work. Also say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run — for example, the owner players may be too generous or too apologetic, workers may collude or sabotage, the stakes may be too small to produce genuine resentment, or the group may defuse the tension by treating the whole thing as a joke — and give concrete rules for handling each of these failures.
