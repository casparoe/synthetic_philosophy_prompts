I own a small pest-control company. My technicians run about forty jobs a week across private homes, restaurants, and two assisted-living facilities. Most treatment decisions are made on the spot, by people who are licensed to handle pesticides but have no training in ethics, and I only hear about the unusual ones. A single job can involve a child with a severe sting allergy, or a state food-safety inspection three weeks away, or a client who simply doesn't want ants in his yard. The trade has a default: reach for the fastest legal chemical that works. Cost is part of it — a client will pay more readily for a four-minute spray than for an hour of exclusion work and follow-up monitoring — but I've been trying to change that, and I keep discovering that I don't have an actual procedure for deciding when a gentler method is enough and when it isn't, so my technicians fall back on habit.

I have no formal background in philosophy, but I've ended up thinking about this on my own. From what I can gather, the honest state of knowledge is something like this: many insects are nociceptive, meaning their bodies respond to tissue damage — they will withdraw from heat, groom a damaged leg, avoid a place where they were hurt — but we don't know whether there is anything it is like to be an insect. Some of that behavior may be pure reflex. On the other hand, insects will sometimes trade off avoiding injury against feeding or mating in the moment, which at least keeps the door open to something more than reflex. I also can't avoid noticing that moral importance seems to track the capacity to suffer rather than being human or cute or useful, and that genuine uncertainty about whether a creature suffers is not the same as knowing it doesn't. But "I don't know" can't simply be treated as "no," and "it might suffer" can't be treated as "it suffers like I do." That is the principle I want the procedure to be faithful to.

Write me a decision procedure my technicians could actually use at the door where the infestation is. Put the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, say where the procedure stops with a no, and say where it has to hand the decision to a person — me, or the client, or a facility director — because the question has become one of values rather than facts. Keep it usable for someone with a sprayer in the truck and no taxonomy of insect nervous systems. At the same time, keep it honest: tell me which steps lean on a philosophical commitment, what the procedure cannot settle, and where it is likely to misfire.

Then run the procedure on these three jobs and show the reasoning at each step:

1. Paper wasps have built a nest inside a soffit void directly above the entrance of a home daycare. A child who attends has a documented history of anaphylaxis from a wasp sting and carries an auto-injector. Two children were stung near the entrance last month. The nest cannot be removed intact because it is inside the void and the homeowner will not allow the wall to be opened. The remaining options are a residual dust applied into the void, which will kill the colony over a day or so, or a repellent spray at the entrance, which will kill foragers but leave the nest alive and defending the entrance.

2. An assisted-living facility has a light, localized German cockroach infestation in the kitchen. A state inspection is three weeks away, and the facility's standard is effectively zero tolerance. Sanitation, sealing, and a targeted gel-bait program are very likely to get the population under control but not certain to eliminate it before the inspection. A crack-and-crevice application of a fast-acting residual is much more likely to pass inspection, and both are legal. No resident has become ill; the urgency is the inspection date.

3. A homeowner with a sound foundation, no young children, and no allergies has a large anthill at the far end of the backyard, well away from the house, kitchen, and walkway. He wants the colony eliminated because he hates having ants on the property. My technicians would normally sell him a perimeter spray.

Show how the procedure handles each case, including any step where it gives an answer we will not like, and any step where it refuses to settle the matter and tells us to make the call ourselves.
