Some readers of Mill’s posthumously published Chapters on Socialism take him to be moving, however cautiously, toward an eventual endorsement of a transformed property system. Others read the same pages as a sympathetic but ultimately defensive account of why private property, reformed rather than abolished, remains the safer path. A third group says the pieces are so internally unstable that they cannot settle either question. The dispute has stalled: the same passages get invoked on all sides, and no one’s reading shifts. The passage I keep returning to is near the end, where Mill frames the practical question as depending on the intellectual and moral condition of the working population—some readers treat that condition as an obstacle to socialism, others as a reason to prepare for it. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about what Mill believed in his last years but about what kind of text this is and what we should use it for: a final doctrinal position, a set of revision notes, or a diagnosis of tendencies in his own time. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement, and say what, given your diagnosis, would actually count as progress.
