In my metaphysics seminar we have been working on the identity of indiscernibles — the principle that no two distinct objects can share all of their pure, qualitative properties — and last week we focused on Max Black's two-sphere universe: a possible world containing nothing but two spheres, perfectly alike in size, shape, material, and every other feature one could specify without naming a particular place or sphere. After class, two of my classmates, Rachel and Sam, carried the argument into the corridor. Rachel said the scenario simply refutes the principle as a necessary truth: the two spheres are distinct, yet no pure description true of one is false of the other, so nothing qualitative makes them two things rather than one. Sam replied that the spheres are not really indiscernible, because each occupies a different region of space; position is a property, and each sphere has the property of being at its own place, which the other lacks. Rachel answered that once positional properties like being at region R are admitted, the principle becomes trivially true of any two distinct objects whatsoever, since every object is trivially at its own location; the principle was meant to say something deeper than that — namely, that qualitative duplication forces numerical identity — and Black's scenario shows exactly that no qualitative fact separates the spheres. Sam then shifted ground and said that, even setting location aside, the two spheres stand to each other in a purely qualitative, symmetric, irreflexive relation — being spatially separated from another sphere — which cannot hold in a world with only one sphere. Perhaps the principle should be weakened so that distinctness requires only that kind of structure: an irreflexive relation holding between two things, rather than a property that one thing has and the other lacks. Rachel countered that this merely redraws the battle lines: the relation is symmetric, so it gives neither sphere a feature the other lacks, and a principle weakened that far would be satisfied by nearly any pair of distinct objects. I could see what each of them was driving at, and I came away unsure who had the better argument.
