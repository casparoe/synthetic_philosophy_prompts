I have been arguing with a colleague about whether algorithmic information theory actually vindicates Occam's razor, and we have reduced our disagreement to the following argument, which he accepts and I find difficult to refute.

(1) Occam's razor says that, among hypotheses that fit the observed data equally well, we should prefer the simplest hypothesis. (2) Solomonoff induction assigns to each computable hypothesis h a prior probability proportional to 2^-K(h), where K(h) is the length of the shortest program that outputs h's predictions. (3) So hypotheses with shorter programs receive exponentially greater prior probability. (4) A hypothesis's simplicity just is the shortness of its shortest program. (5) If two hypotheses fit the data equally well, their likelihoods are equal, so their posterior probabilities are proportional to their priors. (6) Therefore, among hypotheses that fit the data equally well, the one with the shortest program is most probable. (7) Hence Solomonoff induction provides a rigorous foundation for Occam's razor.

I can see that the term simple might be doing two different jobs in ordinary scientific practice and in algorithmic information theory, but my colleague replies that step (4) is a stipulation that operationalizes a vague term, not an empirical claim. Is the argument valid? If not, identify exactly which step fails and explain why. If the argument can be repaired, give the strongest repaired version and state precisely what it would and would not establish.
