Since retiring from a career in biology, I have been reading the philosophical literature on biological individuality, and I keep encountering an evolutionary argument for where to draw the boundary of an organism. A multicellular body, the argument runs, counts as one organism because its cells are genetic near-clones whose reproductive fortunes rise and fall together; selection can therefore suppress conflict among them and shape them into a functionally integrated whole, whereas a loose collection of genetically distinct individuals fails this test because their interests can diverge. The specific development that unsettles me is the holobiont picture, on which many familiar organisms (corals, legumes, perhaps ourselves) function only in partnership with persistent, partly inherited microbial symbionts that are genetically foreign and supply functions the host cannot perform alone. Taken literally, the old argument seems to force an awkward choice: either the host alone is the organism and its microbes are merely environment, or the whole consortium is the organism and genetic commonality has quietly been abandoned as the criterion. Does the argument survive this development, does it need repair, or was genetic identity always a rough proxy for something such as functional integration or shared fate that holobionts merely expose? I am less interested in the empirical catalogue than in what the criterion would commit us to counting as an individual, and whether that commitment is defensible.
