A student of mine has gone full stoic-bro after a reading group on Marcus Aurelius, and he has one image he won't put down: the mind is the sky, and emotions are the weather. Anger, grief, fear are clouds and storms that arise, drift across, and pass, while the sky itself is never damaged or improved by any of it. His punchline is a strong version of apatheia, freedom from disturbance: the sage who stops identifying with the weather loses nothing that was ever his, because the self is the sky. I objected in seminar that this quietly imports a picture of the self as something permanent and untouched; some emotions are more like seasons or floods that change a landscape than weather passing over it. Grief can reconfigure the person, not just happen to them. He said I was bending the analogy rather than breaking it, and I want to bring back a real audit next week.

So here is what I need. Lay out the correspondence table as its used in the popular stoic case for equanimity: what maps to the self, to emotions, to particular passions, to the sage's calm, to the advice to let things pass. Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for the conclusion — that apatheia costs nothing important — and the ones that are harmless or merely cosmetic. Be explicit about what the sky quietly imports: permanence, passivity, separateness from everything that passes over it. Then tell me how far the analogy actually holds, whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if the analogy fails, offer a better one or say why no analogy of this shape could settle whether freedom from disturbance is a good worth its cost.
