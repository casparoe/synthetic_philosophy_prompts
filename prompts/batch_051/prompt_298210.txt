I have no academic background in philosophy. Some weeks ago I listened to an argument between my cousin Elena and her friend Rafiq that began as a familiar disagreement about sweatshops and then moved into something I found much harder. I have been turning the question over on my own since then, and I would like you to adjudicate the argument.

The case they were arguing about was this. Imagine a sportswear company that owns a factory in a country where much of the working-age population depends on informal labour. The factory pays its workers roughly three dollars a day. For most of these workers, the best available alternative is informal work that pays roughly a dollar and a half a day under worse conditions. The workers are legally free to leave, and most say they are glad to have the jobs. The factory is comfortably profitable, and the company could pay noticeably higher wages without serious risk to the operation.

Elena argued that the company is exploiting its workers. The pay, she said, is far below a plausible living wage, constitutes a tiny fraction of the value of the goods the workers produce, and could be raised without much risk to the business. The fact that the workers accept the terms does not settle whether the terms are fair: a person with bad alternatives can rationally accept a bad offer, and an offer can be the best available and still be a bad one. An offer a person cannot reasonably refuse, she added, is exactly the kind of offer that deserves moral scrutiny.

Rafiq disagreed. He said the right comparison is not an ideal wage but the worker's actual alternatives. If a job leaves a person better off than she would otherwise have been, calling the employer an exploiter seems to turn the moral judgment on its head: the employer has added an option where there was none. He argued further that the obvious responses — public condemnation or pressure to pay far higher wages — could easily make workers worse off. The company might automate, move the factory somewhere cheaper, or hire fewer people, in which case the supposedly exploitative job was doing real work and attacking it harms the very people we meant to help.

Elena came back with a comparison. She said a boatman who charges a drowning vacationer everything she has is still doing wrong, even though the vacationer is better off than she would have been without the ride. The wrongness, she said, does not lie in making a person worse off than before; it lies in using her desperation as leverage. The company does something similar when it sets wages with one eye on the fact that its workers' other options are miserable. That, she said, is close to the ordinary definition of taking advantage.

Rafiq conceded that the boatman is doing something wrong, but said the cases differ in the way that matters. The boatman produces nothing and merely inserts himself between a desperate person and safety; the employer has built and is running a factory that produces goods, work, and incomes. The employer did not cause the background poverty, and the wage it offers is set by the alternatives the workers actually have. He added that if the standard is that anyone who benefits from the fact that others are poor and does not pay them more than the going local rate is an exploiter, the word would have to cover every farmer and shopkeeper in the region, which is too broad to be useful. And he pressed his practical point: suppose we grant that the wage is exploitative — what follows, given that the obvious responses could leave the workers worse off?

Elena answered that using poverty is different from causing it, and that the moral character of the transaction is one question while the practical question of what to do is another. A person can condemn the deal as exploitative while honestly admitting that a boycott or a wage mandate might backfire; a bad situation can contain no clean options. But conceding that the options are bad, she said, does not turn a predatory bargain into a fair one.

The argument ended there. Elena maintained that a transaction can leave both parties better off than they would have been without it and still be exploitative. Rafiq maintained that once you grant that a job is the best option available and that interfering with it would likely worsen the workers' situation, the charge has been gutted. They asked me which of them was right, and I could not decide.

Please adjudicate the disagreement as it was actually argued, not as a general question about global labour. I want to know who is right about which specific claim, where each argument overreaches, and where they are talking past one another. I am not asking for references; I want the case decided on the arguments given.

Structure your response under these five headings, in this order:

1. The dispute restated (about 150 words): set out each person's position and the question genuinely at issue, without adding new arguments.
2. The case for Elena (about 250 words): give the strongest version of her view as she argued it.
3. The case for Rafiq (about 250 words): give the strongest version of his view as he argued it.
4. Point-by-point adjudication (about 450 words): go through the exchange move by move and state who is right about which claim, where each overreaches, and which disagreements are merely verbal.
5. Overall verdict (about 120 words): say who is more right overall, and name the main thing both sides are missing.
