You are a senior researcher in algorithmic fairness who designs training exercises for regulators auditing credit-lending systems. The exercise you are about to build targets a tempting closure assumption: that if a lending rule is fair with respect to each of two protected attributes separately, then it is automatically fair with respect to the combinations of those attributes. Take your time, and think step by step. Before choosing numbers, think through how two marginal equalities constrain the four possible intersectional approval rates; only then choose your counts.

Construct a toy lending example satisfying all of the following specifications. Work only from the definitions given; do not cite or look up any sources, and generate all numbers yourself.

1. The population is partitioned by two binary attributes, R and G. Treat them as abstract stand-ins for two protected classifications; do not attach them to any real-world group. The decision is binary: approve or deny a loan. Report the population as whole-number counts, with no more than 2,000 applicants in total. You may give the number approved in each cell directly; you do not need to specify a score threshold, and you may ignore loan repayment for the core construction.

2. The rule satisfies exact demographic parity for each of the two attributes separately. By demographic parity I mean that the approval rate among R=1 applicants, pooling over both values of G, equals the approval rate among R=0 applicants; and likewise the approval rate among G=1 applicants, pooling over both values of R, equals the approval rate among G=0 applicants. The equalities must be exact.

3. The four R-by-G intersections are clearly not all treated alike. In particular, the cell R=1, G=1 — the group disadvantaged on both attributes — must have an approval rate strictly lower than each of the other three cells, and at least 25 percentage points below the population-wide approval rate. In addition, the population-wide approval rate must be between 20% and 50%, and at least one of the other three cells must have an approval rate above 60%. These are population values, not sample estimates.

Then deliver the following:

- Show the full arithmetic: a table of the four cells with their sizes, numbers approved, and approval rates; the two marginal approval rates for R and G; and the population-wide approval rate.
- Verify that every specification in (1)–(3) is met.
- Explain which structural feature of the case is doing the work — that is, what allows two separate marginal parity checks both to pass while a large intersectional disparity remains. Then say what you would have to alter to make the disparity disappear, and what the resulting numbers would look like if that feature were removed.
- Give one honest defense that a proponent of single-axis demographic parity audits could offer in response to this very case — a reason to regard the intersectional disparity as a monitoring artifact rather than a proven instance of discrimination.
- Finally, say (i) what argument or consideration would change your mind about classifying this case as intersectionally unfair, and (ii) where the reasoning in your construction is most likely to fail.

The case is for a workshop module on why auditing separately by two protected attributes can still miss what happens at their intersection, so the disparity must be generated by the numbers rather than stipulated by fiat.
