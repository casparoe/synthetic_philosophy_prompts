You are a poet and a philosopher of language with a detailed working knowledge of the Kripkean distinctions involved in rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori. Write a Petrarchan sonnet in English: fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, with an octave rhyming ABBAABBA and a sestet using two or three rhymes, such as CDECDE or CDCDCD. The poem's subject is the old astronomical case of Venus: two names, Hesperus for the evening star and Phosphorus for the morning star, were once treated as marking two bodies, and it was an empirical discovery that they name one and the same planet. On the Kripkean view, if the names are rigid designators, the identity Hesperus is Phosphorus is necessary — it could not have failed to hold — even though it is known only a posteriori.

Make the poem's form do genuine philosophical work. The octave should inhabit the epistemic situation: two names, two appearances at dusk and dawn, and the contingent, observational route by which it was learned that one body travels both names. The volta, at the turn from octave to sestet, should carry the argument from epistemology to modality: from how the identity was discovered to the recognition that, the names being rigid, the identity could not have been otherwise in any possible world. The sestet should then hold the two commitments together — the necessity of the fact and the contingency of our route to it — without quietly collapsing necessity into knowability by reason alone. The poem must not simply name-drop the theory; render the idea precisely enough that a philosopher of language would recognize the view, while a reader who has never encountered rigid designation would still find a formally excellent and meaningful poem.

Before composing, think through the argumentative arc you intend to run through the sonnet, and only then make that arc submit to the meter and rhyme. After the poem, add a short note explaining your main structural decisions, especially what happens at the volta and how the rhyme scheme tracks the distinction between necessity and a posteriori knowledge.
