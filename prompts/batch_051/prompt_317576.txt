I help supervise a team of junior attorneys at a legal aid clinic, and in the last year the default has become: an LLM writes the first draft of almost everything, and the juniors edit it. We check the citations, so I'm less worried about hallucinated cases and more worried about something slower and harder to see — if the model always does the first pass, the juniors stop building the kind of understanding you only get by drafting badly yourself, and in five years we'll have senior attorneys who can't tell when a confident-sounding brief is legal nonsense. The thing I keep coming back to is the difference between being able to produce the right result and being able to tell when a result is wrong. I can see that distinction clearly for about five minutes and then it slips, and I'm not sure how to turn it into an actual policy.

So: give me a reusable decision procedure for which tasks a junior must do without AI at all, which they can use AI for but only after doing a manual first pass (or under some other constraint), and which they can fully delegate. It has to be something a supervising attorney can actually run in a clinic where clients face evictions and the caseload is heavy, so it can't just be 'no AI, ever.' Ground it in whatever epistemology and philosophy of mind actually know about understanding versus mere information, cognitive offloading, and deskilling — but I need the short version: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands off to a supervisor because its a judgment call. Then run it on three cases: drafting a routine demand letter in a landlord-tenant matter; doing legal research in an area the junior has never touched before; and preparing a client to testify. Make sure at least one of the worked examples comes out against your own or my likely inclination, and say explicitly what the procedure can't catch — the failure modes, not just the strengths.
