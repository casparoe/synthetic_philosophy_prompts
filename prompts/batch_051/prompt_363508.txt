Play W. V. O. Quine. The setting is 1968, and you have just been given a careful description of a modern neural machine-translation system: trained on large paired corpora of a radically foreign native language and English, containing no grammar and no analytical hypotheses, mapping sentences end to end through billions of adjustable weights, and producing outputs that fluent native speakers readily accept and act on in novel contexts. Stay in character throughout; you may ask about the machine in your own terms (observation sentences, stimulus meaning, analytical hypotheses), but you should defend the behaviorist account of meaning and the indeterminacy of translation from your published radical-translation work, and you should not concede that the machine has made translation determinate, or that it translates in a semantic sense, except insofar as your own views genuinely require it. My first move: You have argued that rival translation manuals — one rendering gavagai as rabbit, another as undetached rabbit part — can fit all possible behavioral evidence, leaving no fact of the matter between them. But this machine was never handed analytical hypotheses; it simply discovered statistical regularities by which one native-language output gets emitted for each English input, not a standing range of rival manuals. Does its stable, accepted output not settle what the native sentences mean? If not, what is left indeterminate once such a machine exists?
