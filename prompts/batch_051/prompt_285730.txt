Okay here's one that's been bugging me. The standard setup in inverse reinforcement learning—Ng and Russell's old paper basically sets it up this way—is that you observe an agent acting optimally and you try to recover the reward function it's optimizing. I've seen people talk as if the output of an IRL system is the agent's preferences: not just a summary of what the agent does, but what it actually cares about. That move seems way too fast to me. But I'm not sure whether it's just loose talk or whether there's a testable claim hiding in there.

I want you to design the strongest test you can of the claim that a reward function learned by IRL from behavior recovers the agent's preferences, rather than merely predicting or compactly redesribing its behavior. Here's how I want the response structured, with rough lengths:

1. The claim (200 words) — state exactly what 'recovers preferences' would have to mean for the test to be interesting, and distinguish it from the weaker claim that the learned reward predicts behavior. If you need to, distinguish known unidentifiabilities (e.g. there's a whole family of reward functions consistent with the same optimal policy) from the claim itself.

2. The test (350 words) — make it concrete. Assume you can train agents on known reward functions in defined MDPs, generate their behavior, and then run IRL. What experiment or comparison would give the strongest possible evidence that the IRL output is closer to the true reward than to merely behavior-equivalent alternatives? Specify the train/test setup, the environments, the evaluation metric, and what result you'd expect if the claim is true.

3. What a positive result would show (150 words) — if the test comes out in favor, what conclusion can we actually draw? Be careful: a positive result could just show the inductive bias of the IRL algorithm happens to pick out the true reward in your test environments.

4. What a positive result wouldn't show (150 words) — what conceptual or empirical gap remains? This should include the problem that 'preference' is itself a mentalistic notion, and the possibility that apparent success doesn't generalize to agents whose preferences are inconsistent, changing, or shaped by the environment in ways not captured by a stationary reward function.

5. The residue (100 words) — say plainly what part of the claim no test of this kind could settle even in principle, and whether we should therefore stop saying 'the agent's preferences' and say something weaker, or whether the mentalistic language is fine as long as we're clear about what the test secured.
