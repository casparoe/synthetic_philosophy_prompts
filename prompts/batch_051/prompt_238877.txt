I'm a novelist, and in the current draft there's a dinner-party scene where a translator character who's spent years on the School of Names tries to run the old white horse argument on a skeptical painter. I don't want the painter's objections to be stupid, so I've been trying to write down the underlying reasoning as cleanly as I can, and I keep landing on this:

(1) The word "horse" picks out a shape, or at least a kind of animal considered apart from its color. (2) The word "white" picks out a color. (3) Picking out a color is not the same as picking out a shape, so "white horse" picks out something that "horse" alone doesn't. (4) What "white horse" picks out is therefore not what "horse" picks out. (5) And so a white horse is not a horse.

I actually think the argument is valid, and that people who resist it are just smuggling in the common-sense reading of "not" after step four already did the work. Am I right? If not, tell me exactly where it goes wrong and on what reading of "not" or "picks out" it goes wrong — is it an equivocation between an object and the kind it belongs to, or something else? I'd rather have the character be wrong in a precise way then have him just talk in riddles.
