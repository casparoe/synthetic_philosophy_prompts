You are grading an upper-level undergraduate exam in a course on phenomenology. The question and one student's answer are provided below.

Exam question: Explain Husserl's epoché and its relation to the phenomenological reduction. Your answer should address (i) what, precisely, the epoché suspends; (ii) whether the epoché is a form of Cartesian doubt; and (iii) what the phenomenological reduction adds beyond the epoché.

Student's answer:
The epoché is Husserl's procedure of bracketing the general thesis of the natural attitude: the tacit commitment, operative in everyday life and in the positive sciences, that the world is simply there. To perform the epoché is not to deny that the world exists. I do not cancel this thesis or replace it with its contrary; I put it out of action and abstain from any judgment that relies on it, so that no existential positing is allowed to count. The epoché is therefore not Cartesian doubt. Descartes resolves to treat as false whatever admits of the slightest doubt, thereby turning the world's existence into something to be established; Husserl, by contrast, leaves the world standing as it appears while withholding assent from the existential claim itself. Once this suspension has been carried out, the epoché leads over to the phenomenon by way of a provisional doubt about the world's being. In the reduction I turn away from the world as existing and attend instead to the way the world's sense is constituted in the empirical stream of my own conscious life. Objects become phenomena in the sense of nothing but intentional contents of an individual mind, and the subject reached by the reduction is the reflecting psychological self, now made thematic from the inside. The epoché is best understood as the negative condition of this move, while the reduction is the positive work of describing what comes into view once the natural attitude has been neutralized.
