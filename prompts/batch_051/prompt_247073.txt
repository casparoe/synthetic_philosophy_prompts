I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and in that setting parsimony functioned for me as a genuine but defeasible virtue: with fit held equal I preferred the model with fewer parameters, and I would sometimes choose a slightly less accurate but simpler model on the expectation that it would generalize. Since retiring I have turned to moral philosophy, and I find that my attitude toward simplicity in ethical theory does not cohere. I have recorded my own gut verdicts below, and I would be grateful for help in extracting the principle behind them, if there is one.

On some occasions fit with firm intuitions beats parsimony decisively. Act-utilitarianism is admirably economical, yet I cannot accept its implications about the innocent man whose conviction will prevent a riot or the deathbed promise broken because slightly more good will come of breaking it; I would rather keep a Ross-style list of prima facie duties, complicated as it is, than surrender those intuitions. On other occasions parsimony wins: when two theories capture my considered judgments equally well, I prefer the simpler without hesitation — I would, for instance, merge gratitude and reparation into a single duty of responding fittingly to what others have done for me, since none of my judgments seems to require the split. But I am also, disturbingly, unimpressed when a distinction buys only a little fit: the familiar doctrine of double effect, with its distinction between intended and foreseen harm, would reconcile my verdicts in the trolley cases, yet it feels like an ad hoc patch, and I am tempted instead to abandon the one recalcitrant intuition about pushing the man off the footbridge and keep the simpler numbers-based view. That third verdict collides with the first two, and I cannot say why. I also apply diminishing returns: between a one-principle theory that captures perhaps eighty percent of my considered judgments, a two-principle theory that captures ninety-five, and a three-principle theory that captures ninety-six, I choose the middle one, though this rests on a complexity budget I could not state. Finally, my preference for parsimony is stronger in physics than in ethics, and I do not know whether that asymmetry is justified; in physics simple models are preferred because they tend to generalize, but ethics offers no analogous out-of-sample test.

The case I keep returning to is this. Suppose a theory states a single master principle — for example, that persons may never be used merely as means — but it achieves fit with concrete cases only through a large apparatus of interpretation: specifications, exceptions, and sub-rules for applying the principle. Is that theory parsimonious because it has one principle, or complex because its interpretive machinery is as long as a pluralist's list of duties? I would like a precise account of what work simplicity should do in moral theory choice, stated so that it implies and explains my verdicts where they are defensible and identifies the ones I must abandon; I would also like to know what that account says about further cases I have not considered, and specifically how it classifies the one-principle-with-elaborate-machinery theory.
