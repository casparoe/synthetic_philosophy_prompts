I'm a PhD student in epistemology, working on self-knowledge, and I've been keeping a list of my raw verdicts on cases about knowing your own state of mind. They don't obviously add up, so I want you to extract the principle behind them, or tell me which verdicts I have to drop.

Here they are, roughly as I actually wrote them down:

1. I have a mild headache right now. I know I'm in pain, and I also know that I know I'm in pain. Iterating this a few steps feels unproblematic.
2. Standing outside in freezing weather, I'm clearly cold. I know I'm cold, and I know that I know I'm cold. No issue.
3. But in a room that warms up extremely gradually over an hour from cold to warm, I can't pick out a moment where it stops being true that I know I'm cold. If you quizzed me every few seconds, I'd start hedging at some point and then stop saying I know. I want to say there's no first moment at which I fail to know I'm cold, and no last moment at which I know I'm cold: knowledge of your own state seems to need a margin for error. Yet I'd also admit that at the start I'm cold and at the end I'm not, so somewhere in that series the switch must happen. Those two thoughts feel inconsistent, and that's the main thing I want fixed.
4. A student freezes in an oral exam and can't produce a proof. Afterwards she reconstructs it easily and says she knew it all along—she just didn't know that she knew it. I think that's possible and familiar, but it seems to contradict the idea that knowing guarantees you're in a position to know that you know.
5. A nervous actor knows every line but is convinced before walking on stage that she's forgotten them. I say she knows them but doesn't know that she knows them. A supervisor I mentioned this to says that's contradictory. I don't see why.
6. For a clear fact like 2+2=4, iterating knowledge feels fine all the way up: I know it, I know I know it, I know I know I know it. But for a borderline mental state like feeling cold, it stops making sense after one or two steps; I genuinely can't say whether I know whether I know whether I'm cold.

I keep running into the KK principle—knowing implies you're in a position to know that you know—and the oppositve idea that pretty much no interesting condition is luminous, where a condition is luminous iff whenever it obtains you're in a position to know it obtains. My verdicts seem to cut across both.

Is there a principle that captures all or most of these? If it captures most of them, which one or two verdicts does it force me to drop, and why? And what does your principle say about a case I can't settle: a person with blindsight who says I'm just guessing, but there's a dot on the left, and is right far more often than chance. Do they know there's a dot? Do they know that they know?

Please end your answer with a machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the principle you think fits my verdicts best, or \answer{no single principle} if that's your view.
