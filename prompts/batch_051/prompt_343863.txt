During lunch last week, two friends at my high school got into an argument after one of them discovered that a weather app he had used for years had been selling his precise location history to data brokers. He said it felt as if something had been taken from him. The other replied that the feeling was understandable, but that the underlying claim—that information about you is your property—did not, in her view, survive scrutiny. They went back and forth through four rounds and then asked me to referee. I could not settle it, so I am asking you to adjudicate the exchange.

(1) Amir said: information about me is mine in roughly the way my belongings are; when a company quietly sells it, it has taken value I produced and used it without any real awareness on my part. It should either obtain genuine permission each time or compensate me.

Lena replied: you cannot own facts about yourself any more than you can own the fact that it rained yesterday. The company participated in the interaction and logged what happened; calling that theft assumes there is an object that was taken, when what exists is only a record kept by the other party—like a shopkeeper remembering a regular customer.

(2) Amir answered: a shopkeeper's memory stays in one head, but a profile sold to hundreds of brokers is the industrial extraction of a life pattern. A person should be able to license their own data trail, the way workers sell their labor.

Lena replied: a data trail is not produced by you alone. It records the stores you walked into, other people's Wi-Fi networks, a phone someone else built, and words other people typed. Most personal data is jointly produced, so treating it as individual property would hand each of us power over facts about everyone else. In any case, you consented when you clicked agree.

(3) Amir replied: that consent is close to forced. When refusing means being shut out of ordinary life—school, work, communication—ticking a box is not a free sale. Paying people would at least turn a hollow gesture into a real transaction.

Lena answered: you are already paid, in the form of free maps, search, and messaging; preferring cash does not make the current arrangement theft. And the jointness point is the serious one: if I sell my contacts or location, I am selling information about my friends, none of whom agreed.

(4) Amir came back: that is actually a stronger objection to the current system than to property. Right now one company disposes of facts jointly produced by dozens of people. If individuals cannot sell alone, that is a reason for shared or collective control over such data, not for leaving companies as the only ones who profit.

Lena answered: shared ownership of every fact you appear in would make ordinary life impossible—you could not tell a story about your own day without negotiating with everyone mentioned in it. So ownership is the wrong lens altogether. What we need is regulation of harmful uses, not property rights in facts.

Please go through the exchange statement by statement. For each of Amir's four statements and each of Lena's four replies, assign one of the following verdicts—clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, or clearly right—and justify it briefly. Then say who is more right overall, or whether the correct position requires something neither of them defended, and identify what both are missing.
