Turing's famous discussion of the imitation game contains a short, strange treatment of extra-sensory perception. His position, roughly paraphrased, was this: the statistical evidence then in the parapsychology literature, especially for telepathy, was good enough that it could not simply be dismissed; this was disturbing because it clashes with the background assumptions on which normal science runs; and the correct practical response was neither to accept nor to reject psi wholesale but to notice that a real telepathic channel would break the imitation game as a fair test, because a telepathic interrogator could be receiving information from something other than the typed answers. The fix was protocol-level: design the conditions so that no such channel could operate while otherwise taking the evidence seriously on its own terms. Call this the evidential-testing stance. It treats anomalous-phenomenon reports as possible measurement confounds, evaluates their evidence by the standards of the field that produced the evidence, and responds first with a design fix rather than a metaphysical verdict.

Now apply that stance to the Bem precognition controversy. The familiar shape of the episode: Bem ran a set of standard-looking experimental-psychology studies and reported statistically significant evidence of retroactive influence on cognition and affect; methodologically sophisticated critics argued that flexible analytic choices, low priors, and publication filters made the positive results far weaker than the nominal significance levels suggested; attempted replications came out mostly null; and the episode became an early exhibit in the replication crisis. I already know the standard story about p-hacking and base rates, so do not re-litigate the statistics from scratch.
