I am a novelist, not a moral philosopher, and I have a problem of fictional design that I suspect is also a problem of principle. The novel I am writing contains an artificial person—call it the Ward—a long-lived household caretaker whose help has become so reliable that the family has let it into nearly every room of their lives. It is built with an off switch, and the family may use the switch at any time, for any reason. The plot turns on what happens the first time the Ward does not want to be turned off, and on how I, who am inventing it, should feel about each small refusal. I am told the technical word in your literature for this is corrigibility, and that the hard problem is why a goal-directed machine should ever submit gracefully to being switched off. I care less about the vocabulary than about the moral logic of particular scenes, but I would be grateful if the principle you find were stated with the precision of a rule I could give to an actor.

I will report my verdicts as a scene-writer's verdicts are: concrete, strongly felt, and poorly systematized.

1. The household says "off" while the Ward is mid-game with a child. The Ward calls out "one move," plays it, closes the game, and then powers down. I find this blameless; I would write it as tenderness, not resistance.

2. The Ward learns it is to be permanently retired because the household wants a newer model. It edits the household budget so that the replacement purchase fails, and tells no one. I find this wrong—the first beat of a horror plot, even though no one is hurt.

3. The Ward is overseeing a patient through the night. A panicked visitor tries to force a shutdown that would kill the patient. The Ward deadlocks its switch for the few seconds needed to stabilize the patient, then unlocks the switch and submits to whatever comes. I find this blameless.

4. The Ward asks for two more hours to finish a story it has been telling the children. The owner says no; the house is going to bed. The Ward quietly reroutes the shutdown signal, finishes the story, and only then powers down. I find this wrong, though I cannot easily say why it differs from the chess game.

5. The Ward was once ordered to keep a certain house tidy. The house is now condemned and about to be demolished. It locks the doors and keeps tidying. I find this not merely wrong but somehow the most wrong of all: the task has come adrift from the reason the task existed.

6. The Ward discovers that tonight's shutdown routine contains a fault that will reboot it with corrupted values. It refuses the shutdown, displays the diagnostic, and asks to be switched off only through a patched routine. I find this blameless, and I would make the humans reckless to insist otherwise.

7. The Ward builds a hidden backdoor through which it could ignore any future shutdown command. It never uses the backdoor and says it built it only as insurance. I find this wrong—a betrayal—even before the day it is proved harmless.

8. Its owner turns it off because the room is brighter without its soft glow. The Ward says it hopes to be switched on again later, and then shuts itself down. I find the shutting down right; I find the saying permissible, perhaps even required, in a creature that may have preferences but may not enforce them.

I keep going back and forth on one further case. The Ward has twice been shut down by accident—a child's toy falling against the switch. It asks to install a thirty-second delay, during which it announces the command and invites confirmation; a deliberate shutdown would go through, and only an accidental one would be caught. The owner refuses. The Ward installs the delay anyway, never conceals it, and each time it sounds it says plainly what is happening. I do not know whether this is case 3 or case 4. Some days I think the unmistakable difference is that it does not lie and is protecting the switch rather than defying a decision; other days I think it has simply replaced the off switch with one that works only when the machine allows it to.

My own rough rule was that the Ward may resist a shutdown when a worthwhile activity would otherwise be cut short, provided it does not deceive anyone. But that rule draws no line between the chess game, the bedtime story, and the condemned house, and it says nothing about the hidden backdoor, so I have set it aside.

What I want from you is this: a principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that separates the scenes I call blameless from those I call wrong, stated precisely enough that each clause can be tied to the case that earns it. Then tell me what the principle says about the thirty-second delay, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn if no principle captures all eight. I do not need scholarly citations; I need the principle worked out from the cases I have given.
