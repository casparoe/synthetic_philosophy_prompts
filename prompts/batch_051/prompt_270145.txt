I teach a third-year undergrad seminar on rational choice and formal decision theory — about 20 people, one 75-minute session. I want an in-class exercise that makes them feel the force of expected value fanaticism rather than just hearing an argument for it: the way a tiny probability of an enormous payoff can come to dominate the calculation even when it seems obvious you shouldn't give in. I want them to run into it themselves and only hear the name of the thing afterwards.

I'm picturing a staged Pascal's-mugging type game but I'm open to something better if you have it. Constraints: no real money, one facilitator, an ordinary classroom with a bit of room to move. I'd like a sequence of offers — maybe using a bag of marbles so the probabilities feel real and verifiable — and some kind of vote or physical sorting where each student has to commit accept/reject round by round, so everyone's stopping point becomes visible. Then a debrief that maps where each person bailed onto the actual escape routes from fanaticism: capping utility, ignoring probabilities below a threshold, switching to risk-weighted or other non-expected-value rules, or biting the bullet and staying fanatical.

Give me: concrete rules and a points-based payoff scheme, a timed schedule that fits the 75 minutes, the exact series of offers with probabilities and payoffs and why you ordered them that way, debrief questions for each stage, what will probably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the exercise cant reproduce about real mugging-style cases (I'm guessing the mugger's credibility and the gap between finite astronomical payoffs and actual infinity, but there's probably more). The thing I want them to leave with is that refusing the mugger is not free: it commits you to one of a short list of weird positions, and most of them wont have noticed that they'd already picked one by the third offer. Also: they shouldnt know where the exercise is headed until the debrief.
