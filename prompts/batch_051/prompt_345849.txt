In the Articles of Confederation, drafted in 1777 and ratified in 1781, Article II reads: "Each state retains its sovereignty, freedom, and independence, and every power, jurisdiction, and right, which is not by this Confederation expressly delegated to the United States, in Congress assembled." In the United States Constitution of 1787, Article VI, paragraph 2 — the Supremacy Clause — reads: "This Constitution, and the laws of the United States which shall be made in pursuance thereof; and all treaties made, or which shall be made, under the authority of the United States, shall be the supreme law of the land; and the judges in every state shall be bound thereby, anything in the Constitution or laws of any State to the contrary notwithstanding."

The two passages come from successive frames of government for the same union, drafted about a decade apart by overlapping sets of actors and ratified by many of the same states. Read literally, the first says that the states retain every power not expressly delegated, while the second says that the union's law is supreme and binds state judges even against contrary state law. A state that has retained its sovereignty in the first sense seems unable to authorize the second; a state subject to the second seems to have surrendered exactly what the first says it kept.

Can the two passages be reconciled within a single coherent account of what membership in a confederation or federation is? Work through and assess at least these candidate reconciliations:

(1) The second instrument simply superseded the first, so the two proceed from different premises and there is no single view to reconcile. How far does this go, given that the Constitution's Tenth Amendment later reserves to the states or the people the powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution and not prohibited by it to the states, but does not repeat the Articles' word expressly? Is that omission a deliberate repudiation of the earlier principle or an insignificant variation?

(2) Sovereignty is being used in two different senses: the Articles passage concerns the states' outward political independence, while the Supremacy Clause concerns only the internal supremacy of valid federal law within a limited, enumerated sphere — a supremacy many defenders of the Constitution treated as nothing more than a fully specified system of delegation. Is that distinction drawn anywhere in the texts themselves, or is it a later construction designed to keep both documents respectable?

(3) The two passages record a genuine change of mind among the same political class about what joining a union requires — not two expressions of one stable position but the before and after of a political conversion. If so, what exactly changed, and what became of the earlier express-delegation principle?

(4) The subject of sovereignty was relocated rather than retained: the Articles speaks of the states retaining powers, while the Constitution grounds itself in the people of the United States as a whole. Does this relocation resolve the conflict or merely rename it?

Then give a verdict: which of these readings is the most honest, which is special pleading, and what costs each exacts. Finally, apply the verdict to the familiar contemporary claim that a country can join a customs union — transferring its external tariff, accepting common rules, and subjecting itself to binding adjudication — and still be said to retain its sovereignty. Does the movement from the Articles to the Constitution show that this claim is coherent, that it is a face-saving fiction, or that sovereignty is being stretched past a useful meaning?
