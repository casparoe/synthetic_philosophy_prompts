The Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation has been stuck for decades despite the fact that everyone in the debate accepts the same unitary formalism and the same experimental record. The puzzle, stated plainly, is this: the theory says the universal wave function evolves deterministically, and that every outcome of a measurement is realized in some branch, yet the standard use of quantum mechanics assigns each outcome a probability equal to the squared amplitude of its branch. What is that probability a probability of, and why those weights rather than, say, equal weights across branches? There are by now well-developed families of answers. One family tries to derive the Born weights from decision theory, arguing that a rational agent who knows she will branch should assign credences to her successors in proportion to squared amplitude on pain of being exploitable. Another locates the probability in self-locating uncertainty: before a measurement you are uncertain about which future branch contains the successor you will find yourself in, and the squared amplitude supplies the correct epistemic weight. A third treats the weights as a primitive measure over branches and denies that they are chances at all. A fourth concludes that probability talk is an illusion or merely a practical artifact of how creatures like us navigate a branching world. Every one of these families faces well-known objections: the decision-theoretic derivations are accused of smuggling the Born rule into their rationality axioms; the self-locating accounts are accused of presupposing a fact about which branch is really you that the formalism does not provide; the primitive-measure accounts are accused of making the numbers arbitrary rather than explained; and the deflationary accounts are accused of changing the subject rather than solving the problem. The debate nevertheless does not move. My working hypothesis is that the participants are not really disagreeing about physics, but about what a probability would have to be in a world where all outcomes occur — objective chance, rational credence, self-locating uncertainty, or a measure over possibilities — and about what an interpretation is required to supply before it counts as having explained the Born rule at all.
