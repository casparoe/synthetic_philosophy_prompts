I am a professor of philosophy, and I chair the ethics advisory group of a non-governmental organisation that supports nutrition programmes in low-income and pastoralist regions. A recurring difficulty is that our programme officers are pressured to justify every inclusion of animal-source foods by a plant-based standard developed in affluent countries, where plant alternatives are abundant, affordable, and familiar. In the settings we serve, that assumption often fails: lentils, fortified products, and fresh vegetables may be costly, unreliable in supply, or culturally unfamiliar, while milk, eggs, and dried fish are frequently the most accessible sources of complete protein and are embedded in local livelihoods. I would like you to turn the strongest ethical argument for reducing animal product consumption into a reusable decision procedure that our field and programme staff can follow when they design a food package, a school-meal menu, or a supplementary feeding programme. These decisions are made routinely in each district, under time pressure, by staff who are not philosophers and who often have patchy local data; the procedure should be specific enough that a district coordinator can run it and a supervisor can review and sign off on the resulting decision. Ground the procedure in the principle that animal suffering and death should be minimised insofar as it is genuinely possible and practicable to do so, but do not silently import the infrastructure and purchasing power of a wealthy country. Please provide: the questions to ask in order; what counts as an adequate answer to each; where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation of an animal-source food; where it stops with a plant-based alternative; and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values, cultural knowledge, or local fact that the procedure cannot settle. Then run the procedure on three cases: (i) a school-meal programme in a fishing community choosing between locally dried fish and imported plant protein; (ii) a maternal-nutrition programme in a pastoralist region deciding whether to continue supplying milk from community herds or switch to a fortified plant milk; and (iii) a proposal to remove eggs from an under-five supplementary feeding programme on animal welfare grounds even though eggs are currently the cheapest complete protein available for that age group. At least one of these should yield a recommendation that our vegan-leaning staff will not like. Please also say explicitly where the procedure would misfire and what it cannot decide. Write the answer in British English.
