I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've been reading around in the cognitive science of perception, and an analogy keeps nagging me. The claim I keep running into is that perception is informationally encapsulated: the machinery that assembles your visual or auditory experience does its work without consulting your beliefs, desires, or expectations, which is why illusions survive even after you know they're illusions. The opposite claim, cognitive penetration, is that what you believe or expect can alter what you literally perceive, not merely how you judge or act on what you perceive. To me the whole dispute looks like a debate we have in software, and I want to know whether that's real or just pattern-matching. In code I keep two things apart: information hiding, where a module's internals are private but the module may still depend on its declared inputs and on configuration; and purity or referential transparency, where a function's output depends only on its declared inputs and not on ambient state. Which of those, if either, is informationally encapsulated supposed to match? Cognitive penetration, from where I sit, sounds like a perceptual module reading mutable global state behind your back, a leaky abstraction.

What I'd like from a careful answer is this. Give me the exact mapping: what in the perception story corresponds to the function, the declared inputs, configuration, ambient state, and the leak? Then tell me what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer in either direction. In software we sharpen these disputes with distinctions: a hidden global that's a bug versus a deliberately injected dependency; configuration set at startup versus state mutated at runtime; a leak that makes the system more useful versus one that merely makes it unpredictable. Does the perception side actually have counterparts to those distinctions, or does the analogy quietly smuggle in a line that people working on perception would reject? And I want a stress test: name at least one concrete prediction the analogy would make about perception or about software that turns out false, so I can see exactly where the structure comes apart. If the analogy survives all that, tell me what it's good for and what it can't do.
