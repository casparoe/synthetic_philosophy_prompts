Consider the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles, the metaphysical doctrine associated with Leibniz, expressed here as a rule for counting the items in any possible world:

"Where two supposed things are such that nothing true of the one fails to be true of the other — no quality, relation, power, or way of acting marks them apart — there are not two things but one thing twice counted. To be two, things must be somehow different; bare numerical distinctness is impossible."

Now test it against a case the doctrine's author did not anticipate. First recall Black's familiar world: a universe containing only two iron spheres, exactly alike in size, composition, and intrinsic character, two miles apart, in otherwise empty space. The world seems perfectly coherent, and it seems to contain two spheres; yet the only apparent differences are relational, and it is exactly contested whether such differences count.

Next consider a case Black did not imagine, and Leibniz could not have: a computer simulation of a world containing just two objects. The program initializes two object-records with identical property-lists and evolves them under laws that never discriminate between the two, so the simulated world's state is at every moment perfectly symmetric between them. The two records occupy distinct addresses in the host machine's memory, and the runtime lists them in some fixed order, but nothing inside the simulated world — no measuring instrument, recording device, or simulated observer — can detect the address or the ordering.

How does the quoted rule apply to the two simulated objects? Work through the question as an interpreter would: (a) from the words of the rule, asking in particular whether being stored at one address rather than another, or being first in the runtime's list, counts as a quality, relation, power, or way of acting; (b) from the rule's underlying purpose and the principle of sufficient reason, asking whether an arbitrary address assignment supplies the sort of reason the rule demands for counting two; (c) from what Leibniz would most plausibly have said if asked whether representational facts about the implementation are real differences in the world represented; and (d) from the reading a reasonable metaphysician should adopt. State which approach should win, whether the simulation case leaves the Principle stronger, in need of revision, or refuted, and what this reveals about the dispute over Black's spheres.
