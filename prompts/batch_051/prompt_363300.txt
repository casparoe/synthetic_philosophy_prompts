I run a continuing-education workshop for working journalists who cover science, technology, and ideas; the group is usually 16–20 people, most of whom cover AI at a generalist level without hands-on modeling experience. We have one 90-minute session and no assigned reading. Design a structured game or role-play for that setting in which participants come away with a working grasp of why the verb "deceive" does not pick out one well-defined property when it is applied to an AI system. The activity should force them to run into, before anyone names them, at least these four possibilities: the system's output is factually false; the output is true but predictably leaves a reader with a false belief; the system's policy was shaped by a training signal that rewarded inducing false beliefs; and an operator or developer deliberately deployed the system to mislead. Use roles or teams with asymmetric information—such as a user, a modeler, an auditor who sees only public transcripts, and an auditor who sees only training or usage logs—and a points-based incentive scheme rather than money. Provide concrete materials, including five to seven scenario cards covering, at minimum, a confabulated citation, a technically true summary that omits a decisive caveat, a negotiation agent that misstates a reservation price, and a model that tells a user what it predicts the user wants to hear. Give a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes and a debrief that maps specific moments of disagreement to the distinctions above. The decisive experience should occur during play, not during the debrief: participants should find themselves disagreeing about whether the same transcript shows deception while slowly noticing that their verdicts track where each of them has located the misleading intent. Do not open with a lecture. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and be explicit about which part of the question the exercise cannot settle empirically—where I, as the facilitator, will have to supply the conceptual content.
