Write the desiderata for a theory of ritual substitution. By ritual substitution I mean the family of practices in which a group or person transfers guilt, pollution, or misfortune onto some vehicle — an animal, a person, an object, or a place — which is then destroyed, expelled, buried, or otherwise removed from the community's ongoing life. The Leviticus scapegoat is the paradigm case, but the theory should also cover analogues in which nothing is sacrificed, and secular cases such as dismissing a designated culprit in order to restore an institution. Adequacy should be judged relative to this purpose: the theory must let someone compare apparently dissimilar cases — one goat slaughtered, another driven into the wilderness; impurity poured out in water that is then disposed of; an employee fired after a systemic failure — and diagnose why structurally similar actions sometimes fail to count as substitutions for the participants.

Produce the requirements list as the main deliverable. For each requirement: give the case or consideration that forces it, explain why an adequate theory cannot quietly drop it, and mark it as hard (the theory fails without it) or soft (negotiable only if the theory pays a clear price and says so). The requirements should discriminate between types of theory, not merely redescribe the phenomenon.

At minimum, your list must handle the following.

First, the difference between transferring moral guilt, which attaches to an agent's misdeed, and transferring pollution or impurity, which is acquired by contact or circumstance; the theory must say how these two are related and whether one mechanism can cover both.

Second, the direction of transfer. In the paradigm cases, the bad condition moves from the community to the vehicle, not the reverse, and after the transfer the vehicle becomes the very thing that must not be touched, kept, or allowed back — even though it was innocent before. A theory must explain that asymmetry and the changed status of the vehicle.

Third, the fate of the vehicle. Release, killing, burial, and burning all appear as recognized ways of disposing of the substitute. The theory must say whether the transfer itself requires one of these fates, or whether the manner of disposal is a separate, culturally variable matter that the transfer mechanism leaves open.

Fourth, a test case in which the same words and actions are performed by a single officiant with no congregation or witness, and the participants afterwards treat the rite as void or as merely private therapy. The theory must say what the missing ingredient is, and why publicity or authorization matters to the transfer.

Fifth, a test case in which the vehicle returns or survives intact after the rite, so that the community treats the guilt as still present and the performance must be done again. The theory must specify what re-transfer would mean, and what kind of failure has occurred.

Sixth, the problem of repetition. If the guilt has been removed by transfer, why do communities that practice such rites perform them again on a fixed cycle rather than once and for all? The theory must explain what is left behind, or what re-accumulates, without rendering the original transfer meaningless.

Seventh, the relation between representation and substitution. The theory must address whether the vehicle stands for the community, stands in for it, or actually becomes a bearer of something, and it must explain how to respect what participants say is happening without simply asserting that the guilt really is a substance moved by magic — or, alternatively, why no middle path is available.

Eighth, at least one secular analogue of your own construction, described in enough detail to test whether the theory genuinely generalizes or merely redescribes religious cases. For example, you might work with a case in which an organization dismisses a mid-level employee after a system-wide failure and issues a report locating the cause in his decisions, after which the institution announces that its standing has been restored.

Also respect these distinctions rather than collapsing them. An account of what changes for the community — its social standing, moral record, ritual purity, or collective mood — cannot be substituted for an account of what the ritual does to the vehicle. The after-status of the vehicle, as loaded, dangerous, sacrosanct, or untouchable, is a datum to be explained, not an incidental piece of etiquette. And a private act of ventilation is not automatically the same kind of transfer, while a public rite is not automatically more than theater; the theory must draw the line somewhere and defend it.

Finally, name the two or three broad families of candidate theory you take to be available — possibly including psychologizing or cathartic accounts, social-functional accounts, structuralist or symbolic accounts, or the participants' own supernaturalist account taken at face value — and give each family a one- or two-sentence score against your hardest requirements. Then state which single requirement in your list does the most work to exclude candidates, and why. Keep the main weight on the requirements and their motivating cases; the scoring should be brief.
