I have no formal background in philosophy, so I will put this in my own words rather than the standard ones. I have been turning over a view about fairness and I want to know what would count against it.

The view is this. What settles whether a rule or policy is fair is whether any single person it burdens could reasonably reject it, given what it would do to that person. Because the test looks at one person at a time, I am not willing to let a large number of smaller complaints outweigh one person's serious complaint. Each objector gets a separate hearing, and the fact that many people stand on one side does not, by itself, make their side stronger. To be clear about what this commits me to, I think it implies that if I had to choose between saving one person from a very grave harm and saving a hundred people from a much smaller harm, I would not be required to save the hundred, and it might even be wrong to save them, because a hundred small complaints cannot gang up on one grave complaint.

The place where I feel the view strain is this comparison. Inside a single life, I am perfectly willing to say that a hundred small pains could add up to something worse than one large pain, and that a person could reasonably trade accordingly. The moment I move across lives, I refuse to make the same calculation. I cannot yet say why the boundary of a single life matters in that way, and I suspect the answer to that is either the deepest part of the view or the exact place where it breaks.

What I would like from you is a map of the pressure points. Tell me what would have to be true for this position to be false, and what sorts of cases or judgments would be evidence for the rival view, the one that is willing to add up burdens across lives. I want the fatal problems separated from the merely awkward ones, and I want to know which objections a sympathizer would themselves admit count against the view, not only what an opponent would say. I would also like to know which further judgments the position pushes me toward, the ones a rival position would describe differently, and whether those commitments are damaging. Give me an honest answer about whether the view can be modified to dodge each objection, and whether making those modifications is a sign of a view getting sharper or of a view being insulated so that nothing could ever refute it. If the honest conclusion is that the position has been qualified until it rules nothing out and risks nothing, say that outright and tell me whether that is a fatal problem for a view of this kind.

Please write as a single continuous piece of prose, in the manner of an email or a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables.
