As a journalist covering science and ideas, I recently chaired a discussion on long-range forecasting. One panellist, a climate modeller, deflected a question about a conditional projection by saying that 'the future is not written'. A philosopher on the panel countered that, taken literally, the remark lands one in the old sea-battle problem: if there is now no settled fact about tomorrow, then future-tensed statements look as though they are neither true nor false, and that brings odd consequences for the logic of 'will'. I left the room unsure whether the two speakers were using the same sentence to make different claims.

I would like a careful disambiguation. Please set out the distinct precise claims that 'the future is not written' could be used to express — for instance, claims about determinism, about predictability in principle versus in practice, about bivalence for future-tensed statements, about branching time, or about what is 'settled' at a moment. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude or a substantive thesis, whether it is plausible or false, and whether it would actually license deflecting a conditional forecast. I would also like to know which reading, if any, the sea-battle objection is best understood as targeting. A menu of readings with verdicts would serve me better than a sustained development of a single interpretation. Please answer in British English.
