ive been chewing on this coin flip game for a while and its making me distrust a rule i use all the time, but the conclusion is too much to swallow. i'm not an academic or anything, just someone who got stuck thinking about it. the game: you pay a fee up front to play once. the house flips a fair coin until it lands heads. if the first heads is on flip 1, they pay you $2; on flip 2, $4; on flip 3, $8; doubling each extra flip. so if the first heads comes on flip n, the payout is 2^n dollars. (i later learned this thing has a name, the st petersburg paradox.)

the argument i keep coming back to:

(1) the rational price for a bet is its expected value: you should take it whenever the price is below the expected value and refuse it whenever the price is above.
(2) the probability the first heads is on flip n is (1/2)^n, so the expected value is (1/2)2 + (1/4)4 + (1/8)8 + ... which is just 1 + 1 + 1 + ... and has no finite sum; its infinite.
(3) so any finite price is below the expected value, and (1) says you should pay any finite fee to play once: your savings, a billion dollars, whatever. worse, if you already held a ticket, (1) says you should refuse to sell it for any finite amount of cash, even though the game will only ever actually pay out a finite amount.
(4) but that's insane. almost every play pays $16 or less, and no real person would mortgage their house for one go at this, even with the tiny chance of an astronomical payout.
(5) so one of (1), (2), or the move from expected value to what you should actually pay must be bad. but the arithmetic in (2) is just adding, and (1) is the thing that makes ordinary bets, poker, insurance all hang together. i'd like to keep (1) if i can without ending up at (3).

where does this actually go wrong? which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? if (1) has to be restricted, tell me the exact restriction and what ordinary decisions it changes. and make the last line of your response a one sentence summary of the diagnosis.
