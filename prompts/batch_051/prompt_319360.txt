I have no academic background in philosophy or machine learning; I have been thinking through the tool-AI-versus-agent-AI disagreement on my own, mostly from AI-safety discussions. Draft the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a committed tool-AI advocate and a committed agent-AI advocate would produce. Assume both participants are mainly motivated by preventing catastrophic outcomes from advanced AI and are each willing to revise specific sub-positions when the evidence or argument calls for it.

Output the joint statement itself, not a third-party account of how the collaboration went. Structure it in three sections.

First, a numbered Agreed claims section. These should be substantive rather than diplomatic: what each label commits its side to and does not commit it to, what both agree about current systems, what both agree the actual source of danger is, and which common claims about the other side are caricatures.

Second, a Disagreements table. Each row should give: the issue; the tool-AI side's own preferred formulation of its view; the agent-AI side's own preferred formulation; and an explicit judgment about whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal. Where a disagreement is partly verbal, say exactly which term is doing the work, give the two definitions in play, and state the residual factual question after the verbal issue has been isolated. I suspect goal-directedness is one such term, but do not force it.

Third, a Cruxes and tests section. For each crux—a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side on a nontrivial commitment—specify a concrete test, experiment, or engineering case study both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance with a rough confidence level. Make the tests concrete enough to turn into a research plan; examples of the kind of thing I mean are interrupted-task experiments, scaling comparisons in which the same base model is fine-tuned with a tool-like objective versus an agent-like objective and then probed for convergent instrumental behavior, and red-team studies of approval-directed agents.

The document should engage with at least these questions, unless the two parties would not put them at the center: whether goal-directedness is an avoidable design choice or an attractor as capability, competition, and task complexity rise; whether corrigibility can be layered onto an agent or requires avoiding agentic design from the start; whether a system whose only terminal goal is human approval is safe in practice; and whether current pretraining-then-fine-tuning-then-scaffolding pipelines are already drifting toward agents regardless of anyone's intent.

Begin with the document itself. No preface, no commentary, no restating of the assignment.
