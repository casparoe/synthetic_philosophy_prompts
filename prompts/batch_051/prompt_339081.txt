A popular book on rationality contains the following summary of why expected utility maximization is rationally required:

"Economists and decision theorists have proven that any rational person must maximize expected utility, and anyone who does not is, in effect, betting against themselves. The argument runs as follows. A rational person's preferences satisfy a few basic consistency conditions: they can rank any two options (completeness), their rankings are transitive, and if they prefer one gamble to another, mixing each with a common third gamble in the same proportion cannot reverse that preference (independence). From these conditions, a theorem shows that such a person can be represented as having a probability function over states and a utility function over outcomes, unique up to positive rescaling, and that they choose whichever act maximizes expected utility computed from those. Moreover, if someone violates any of these conditions, there is a series of trades, each of which they would accept by their own lights, whose combined effect is a guaranteed loss — a Dutch book. Since knowingly walking into a sure loss is plainly irrational, the conditions are rationally required, and therefore so is maximizing expected utility. People who, like Allais's subjects, prefer a certain million dollars to a gamble with a higher expected payoff are simply exhibiting incoherent preferences; that pattern of choice is a mistake, not a legitimate expression of risk aversion."

I'm not sure how much of this to trust. Which parts would a decision theorist accept as accurate, which are overstatements or conflations, and does the closing verdict about Allais-type choices follow from the parts that are accurate?
