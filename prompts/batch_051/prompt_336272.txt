Give me a working definition of "physical" that is precise enough to state the doctrine of physicalism — the claim that everything that exists, or at least every fundamental entity and property, is physical — and then test that definition against the following cases. I want to know, for each: does your definition classify it as physical, nonphysical, or is the verdict unclear?

The cases: a piece of granite; an electron; the electromagnetic field; spacetime itself; dark matter, assuming it is real; the quantum wavefunction interpreted realistically rather than as a mere predictive instrument; the number 7; the rule that a bishop moves diagonally; the felt painfulness of a migraine; a corporation's obligation to honor a contract; and a law of nature that happens never to be instantiated. Mark the borderline cases and tell me which of your verdicts is least secure.

I also want the definition to avoid two dead ends that have been bothering me. If "physical" means "whatever current physics says exists," then physicalism is hostage to the next scientific revolution: it was false before fields or spacetime curvature were part of physics, and it will be false again whenever physics changes its fundamental ontology. If "physical" means "whatever an ideal completed physics would say exists," then physicalism seems to become empty — any coherent thing could be labeled physical if a future science posited it. A completed physics that counted a soul-substance or an irreducible mental field among its fundamental entities would make "everything is physical" true by definition, not by discovery. Give me a definition that avoids both horns, or argue that one horn can be made habitable and show how.

Finally, if you think the term "physical" is doing two different jobs — one ontological, picking out a kind of entity or property, and one methodological, picking out whatever a certain kind of inquiry can in principle study — say so, and either unify them into a single criterion or explain how the two should be related.
