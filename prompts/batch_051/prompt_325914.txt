I am a novelist, so I shall describe the phenomenon as it shows up in my craft rather than in philosophical terms. It concerns what I mean when I say that a character in two tellings is 'the same person'.

When I revise a novel, I alter facts about a character between drafts. In the third draft the protagonist is an only child; in the fourth she has an elder brother. I usually treat her as one woman, differently specified: the change is a correction to my notes, not a new person. Yet if an adapter gives her a different profession, or lets her choose differently at the novel's central crisis, I find it equally natural to say that this is a different character wearing the same name — the adapter's character, not mine. And when a reader writes a story in which she acts against what I take to be her deepest commitments, I reach for a third formulation: neither the same character nor simply a new one, but my character under a description that is somehow false and yet is the whole point of the exercise.

The puzzle is not a quarrel over the word 'same'. I seem to need two incompatible pictures. On the first, the character is a single individual who could have had a brother rather than none, and each telling is one way that individual might have gone. On the second, no single individual threads through the variants; there are only several individuals who resemble one another, share a name and an origin, and stand in some looser relation I lack a word for. The two pictures yield different answers to ordinary questions: whether the protagonist of draft four is the protagonist of draft three; whether the Lear of a modern retelling who runs a family firm is Shakespeare's Lear; whether a contradiction introduced by a careless adapter makes the tale inconsistent or merely splits the character in two.

What unsettles me is that my instinct shifts with facts that ought to be irrelevant if the question were really about identity. A variant I compose in an unpublished draft feels like the same character; the same alteration made by another author under licence feels less so; the same alteration made by a parodist feels not so at all. Perhaps authorship and intention merely govern how we apply a name. If so, I want to be told so, because then talk of 'versions of a character' is only a façon de parler.

I am asking whether this configuration has an established name and, more importantly, what its edges are: how my unpublished revision, an authorised adaptation, and an unauthorised parody relate to one another; and whether the difference between finding one individual across the variants and finding many similar individuals is the same distinction I half-remember being drawn for possible worlds. A bare label would not satisfy me. I want to know which established distinctions my case sits between, which of them it resembles only superficially, and where its boundaries lie.
