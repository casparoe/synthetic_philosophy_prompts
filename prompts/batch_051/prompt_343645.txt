I've been arguing with a friend who defends the hedonistic imperative—the view that biotechnology should be used to phase out suffering by redesigning the neural machinery that produces it. His argument is a regimented version, he says, of what is already accepted whenever someone defends anaesthesia, palliative care, or antidepressants. I reproduce it here:

(1) Each episode of pain, fear, grief, or shame is intrinsically bad for the subject who undergoes it.
(2) If a state is intrinsically bad, and the functional work it does could be done equally well by a state that is not bad, then we have strong prima facie reason to bring about the replacement rather than the original.
(3) For the standard negative affects, the functional work—damage signalling, avoidance learning, motivation to protect or repair, and communicating distress to others—is in principle separable from the felt negative valence; a valence-neutral or mildly positive signal could carry the same information and move the subject just as reliably.
(4) Therefore, for any given episode of pain, fear, grief, or shame, we have strong prima facie reason to replace it with its valence-neutral functional equivalent.
(5) If we have strong prima facie reason to replace each episode, then we have equally strong reason to eliminate the underlying capacity for negative affect outright, rather than waiting to treat episodes one by one.
(6) Hence the imperative: we ought to phase out the capacity for suffering across sentient life.

I cannot accept (6), and not only because the technology is distant. Taken seriously, it commits us to saying that a parent who registers a child's danger without any flicker of fear, or a mourner who processes a loss without any hurt, is in every relevant respect better than one who is stricken—provided only that the alarm signal still fires. I find that not merely counterintuitive but a distortion of what it is to love, grieve, or have a conscience. So one of the steps must go. I would most like to keep (1) and (2); (3) strikes me as the weakest link, but treating it as a merely empirical failure does not answer the argument, since the proponent can retreat to a weaker claim about whatever replacement is possible. I have a persistent feeling that the middle of the argument moves faster than its premises allow, but I cannot make the objection precise, and I worry that this is just a failure of nerve.

Where does the argument go wrong? In answering, please say what would change your verdict—which argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis—and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
