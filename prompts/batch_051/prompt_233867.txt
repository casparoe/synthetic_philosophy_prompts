So me and a friend spent way too long last night arguing about promises, of all things, and we never managed to settle one specific point. His line was that when I feel bound by a promise, that feeling is really just anticipated regret, guilt, and damage to my reputation if I break it—nothing over and above the consequences. I pushed back with the old case of a promise to a stranger who forgets it, where I'd have to do something costly and literally nobody but me would ever know if I didn't. He said my lingering sense of being bound is just how I was trained to feel, not evidence of a distinct promissory reason.

So here's what I want to pin down. Take the claim: a promise creates a reason to keep it that isn't reducible to the expected costs of breaking it (guilt, lost trust, reputational damage, and so on). I know that sounds like pure conceptual analysis, but I suspect at least part of it can be brought under test. Design the best empirical or quasi-empirical test you can for the reducibility claim, and be concrete: what exactly gets varied, what behavior or judgment gets measured, and what pattern would count in favor of the irreducable-promissory-reason view versus the reductionist view. Then do the part I actually care about: say what even a clean positive result wouldn't settle. For example, could any experiment distinguish between "the promise itself adds a reason" and "people have a stubborn internalized norm that makes them anticipate guilt anyway," or does the test only ever catch the shadow of the reason rather than the reason itself?
