In a welfare economics seminar, a classmate objected to a congestion-pricing proposal that would impose tolls on some drivers to fund transit passes for others by leaning on an analogy. Comparing one person's utility to another's, he said, is like comparing the readings on two thermometers manufactured by different firms, each marked with a different scale, and neither ever calibrated against a fixed reference point. With no conversion formula between the scales, no proposition of the form 'the gain to the beneficiaries exceeds the loss to those who pay' can be true or false. He concluded that the toll could only be justified if it passed a Pareto test, since any appeal to the overall balance of gains and losses across persons is literally meaningless. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I want the flaw made precise rather than rejected by slogan.

Audit the analogy. Lay out the intended mapping: what in the utility case stands for the thermometer, the reading, the scale, the calibration, and the missing conversion formula. Then identify where the analogy breaks down; identify which features of the measurement scenario it quietly imports into the utility scenario, such as the existence of an independent physical quantity that both instruments are measuring; and say which disanalogies matter for the specific conclusion about the toll. Finally, state how much of the classmate's conclusion survives, and propose a better analogy if one is available.
