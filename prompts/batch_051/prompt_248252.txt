Suppose that in 2030, the major scientific journals and public funding agencies of a large bloc of wealthy countries jointly adopt an enforceable falsification requirement. Before collecting data, every registered study must state a specific set of outcomes that would lead the authors to abandon the hypothesis under test. An audit body then checks a random sample of completed studies: if the specified outcomes occurred and the authors nevertheless retained the hypothesis, they must show either that they abandoned it, or that a clearly identified background assumption was at fault; if they save the hypothesis by revising an auxiliary assumption, that revision must be declared and the study is recorded as a failed test of the original hypothesis rather than as confirmation of a modified one. Assume the audit is competent, adequately funded, and resistant to capture, though not omniscient.

Predict, with reasoning, how the day-to-day practice of the natural, social, and biomedical sciences changes over the following forty years. I want a chain of reasoning about mechanisms and incentives, not a story. Address at least these questions, taking a definite position on each rather than surveying options: Which fields or research styles adapt most easily, and which are most disrupted? How do researchers change the way they choose hypotheses, design studies, and write papers? What new forms of gaming or perverse incentive emerge, and how serious are they? Does the rule make the published literature more likely to survive independent replication, and at what cost to exploratory or descriptive work? Does it make science more or less able to handle theories whose core claims are difficult to state as single hypotheses? Finally, does the rule succeed in drawing a sharper line between science and non-science, or does it mostly change the rhetoric? Be explicit about which of your predictions are robust, which are guesses, and which background fact about scientific communities, if false, would most alter your conclusions.
