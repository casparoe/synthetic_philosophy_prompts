I teach a second-year undergrad module on lying and deception, and I want a 80-minute structured activity for about 22 students that makes them feel the pull of the view that misleading can be just as bad as lying — or, if it can't, that makes them see what the difference actually rests on. my rough idea is a market game: half the sellers are only allowed to say literally true sentences, the other half may lie outright, buyers have to act on what they're told and keep points, and then at intervals there's a 'jury' round where everyone assigns blame and penalties to the exact words used rather than the harm done. after a few rounds the class should notice the true-sentence sellers are earning nearly the same while staying deniable, and the group will probably split on whether that's worse, better, or the same. Design the rules, roles, point scheme (no real money — sweets or tokens are fine), a timeline, and the debrief. I want people to reach the problem themselves before anyone says 'assertion' or 'warrant'. In the debrief, force them to vote on whether the misleader is less, equally, or more blameworthy, and then get them to say which feature of the game is doing the most work for them: the words spoken, the intent, the outcome, or the deniability. Tell me what will predictably go wrong — sellers using only absurdly transparent truths, buyers learning to ignore everyone, the jury sliding into outcome-based blame — and how to handle each. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce (long-term trust, the social meaning of explicit promises, the fact that real lies are often found out) and what I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.
