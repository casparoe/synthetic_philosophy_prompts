There is a structural parallel I keep noticing and cannot evaluate alone. In combinatorial optimization, a greedy algorithm takes the locally best available step at each stage; on most problems this produces a poor overall result, but on a special class of structures—where no early choice can foreclose an optimal completion, a property captured by the matroid exchange axiom—locally best choices are provably composed into a global optimum with no foresight at all. That looks to me like the old argument about acquisitiveness: one side says that individuals each maximizing their own gain, without aiming at any common good, nonetheless produce general prosperity, while the other side says such uncoordinated local maximizing leads to ruin unless a larger structure disciplines it. On the analogy, the pro-greed side is claiming that markets play the role matroids play in the theorem. I suspect the shared word "greedy" is doing some work here, so I want the parallel tested rather than taken on trust. What exactly would an economy or a market have to be like for the greedy-algorithm results to transfer, and how close does any real institution come? Where specifically does the analogy come apart—for example, does it matter that economic agents anticipate and revise while a greedy algorithm cannot look ahead? And if the parallel were deep, what would it predict about where markets fail, and where would that prediction be falsified?
