I keep noticing a family of cases in which a disposition and its test come apart, and I want to know whether the pieces have established names, and whether the distinctions among them are real. A wine glass is fragile. Normally, if you strike it, it breaks, and the breaking is evidence of the fragility. But suppose the glass is packed in impact-absorbing foam: you strike it and it does not break. No one concludes that the glass was not fragile; the fragility was there, and the foam stopped it from manifesting. Now suppose instead that the glass sits unprotected, but a device watches it and, at the instant the blow begins, alters the glass so that by the time the force would do damage, the glass is no longer fragile; again it does not break. Here we are pulled toward saying something different: the disposition was removed at the moment of testing, rather than present but blocked. Finally, suppose a material is not water-soluble, but on contact with water it changes so quickly that it becomes soluble at exactly the moment it is immersed, and so it dissolves. The test succeeds even though the material lacked the disposition just before the test. I would like an account of what these configurations are, especially the first two, and of how the boundary between them is drawn. Can a single failed test ever determine which of the first two one is dealing with? And does the very possibility of such cases undermine the simple idea that a disposition is nothing over and above what would happen if the object were put to the test? I am less interested in labels than in the carving, but if established terms exist, I want them, with their boundary cases. Please answer in American English.
