I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so cut me some slack if I mangle the vocabulary. I'm drafting a novel where a corporation is the villain — in the sense that the harm is really the company's doing, not just one evil CEO's — and I keep running into the same wall: can you literally blame a company, or is that just a lazy way of blaming a few people inside it? I don't want a reading list or citations; I want you to think through a concrete case with me.

Use the Volkswagen emissions scandal (Dieselgate) as the test case. Here are the facts as I'll use them, so we aren't talking past each other: in 2015 it emerged that VW had programmed certain diesel cars to detect when they were being tested in a lab and to run cleaner under test conditions, while on the road they emitted far more NOx than allowed; the company eventually admitted it had used the defeat device in millions of cars; some individual engineers and at least one senior manager were prosecuted or pleaded guilty; and VW as a corporate body pleaded guilty and payed billions in fines.

Now analyze the episode twice. First under the view that only flesh-and-blood individuals are the kind of thing that can be morally blamed, and a corporation is at most a convenient fiction for the messy network of them. Second under the view that a company can be a genuine moral agent in its own right because it has decision procedures, aims, and an internal structure that aren't identical to any particular person's mind.

I'm less interested in which label wins than in the mapping: what each lens lets you say about the case, and where each one strains or goes silent. In particular, sit with the fact that this scandal seems to have been produced by many people each doing a small, procedure-shaped peice of it, under a culture of hitting the targets, rather than by one mastermind. Does that make the company the natural object of blame, or does it dissolve the blame into a thousand tiny pieces? Also weigh in on whether moral emotions like resentment or indignation can be fittingly aimed at a company, or whether that's a category mistake — something for which a company can at best be feared, sued, or regulated. And end by saying what this case should do to the two views: does it support one, expose problems in both, or show that the question was badly posed?

Structure your answer in five sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: "The facts that matter" (~250 words), "The individualist read" (~350 words), "The collectivist read" (~350 words), "Where each lens strains" (~350 words), and "What the case does to the theory" (~250 words). If you think the last section partly contradicts what you said in the middle, say so; that tension is exactly what I want for the novel.
