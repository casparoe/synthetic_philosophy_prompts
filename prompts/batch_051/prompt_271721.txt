I've been thinking about Ramsey's "Truth and Probability" and, in particular, the argument about the "cunning bettor" who makes a book against you, and I'm noticing something about how people describe what Dutch book arguments are supposed to show. I'm not sure the following is right, so I want to see whether this distinction actually holds up if someone knows the literature better than I do.

The standard move is: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there is some set of bets such that each bet looks fair to you individually, but the whole set guarantees a loss, no matter what the world is like. The conclusion people draw is that your degrees of belief are incoherent, and that coherent degrees of belief are degrees of belief that obey the probability axioms.

But there's a difference between two things one might be show with such an argument. One thing is that there exists a function from your degrees of belief to net outcomes, where the function makes every individual bet acceptable to you and the total necessarily negative. Another thing is that this function is something like a strategy that could be executed against you in the actual world, by someone who only does the things that traders can actually do. 

The problem is that the first is a static, almost arithmetic fact about the numbers: if you have .6 in A and .5 in not-A, then there exist stakes at which you'll pay more than you can possibly get back. It does not depend on any claim about how you would revise if someone offered you the bets in sequence, or whether you would notice the package, or whether there's a market, or whether the bettor has some weird advantage. The second is a dynamic claim about a person with those degrees of belief being exploitable by a counterparty in some real setting.

Ramsey's own text seems to run them together. He writes, in the 1926 paper "Truth and Probability":

'If anyone's mental condition violated these laws, his choice would depend on the precise form in which the options were offered him, which would be absurd. He could have a book made against him by a cunning bettor and would then stand to lose in any event.'

Now, the first sentence sounds like the arithmetic version: violating the laws means your choices depend on how the same options are framed. But the second sentence sounds like the dynamic version: someone could actually exploit you. And the "cunning bettor" language, in particular, seems to do a lot of rhetorical work there. A 'cunning bettor' is not just a name for the fact that your numbers violate an axiom; it's a person who knows your numbers and can move against them.

What I want to know is whether the dynamic, cunning-bettor version is actually doing any justificatory work in Ramsey's argument, or whether it's just a vivid way of describing the static fact. In other words, if the static arithmetic fact holds, is the cunning-bettor scenario redundant? Or does the static fact alone not get you to irrationality in the way Ramsey seems to want, so the dynamic scenario is doing important work? And if the dynamic scenario is doing important work, what does it presuppose—about a market, about your not updating when shown the package, about your willingness to take bets in sequence, etc.—that Ramsey leaves unstated? Could someone have degrees of belief that violate the axioms but be immune in principle to the dynamic exploit, even while the static arithmetic fact is true?
