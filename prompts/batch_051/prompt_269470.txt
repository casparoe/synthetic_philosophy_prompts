Here are the criteria I use for calling something a code: a finite set of discrete symbols; a systematic mapping from those symbols to items of a different kind; a mapping that is arbitrary, or at least not uniquely forced by the physics of the symbols; and encoding and decoding operations that implement the mapping, along with some channel through which the symbols carry information about the paired items.

Now consider two molecular-biological cases. First, the genetic code in the narrow sense: the mapping from 61 sense codons (plus three stop codons) to the 20 amino acids, realized by tRNAs and the ribosome. The assignment looks at least partly contingent—alternative codon assignments exist in some mitochondria and ciliates—and is implemented by dedicated decoding machinery, but the system contains no sender that intends the message. Second, DNA treated as a store of 'genetic information': here one strand's sequence fixes its partner's sequence by base-pairing chemistry, so the message is physically forced rather than conventionally assigned.

Go through the criteria honestly for each case. Does the genetic code in the narrow sense qualify as a code? Does DNA as an information store? Where the answer is no, say whether that is because of the criteria, because of the biology, or because 'code' is being used metaphorically. Then say whether the criteria themselves should be revised, and if so, exactly which criterion you would change and why.
