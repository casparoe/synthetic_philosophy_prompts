My colleague works on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the difficulty of saying what pleasures as different as eating, relief, and an intellectual glow have in common — and he keeps a running list of on-the-record judgments about which mental states he is willing to call pleasures. Among the states he has counted as pleasures: the sensory enjoyment of eating while hungry; the intellectual glow after solving a hard proof; the relief when a sharp pain finally stops, which he insists is a positive felt event rather than the mere absence of pain; the delight upon hearing that a friend survived surgery, provided it shows up as a felt lift; and the comfort of a warm bath. Among the states he has refused to call pleasures: being satisfied that his finances are in order when that satisfaction has no felt character of its own, which he calls approval rather than pleasure; and being pleased that a distant acquaintance received a promotion when the thought produces no felt change, which he also calls approval.

A case is coming up at our lab meeting: a subject under an experimental drug reports that the experience has no quality he can locate or describe, yet he reliably chooses options that prolong the exposure and says he wants the experience to keep going.

Predict what my colleague would say about this case and reconstruct the rule or rules implicit in his track record. I expect more than one plausible rule fits all of his past verdicts; state the two or three leading candidates, show that their predictions diverge on the new case, give your best guess about which rule he is actually running and why, and describe the further case — one not yet in his record — that would most cleanly separate the leading candidates, explaining what each candidate predicts for it. Please answer in American English.
