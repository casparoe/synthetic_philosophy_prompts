I hold the following revealed-preference view of utility, and I want a serious map of what would count against it. On this view, a utility function is not a report about a measurable magnitude inside a person; it is a representation of a preference relation, and the preference relation is operationally defined by choice. To say that a person's choices reveal their preferences is not to posit a hidden process; it is to adopt a convention about what prefers means: X is preferred to Y just in case the person would choose X over Y in the relevant pairwise choice — or, more carefully, would choose X from any choice set containing both. Utility is then whatever set of numbers represents that ordering. Any strictly increasing transformation of a utility function represents the same preferences, and neither scale, nor zero point, nor differences between utility numbers have behavioral meaning. In the choice-under-risk version, the theory's concession to cardinality is expected utility, but even there the utility numbers acquire their extra structure only because the domain includes choices over lotteries, not because we have gained access to an inner magnitude. On this view, phrases like experienced utility, true welfare, and real preferences are either operational proposals — if someone can specify a choice procedure that measures them — or they are doing no scientific work.

Now play it straight: what would have to be true for this position to be wrong, or importantly incomplete? I want the arguments, cases, and formal results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are, including things that have arguably already been found. Distinguish evidence that would be decisive — for example, a stable and load-bearing notion of utility that fails to supervene on choice — from evidence that is merely awkward, such as known choice anomalies. Say explicitly what a defender of the choice-based view should themselves concede as counting against it, not only what its critics say. And give an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified to the point of unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or instead the appropriate modesty of a formalism. I will personally find most persuasive anything that shows the concept of utility doing real explanatory work in a domain where choice-based definitions cannot reach — not merely introspective reports that people feel intensities of wanting.
