Compare two pictures of what is going on when a word seems to have several meanings. On the first picture, a word like "run" or "get" has a list of separate stored senses, and in each use context simply selects one; the senses are discrete lexical entries. On the second picture, a word has one broad, fuzzy meaning potential, and the apparent senses are just stable regions in that space that get crystallized by context and syntax. We only treat them as separate because dictionaries need tidy entries.

To keep this from being just lexicography gossip, work through a concrete batch of uses for one word of your choice (run, set, over, line, or break are all fine). Pick cases that are a little awkward for both pictures — e.g. the dye ran, the story ran, the candidate ran against someone, her nose ran, the trail runs along the ridge.

Then wrestle with the following diagnostic for telling ambiguity apart from mere flexibility: if you coordinate two uses under the same verb and the result sounds like a joke or a slip (she ran out of patience and out the door), that is evidence the uses are pulling toward seperate senses. But if a phrase like "so did he" can pick up both uses without a weird re-parse, that is evidence they share one sense. Does this diagnostic actually support the discrete-senses picture, or does it beg the question against the meaning-potential picture? Be fair to both, then say what you'd keep from the wreckage.
