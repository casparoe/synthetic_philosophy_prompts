I'm in a course on Stoicism and its modern self-help revival, and I've been chewing on a distinction I made up, which I'm tentatively calling the mirror model of the middle zone. Could you act as a professional philosopher who works on Stoic ethics, think carefully, and give me an honest assessment rather than a cheerleader? Oh, and please write the whole answer in American English.

Here's the idea. The standard dichotomy of control says everything is either up to us — my judgments, my choices, how I frame things — or not up to us, like health, reputation, other people's reactions, outcomes. Modern versions usually add a middle category: things I can influence but not determine. Let's call that the partial-control zone. I basically accept that picture.

But here's my actual thesis, and I haven't seen it put quite this way. I think the partial-control zone has no indepedent psychological content: everything in it that actually disturbs us does so only because it's functioning as evidence about something in the full-control zone. Say I'm anxious about a job interview. The outcome — hired or not — is not up to me, but what's really doing the disturbing isn't the outcome itself; it's what I take the outcome to say about me: was I competent, did I prepare the right way, was my impression that I'm good at this correct. Those are judgments, and judgments sit in the full-control zone. Same with health: part of what scares me about a diagnosis isn't only future pain; it's that the diagnosis seems to reveal something about how I've been living or whether I understand my own body. So apparent desires for partial-control outcomes are, on my view, disguised desires for a flattering self-assessment.

So the mirror model: the middle zone reflects our own judgments back at us, and the Stoic move is to aim all therapeutic work at assent and self-assessment in the two-part scheme. My tentative conclusion: the three-part scheme isn't a real third category; it's just a pedagogical ladder for people who can't yet see they're actually disturbed by their own judgments, and once they see it the ladder collapses.

I know this sounds too clean. I'd also like you to steelman the proposal before attacking it, so I can see what the strongest version sounds like.
