ok so in my philosophy of biology seminar we keep hitting the same wall: one guy ends every discussion about functions and teleology with his catchphrase "no reasoner, no reason." The professor had just floated the idea that natural selection can leave behind what he called a free-floating rationale — e.g., the reason your heart pumps is that pumping blood is what hearts were selected for, even though no mind ever entertained that reason. And this guy's entire response was "no reasoner, no reason," as if that settled it. He also used it to dismiss the thought that a gene's "reason" for spreading through a population needn't be represented in the gene or anywhere else. Im pretty sure the slogan is doing a lot of hidden work, but I couldn't say exactly what without sounding like I'm defending intelligent design. Can you pull apart the distinct precise claims "no reasoner, no reason" could be making — what a reason would have to be, what a reasoner would have to be, whether a reason has to be represented or grasped by someone, whether a selection process or a lineage could count as a reasoner in the relevant sense — and then say for each reading whether it's trivial, substantive, or false, and which ones would actually rule out free-floating rationales? A menu of readings with verdicts would help more than one definitive reading.
