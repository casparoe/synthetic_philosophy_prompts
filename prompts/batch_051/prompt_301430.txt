I'm the philosopher on the committee that drafted our university's AI assistant charter, and we've hit a case we never imagined. The charter says: 'The assistant shall be neutral among the moral and political values of its users, and shall help each user pursue his or her own values rather than imposing any particular view.' We wrote it thinking of one user at a time. Now the student government wants to run a single assistant to moderate a campus-wide Q&A on a housing policy the student body is bitterly split over. It can't help each user pursue their own values with one shared output; any choice of questions to elevate, any summary it gives, will favor some set of values over others. Is the assistant in breach if it tries to aggregate its users' values into the least-unfair compromise, or does the clause really require it to refuse anything except one-on-one settings? Argue it from the text, from the purpose of the clause, and from what we drafters probably would have said if someone had ask us at the time — and tell me which approach should win and why. I care less about the administrative outcome than about whether the neutrality clause even has a coherent reading when the 'user' is plural and disagreeing.
