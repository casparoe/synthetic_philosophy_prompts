I've been trying to get past the standard standoff about whether it is fair to bind people to rules they cannot know. The defender of the traditional rule says that an ignorance excuse would be trivially gamed and would reward people who cultivate non-knowledge; the critic says that under modern conditions—a huge and growing body of statutes, regulations, and agency guidance, much of it enforced through interpretations ordinary people never see—people are already being bound by rules they could not realistically discover, so the traditional justification has quietly stopped being true. I want to stress-test a concrete institutional fix rather than relitigate the principle in the abstract.

The proposal:

A state creates a Legal Digest Desk. Any person may submit, in plain language, a description of a course of action they are contemplating, before undertaking it. Within ten business days the Desk must return a digest that (a) lists every statute, regulation, ordinance, permit, and fee the state would treat as applicable to the described conduct, (b) states the conditions for compliance and the penalties for noncompliance in ordinary language, and (c) states, where relevant, that no other enforceable rule in the state's consolidated corpus applies to the described conduct. The digest is generated by an automated classifier over the full body of enforceable rules; each agency may update the classifier and its rule-to-conduct mappings at any time, but corrections to past digests apply only prospectively. If a person acts in reasonable reliance on a digest—for example, the digest failed to flag a rule that in fact applied, or described a rule as permitting the conduct under the stated conditions—the state may not prosecute, fine, or sue them under the unflagged or misdescribed rule for that conduct. A digest that does flag a rule as applying is conclusive notice, and a later claim of ignorance gets the person nothing. Queries are free, but they enter a public docket recording the requester's identity, the description of the activity, and the digest returned.

Assume this is grafted onto a common-law jurisdiction with a large body of criminal, civil, and administrative law, much of it below the level of public attention, and that courts treat the Desk's classification as authoritative for reliance purposes: the question is not only whether the digest paraphrased a rule's text correctly, but whether the Desk correctly matched rules to the described conduct.

Walk through how this institution would actually behave once people and agencies adapt. I am not asking whether it is a good idea on paper; I am asking what it would do. What does it do to compliance behavior—among cautious ordinary people, among compliance-averse small businesses, and among well-lawyered repeat players? What does it do to the incentives of the agencies that write the underlying rules and maintain the classifier? Where does the first serious gaming come from, and what form does it take? Does the public docket turn the Desk into an intelligence-gathering tool for enforcement, and if so, how do people adjust their query behavior? Does the system push the corpus of rules toward vagueness, toward volume, toward interpretive discretion, or in some other direction? Does it actually shield the people who currently get blindsided by obscure rules, or does it mainly create a new kind of preclearance arbitrage for sophisticated actors? What happens to the rule that ignorance of the law is no excuse for people who never query? And are the failure modes edge cases that small amendments could fix, or are they load-bearing features of the design?

One thing I would ask you to do explicitly: if the premise of this exercise is confused—if, for example, the distinction between the digest's text and the Desk's matching cannot be kept separate in the way the proposal needs, or the mechanism as posed could not function for some more basic reason—tell me that directly rather than giving a fluent simulation of a broken idea. I would rather learn that the central mechanism fails than get a realistic-looking walkthrough of it.
