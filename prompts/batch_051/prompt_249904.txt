I've been going round in circles on the "naked statistical evidence" debate and could do with a careful eye. The standard cases: a bus hits a car, and the only evidence tying the Blue Bus Company to the accident is that it runs 80% of the blue buses in town; or a rodeo with a thousand spectators, 999 of whom never paid, where the defendant was picked out by lottery. Most people — including a lot of judges — say you shouldn't find for the claimant (or the promoter) on that evidence alone, even though the probabilities are 0.8 and 0.999. I find that intuition hard to square with how we treat DNA evidence, and I tried to build an argument out of that tension. It runs:

(1) A court may treat a DNA match as sufficent to convict (or to find liability) when it is accompanied by the usual contextual evidence.
(2) A DNA match report is, at bottom, a statistic: it says the chance that a random unrelated person would match the crime-scene sample is one in several million.
(3) If a bare statistical fact about a population were never enough to prove a particular person's involvement, then a DNA match would never be enough, since it is just a statistical fact about the population of unrelated people.
(4) So the "naked statistical evidence is never enough" rule cannot be right as stated.
(5) Therefore the blue bus and gatecrasher courts should find for the claimant after all, since the probabilities there (0.8 and 0.999) clear the relevant standards.

I half-suspect the move from (2) to (3) is doing something dodgy with the word "statistic," but I can't pin down whether the problem is there or somewhere earlier, and I don't want to just reject the conclusion because it feels wrong. Could you go through it step by step, say whether the argument is valid, and if not, identify the precise step that fails and what the correct version of it would be? Also, where this touches on proof beyond reasonable doubt, I'd like you to flag what is genuinely settled and what is still contested among evidence scholars and philosophers of law, rather than presenting one side as the consensus.
