At a small conference on rational choice and risk that I covered last spring, two researchers spent a long lunch arguing about whether the standard coherence arguments for expected utility theory actually work, and I have not managed to settle the matter in my own mind since. Both were arguing in good faith and neither was attacking a strawman. I wrote the exchange down in my notebook as it happened, and I would like a careful adjudication of each step.

1. The first speaker, a theorist sympathetic to expected utility maximization, opened with the money-pump argument. He noted that agents whose preferences follow the Allais pattern — preferring a certain one million dollars to a gamble with an 89 percent chance of one million, a 10 percent chance of five million, and a 1 percent chance of nothing, while also preferring a 10 percent chance of five million with otherwise nothing to an 11 percent chance of one million with otherwise nothing — violate the independence axiom, which says that mixing two gambles with a common third prospect in the same proportions should not reverse your preference between them. Such an agent, he said, can be led through a sequence of individually attractive trades that ends with them holding a prospect strictly worse than the one they started with. The existence of that sequence, he claimed, shows that the preferences are incoherent, because no coherent assignment of value can be cyclically exploited in this way.

2. The second speaker, a behavioral researcher, replied that the pump only works on an agent who chooses myopically, accepting each trade as it is offered. A reflective agent sees the whole sequence coming and refuses to enter it. Vulnerability to a clever bookie, she said, is not the same thing as irrationality; a rational person can have preferences that a bookie could exploit if the person were naive, but the relevant fact is that the person is not naive. The argument, she concluded, demonstrates the bookie's ingenuity rather than the target's irrationality.

3. The theorist answered that this reply backfires. If the agent declines the sequence, they are refusing a series of trades each of which, by their own lights at the moment it is offered, makes them strictly better off. The refusal therefore commits them to knowingly ending up in a position they regard as worse than the one they could have reached at each step. That is not an escape from the charge of incoherence; it is the charge restated. The sequence shows that the pairwise choices cannot be represented by a single ordering over prospects, so for such an agent there is no stable fact about what counts as improving their position.

4. The behavioral researcher's rejoinder was that the sequence only shows her preferences are defined over whole plans, with context and framing doing real work, rather than over isolated gambles. The independence axiom, she said, is a modeling convention — legitimate for some purposes but not a requirement of rationality. The money-pump argument assumes exactly what it is trying to prove, namely that a rational agent must be evaluable by a single context-independent utility function, and nothing in the exchange supplies a reason to accept that assumption. She is not, in any ordinary sense, irrational; she is simply not an expected utility maximizer.

Please adjudicate this exchange. Go through the four moves in order and say, for each, what it establishes, where it overreaches, and how strong it is, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification. Then give an overall verdict on who is more right, and say what, if anything, both are missing. In particular, I would like to know whether the argument has stalled partly because the two of them mean different things by coherence, and what could in principle resolve the dispute.
