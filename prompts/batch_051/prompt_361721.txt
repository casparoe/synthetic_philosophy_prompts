I spent my career as a population geneticist, so I'm not the kind of person who gets impressed by a wild story. I know the birthday problem cold — 23 people, better than even odds — and I've explained selection effects more times than I can count: you never see the misses, you never remember the dream that didn't come true, and you never divide by the size of the denomenator. On paper, the "it was bound to happen to someone" account of coincidence strikes me as just about the best theory I've ever met: simple, quantitative, predictive, and it gives a clean recipe for evaluating any anecdote you care to bring it. I even catch myself deploying it on other people's stories, and it works. Lately I've been reading epistemology — testimony, anecdote, that sort of thing — and it's made an old unease feel sharper.

Last fall I spent an afternoon thinking hard about an old friend I hadn't spoken to in years — not a passing thought, a real hours-long preoccupation — and that same evening he called, out of nowhere, and said he'd been thinking about me all week. I'm an atheist; I don't believe in synchronicity, cosmic significance, or hidden causal wires. I can explain to myself in real time why this was going to happen to someone, and why the fact that it happened to me is just an indexical accident. And I find that explanation fully convincing — right up until I consult the experience itself. The intuition that this particular coincidence is not noise, that it is in some epistemically relevant sense evidence of a real connection, will not go quiet. It doesn't feel like a superstition I half-disown; it feels like something my theory is missing.

So I want to know which should yield. Give me the strongest version of both sides. If the intuition should go, what would it actually take to debunk it responsibly, rather than just not listening to it? If the theory should give, what would a revision look like that isn't just "well, sometimes chance is meaningful" — what would it cost in simplicity and predictive power? And is there a reading on which both survive, maybe by separating personal significance from epistemic warrant, or is that just a comfortable way of avoiding a decision?
