I keep noticing a weird pattern in how people enforce small commitments, and I want a committed explanation, not a survey of options. If a friend is consistently ten minutes late, or says "I'll send it tonight" and doesn't, people eventually decide the friend is unreliable; they get genuinely hurt, and they treat it as a moral failing rather than a mild annoyance. But if you ask them what the wrong consists in, nobody can say. It isn't the lost time—ten minutes is nothing, and we all waste that much on our phones without owing anyone an apology. It isn't lying, because the person usually really did intend to show up when they said it. And it isn't the size of the harm, because the same people will forgive a one-off broken promise of something much bigger if the person has a halfway decent story about being overwhelmed. The pattern even seems backwards: if wrongness scales with harm, the big breach should be the one that gets treated as a character flaw; if wrongness is about disrespect, the big breach seems more disrespectful, not less. So the practice appears to presuppose something about the moral weight of small promises and punctuality that has nothing to do with consequences, and not all that much to do with harm or sincerity either. Reconstruct the implicit theory of obligation or character that would make this pattern make sense. Does the practice strictly presuppose something like the view that a promise gives the other person a special standing to rely on you, so that habitual flakiness is a vice of the will rather than a failure of welfare-maximizing? Or is it equally consistent with a sophisticated consequentialism that treats repeat offenses as evidence about future unreliability? Don't just lay the options out—earn a definite position and defend it. Then say what else in our everyday moral reactions we would have to give up or revise if that view were taken seriously.
