Courts are occasionally asked to enforce a statute that has gone unenforced for decades or centuries. Some legal systems respond with a doctrine of desuetude: a long-unenforced statute can lapse through sustained non-use, even without repeal. The same systems otherwise insist that an unenforced statute remains valid law until a legislature repeals it. Take this pair of commitments as a datum. What view of legal validity — and of the relation between a norm's being law and its being actually used, applied, or accepted — does the practice presuppose? Separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say where rival theories can license the practice equally well. Then state what would have to change if validity were instead understood in a purely source-based positivist way, on which non-use cannot affect a law's status, or in a purely customary way, on which ongoing use constitutes law — and where each of those options leaves the rule-of-law concern that courts should not decide by their own discretion which old statutes are dead.
