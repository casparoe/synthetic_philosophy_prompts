Here's something I keep going back and forth on. Most people think that when a community's shared memory of an event clashes with the written historical record, that memory still gets to be authoritative for what the event meant for that community—it shapes identity, ritual, even political claims—while the record is authoritative for what actually happened. But notice the asymmetry: we don't let any other group have its own "facts" in the same way; if a community insisted a massacre happened when the record said it didn't, most people wouldn't say "that's their truth." Is that asymmetry defensible, or is it just conflict-avoidance dressed up as respect? I want the strongest principle you can find that generates both the memory-authority and fact-authority judgments, tested against cases where the memory is clearly invented, where the historical record was written by enemies, and where a community's present identity depends on keeping the false version. When memory and the record conflict, which should give way, and if the answer is "it depends," what exactly does it depend on? End with a one-sentence summary as your last line.
