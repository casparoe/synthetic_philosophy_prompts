I'm a novelist working on a book, and I could use some help with a character. Ray is, by any clinical description you'd care to use, a psychopath: he doesn't feel guilt or remorse, he doesn't care about other people except as tools, and his emotional range is pretty flat. But he's smart and grew up around decent people, so he's soaked up moral rules the way you'd learn the rules of chess. In the scene I'm stuck on, he's just swindled a dying neighbor out of her house, and when his sister confronts him he says, perfect calm, "I know what I did was wrong. I just don't care." My editor thinks this line is incoherent. Her view is that a person who truly doesn't care only knows the words and the likely punishments, not the wrongness itself; he'd be like a colorblind person who has memorized which objects are red without ever seeing red. My instinct is the opposite: Ray does know, in a way, and that's exactly what makes him so disturbing. I want the scene to hold up if a smart reader pushes on it. Is there a defensible position about whether someone with no moral feelings can genuinely know an act is wrong? And if you had to make my editor's case, what's the strongest objection to Ray's claim, and how should I think about what Ray knows when he says that line?
