I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been reading about an old argument in mathematics over what you're allowed to count as a real mathematical object. The rough story as I understand it: the Greek tradition favored things you could actually construct with compass and straightedge, Descartes opened the door to algebraic curves, and then transcendental curves like the quadratrix snuck in because they solved problems the old toolset couldn't. I'd like a working definition of "legitimate mathematical object" in that debate — not a formal existence criterion, but the threshold a thing has to cross to be let into respectable mathematics.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle. (1) The diagonal of a unit square: constructible with compass and straightedge, it's just a length, but it gave the Pythagoreans trouble because they couldn't express it as a ratio. (2) Trisecting a 60-degree angle with unmarked straightedge and compass alone, which is impossible. (3) The same trisection done with a marked straightedge, or with origami, where it is possible. (4) The quadratrix, a curve traced by combining a straight-line motion with a circular one, which squares the circle but is not compass-and-straightedge constructible. (5) The Dirac delta, which physicists used productively for decades before distribution theory gave it a rigorous footing. (6) A function that is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere — the sort of "monster" function the 19th century didn't know what to do with. (7) A nonmeasurable set, whose existence follows from the axiom of choice but which nobody can exhibit explicitly.

My own instincts: (1) and (3) count, (2) doesn't, and I go back and forth on (4)–(7). Give me a definition, run it through all seven, say where it suprises you (for instance by excluding something I thought was in, or letting in something I thought was out), then revise it if needed. Since I'll actually be reporting on this, also tell me how confident you are in the definition as a whole and which single case you're least sure about.
