I've been trying to work out whether novelty or variety is a genuine part of well-being, rather than merely a useful instrument for staving off boredom and habituation. My verdicts on particular cases keep refusing to add up, and I want to know whether some principle captures them or which verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases.

1. Clara and Ruth have lived forty years with, by every measure I trust, equal subjective well-being: the same balance of pleasant and unpleasant days, the same life satisfaction, the same felt interest and meaning. Clara's life has been objectively full of change — cities, careers, relationships, unfamiliar surroundings — while Ruth's has been objectively repetitive: one town, one job, three friends, the same week in its basic shape for decades. Ruth has not been bored; the repetition suits her. I think Clara's life is somewhat better, though only somewhat.

2. If Ruth's routine includes a modest floor of variety — a hobby that rotates by season, one new person each year, an annual trip, and one skill she slowly masters — I no longer think Clara's life is better at all. Beyond that floor, extra variety seems to add nothing.

3. Sam has eaten the same seven meals, walked the same route, and used the same handful of conversational scripts for decades. He reports being content, insists he is not bored, and finds novelty faintly threatening. I think something is missing from Sam's life, even though he cannot feel its absence.

4. Nina is made genuinely miserable by change: travel, new people, and broken routines leave her anxious for weeks. For Nina, a repetitive life is not a deficient life; I would not prescribe novelty for her at all.

5. Leo changes apartments every year and jobs every two years, but each apartment and each job is almost indistinguishable from the last. The changes are driven by logistics rather than by interest, and he experiences them as administrative noise rather than as fresh engagement. I register no gain in Leo's well-being from all the churn.

6. Marta's last decade has been full of novelty and danger: expeditions, risky work, volatile relationships. By her own accounting, the decade has contained less net happiness than staying home would have. I don't think the variety makes the decade better; if anything, it makes it worse.

7. Uche spends a year unresponsive in a hospital bed while a device gives him the vivid, continuous belief that he is living through a year of intense novelty. Nothing of the kind actually happens. I do not count that year as a good one for him, or at least as much less good than a real year of the same experiences would have been.

I keep drifting among candidate principles: variety as a purely instrumental guard against habituation; variety as a final good above some modest threshold; variety as valuable only when the person wants or can tolerate it; variety as valuable only when it is real rather than merely felt. But Sam and Uche pull me toward an objective account, while Nina and Leo pull me back toward an experiential or desire-based one. What is the most defensible principle behind these verdicts? Which of the seven does it force me to overturn or reinterpret? And what does it say about Tariq, whose life is full of genuine variety by any objective standard but who is tormented by the fear that it is secretly boring?
