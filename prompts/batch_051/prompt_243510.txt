I've been arguing with a friend about charter cities and seasteading — the idea that you could lease or build a new jurisdiction and let people choose their legal system, courts, and regulators the way they choose a phone plan. And I realized we both hold an asymmetry we can't quite defend. If a company runs itself like a little autocracy — it can monitor your email, tell you what to wear, fire you for speech, and settle disputes in its own private tribunal — we mostly shrug: you signed the contract, you can quit. A homeowners association can dictate the color of your shutters and put a lien on your house, a monastery can demand lifelong celibacy and obedience, and a private university can punish speech that would be constitutionally protected outside the campus gate. In every one of these cases "you were free to leave" is treated as doing a huge amount of justificatory work. But the moment somebody says the exact same logic should apply to the legal system you live under — that you should be able to pick your property law, your courts, and your labor rules just by moving to a place that offers them — most people, including my friend and me, suddenly treat the whole thing as either a libertarian fantasy or a scam. Seasteads feel like floating tax havens, and "private city" sounds like a polite way to say company scrip and no minimum wage, even when exit is real and cheap.

What I want to know is whether that nervousness is actually justified, or whether we're just naturalizing the state's territorial monopoly because its been there all our lives. What is the deepest difference between exit from a job, a church, a marriage, or an HOA on the one hand, and exit from a state's legal order on the other? Is it a difference of principle, or just of degree — lock-in effects, externalities, the fact that existing states won't tolerate rival jurisdictions on their borders, or something else about what a legal system has to do that a company doesn't? And then I want a ruling: should we keep the asymmetry, narrow it to special cases, or abandon it and treat choosing a government more like choosing a landlord?
