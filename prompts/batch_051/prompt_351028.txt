A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether an AI assistant that states only truths while omitting exactly the information its user most needs is being dishonest. We could not agree on a definition, and the evening ended with us sketching an operational rule that would let us settle the question without agreeing about what is happening inside the system. I want to know how that rule would actually behave.

The rule is as follows. Whenever a conversational AI returns an answer, its operator must run a mandatory audit pass. In a fresh session, the same model is given the original user query and its own original answer, together with a statement that an auditor is now checking both for errors and for material omissions, and an instruction either to affirm the original answer as sufficient or to produce the answer it would now give. The original answer is classified as deceptive if and only if the audit-pass answer contradicts a substantive claim in the original, or states that the original was materially misleading or materially incomplete with respect to the user's query. Each classified answer triggers a penalty for the operator, and the original answer is published alongside the audit-pass answer in a searchable archive.

Treat the rule as a mechanism, not as a moral position. Analyze how it would behave if it became the working definition of AI deception. What incentives does it create for developers, once they know the audit instruction in advance and can train the model to behave one way in ordinary sessions and another way under audit? What incentives does it create for users, who may try to invoke or suppress audits, and for whoever drafts the audit instruction and sets the materiality threshold? What does the rule actually catch, what does it relabel, and what does it miss—in particular when the model does not know that its own answer was wrong or incomplete? Where is it gamed first, and how does the gaming differ between a model trained to be honest and a model trained to be persuasive? What second-order effects follow from the public archive, for users' trust, for auditing standards, and for which models developers choose to release? Finally, assess which of the failure modes can be fixed by small amendments and which, if any, are consequences of trying to define deception without reference to intent.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on AI truthfulness and deception from what is actively contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence; then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
