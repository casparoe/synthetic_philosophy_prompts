I've spent my career in condensed matter physics and only got into philosophy after I retired, so forgive me if I mangle the terminology. Ive landed on the view that everything is physical — roughly, that the physical facts are all the facts there are, and that once they are fixed, everything else (including the mental) is fixed too. But I keep tripping over Hempel's dilemma, which I only recently learned has a name. As I understand it: if the word 'physical' in physicalism means "whatever current physics describes," the doctrine is probably false, since current physics is almost certainly incomplete — dark matter, the measurement problem, quantum gravity, whatever. But if it means "whatever the ideal completed physics of the far future would describe," then the doctrine starts to feel empty, or at least unfalsifiable, because that future physics could just absorb whatever we now call non-physical. So my question isnt whether physicalism is true; it's what could count against it. I want the defeaters laid out for someone who actually holds the view: which observations, arguments, formal results, or unacceptable consequences should move me, and which of them would a committed physicalist concede as counting against them rather than dismissing. Be honest about whether Hempel's dilemma has already insulated physicalism so much that it risks nothing, and whether that is a problem for a metaphysical claim of this kind.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Sort them from whatever would count against physicalism most decisively down to whatever is merely awkward, and use the fifth item for your verdict on whether the Hempel's dilemma point leaves the view with nothing at stake. Write in American English.
