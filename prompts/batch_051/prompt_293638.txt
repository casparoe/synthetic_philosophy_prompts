I want to defend a thesis about respect: that part of what we owe one another resides in the attitudes rather than in outward conduct — in recognition, not merely in treatment. But the following argument has been pressed on me as a way of showing that this thesis demands the impossible. Each step sounds right as I read it, yet the conclusion seems ruinous for the very idea of attitudinal respect. I need to know where the argument goes wrong, or, if it does not go wrong, what the least costly thing to give up is.

Here is the argument:

(1) It is possible to fail morally toward another person purely in how one regards them, even when one's outward conduct is faultless. Suppose one person behaves toward a colleague with exactly the courtesy and fairness that every rule of conduct would require, while privately regarding that colleague as a mere instrument whose interests carry no independent weight. It would be hard to deny that this person is subject to moral criticism, even though no particular action can be singled out as the failing.

(2) If such an attitude is a genuine moral failing, then the person who holds it is under a moral requirement not to hold it: they ought to regard the other person otherwise. Otherwise we would be calling the attitude morally wrong while denying that anything is required of the person, which seems to sever wrongness from oughtness.

(3) The attitudes in question are, in significant part, belief-like. To regard a person as an equal, or as a source of valid claims on one's conduct, is to take it that certain things are true of them: that their interests matter in something like the way one's own do, that their reasons carry authority, that they are an end and not merely a means.

(4) Belief-like states are not under voluntary control in the way a requirement would demand. A person cannot adopt or discard such a view merely by deciding to, any more than they can decide to believe it will not rain while the sky is visibly dark; and the indirect routes to changing a belief — seeking out evidence, attending to certain considerations, acting as if one already believed — do not place the target attitude itself within one's control, because their outcome depends on what the evidence supports and on attitudes one already has.

(5) One cannot be under a moral requirement to bring about what is not within one's control. Ought implies can.

(6) Therefore, one cannot be under a moral requirement to hold, or to avoid, the belief-like attitudes mentioned in (3).

(7) By (2) and (6), the sort of purely attitudinal failure described in (1) cannot be a genuine moral failing.

(8) So the view I wanted to defend is false: respect can be fully discharged by outward conduct, and what a person privately thinks of another has no moral significance except insofar as it shapes their actions.

I find (8) almost impossible to accept, because it implies that someone who is courteous and fair while at bottom denying another's equal standing is morally beyond criticism. But I do not want to keep a thesis merely because I find its denial unappealing. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If I had to preserve one claim, I would most like to preserve (1); I would rather not abandon (5) unless the argument forces it. When you answer, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis as a whole, and identify the step or background assumption you are least sure about.
