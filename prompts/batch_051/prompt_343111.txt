I'm a journalist working on a piece about how governments reckon with their own histories, and I keep being struck by how philosophically odd the practice of state apology is once you look directly at it. A sitting government — most of whose officials and citizens had no hand in the original acts — formally apologizes on behalf of the state for a wrong committed decades or centuries earlier: colonial conquest, enslavement, the forced removal of children, wartime atrocities. The victims are usually dead; the apology is addressed to descendants or to the public at large. The state itself may have changed its constitution, borders, or form of government in the interim. And yet the apology is not treated as theater or metaphor; it is treated as doing morally serious work. I'd like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. In particular, does it presuppose that a state is a single moral agent persisting through time and capable of inheriting obligations from its past selves, or only that a current government is a convenient custodian of a legacy? Does it presuppose that descendants of victims, or a present-day community, have standing to receive an apology for wrongs done to people they never knew? Does it presuppose a particular theory of what apology is — that its office is acknowledgment and commitment rather than the expression of an individual perpetrator's remorse? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say whether the commitments are mutually consistent, and tell me what a state apology could still amount to if one denied, say, that the state is literally the same agent across time. Finally, say how confident you are in the reconstruction and which point you are least sure of.
