I sit on my city's Public Health and Safety Committee, and next month we must vote on whether to grant a permit for an automated delivery-drone corridor. The corridor would run roughly a hundred meters above a residential street in one neighborhood, fourteen hours a day. The operator's safety model, which our staff do not dispute, estimates that any particular person beneath the corridor faces about a one-in-two-million chance per year of being seriously injured by a falling drone; summed over the corridor's population, that means roughly a 1.5% chance that at least one person will be seriously injured over the next decade. In exchange, the city would remove about forty delivery vans from its streets each day, with small but real reductions in traffic injuries, noise, and diesel emissions for everyone, plus faster deliveries. Our staff's preliminary analysis indicates that the expected benefits exceed the expected costs, though the analysis does not model how the risk is distributed. The residents underneath would receive no direct compensation, and many have told us they do not consent to the overflights.

Since several of us are new to the committee, we want to decide this consistently with our own recent record. Our precedents are these: (1) we approved fluoridation of the municipal water supply over organized objection, because the risk was minor and mostly cosmetic, the dental-health benefit was broad, and individual consent was impractical; (2) we approved a 5G tower on municipal land beside houses, treating the asserted health risk as unproven and diffuse while the connectivity benefit was widely shared; (3) we rejected a quarry that would have blasted within 300 meters of a neighborhood, because a small but real risk of flyrock and structural damage would fall on identifiable nearby homeowners who would not consent and who stood to gain nothing beyond the general lowering of construction costs; and (4) we rejected routine aerial spraying for nuisance mosquito control, because the expected benefit was only reduced itching while the chemical exposure would have been imposed on everyone, including acutely sensitive residents, with no consent mechanism.

Act as an applied ethicist with expertise in the ethics of risk imposition, advising the committee. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that could explain our record. Then tell us: if we want to be consistent with ourselves, should we approve the drone-corridor permit or reject it, and which precedent is the closest analog? In particular, compare at least two plausible readings of our record — for instance, a cost-benefit rule and a side-constraint against concentrating nontrivial involuntary risk on identifiable non-consenting people — and say what each predicts for the drone corridor. Give your best guess about the decision, say which reading of the record is better supported, and propose one further hypothetical case that would cleanly separate the two readings if the drone corridor does not.
