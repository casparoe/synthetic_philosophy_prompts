Last week in our formal epistemology seminar, two of the other grad students got into it after a session on ideal rationality, and they've been relitigating it ever since. The session was on logical omniscience: standard Bayesian models assume an agent is certain of all logical truths and believes every consequence of what it believes, which no real reasoner can do. Mina argued that the whole "problem" is overblown — it's just a harmless idealization, like frictionless planes. Ideal rationality is normative, she said, so of course it says an agent should believe what follows from their beliefs, even if no finite mind always manages to. Dev disagreed. He said this particular idealization doesn't approximate anything: if you idealize away inference itself, the model has nothing left to tell a real reasoner about which next inference to make, so it isn't an idealization of bounded reasoning, it's a model of a different subject. Mina shot back that we idealize away computational limits all the time in economics and in game theory, and nobody calls those fields bankrupt; you add computational costs later and keep the core notion of rationality intact. Dev replied that logical omniscience is different, because "adding costs later" means replacing the model wholesale: once you cap the number of proof steps, the agent stops being closed under even modus ponens — it can believe P and believe P implies Q, but not believe Q — which is exactly the sort of incoherence the idealization was supposed to rule out. Mina said that's precisely what impossible-worlds semantics is for: you let the truncated, inconsistent epistemic states live in impossible worlds while keeping the actual world logically well-behaved. Dev said that's a technical band-aid, not a solution — someone still has to draw a principled boundary between the worlds where normal logic holds and teh impossible worlds, and if the boundary is just "whatever the agent hasn't derived yet," the semantics is a notational variant of the original problem. That's where they stopped.

I ended up unsure whether either of them had landed a real point or they were just talking past each other. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each main claim, say who's right and to what degree, and then say what (if anything) both of them are missing. I'd especially like to know whether "logical omniscience is just an idealization" and "capping proof steps breaks modus ponens" can both be true, and what that implies for whether a usable model of bounded rationality is even possible.
