I have spent much of my career teaching the history of ethics, and I keep noticing a move in ancient and early modern writing on old age that I have never seen given a name. The losses that supposedly define old age are not merely accepted or consoled; they are converted into positive reasons for preferring the state itself. Bodily weakening is presented as a deliverance from the tyranny of appetite; the cooling of sexual desire is described not as a privation but as a form of freedom; retirement from public office is treated as relief from the demands of ambition; and the approach of death is re-described as reaching a calm harbour after a long voyage. In Cicero's dialogue on old age, Cato pushes this so far that the standard complaints are stood on their head: what the complainers call a deficit becomes, on his account, the removal of something that was never an ingredient in a good life in the first place. I know that psychologists have their own terms for the nearby territory — sour grapes, and the adaptation of one's preferences to deprivation — and that the Stoics recommend exercises of negative visualisation. But those are either post hoc rationalisations or techniques for weakening attachments in advance of loss. The thing I am trying to identify is more specific and more normative: a mode of argument in which a loss is not softened but inverted, and the inversion is offered as a substantive evaluative claim, namely that what was lost was never a genuine good. Does this mode of argument have an established name in the philosophical literature? If it does, I want to know its boundaries: how exactly is it distinguished from adaptive preference formation, sour grapes, and Stoic negative visualisation, and where does a genuine instance shade into mere rationalisation? If it has no name, what would you call it?
