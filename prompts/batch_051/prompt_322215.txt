I am a PhD student in applied entomology, and alongside my doctoral work I hold a part-time advisory position with a regional integrated pest management (IPM) extension program that advises grain storage facilities and specialty crop growers. Our office receives two or three proposals a month that are justified partly or wholly by appeal to the welfare of the insects themselves: replacing an inexpensive fumigant with controlled-atmosphere storage that kills stored-product beetles more slowly but is claimed to be less painful; deploying pheromone-based mating disruption that leaves a pest population to starve gradually rather than be killed outright; or mass-rearing a parasitoid wasp that destroys a caterpillar population over several days. Each decision involves tens of thousands of dollars annually, and the director must approve a written recommendation within a week. My colleagues are skeptical: the prevailing view in our field is that the probability that these insects are sentient in any morally significant sense is low, so spending real money on an uncertain possibility strikes them as unserious. I have been arguing the scale argument, namely that the affected populations are so enormous that even a low probability of sentience, multiplied by the number of individuals and by the welfare difference between methods, could swamp more familiar costs; we have concluded that we cannot keep deciding these cases on intuition.

Please write a decision procedure, as a flowchart in words, that our program could actually run on each incoming proposal. It should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must be usable by staff with entomology or agronomy training but no formal philosophy, should take no more than a few hours per proposal, and should take uncertainty about insect sentience seriously without pretending that uncertainty can be resolved at the desk. Please apply the procedure to three concrete cases — the fumigant replacement, the mating-disruption plan, and the parasitoid release — making at least one a case where the procedure returns an answer my colleagues will not like, and say plainly where the procedure will still misfire. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
