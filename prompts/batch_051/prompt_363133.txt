Since I retired from the lab I've been reading political philosophy, and I've become mildly obsessed with the line between a gift and a bribe. A friend who spent decades in development economics insists everyone in that field carries around some rough test, but none of them survive contact with the actual cases: the box of mangoes to the customs officer, the campaign donation, the envelope under the table at a land-registry office. In the lab I'd want an operational definition; here I cant get even a stable criterion. So far I've collected these candidate tests, which I take to be the standard ones: size (under some amount it's a gift, over it a bribe); timing (before the official's decision it's a bribe, after it's a thank-you gift); the counterfactual (would they have done the same thing anyway?); explicitness of the quid pro quo; intent to influence that specific exercise of discretion; the local-custom test (if the practice is a locally accepted tradition, Western anti-corruption standards are just parochial); and the secrecy test (open gifts are fine, hidden payments are bribes).

I want a candidate criterion that is not on that list and is not a variant, sharpening, or combination of anything on it. Then tell me honestly whether it's any good: does it catch the cases that actually bother us and let the harmless ones go, or does it collapse under the first obvious counterexample? If you think no such criterion exists, say wether that's because the list really is exhaustive, or because the project of drawing one clean line is itself the mistake.
