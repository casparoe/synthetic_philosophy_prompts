I've been going down a rabbit hole with Descartes' La Géométrie and got stuck on the passage where he sorts curves into geometrical and mechanical. His rule, as I understand it, is that a curve belongs in geometry only if you can capture it as an algebraic equation in coordinates—so circles, parabolas, and the other conics are in. But the spiral and the quadratrix, the curve people used for squaring the circle, are out because they come from two independent motions that can't be related by a finite equation. I find this criterion weirdly attractive. It gives a crisp answer to the question of what counts as a legitimate mathematical object: can you reduce it to a polynomial relation between x and y? If not, it's mechanical, not really a geometric curve.

But then I think about a hanging chain making a catenary, or a vibrating string tracing a sine wave. Those look exactly like the sort of thing geometry should be about—smooth, precise, drawn by nature. And yet according to Descartes' criterion they'd be excluded too, because catenary and sine are transcendental; no polynomial equation expresses them. So either the rule is too narrow, or my intuition is smuggling in physics and pictures and calling them mathematical. I honestly don't know which should give. Walk me through what each option costs. If I keep Descartes' rule, do I really have to say the catenary isn't a legitimate geometric object, or is there a reading where it sneaks back in? If I drop or loosen the rule to include transcendental curves, what do I put in its place so the boundary doesn't dissolve and let every wiggly gadget in? And is there a version of this debate where Descartes' point was about constructions, not about which curves exist mathematically, and my confusion is from conflating the two? Give me a negotiated verdict, not just a winner. I want to know what would justify debunking the intuition, amending the theory, or saying they were never in conflict.
