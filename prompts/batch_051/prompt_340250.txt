I teach a seminar on decision theory, and a former student of mine now runs a small hospital system's formulary committee. She has to decide, maybe twice a month, whether to add a new drug or test when the clinical trial data gives a clear estimate for one outcome but leaves another risk essentially ambiguous — e.g., a new anticoagulant reduces stroke by about the same amount as the standard one, but the rate of a rare but terrible bleeding side effect is reported only as "between 0 and 2 percent" because the trial wasn't powered to nail it down. Her boss wants a decsion in 30 minutes and the committee has to sign off, so she can't just say "we need more data."

I've been rereading Daniel Ellsberg's 1961 paper "Risk, Ambiguity, and the Savage Axioms," and I keep coming back to the classic urn example. He describes an urn with 30 red balls and 60 balls that are either black or yellow in an unknown proportion. If you win by drawing red, people tend to prefer that bet to winning by drawing black; but if the payoff condition is "not red" versus "not black," the same people tend to switch. That pattern seems to show something deeper than just "people don't like not knowing the odds" — because in each pair the odds are identical if you do the arithmetic, only the locus of the unknown proportion shifts.

I'd like you to write a decision procedure she could run on a proposal in that 30-minute window, grounded in whatever the Ellsberg paradox actually teaches about ambiguity aversion. It should be a checklist in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the issue is values rather than facts. Then run it on two proposals: the anticoagulant above, and a new cancer screening test with a known false-positive rate but an unknown false-negative rate because follow-up data is incomplete. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire.
