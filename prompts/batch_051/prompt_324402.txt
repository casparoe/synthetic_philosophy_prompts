I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I would like a careful piece on whether the doomsday argument survives a specific change in assumptions. I am less interested in the ordinary back-and-forth over whether the argument is sound in its original form than in what happens to it once we grant a premise that much of my corner of the internet now treats as live.

Set up the argument in its standard two-hypothesis form. One hypothesis says the total number of humans who will ever live is only a small multiple of the number who have lived so far, so a birth rank of R, somewhere in the tens of billions, is unremarkable. The other hypothesis says a long future contains many orders of magnitude more humans, in which case a birth rank of R would be surprisingly early. Assume the self-sampling assumption in its usual form: you should reason as if you were randomly drawn from some reference class of observers, so an observation of your birth rank is more likely under hypotheses in which the total size of the class is closer to R. That likelihood boost toward the short-future hypothesis is the doomsday argument.

Now take as given the development I want the argument tested against. Suppose a technologically mature civilization eventually builds machine minds, whether AIs or emulations, whose number of observers or observer-moments exceeds all biological humans who have ever lived by many orders of magnitude, and suppose also that whether these minds come to exist at all depends heavily on choices our civilization makes in roughly the next century or two. Do not argue with this assumption; accept it and ask what it does to the argument.

Then answer the following. Work through the argument under each natural way of fixing the reference class. If the class is restricted to biological humans, the argument appears to run unchanged, but the restriction is now carrying enormous weight; say what could possibly justify excluding machine minds, and whether that justification can be supplied without begging the question. If the class includes machine minds, then the long-future hypothesis now contains an astronomically larger total observer count, and finding yourself as an early biological human becomes even more surprising under that hypothesis; say exactly what follows, and whether the argument now predicts human extinction sooner, predicts instead that the vast machine-mind future will not happen, or forces some other conclusion. If the natural repair is to adopt something like the self-indication assumption instead, show how that changes the structure. If priors would do important work at some step, say where.

Finally address the decision-theoretic wrinkle. In the original argument the total observer count is a fixed fact about the world over which we are merely uncertain. Under the stipulated development, the size of the future observer population is partly an object of our own present decisions. Explain whether this breaks a premise of the argument and whether the self-sampling assumption can be applied innocently when the larger-population hypothesis describes a future that present agents might choose to bring about or prevent. Throughout, identify precisely which premise the development bears on and whether the argument can be restored in another form.

End with an honest verdict: does the doomsday argument survive this development, survive only in a repaired form whose usual conclusion has changed, or is it dead for this purpose; and state in a sentence or two what, if anything, remains usable.

Please write in American English. Use continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in an email or letter. Do not cite or list any sources and do not rely on specific commentary on the argument; the reasoning should proceed entirely from the setup above.
