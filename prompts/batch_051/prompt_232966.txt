I am writing from an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, where we have been arguing about whether a language model's chain-of-thought output is in any real sense a report of the processing that produced its answer. One side says that when a model writes out some intermediate line of reasoning and then reaches a conclusion, the model is, at least to a useful approximation, telling us why it answered as it did. The other side says this is a post-hoc story: the answer is determined by computational processes that the generated text does not describe, and the chain is a fluent narrative draped over that process. The trouble is that neither side has specified what would count as evidence, and I am starting to suspect the dispute may be partly conceptual.

My question is whether the claim that a self-generated chain-of-thought is faithful to the model's own reasoning can be given a concrete empirical test, and, if so, what the strongest such test would look like.

Please be concrete about the setup: the kind of model, the task, the intervention or measurement, and the design. State the result that would favor the faithful-report reading and the result that would favor the post-hoc reading. Then be precise about what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, address this difficulty: if I edit, delete, or insert an intermediate token in a chain and the final answer changes, that shows the token was causally upstream of the answer, but it does not by itself show that the token was a faithful description of prior computation rather than simply one more piece of context the model processed. Is there any further manipulation, control condition, or analysis that could pull those two apart? Finally, say whether we should keep faithfulness as a binary notion or replace it with a graded or multidimensional one, and if the latter, which dimensions you think cannot be collapsed into one another.
