I cover science and ideas for a living, and for the past several months I have been reporting a long feature about the ways people who take doing good seriously — aid economists, animal welfare researchers, a retired philosopher or two — decide that one cause outranks another. A recurring question in my notebooks is whether different kinds of value combine by addition, by multiplication, or by priority ranking. In the middle of the scale, small harms and benefits seem to sum. At the extremes, they seem to stop summing and stand in a fixed order. And the formula that keeps resurfacing in these conversations — that the value of an experience is the product of how conscious it is and how good it feels, so that either factor at zero gives zero — feels right to me, but also sits badly with some of my own judgments.

To make myself precise, I have written down five verdicts. (1) I would fund a program that relieves thirty thousand adults of a year of frequent, moderate headaches over one that prevents a single child from acquiring a permanent, moderately painful condition, and I would not hesitate; in the middle range, enough small things can outweigh a larger thing. (2) I would not cause one person an hour of overwhelming, life-shattering pain in exchange for any number of other people each receiving a few seconds of barely noticeable warmth; quite suddenly, the exchange rate is undefined. (3) I think vividness of awareness is a multiplier rather than a separate good: intensifying awareness of an already pleasant experience adds value, but intensifying awareness of a painful one makes the situation worse, and I cannot name any amount of additional alertness that is good in itself when added to agony. (4) The same multiplication seems right at zero: there is no way to benefit a plant, a corporation, or a rock by arranging things in its interests, because one factor is zero; and an alert person in a state of absolutely flat affect, neither pleasant nor unpleasant, is not being benefited or harmed at that moment, because the other factor is zero. (5) But the lower end gives me trouble. I count an hour of fear in a much dimmer consciousness, a fish, as less important than the same hour of fear in a human, and I do not know whether that is a continuous multiplier or a brute ranking. If the multiplier is finite, then enough frightened fish should eventually outweigh one human's year of severe, treatable depression; if it is a ranking, I need to believe in a threshold I could not point to.

What I want is the principle behind the pattern. Tell me which single principle — additive, multiplicative, lexical, or an explicit hybrid — fits these verdicts best, and state it precisely enough that each clause is motivated by the case it explains. Then test it on two situations I have not settled: (i) a fund that slightly improves the cramped lives of millions of farmed animals for a year versus one that gives excellent end-of-life care to a few thousand terminally ill people; and (ii) whether increasing the richness and intensity of conscious experience in lives that are already flourishing is a real charitable aim, or merely a pleasant side effect. Finally, tell me which of my listed verdicts the principle forces me to overturn, and whether the cost of saving all five is a complication so baroque that I should prefer a simpler principle with a single casualty.
