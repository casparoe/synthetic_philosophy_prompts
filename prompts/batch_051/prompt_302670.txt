In *How to Do Things with Words*, J. L. Austin begins by separating two kinds of utterances. One kind, constative utterances, includes statements, descriptions, and reports; these are said to be about the world and are properly assessed as true or false. The other kind, performative utterances, includes promising, betting, naming a ship, and marrying; these do not describe an independent state of affairs but are themselves the doing of, or part of the doing of, the act in question, and they are assessed not as true or false but as happy or unhappy depending on whether the relevant conventional procedure exists, is invoked correctly and completely, and is accompanied by the appropriate intentions and commitments. Later in the same book, however, Austin subjects this divide to sustained pressure. He observes that stating is itself an act performed in saying something, that constatives can suffer from infelicities of the same broad sort as performatives, that performatives can be answerable to the way the world is, and that the supposed contrast between saying something and doing something will not survive close examination. By the time he introduces the locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary distinction, the constative-performative contrast appears to have been subsumed into a general theory of speech acts rather than retained as a fundamental division.

This raises a question of whether the two stages of the book are ultimately in conflict. Can they be made consistent, and if so, at what cost? Is the later account best read as rejecting the earlier distinction, refining it, or treating it as a useful but unstable heuristic whose real content gets relocated in the new framework? If the distinction is heuristic rather than strictly true, what exactly is the residue it was tracking, and does the full illocutionary framework preserve that residue? If, on the other hand, there is a genuine inconsistency, should we attribute it to a change of mind, to the cumulative pressure of Austin’s own examples, or to something about the lecture format itself? I want a reading that specifies what the early constative-performative distinction actually accomplishes, which of Austin’s later observations force the revision, and whether a fully general theory of illocutionary acts really eliminates the special status of truth-evaluable language or merely relocates it.
