I've been rereading Marx's Capital for a graduate seminar on social ontology—specifically the section on commodity fetishism—and I keep tripping over a passage that I think gets misused. In Chapter 1, Section 4, Marx writes:

"Exchange value, at first sight, presents itself as a quantitative relation, as the proportion in which values in use of one sort are exchanged for those of another sort, a relation constantly changing with time and place. Hence exchange value appears to be something accidental and purely relative, and consequently an intrinsic value, i.e., an exchange value that is inseparably connected with, inherent in commodities, seems a contradiction in terms."

The seminar has been discussing money as a social kind: the idea that something counts as money not because of its physical stuff but because of a network of collective attitudes, institutions, and practices. Marx here seems to be saying that exchange value is a social relation that gets mistaken for a property of the thing itself. My question is whether this argument survives the shift to purely digital fiat money. In a system where the money object is a database entry with no use value, no material substance, and no commodity backing, does Marx's claim about exchange value appearing as a property of a thing still land? Or does the argument only work against the background of a world where money is still imagined as a commodity like gold or silver? I'm interested in which exact premise is under pressure, and whether there's a repaired version of the fetishism argument that says something weaker but still useful about digital money.
