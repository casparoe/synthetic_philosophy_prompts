Hey, I'm working on a short essay for a blog (no citations, the venue hates footnotes) and I want comments before I send it in. It riffs on that old thought experiment — I think it's the Hofstadter dialogue in The Mind's I — about whether a book encoding all of Einstein's brain states could itself be conscious, or whether reading the book could recreate his stream of thought. I've been trying to argue the book can't be conscious, but not for the usual "it's just a representation" reason. Here's my draft so far. I'm not looking for line edits; I mostly want to know if the argument actually works and wether I'm being fair to the functionalist.

Draft:

Setup: suppose a book's text encodes every neural state of Einstein's brain over a ten-minute stretch, together with a finite rule for moving from each state-plus-input to the next state. A reader who applies the rule at the right pace reproduces the exact causal sequence of Einstein's brain. The tempting thought is: the mind just is the sequence plus the rule, and the book contains both, so the book is (or at least could be) conscious. Reading it would be like playing a score: the experience is simply there in the text.

I want to resist the tempting thought, not by saying the book is a mere representation, but by saying the book has no dynamics of its own. A brain doesn't merely pass through a sequence of states; the transitions are produced by the system itself in real time, as a function of its own prior states and inputs. The book never produces its next state. The reader turns the page, the reader applies the rule, the reader supplies the tempo. The book is a set of states waiting to be animated. So if anything is conscious in this setup, it's the reader-book hybrid or the reading process — not the book.

The worry: this same objection seems to hit a computer running a brain simulation, where a clock and a program counter also supply transitions from outside the simulated states. If I want to allow the computer but exclude the book, I need to explain why a clocked machine has its own dynamics and a read book doesn't. My best shot is that in the computer the state changes are lawfully connected to the machine's own physical states from moment to moment, while in the book they're connected to the book's physical states only through the reader's choices. The clock is part of the machine and runs under the same continuous causal laws as the simulation; the reader is an external agent. But that boundary-drawing is suspiciously convenient, and it's the same move people criticize in the extended-mind debate, where you draw the system boundary to get the verdict you wanted in advance.

So my tentative thesis: a book can't be conscious because consciousness requires endogenous dynamics, not just the right sequence of states. Once you accept that, the interesting question shifts from "which substrates can implement minds" to "what makes a system's dynamics its own." I'm worried that's just relocating the mystery.

Comments I'd especially like:

1. Am I mischaracterizing the functionalist? Some functionalists would say the book doesn't even realize the right causal organization because the dispositional profile of the book is wrong; is that a separate and better objection, or just mine in disguise?
2. Does the "endogenous dynamics" requirement do real work, or does it just beg the question by defining consciousness as the thing books lack?
3. Is the conclusion too strong? Maybe the right conclusion is that the book is only contingently not conscious, since a book could be built with its own page-turning mechanism.
4. What's the strongest objection someone would make, and do I need to answer it in a blog-length piece?
