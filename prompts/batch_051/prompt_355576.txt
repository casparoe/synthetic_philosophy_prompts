Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed testimonial reductionist and a committed testimonial anti-reductionist. The two have spent the past year trying to determine whether their disagreement about the epistemology of testimony is a genuine clash of principles or partly an artifact of different terminologies, and they have agreed to treat the following passage from David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X, Part I, as the reductionist's canonical statement of the view:

> It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses. It being a general maxim, that no objects have any discoverable connexion together, and that all the inferences, which we can draw from one to another, are founded merely on our experience of their constant and regular conjunction; it is evident, that we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other. Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony. A man delirious, or noted for falsehood and villany, has no manner of authority with us.

The deliverable should be the document such a collaboration would produce. Begin with a numbered list of claims both parties can sign. It should include substantive claims about what the passage does and does not assert, about the difference between the genealogy of testimonial trust and its justificatory structure, and about which cases any adequate account of testimony must handle. Do not let the agreed list collapse into commonplaces; it should force both sides to take positions on, for example, whether a hearer's reliance on testimony can be rationally sensitive to evidence about a speaker's reliability without any explicit inferential step, and whether the acquisition of trust in early childhood is a special case or a central one for the theory.

Then produce a disagreements table. For each row, include: a neutral statement of the dispute; a formulation of the reductionist position that a reductionist would accept as fair; a formulation of the anti-reductionist position that an anti-reductionist would accept as fair; the crux or sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; and, where possible, a concrete case, argument, or empirical finding both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. At least one row should turn on the passage's claim that assurance is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony. The reductionist may read this as requiring that a hearer's justification rest, at least tacitly, on a posteriori inference from observed regularities; the anti-reductionist may reply that this either misdescribes our actual epistemic dependence on others or reduces to a claim about the causal history of belief that does not itself settle the belief's epistemic standing. The two should address the following hard cases: a child who accepts a parent's report before possessing any track record of the parent's reliability; a witness whose reliability one has observed only in a domain unrelated to the present report; and a speaker who tells you something you already believe on independent grounds, so that the testimony is not your basis for belief. The two should also register whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—for instance, whether the word "derived" is being used psychologically or epistemically in the passage, and whether the two camps can accept a single disambiguation.

The document should be written so both parties would sign it, including the characterizations of the other side's view. End with a short note stating what each side would count as genuine evidence against its own position.
