I sit on a standing committee at a seminary that assesses reports of religious experience submitted by students, to decide whether such reports may be treated as evidence for God. The committee's governing rule, adopted in the early 1960s, reads:

'No report of a private religious experience shall be credited as evidence for the existence or action of God if the experience admits of a sufficient explanation by known natural causes. To be credited, a report must also be consistent with received doctrine and be accompanied by an observable deepening of the claimant's moral and spiritual life.'

The drafters plainly had in mind dreams, voices, visions, and sudden transports in prayer. We now face a case they did not foresee. During a cognitive science demonstration, a graduate student at the seminary underwent a non-invasive brain stimulation technique that, when applied to a specific site on the temporal lobe, reliably produces in healthy subjects a vivid sense of an unseen benevolent presence that fades when the stimulation ends. The student, who had not been told what the technique does, felt an overwhelming presence she took to be God; afterwards the researchers explained that the experience was entirely produced by the equipment and that the effect is well understood. Since then, the student has begun daily prayer and charitable work and reports no loss of conviction. The committee must decide whether the first clause bars her report, and more broadly whether a religious experience that admits of a complete natural explanation can remain evidence for God. I am less interested in the committee's actual vote than in what the text, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if asked, and a reasonable contemporary reading each require—and which of those approaches should govern now. In particular, I want to understand whether the clause excludes her experience simply because it was caused naturally, or only when a natural explanation should rationally reduce her confidence that it was divine, and what each answer would do to the standing of religious experience as an independent source of evidence.
