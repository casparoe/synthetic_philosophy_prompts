I spent forty years as a physiologist, most of it on thermoregulation and circadian control loops, and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously. The problem I keep coming back to is the paradox of hedonism: the harder you aim at happiness or pleasure as you're goal, the less of it you seem to get, while people who throw themselves into something for its own sake often end up happy as a byproduct. To my old eye that looks like a control problem: happiness behaves like a variable that destabilizes when you close a feedback loop around it, and that accumulates when it's left as an uncontrolled output of a system aimed at something else.

What I'd like is a systematic translation. Restate the paradox of hedonism in the vocabulary of control theory — set points, error signals, feedback loops, stability, controllability, observability, multi-input multi-output plants, cost functions — as faithfully as you can, and I'm fluent in that vocabulary so don't water it down. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the paradox map cleanly onto control theory (I suspect the advice "don't make happiness your reference signal" maps onto controllability and observability limits), which parts you had to distort, and which parts have no expression in control-theoretic terms at all. The one I keep snagging on is that control theory seems to have no natural way to say "do the activity for its own sake rather than for an outcome," since every behavior in a control setup is in service of a reference or a cost. I want to know whether that residue is a limitation of my vocabulary, or a sign that the philosophers' way of putting the paradox is vaguer than it looks. Is the paradox just a fact about a badly controllable, badly observed variable, or is it pointing at something control theory can't say?
