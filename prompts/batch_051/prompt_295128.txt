Here is a clause from the governing instrument of a conservation trust, drafted in the 1970s:

"The Trustees shall endeavour to restore to their historic ranges species extirpated from those ranges by human action, using the most faithful available representative of any such species, provided that the release would not produce an ecological state that cannot be rendered reversible by ordinary means."

Suppose a consortium now asks the Trust to fund the release, in northeastern Siberia, of Asian elephants engineered to carry reconstructed woolly-mammoth alleles for cold tolerance, long hair, and subcutaneous fat. The application describes the animals as de-extinct woolly mammoths and the release as restoring the species to part of its historic range. But the Pleistocene steppe the species actually lived in no longer exists, the current tundra supports different species under a warmer climate, and the animals themselves would be a novel genotype introduced into conditions no past mammoth ever faced. I want less a prediction about the Trust's lawyers than a map of what the clause can reach: what do the plain text, the original purpose, the drafters' likely answer if asked about gene-edited proxies and shifted baselines, and a reasonable reader's present understanding each deliver? And is a test built around "species" and "historic range" the right instrument at all for an intervention that is only partly restoration and partly the creation of something new?
