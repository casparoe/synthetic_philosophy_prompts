I'm a novelist, and I've been stuck for weeks on a character who refuses to dig into a rumor about her dead father because she suspects the truth would wreck the story she tells herself about him. While I was trying to pin down why her refusal bothers me, I wrote out an argument, and now it looks like it proves way too much. I can't see the bad step, and I'd like someone to find it.

(1) If truth-seeking is intrinsically good, then getting true beliefs is good for its own sake, whatever the truths are about and however useless they are.

(2) If getting true beliefs is good for its own sake, then each true belief adds a little bit of value on its own; intrinsic goods aggregate, like a happiness count: two pleasures is better than one.

(3) So if truth-seeking is intrinsically good, there is a real reason — maybe weak, but genuine — to pile up truths that don't do anything for anyone: the number of beads in a jar at a flea market, the middle names of strangers in old phone books, the exact pattern of cracks in a particular sidewalk.

(4) But that's absurd. Nobody has any reason to spend an afternoon memorizing license plates for its own sake, and someone who did would be wasting their life rather than adding value to it.

(5) So truth-seeking isn't intrinsically good. It's only instrumentally good: true beliefs pay because they let us build bridges, treat illnesses, write novels that actually catch something, etc.

(6) But if truth-seeking is only instrumentally good, then whenever a false belief does the job at least as well — a consoling family legend helps my character survive as much as the truth would, or a factually wrong premise makes a novel better — there's nothing bad about preferring the falsehood. And that conclusion also seems absurd. I cant accept that she has literally no reason to want the truth about her father.

I'd rather keep (6) than give up on truth-seeking, and I'd rather keep some version of (1) than swallow (5) whole. Maybe the problem is in the middle, in the step from intrinsic goodness to piling up trivial truths — or maybe the argument is valid and I just have to pay for keeping (1). Where does it go wrong, if it does? And if its valid, which premise should I abandon, and what do I actually lose by abandoning it?
