I'm giving a talk in a few weeks to a small workshop—about fifteen people, mostly moral philosophers, but not a Leibniz crowd. Half are Kantian or post-Kantian ethicists who are suspicious of any view that gives sentiment a foundational role; the other half work in empirically informed moral psychology and keep asking what a normative theory is supposed to do with the fact that people are biased, lazy, and bad at ranking abstract goods. My thesis is that Leibniz's account of justice as charity of the wise is a neglected third option: it avoids the pure rationalism of Kantian duty and the flattening benevolence of early utilitarianism, because it treats moral motivation as love, but love corrected and structured by knowledge of degrees of perfection. The centerpiece of my paper is a passage in the Meditation on the Common Concept of Justice where Leibniz argues, roughly, that justice is not the same as indiscriminate benevolence and that right action requires love ordered by wisdom. I take him to be saying that the proper object of moral love is not whoever happens to be nearest or neediest, but minds in proportion to their perfection, which is why justice has real normative shape and is not just a warm feeling.

I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a summary of the paper. Give me the objections this specific audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they are likely to come, phrased the way a Kantian or a moral psychologist would phrase them. I am especially anxious about the Kantian charge that any account of moral motivation built on love is heteronomous, and the psychologist charge that graded love licenses partiality. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it would hurt. And be honest about the biggest structural weakness in my position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
