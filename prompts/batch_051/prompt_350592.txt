I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how researchers pick the numbers that go into their models when the data don't fully pin them down—priors, in the Bayesian sense. While reporting I read a recent popular book on probability and rationality that has a chapter on something called objective Bayesianism and the "maximum entropy principle." The book's summary of the view runs roughly as follows: for a long time, Bayesian reasoning seemed to have an embarrassing hole, because the prior is just whatever you happen to believe, so two reasonable people can start with different priors and wind up with different conclusions from the same data. But objective Bayesians, the book says, solved this problem. They showed that for any body of evidence there is exactly one rationally defensible prior: the distribution that maximizes entropy subject to whatever constraints the evidence imposes—intuitively, the flattest, least opinionated probability assignment consistent with what you know. Picking a prior is therefore no longer a value judgment; it's a technical matter of listing the constraints, and anyone who shares those constraints is rationally obliged to use the same prior. The book concludes that the old worry about Bayesian subjectivity is mostly settled and the remaining fights are just about computational details.

I'm enough of a generalist to suspect this is to clean—history rarely ends in a theorem—but I don't have the technical chops to say exactly where the summary is fair and where it's spinning. For my piece I'd like an accuracy audit: what would a philosopher or statistician who actually defends objective Bayesianism accept in that paragraph, where would they wince, and what is just wrong? And does the book's closing conclusion, that the subjectivity worry is settled, actually follow from the parts that are accurate? Answer in American English.
