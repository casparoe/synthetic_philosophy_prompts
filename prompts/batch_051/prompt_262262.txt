I used to think a recording could settle a dispute by showing the event directly. Now that plausible-looking video can be fabricated, I have been trying to work out what I actually think about when a recording should be believed, and I find that my trust no longer tracks the quality of the image; it tracks something else I cannot name.

Here are my verdicts. A grainy, unsteady phone clip of an off-duty officer shoving a teenager at a bus stop, posted by the teenager's mother and not yet reported anywhere: I believe it shows what happened. The same clip reposted by an anonymous account, claiming the incident happened in my city last night when no news outlet, police report or witness has said a word: I am far less inclined to believe it. A slick, high-definition video of a government minister making a bigoted joke at a private dinner, released by an opposition-aligned group three days before an election: I catch myself discounting it, though I can find no digital artefact. A video call from my sister's own number, showing her face and voice, saying she has been arrested abroad and needs money wired at once: I trust it and act. The same call from an unknown number, same story, same quality: I would not trust it at all. A tape played in court, with a chain-of-custody log and a forensic examiner testifying that it is unaltered: I accept it as reliable evidence, even though I know a good fake could still slip through. A secret recording of an executive ordering the destruction of safety-test results, leaked by an employee whose word is the only provenance: I find it credible enough to investigate, but I would not act on it without the underlying documents.

The principle I keep reaching for is this: treat a recording as testimony from whoever presents it to me, not as a direct look at the event, so credibility attaches to the presenter. That fits the reposted clip and the two calls, but it strains against the mother's clip, where I may simply be trusting her as a witness, and against the court tape, where I seem to trust a system rather than any person. Is there a single cleaner principle that gets all or nearly all of these verdicts, or should I think of the cases as governed by two or three interacting principles? Please state the candidate principle or principles precisely, say which of my verdicts each cannot accommodate, and apply them to the case I keep going back and forth on: a live video call from my elderly neighbour, who looks and sounds right, asking me to transfer money to a taxi driver because her wallet has been stolen. If you think the question is badly posed—that I am asking a rule about evidence to do the work of a relationship, or that some of my verdicts are driven by unstated background beliefs I have not examined—tell me that plainly rather than forcing the cases into a tidy theory, and where the principle you propose is least secure, say so instead of presenting it with false confidence.
