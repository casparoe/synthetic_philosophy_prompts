I am taking a media law seminar, and we have been working through the fact/opinion distinction in defamation after Milkovich v. Lorain Journal, 497 U.S. 1 (1990). The framework the professor keeps returning to is this sentence from the syllabus: "Foremost, where a media defendant is involved, a statement on matters of public concern must be provable as false before liability can be assessed, Philadelphia Newspapers, Inc. v. Hepps, 475 U.S. 767, 106 S.Ct. 1558, 89 L.Ed.2d 783, thus ensuring full constitutional protection for a statement of opinion having no provably false factual connotation." On this reading, the decisive question is not whether a statement is labeled opinion but whether it carries a provably false factual connotation.

For my paper I want to test that criterion against ratings and forecasts, which feel like a hard borderline case. Imagine a municipal credit-rating agency downgrades a public utility district from Aa3 to Ba1 and publishes this sentence: "Based on projected revenue shortfalls, the district's probability of default over the next five years has risen to roughly 20 percent, materially above the market-implied probability." The district sues for defamation, arguing that the revenue-shortfall projection rests on false factual premises about its pension obligations and that the 20 percent probability is itself an assertion of fact that can be checked against the district's audited finances. The agency replies that a forward-looking probability is an evaluative opinion, not a claim about any particular past or present fact. How should the Milkovich test apply here? Does the rating or the forecast contain a provably false factual connotation, or is it protected? And if a probability cannot be verified like a historical fact but can nonetheless be shown to be derived from false inputs, does that make it actionable under the First Amendment?
