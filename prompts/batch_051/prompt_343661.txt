I convene a workshop on the political economy of AI risk for sixteen people, half from computer science and half from policy or philosophy, over two hours. Most arrive skeptical that the profit motive or capitalism has anything to do with AI risk; they expect the risk to live in bad code or bad individuals. I want a structured game, not a lecture, that makes them feel the structural-force claim: that actors can be individually reasonable, even reluctant, and still be driven to cut corners on safety by competition and the profit motive. What they should come away seeing is not a slogan but a mechanism. I also want the game to be diagnostic rather than merely illustrative: it should include at least one condition, round, or treatment that lets participants compare competitive pressure against a baseline where the same technical uncertainty and the same short-term temptations are present but the competitive, profit-driven structure is absent. In other words, the game should let us ask whether the corner-cutting behavior comes from the market structure, or whether we would get it anyway.

Ground rules for the design: sixteen players, roughly two hours including setup and debrief. Payoffs must be cheap and must not involve real money; points, tokens, rankings, or play currency are fine. Participants should not be told in advance what thesis they are supposed to discover. The race should emerge from the rules and incentives themselves, and only in the debrief should the facilitator name what happened and connect it to the claim that capitalism or the profit motive is the root cause of AI risk.

The deliverable: full rules; a materials list; a timeline broken into setup, play, and debrief; a payoff scheme with enough specificity that I could implement it tomorrow; and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the play to three claims: first, that sacrificing safety became individually worth it even when everyone ended up worse off; second, that this was a feature of the competitive structure rather than a defect of character; and third, that the comparison with the non-competitive baseline either supports or undercuts the claim that capitalism as such is the root cause. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as players colluding, the baseline condition feeling rigged, or participants moralizing instead of maximizing, and how to handle each failure. Finally, be honest about what the game cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must therefore supply: the actual probability of catastrophic misalignment, the real sums of money, and the ordinary exit option of regulation are not in the room. Say which of these gaps matter for the inference the game is meant to support, and whether the game can do more than illustrate the thesis.
