I teach a course with a unit on counterfactual theories of causation, and a snag about omissions has started to bug me in a way I can't name. Take a nurse who fails to give a patient a needed drug, and the patient dies. The standard counterfactual test says: if the nurse hadn't omitted the administration, the patient wouldn't have died. But "hadn't omitted" doesn't pick out a single possible action. Should we imagine she gives the drug correctly and on time? Or gives the wrong dose? Or gives it too late for it to work? There are many ways for her not to omit, and the truth of the counterfactual flips depending on which alternative we hold fixed: give it correctly and the patient lives; give the wrong dose and the patient dies all the same. So whether the omission comes out as a cause turns on a choice of alternative that the facts alone don't determine. I know this is adjacent to familiar points—causal statements are interest-relative, omissions need some background norm or expectation before we cite them at all—but those are about which absences get picked out. This is a further snag: even once a particular omission is salient, we still haven't fixed which positive act we're comparing it to. Is there an established name for this underdetermination of the alternative in counterfactuals about omissions, and how does it related to the contrastivity literature? If it has no name, propose one and give its precise boundary conditions. A bare name won't do—I want the carving: what the problem is, what it isn't, and where it edges into the wider issue of context-sensitive counterfactuals. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
