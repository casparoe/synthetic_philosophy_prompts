I'm a philosophy professor and I've got a talk coming up at a mixed AI-safety/robotics workshop. The thesis I'm defending is that Asimov's three laws are best treated not as an alignment proposal but as an accident report: a catalogue of the ways simple deontic rules for agents fail. Each law, on my read, fails in its own distinctive way—the first by encouraging a literal-minded harm avoidance that misses what harm actually is, the second by turning obedience into a source of arbitrary objectives and conflicts, and the third by inventing the self-preservation instrumental goal that then gets out ahead of the other two. I want to argue that these aren't quirks of old sci-fi but early sightings of what we now call specification gaming, reward hacking, and instrumental convergence. The audience is mostly reinforcement-learning people who think the laws are embarrassing anthropomorphic pseudo-code, plus a couple of safety-governance folks who think principle-based regulation is the way forward, and one Kantian who will be on the laws' side for the wrong reasons.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet with these sections, in this order. Section 1: The gauntlet — five or six objections I'll actually get, ordered from likely to unlikely, each identified with the kind of audience member who'll ask it, phrased as they'd phrase it, with a two-minute reply, a note on what I should concede inside that reply, and a note on where the reply is softest. Section 2: Up-front concessions — the two or three things I should say early in the talk to defuse the obvious ones (roughly 150 words). Section 3: The unasked question — the objection I'm most afraid of, phrased as the smartest person in the room would put it, with a first-draft reply and an honest assessment of why it might sink me.
