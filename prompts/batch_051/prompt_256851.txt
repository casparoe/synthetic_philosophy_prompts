I'm a PhD student working on the philosophy of intellectual property, specifically what should happen to creative work and software when the rightsholder is gone and there's nobody left to ask. My advisor runs a small digital-preservation lab, and over the last year she's made a bunch of calls on exactly these cases. I've been writing them down because I want to reverse-engineer the principle she's actually running, not the one she says she's running. I know the law here doesn't have a general orphan-works exception, so her calls aren't just statutory readings. Her track record:

- A 1920s novel, long out of copyright, no heirs: a small press reprints it. Allowed.
- A 1994 novel whose author died in 2009 and whose heirs can't be located after a diligent search: a small press wants to reprint and sell it. Not allowed — still in copyright, and orphan status isn't a license.
- A 1990s multiplayer game whose studio folded in 2001. Before it folded, the studio posted that fans could run there own servers. A fan group has kept it playable for free since then. Allowed.
- Same game: someone starts selling boxed copies of the patched game online. Not allowed.
- A street photographer died in 2011 with no heirs. A university library scans her negatives for preservation and lets researchers view them in the reading room, but doesn't post the scans online. Allowed.
- An unpublished diary of a poet who died in 1985, no heirs: a biographer wants to quote thirty pages in a commercial biography. Not allowed.
- A defunct company's proprietary database format from 2003, company dissolved and the IP never sold: a researcher reverse-engineers the format so she can open her own old files, but doesn't distribute the tool. Allowed.

The new case: a solo developer released a paid utility app in 2009, then vanished around 2013 — company dissolved, emails bounce, no estate, no successor owns the rights. The app is still under copyright, all rights reserved, and won't run on modern systems without a patch. A nonprofit software archive wants to do two things: (a) patch it and let visitors use it on a machine in the archive's reading room, and (b) let visitors download the patched copy for free. No one is selling anything, and the developer can't be found to ask.

What would she say about (b), and which of her earlier calls is the closest analog? More importantly, what are the candidate rules that fit her whole track record, and which rule does (b) force her to choose between? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would she be adopting with each answer? And be explicit about what would change your mind: which of her verdicts, if I'd misrecorded it or she revised it, would do the most to flip your prediction, and where is your reading of her rule most likely to break down?
