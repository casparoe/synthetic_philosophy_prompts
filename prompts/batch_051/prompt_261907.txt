I convene a discussion group at a community center, and for the past year we have been stuck on one question: when a community performs a scapegoat rite—loading its wrongdoing onto a goat, a lamb, an effigy, or a stand-in person, then sending it away or destroying it—does anything actually change hands, or does the guilt stay exactly where it was while only the community's feelings and its willingness to notice change? The group has now split into two camps, and neither has moved the other in months.

One camp—the real-transfer side—holds that the transfer is genuine in the cases that matter: the wrong is not merely hidden or re-described but reassigned, so that afterward the community's situation is different in something more than mood. The other camp—the expressive side—holds that guilt cannot in fact be handed to a bystander or an animal; the rite changes what the community sees, says, and permits itself to feel, while the guilt itself remains with the wrongdoer, and any sense that the matter is settled is a useful collective story.

I want to turn the standoff into an adversarial collaboration. Draft the document these two positions should be able to issue together: a text each side can sign because it states that side's view in wording the side accepts, without forcing either side to accept what it considers false. Do not write a summary of the two positions, a debate transcript, or a verdict. Write the actual working document the two camps would put their names to.

The document should have this structure. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign: observations about what scapegoat and substitution rites plainly do, plainly fail to do, and about the conditions under which each side concedes that the other's account is at least as strong as its own. Avoid vacuous claims, and avoid wording that either side would experience as already a concession. Second, a disagreements section in table form: for each live point of difference, one column giving the neutral phrasing both sides accept as the true statement of the disagreement, one column giving the real-transfer side's own wording, and one column giving the expressive side's own wording. Third, the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux, provide a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree genuinely bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case. I suspect two cruxes may be whether guilt behaves like a debt that can be paid by another party or like a historical fact about who did the act, and whether a collective performance can itself change what is owed rather than only announce or express such a change. Do not assume these are the cruxes; test them and replace them if the drafting requires it. Fourth, a short closing note stating which disagreements are substantive and which are partly or wholly verbal, and why.

One concrete case the group has returned to repeatedly, and which I ask you to use at least once among the crux tests if both sides would accept it as bearing on a crux, is this. A town performs an annual rite in which residents write down an unaddressed wrong they committed during the year—a harm they never apologized for or repaired—on an unmarked slip, and the slips are burned together with no one reading them and no one named. Both camps accept that the town's mood changes in the room. They cannot agree on whether, after the burning, the writers owe anything different to the people they harmed, or whether the town may treat the slate as clean. I also have a suspicion that some of the standoff is verbal, because the sides do not always seem to mean the same thing by guilt: a private feeling, a standing in the eyes of others, a stain that outlasts the act, a debt. The closing section should test that suspicion rather than assume it.
