I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and I've been mulling over what it actually means to be reliable or to keep a small promise. I keep finding cases where my intuitions pull in different directions, and I can't settle on a definition that handles all of them. Here are the ones that keep tripping me up: (1) You tell a friend you'll meet at 3:00 and show up at 3:08, and they show up at 3:07 — is that a broken promise at all, or just noise? (2) You RSVP yes to a small gathering while strongly suspecting you won't go, and indeed you don't. (3) You're late because the train broke down versus because you were scrolling on your phone and lost track of time. (4) Someone is chronically 5–10 minutes late to everything, and their friends have learned to tell them an earlier time on purpose — is that person less reliable in a morally interesting sense, or just calibrated differently? (5) You text "on my way" when you haven't left yet, to keep the other person from getting anxious. (6) You tell a colleague "I'll send the comments by Friday" and send them Friday at 11:58pm. (7) You agree to call someone back "later" and never do, but no time was ever specified.

I'd like a working definition of reliability — or of keeping a small promise, whichever does the real work — that gets these cases right. My hunch is that the concept might do two jobs at once: one about respecting another person's schedule and expectations, and one about being an accurate predictor of your own future behavior. But those don't always track together, and I'm not sure whether punctuality is best thought of as a moral virtue, a social convention, or a cheap-but-real piece of interpersonal glue. Run the definition through all seven cases and tell me where it surprises you or where it says my intuitions are wrong. I'm also curious whether the definition should count a person who is always 8 minutes late as reliable in one sense and unreliable in another, or whether it forces us to pick one.
