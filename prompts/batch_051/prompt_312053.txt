I am the person on my team responsible for specifying what our reasoning system knows, for a safety case that will be reviewed externally, and I have hit a wall I cannot find my way around. A colleague keeps insisting that the cleanest formal model of knowledge is the standard possible-worlds one, and he pressed the following argument on me. I need your help locating exactly where it goes wrong; if it does not go wrong, our system's "known" predicate is going to be useless.

Here is the argument as I understand it, with the steps numbered:

(1) On the standard possible-worlds semantics, an agent knows a proposition P just in case P is true at every possible world compatible with the agent's information — that is, every world the agent cannot rule out.
(2) Logical truths are true at every possible world, full stop; and on the usual way of setting up these models, mathematical truths are likewise true at every possible world.
(3) Therefore, any logical or mathematical truth is true at every world compatible with any agent's information, no matter what that information is.
(4) By (1), any agent whatsoever knows every logical truth and every mathematical truth.
(5) But that is too much: the agent would know arbitrary tautologies and theorems it has never considered, would know the truth-value of conjectures no one has proved or disproved, and would have known Fermat's Last Theorem before any proof existed.
(6) So either the standard possible-worlds semantics is wrong about knowledge, or we must accept that agents know vastly more than the ordinary practice of knowledge attribution allows.

My colleague calls this the problem of logical omniscience, but naming it has not shown me where the argument breaks.

I would strongly prefer to keep the possible-worlds framework if I can: it composes cleanly, and we already know how to implement reasoning over it. But I cannot see how to avoid the conclusion without either quietly changing the framework or admitting that the model's "knows" means something different from what our safety case needs it to mean.

Please act as an expert in epistemic logic and formal epistemology, and think carefully before committing. Begin by brainstorming the places where this argument could be blocked. Then tell me which step I should reject and what rejecting it costs. If what goes wrong is better located in the relation between steps, or between the formal model and the real phenomenon, say exactly where and what the shift is. I want a diagnosis, not a consolation: I need to know which premise or step is the least costly one to give up, and what giving it up does to the rest of the framework.
