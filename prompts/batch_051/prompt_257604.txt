I want a working definition of bad faith (mauvaise foi) in the Sartrean sense: not merely self-deception as commonly understood, but the distinctive way of lying to oneself through which radical freedom is evaded. I want a definition you would defend on its own merits and which is precise enough to decide hard cases, tested below rather than merely restated.

A piece of scaffolding, which you should not treat as the definition unless it earns its place. The useful background is the duality of human existence: each person is at once facticity — the givens one does not choose, such as a past, a body, a temperament, a social position, an identity assigned by others — and transcendence, the freedom to make something of those givens, to reinterpret their meaning, and to choose what to do next. On my rough understanding, bad faith exploits this duality by letting a person count as only one pole: as a finished thing whose possibilities are already settled, or as a pure freedom with no facticity to answer to. The move is made to avoid owning a choice. I am not confident this rough understanding survives the cases, and I want your definition to be tested against them, not assumed from the start.

The cases:

1. Sartre's café waiter, who performs his role with exaggerated mechanical precision, as if being a waiter were his essence, and who keeps any suggestion that he could be otherwise at a distance.
2. A person who says "I'm just conflict-avoidant; that's who I am," and arranges her life so that the claim is never disconfirmed, treating the label as a settled fact about herself rather than a pattern she sustains.
3. A student who follows the career her parents selected and later tells herself "there was never really a choice," although no one was in a position to force her.
4. A person in a controlling relationship who says "there is nothing I can do; this is simply my life," and turns away from every mention of an exit. I lean bad faith but am uneasy, because the constraints are real.
5. Someone with a severe substance dependence who says "I can't stop; it's stronger than I am." One reading hears the flight from freedom; another hears a true description of compulsion, an acknowledgment rather than a lie to self.
6. An actor fully absorbed in a role onstage, so that the character's fear and desire are experienced as her own for the duration of the performance. Is she in bad faith the way the waiter is? I do not know.
7. A politician who repeats a slogan he knows to be false until, years later, he sincerely believes it. Is this bad faith, ordinary self-deception, or something else? I do not know.
8. A debtor who tells a creditor a false story about a lost check while knowing perfectly well that he has the money and intends not to pay. This seems clearly not bad faith, because there is no lie to himself; I want the definition to say exactly why.

My starting verdicts: (1), (2), and (3) are clearly bad faith; (8) is clearly not; (4) I lean bad faith with unease; (5), (6), and (7) are genuinely open. Propose the definition, run it through all eight cases, and say where it produces a verdict I did not expect or no verdict at all; then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Finally, decide one further case: a person whose explicit project is total self-honesty, understood as the effort to coincide exactly with what she is. Does your definition classify that as bad faith, and if it does, is that a defect in the definition or a discovery about sincerity?
