I have been reading Adam Smith’s *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* on my own, without any background in philosophy, and one passage has stayed with me. In Part III, chapter I, he writes:

"We can never survey our own sentiments and motives, we can never form any judgment concerning them; unless we remove ourselves, as it were, from our own natural station, and endeavour to view them as at a certain distance from us."

I have come to accept the view I take this passage to express: that conscience, or the way we judge our own actions, is not a direct inward look at our motives but an attempt to see ourselves from outside, through the eyes of an imagined impartial spectator. In practice, the spectator is built up from the real and possible reactions of other people; we adopt their perspective by sympathy and then use it to approve or condemn ourselves. I also accept what seems to me a troubling corollary: because the spectator is something we can shape and flatter, self-deceit is a standing temptation, and the only reliable check on it is the more fixed rules we have generalised from repeated social experience.

What I want is an honest account of what would count against this position. I am less interested in whether the position is right than in knowing what it risks. Which observations, arguments, or psychological findings should genuinely move me away from it? Which objections would a defender of this view themselves concede as counting against it, and which would they dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding? I would like the evidence and arguments ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a clear statement of what would have to be true for each to succeed. And I would like to know whether the view is in danger of being so flexible — for example, because one can always say that a person’s conscience is a distorted or partial spectator — that it cannot be tested at all, and if so whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. What would I have to believe about, say, sociopathy, small children, or moral disagreement for the spectator picture to be in trouble?
