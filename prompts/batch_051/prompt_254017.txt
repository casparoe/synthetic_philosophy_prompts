I'm TAing an undergrad public heath ethics section this fall—14 students, mostly pre-med, 75-minute slot—and next week is the unit on rationing, QALYs, and the value of a statistical life. In past semesters the conversation stalls out at "you can't put a price on a life" and then the hour is gone before anyone actually moves. I want to front-load the discussion with a structured activity that makes them make the call before they can retreat to that slogan.

Here's what I'm picturing: a forced-allocation exercise where the group has to distribute some scarce resource (ventilators, ICU beds, a fixed drug budget) across patients or programs whose profiles differ by age, baseline health, chance of survival, and expected years left. Round one: everyone privately ranks or allocates, no criteria given. Round two: they have to agree on a single public rule and defend it while the rest of the room tries to find cases where the rule does something none of them wants to own. Round three: I show them what their rule implies in QALY terms or dollars-per-life-year, so they can see that whatever they chose already put a finite price on a life—it was just hidden. The collisions I'm hoping to engineer are (a) saving the most lives versus saving the most life-years, and (b) the rule of rescue pulling against the rule they say they'd want applied to strangers.

Write me the actual running materials: the patient or program cards with numbers (simple enough for undergrads), the exact sequence with rough timings, rules for each round, what I as facilitator should and shouldn't say, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto QALYs, VSL, and aggregation. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong—someone refusing to choose, the group inventing a tiebreaker that dodges the tradeoff, the room insisting the numbers are rigged—and how to handle each one. And be honest about which feature of real triage the activity can't reproduce, and what I'll have to just tell them afterwards.
