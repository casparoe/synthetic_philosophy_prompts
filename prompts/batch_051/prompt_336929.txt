I'm deciding whether to take a job at a company whose whole pitch is making supermarket packaging a bit cheaper, and I can't settle whether making everyone 2% richer is a real contribution or just a shrug. Here's the asymmetry that's been stuck in my head. When a firm makes some good cheaper, we say it created value, as if the extra aggregate abundance were itself good, even when no individual consumer would notice. But when you ask whether any particular person is better off, everyone immediately translates the higher income into what it buys — more time, better health, less stress — and talks as if the money itself were worth nothing. So abundance seems to matter in the aggregate but not at the level of one life. I want the strongest principle that can hold both of those together (options, security, innovation capacity, future people), tested against an individual who becomes vastly richer and never spends any of it and a country whose measured output goes up because of things nobody enjoys. Then give me your honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or drop one side.
