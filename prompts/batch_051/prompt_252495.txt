I am preparing to lead a seminar on Boethius's treatment of divine foreknowledge, and in the course of that reading I built the following argument. It now strikes me as proving too much, and I need a precise diagnosis of where it goes wrong and what the repair costs.

(1) On the traditional doctrine, God is omniscient and therefore foreknows every future act.
(2) Suppose that tomorrow at noon I will either sign the contract or not. If God foreknows that I will sign it, then it is already the case, before noon tomorrow, that I will sign it.
(3) What is already the case before my choice is fixed: my choosing tomorrow cannot make the present truth about that future event other than it is.
(4) Therefore my signing is fixed ahead of time, and so my signing cannot be a free act.
(5) The same reasoning applies to every future act that God foreknows; so every future act is fixed and unfree.
(6) This includes God's own future acts if God foreknows them, and every action of every creature.
(7) But the tradition that affirms divine foreknowledge also needs there to be free acts, because without them moral responsibility, sin, and theodicy lose their footing.
(8) So either God does not foreknow future free acts, or there are no future free acts. On the first horn, God is not omniscient in the traditional sense; on the second, there is no human freedom. Either way the traditional package is false.

Each step seems plausible to me on its own, but the conclusion is unacceptable, so something must give. I have read Boethius's two-part move: he denies that God strictly foreknows, because God is eternal and sees all times at once rather than ahead of them; and he distinguishes the necessity of the consequence from the necessity of the thing itself, so that a known future event is certain without being under an internal necessity. I can state those distinctions, but I cannot make them bite against step (4). If God timelessly sees my whole life in one intuition, my signing still seems to be there in the divine sight, and therefore unavoidable.

What I want from you is a numbered diagnosis. Which step or steps should I reject, and what does each rejection cost? In particular, tell me whether the real fault is in the inference from being known and true in advance to being fixed in advance, or in the assumption in step (1) that God foreknows in time at all. If I keep Boethius's eternity doctrine, do I thereby give up the claim that God foreknows future acts in any ordinary sense, and is that a cost or a clarification? And which of Boethius's two moves is doing the real work?

After the diagnosis, give me a brief structured summary as a table with one row per numbered step and these columns: Step; What the step claims; Keep, reject, or reinterpret; Why it looked plausible; Cost of rejecting it.
