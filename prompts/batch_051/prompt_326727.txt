Benacerraf's dilemma is usually set up as three commitments that cannot all be true. A mathematical platonist holds that statements such as 2+3=5 are literally about abstract objects, which are non-spatiotemporal and causally inert. If knowledge of a statement requires an appropriate causal link to the objects the statement is about, then no one can know anything about the numbers. But mathematical knowledge seems real; so one of these commitments must go.

Now add a development unavailable to Benacerraf: a language model trained only by next-token prediction on ordinary language texts plus a large corpus of human mathematical writing, with no tools, no environment, and no way for the numbers themselves to affect it. Its only window onto mathematics is finite strings produced by creatures who, on the platonist picture, also had no causal contact with abstract objects. Suppose the model reliably produces proofs and solutions that working mathematicians judge correct and sometimes genuinely new, including problems unlike anything in its training data.

Does Benacerraf's dilemma survive this development? On one reading, the model's reliability shows that correctness about mathematics can be achieved without causal contact with mathematical objects, which breaks the epistemic premise and leaves the platonist with a milder problem — for instance, about how expression-tokens get their content. On another reading, the model lacks exactly what the causal requirement was protecting: understanding, justification, or genuine reference — so the original dilemma is untouched. Which is right, or is the truth a repair somewhere in between? If the dilemma survives, say precisely what the model lacks and why the causal requirement still does its job. If it does not, say which premise should be replaced and whether the resulting problem is any easier for the platonist. Answer in no more than 700 words.
