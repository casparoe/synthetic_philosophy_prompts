It is by now common ground among researchers working on AI alignment that a system should be responsive to human values, but there is a long-running and apparently immovable dispute about which human values, and whose. One camp holds that the proper target is an aggregate: collect preferences or interests across the affected population and have the system promote or optimise that aggregate, treating the problem as one of social choice under conditions of deep value pluralism. A second camp, influenced by liberal neutrality, holds that this is illegitimate for an assistant-like system: because there is no neutral way to combine competing conceptions of the good, the system should not take sides at the population level, and should instead help each person pursue their own values within settled constraints, deferring contested trade-offs to users or to legitimate institutions. Both sides agree on the standard formal results in social choice; both agree that real people disagree deeply and reasonably about the good; both agree that the system will often face trade-offs where not everyone can be satisfied. And yet neither side has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily an unsolved technical problem about aggregation, but a disagreement about what kind of thing an aligned AI is: a private tool serving one principal, or a public institution serving everyone, with the accompanying standards of legitimacy that each role imports. Is that right? Diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it a substantive moral dispute, a dispute about institutional role disguised as a dispute about aggregation, a hidden empirical claim about whether neutrality is feasible in practice, or something else? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions, and an honest account of what would count as progress: a new formal result, an empirical finding about how systems are actually deployed, a distinction, or a change of question.
