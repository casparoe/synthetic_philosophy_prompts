I coordinate a working group in science education research, and for the past year two of its members have been arguing about the same corpus of middle-school reasoning data. The data include students who claim that a ball keeps moving only while a force is being applied to it, that heavier objects fall faster, that friction is not a force, and who sometimes answer a problem correctly while failing a close variant of it. The two positions are these. One member holds that the errors are best explained as the expression of stable, internally coherent alternative theories: the student who says a rolling ball will stop when the push stops is not merely producing a phrase, but holds something like a unified principle — motion requires a continuously applied cause — that generates predictions across projectiles, constant velocity, and friction, and that is revised only when the whole structure is challenged. The other member holds that the same errors are best explained by the activation of many small, context-sensitive knowledge resources built up from everyday experience: a sense that effort produces motion, that more of a cause means more of an effect, that a cause must be present for an effect to continue, and so on. On this view there is no single theory to refute; the apparent consistency of an error is a product of the same perceptual-motor resources being reliably triggered in similar classroom situations, while the same student answers correctly elsewhere because different resources get assembled. Call the first view the coherent-theory view and the second the pieces view.

The two members have agreed to write an adversarial-collaboration document together rather than continue arguing past each other, and I would like you to draft it. Produce a document they could both sign, but one that is not vacuous. It should contain: a numbered list of claims both camps accept about the data, including claims about robustness, context-sensitivity, and what would count as explaining an error; a disagreements table in which each remaining dispute is stated so that each party would accept the wording of its own side, with the neutral version of the question made explicit wherever the dispute is substantive rather than verbal; an explicit statement of any part of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal; and a set of cruxes, meaning sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side, each paired with a concrete study or classroom observation both parties agree would bear on it, plus each party's prediction stated in advance. I suspect one crux is whether a principle elicited from a student in one context is actually reused in superficially different contexts or only in near-identical ones; another is how to classify students who answer inconsistently across contexts. Do not feel bound to those two. The document should separate what is agreed from what is genuinely open, and each camp should recognize its own view in the way the disagreement is worded. Do not include citations or a reading list; the document should stand on the reasoning and the data described above.
