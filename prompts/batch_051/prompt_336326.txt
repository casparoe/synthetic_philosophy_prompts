I am a novelist, and while drafting an essay about why my characters sometimes feel more like genuine authors of their lives than I do, I built an argument that now strikes me as proving too much. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher working in the philosophy of mind, personal identity, and the metaphysics of fiction. Think through the argument carefully and step by step; before settling on a diagnosis, canvas the candidate weak points.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) A self is a center of narrative gravity: what makes a life the life of one self is the story — partly told in the first person, partly told by others — that unifies its memories, actions, traits, and projects into a single intelligible arc. This is close to the picture Daniel Dennett has defended, and it is also the picture forced on me by my own craft: my characters are exactly such centers, with no hidden substance behind the accumulating text.

(2) A story is a made thing. Whatever unity it possesses is imposed by a teller who selects, orders, and retrospects, using the genres and conventions available to that teller. A story's unity is not discovered by the telling; it is an artifact of the telling.

(3) If a self really is nothing over and above its stories, then the existence, boundaries, and persistence of that self are fixed by acts of telling. There is no independent biographical fact — no pre-narrative unity — to which the telling is answerable.

(4) Therefore no self exists except relative to some telling of its story.

(5) Every story requires a teller, or at least a telling. But the telling that constitutes a self cannot be done by the very self it constitutes, on pain of a circle: the self would have to be there before itself in order to tell itself into being, much as a character cannot cause the novel in which she appears. So the telling must be done by something other than the self — a brain, a culture, a language, a repertoire of inherited genres, or all of these.

(6) If the constitutive story of a self is told by something other than the self, then the self is a character authored elsewhere.

(7) A character authored elsewhere is not the author of its own life. What look like its decisions, commitments, and self-descriptions are themselves events inside a story whose authorship lies outside it.

(8) But that cannot be right. Responsibility, deliberation, and first-person authority all presuppose that a person is, in some substantial sense, the author of her own life — and so does my own practice whenever I write as an I.

(9) Therefore the narrative view of the self, and Dennett's center-of-gravity picture with it, must be false somewhere in steps (1) through (8).

I would most like to keep (8), and I would like to keep something like (1) if I can, since it explains what I see myself doing at my desk every day. But I cannot tell which step to surrender, nor what surrendering it would do to the rest of the view. Tell me: which premise or step should be rejected, why did it look plausible, and what exactly does rejecting it cost someone who remains drawn to the picture in (1)?
