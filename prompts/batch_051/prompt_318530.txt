I keep coming back to a weird asymmetry in how medicine treats people. Walk into a clinic with high cholesterol and no symptoms: you get a diagnosis, a prescription, and your insurer will cover it for the rest of your life, even though the harm is all hypothetical and in the future. Walk in with months of crushing fatigue and widespread pain, every test normal: you're likely to be told your fine or that it's stress, maybe offered an antidepressant for the distress, and sent home, even though the suffering is happening right now. The first person is treated as having a real disease; the second sort of isn't. What theory of disease could make that division of labor rational? Does the practice presuppose naturalism (disease is objective biological dysfunction, something "inside the body"), normativism (disease is a matter of values, harm, and what society decides medicine should handle), or some awkward mix? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what just happens to fit it, and say what would have to change about how the system classifies and pays for care if it actually ran on a consistently normativist view.
