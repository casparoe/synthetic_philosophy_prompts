Here is an argument I constructed after reading Merleau-Ponty’s *Phenomenology of Perception*, and I cannot find the exit. He describes the organist who sits down at an unfamiliar instrument and, after an hour of practice, plays as if the instrument were an extension of his body; the action is not driven by an explicit representation of the positions of stops and pedals but by a bodily inhabitation. The blind man’s stick, likewise, ceases to be an object for him and its tip becomes a sensitive zone. So far so good. But:

(1) A skilled action such as the organist’s is not guided by an explicit representation of the goal or of the body’s position.
(2) A reflex such as the knee-jerk response is also not guided by an explicit representation of the goal or of the body’s position.
(3) If two behaviors are both not guided by an explicit representation of the goal or of the body’s position, then they belong to the same explanatory kind.
(4) Therefore skilled action belongs to the same explanatory kind as a reflex.
(5) A reflex is a fixed, mechanically triggered response, whereas skilled action is flexible, adjustable to novel circumstances, and improved by practice.
(6) If skilled action belongs to the same explanatory kind as a reflex, then it cannot be flexible, adjustable, and improved by practice in the way that it is.
(7) Therefore either skilled action is not flexible, adjustable, and improved by practice, or premise (1) is false.

I am unwilling to accept (7), and I would rather keep (1) and (5) than any of the others. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if the problem is that “not guided by an explicit representation” covers two different things in (1) and (2), what positive account of reflex action does Merleau-Ponty’s framework require, and how does it avoid reintroducing the very representational picture he was trying to escape?
