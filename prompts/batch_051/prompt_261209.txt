I keep going back and forth on whether physicists could ever know they'd hit the end of physics, so I wrote out the arguement that's been bugging me:

(1) To be justified in believing some theory T is the final theory, we'd have to be justified in believing that no future experiment will overturn T.

(2) For any finite set of observations, there are infinitely many rival theories that are compatible with all of them but disagree about some observation nobody has made yet.

(3) So no matter how much evidence we pile up for T, there will always be rival theories that fit the evidence just as well, at least as far as the data go.

(4) But if rival theories always fit the evidence just as well, we can't be justified in singling out T as final unless we lean on something beyond the data, like simplicity or elegance, and we have no proof those extras track the truth.

(5) Therefore, even if a final theory exists, we could never be justified in believing we've found it.

I can tell this is doing something sneaky, but I can't locate the problem. Is the argument valid? If it is, which premise is the weak one and why? If it isn't, exactly which step fails? Keep it under 400 words, American English.
