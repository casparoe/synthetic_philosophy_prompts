In my global climate governance seminar, an asymmetry keeps surfacing that nobody around me seems to find odd. A state that decides on its own to build a dozen new coal plants is treated as exercising ordinary sovereignty, even though the resulting emissions change the climate other states' citizens must live with. A state that decided on its own to inject sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere—openly and to hold temperatures down while mitigation proceeds—would be treated by many of the same people as acting illegitimately, maybe even dangerously. Both are unilateral decisions that significantly affect the shared atmosphere, and both impose serious effects beyond the deciding state's borders. Yet only the second is readily described as playing God, and only the second invites talk of moral hazard and of termination shock: that if the injections stopped, the masked warming would return rapidly. I want to know whether anything actually justifies this asymmetry. Take the strongest objections to unilateral solar geoengineering—moral hazard, abrupt-termination rebound, and the thought that no single state should adjust the planetary climate—and tell me whether they amount to a principled difference or mostly to a status quo bias about which ways of changing the climate count as intervention. Stress-test your principle against close cases, such as a state that unilaterally shuts down its emissions at great domestic cost versus one that unilaterally deploys marine cloud brightening over a shared ocean. Then give an honest verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up?
