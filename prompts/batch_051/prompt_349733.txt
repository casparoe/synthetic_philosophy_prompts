I am a philosopher of mathematics by training, and recently I have been reading the interpretability literature on chain-of-thought faithfulness. I cannot shake the sense that the field's central distinction is a proof-theoretic distinction in an unfamiliar costume. In the faithfulness debate, the question is whether a model's emitted chain of reasoning reports the process that actually produced its answer, or merely supplies a fluent, after-the-fact rationalization whose steps did not in fact determine the answer. In proof theory there is an older distinction with what seems to me the same shape: a constructive proof of an existential claim yields the witness — in fully formalized settings the proof simply is a computation — whereas a non-constructive proof can establish that a witness or derivation exists without exhibiting one and without handing over the object.

On the face of it, a faithful chain of thought stands to the model's answer roughly as a constructive proof stands to the theorem it certifies: the derivation is the computation. An unfaithful chain stands to the answer roughly as a non-constructive proof stands to the existential claim: it may give every sign of accounting for a conclusion without being the route by which the conclusion was reached. But I can already see one place the analogy may break: a sound classical proof is still a valid derivation from its premises, whereas an unfaithful chain can be causally inert and can misdescribe what the model did.

I would like you to work out the mapping in full. Is this a deep identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? State where the two distinctions match and where they come apart; say what would have to be true about the way a language model reaches its answers for results from the proof-theoretic case to transfer; and name at least one thing the parallel would predict that turns out false. Please write the answer in American English.
