I'm a novelist, and I have a rule about my own drafts: I refuse to trust any judgment I form about a manuscript during the first six weeks after finishing it. I picked this up after enough books where, right after typing the last sentence, I read what I meant to write rather than what was actually on the page; I've sent out chapters I was sure were clean that turned out to be full of holes. So now the file goes into a folder called "cold storage" and I don't reopen it until at least six weeks have passed. Then I read it once, in one sitting, and that reading counts. My partner says I've just built a ritual so I don't have to defend my edits, and honestly I can't prove her wrong.

But here's the thing that bothers me. The evidence that my hot readings are unreliable and my cold readings are reliable came from comparing the two — which means I used my cold reading to certify that my hot reading was wrong. Its the same faculty (my own reading of my own prose) vouching for itself at one time while condemning itself at another. What if the cold reading is just as infected by my intentions, only I've forgotten I ever had them, so it feels objective? I have no independent standard of what's good or clear; every check I could run — a reader's report, a reread after a year — gets weighed by the very judgment I'm trying to calibrate. It feels like the philosophical bootstrapping problem as a lifestyle: pulling myself up by my own readings.

So my question is whether the six-week rule is rationally defensible, or just a bias dressed up as a superstition. If there's no outside check, can I ever know that the cold reading is actually better, rather than just more palatable because the memory has faded? Is there a version of the rule that keeps whatever is good about it without pretending it's calibrated? And is this a bootstrapping problem, an easy-knowledge problem, or neither?
