I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, and I've been trying to write a piece about whether large language models understand anything, or just sound like they do. The strongest version of the sceptical case, at least as I understand it, isn't simply that these models make stuff up. It's the stochastic parrots argument: a system trained only to predict the next token in a huge corpus has access to nothing but the distribution of linguistic forms. It never gets to point at the thing the word picks out. No referential grounding, no communicative intent, so no understanding—however fluent the output.

But the factual premise there seemes less solid than it did even three or four years ago. A growing number of systems aren't shaped only by next-token prediction over static text. After pre-training, they're fine-tuned with reinforcement learning on outcomes that go beyond the string itself: they write code, execute it, read the output or the error message, and revise; they call tools and APIs; the more agentic ones act in web or simulated environments where a choice has observable consequences. Even if the base model only ever saw text, the final system has been nudged by a loop in which a generated string has downstream effects—a kind of grounding by feedback, if a thin one.

So the essay I'd like is this: does the stochastic parrots argument survive that development, or does it rest on a picture of language models as pure text-prediction engines that no longer captures how the systems are actually built? Pick out the exact premise under pressure, and then say whether the argument is dead, needs repair, or still goes through. If it can be repaired, give the strongest repaired version. Roughly 1000 words, in British English, and don't end on a wishy-washy both-sides note—land it.
