I am a professor of philosophy working at the boundary of logic, computation, and epistemology, and I want your help diagnosing a debate that has looked stalled to me for decades. The debate concerns the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis: roughly, the claim that the intuitive notion of an effectively computable function coincides with the formally defined class of Turing-machine-computable functions. Everyone in the debate agrees on the formal facts—the equivalence of Turing computability, lambda-definability, and general recursiveness—and everyone agrees that no accepted counterexample exists. What they disagree about is what kind of claim the thesis is. One camp treats it as an empirical generalisation about human or physical computation, established inductively but open to revision. A second camp treats it as a definition or explication of a preformal concept, neither true nor false but a proposed sharpening of an open-textured term. A third camp holds that once one adopts suitable axioms for discrete deterministic computation, restricted versions of the thesis are theorems. A fourth treats it as a methodological or normative principle: any supposed notion of computation that outruns Turing computability is to be rejected as a change of subject. These camps appeal to the same history and the same results against one another, and the literature does not converge.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is neither mathematical nor straightforwardly empirical, but concerns what an explication of an informal notion is required to accomplish—in particular, whether the preformal concept of effective computability is determinate enough for the thesis to be the kind of thing that can be true or false at all. The physical version of the thesis, I suspect, is a different matter: a genuine empirical claim, but a highly speculative one, since proposed hypercomputational devices are either mere broad metaphysical possibilities or physically unrealised. If that is right, the persistence of the debate is mostly explained by the parties answering different questions under one name, with a residual empirical disagreement about the limits of physical computation.
