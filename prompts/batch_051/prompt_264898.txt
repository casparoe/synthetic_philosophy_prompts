Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about a rule I've adopted for taking in information online, and we both walked away annoyed, so I want a second opinion. My policy, which I picked up after reading too much about the attention economy and persuasive design, is this: any factual claim that reaches me through an engagement-optimized feed — algorithmic recommendations, push notifications, autoplay, variable rewards, infinite scroll — gets effectively zero weight. I don't believe the opposite; I just refuse to let it update me at all until I meet the same claim somewhere that wasn't engineered to keep me inside an app. So a study summarized in a TikTok doesn't count, but the same study described in a journal abstract or on a newspaper's site does count (less then the abstract, but it counts). My friend says this is just the genetic fallacy wearing a media-criticism costume: an argument stands on its own, and the feed might coincidently surface true things. He also says my "non-engineered source" cutoff is incoherent, because every site is A/B testing it's headlines now, and even the journal is optimizing for citations. I told him he was purity-spiraling — there's a real difference between a feed that randomizes my next hit and a table of contents I actually navigate. So: is my discount rule defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? Can it be restated to keep the benefit (not letting my attention get rented out by the highest bidder) without the cost (walling myself off from true claims that happen to circulate in those feeds)? And rather than giving me a "both sides have a point" answer, please tell me which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about this, or the point where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
