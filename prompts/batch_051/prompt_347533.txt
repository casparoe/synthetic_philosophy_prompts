I've been reading Abelard in a medieval philosophy course and I'm stuck on how far the consent-not-the-act view is supposed to go. The way I understand it, the sin is already complete when you consent to the deed in your heart -- full stop, even if the opportunity never comes. So someone who truly consents to stealing, and would do it the moment it became possible, sins as gravely as someone who actually steals. I get that part, at least I think I do.

But now here's the case that's tripping me up. Suppose the first person consents to steal from his neighbor and then never gets the chance, so nothing actually happens. Then later he does get the chance and goes through with it. On Abelard's account, is the actual theft a second sin, or is it just the same old consent stretching forward into action without adding anything new? If it doesn't add anything, then I don't see what the neighbor who actually lost his money is even angry about, morally speaking. Abelard seems to be saying that a real victim has nothing more to be mad about than someone whose neighbor merely planned and then was thwarted by circumstance. That sounds obviously wrong to me, but I cant locate which step in Abelard's reasoning I should be rejecting instead. Can you untangle this?
