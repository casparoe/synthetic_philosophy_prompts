I was recently appointed to a hospital committee that allocates scarce health resources — ventilators in a surge, transplantable organs, and the small medical risks imposed by population-wide measures — and I have spent the past few months reading about the objection that aggregative moral reasoning treats distinct persons as though they were one big person whose losses and gains can simply be netted out. From that reading I have built a rule of my own, and I would like you to assess it candidly: first reconstruct it in its strongest form, then say exactly where it fails or stops being distinctive.

The rule, as I have been using it, is this. When a policy would impose losses on some people in order to benefit others, list each person who is made worse off. Each such person contributes one entry, whose size is the amount by which the policy makes that person worse off. An entry may be set against benefits that go to other people only if the entry falls below what I call the shrug threshold: the level of harm that a reasonable person could be asked to absorb without needing a justification addressed to them personally. An entry at or above the shrug threshold can be justified only by a benefit to that same person.

The rule seems to me to get the clear cases right. Taking organs from an unwilling healthy person to save five patients is forbidden: the harvested person's entry is enormous and no benefit goes to them. Building a flood barrier that protects thousands but leaves a handful of nearby residents with a permanently damp, noisy home may be permissible: each resident's entry is a nuisance below the threshold, even though no individual resident's own benefit outweighs their own nuisance. It also forbids diverting a trolley onto one person to save five, since the one person's entry is above the threshold with no personal benefit, and I am prepared to accept that result.

The difficulty is risk. Suppose a vaccination programme is compulsory and carries a one-in-two-hundred-thousand chance of a severe adverse reaction. At the moment of decision, each person's expected loss is tiny and sits below the shrug threshold, so the rule authorises the programme. Yet we know in advance that in a large enough population a few people will actually suffer the severe reaction, and for those people the entry, viewed after the fact, is catastrophic and is not outweighed by any benefit to them. I find it abhorrent to say that their injury was below the threshold when it plainly was not, but I also cannot accept that we should withhold a vaccine that will prevent hundreds of serious illnesses merely because some statistical victims will be created.

So my question is this. Can the rule be stated so that it treats the decision-time entry (a small expected loss) differently from the outcome-time entry (a realised catastrophe) without becoming ad hoc, and is this distinction already a recognised position in debates about aggregating harms across persons? Please do not just lay out options. Take a definite position: give me the best defensible version of the rule, or tell me plainly that it should be abandoned, and defend that verdict. In particular, tell me whether the core idea — that aggregation across persons is legitimate below a threshold of harm but illegitimate above it — can survive the risk case, and whether any version of it lets me keep my judgments about the organ, flood, and trolley cases while treating the statistical victims of vaccination decently.
