I'm on the board of a small community land trust, and we've been left a conditional bequest that's tying us in knots. The donor's will says the trust gets the full amount only if exactly one board member writes to the executor before the deadline to claim it on the organization's behalf. If nobody writes, the gift goes to a default beneficiary; if two or more of us write, the entire gift is void and it goes to the same default. The executor says the condition is strange but enforceable, so it's not really a legal question anymore—it's a decision we have to make. There are seven of us, and we all genuinely want the trust to get the money. But each of us, sitting alone the night before the deadline, faces the same private reasoning: if I hold back and everyone else also holds back, we lose; if I write and I'm the only one who does, we win; if we all write, we lose. It's basically the Platonia dilemma, where the prize goes to the person who acts alone and nothing happens if nobody acts or several people act.

At our meeting, some of us want to just draw straws and instruct everyone except the winner not to write. Others think that's condescending and also fragile: they say seven people with the same goal and the same information should be able to produce the single claim without being bossed around, maybe by each privately applying the same rule (everyone rolls a die and writes only on a one, say) or by reasoning as a kind of collective "we" rather than as separate players. I've been asked to draft a short policy memo for the board laying out what we should actually do before the deadline. The memo needs a clear recommendation, the reasoning behind it, and a fair handling of the strongest argument on the losing side—something we could actually act on.
