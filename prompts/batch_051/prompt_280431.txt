I am currently taking a course on ancient historiography and the rationalization of myth, and we have spent the last few weeks on the euhemerist tradition in Hellenistic and early Christian writers. We are reading Diodorus Siculus as quoted by Eusebius of Caesarea in the Praeparatio Evangelica, Book II, in E. H. Gifford's 1903 translation. I keep returning to the following passage, where Diodorus is summarizing the Egyptian account of their gods:

"The gods, they say, had been originally mortal men, but gained their immortality on account of wisdom and public benefits to mankind, some of them having also become kings: and some have the same names, when interpreted, with the heavenly deities, while others have received a name of their own, as Helios, and Kronos, and Rhea, and Zeus, who is by some called Ammon; and besides these Hera and Hephaestus, and Hestia, and lastly Hermes.

Helios, they say, was the first king of the Egyptians, having the same name with the celestial luminary: some, however, of the priests say that Hephaestus was the first who became king, because he was the discoverer of fire."

The sentence about names is the part I cannot quite make cohere, and I would be grateful for help. The opening says that the gods were originally mortal men who gained immortality "on account of wisdom and public benefits to mankind," which seems to make the human benefactors primary and the gods a later religious projection. But then Diodorus distinguishes some deified mortals by saying they have "the same names, when interpreted, with the heavenly deities," and immediately adds that Helios, the first king, has "the same name with the celestial luminary." That sounds as though the heavenly bodies are the fixed point and the kings are being named after them. Could you help me sort out which way the explanatory priority runs in the passage, and whether the apparent ambiguity matters for what euhemerism is claiming about the relationship between the gods and human rulers?
