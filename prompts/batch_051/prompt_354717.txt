I'm a novelist, not an academic, but I teach an evening fiction workshop for adults, most of them writing their first serious novel. The thing I keep running into is that my students write perpetrators of violence as if the villain was born with a demon switch: by page two he's already torturing cats, and nobody asks how he got there. So I've decide to build the next session around something I stumbled on years ago and never shook, the Browning versus Goldhagen debate about one German reserve police battalion in occupied Poland in 1942—ordinary middle-aged men, most of them not fanatics and many not even party members, ordered to shoot Jewish civilians, and mostly they did it even though the commander gave them the chance to step aside. One camp sees ordinary men in a bad situation—authority, peer pressure, the gradual slide, not wanting to break ranks—while the other camp sees willing executioners whose culture and disposition had already done the real work, with the situation mostly releasing what was there.

I don't want to explain the debate to my students; I want a plan for how to run the ninety-minute session. What order do the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what? Should the battalion be the carrying example, or should I build the session around a smaller fictional scenario and bring the battalion in only near the end—and if so, what exactly does the example have to have, and what must it lack, for the distinction between situational and dispositional explanations to actually bite? I also want the misconception map. I already expect 'so this excuses them,' 'anyone would have done it, prettty much,' 'that was just Germans then,' and 'so character never matters now'—but I suspect there are ones I'm not seeing, and in any case I don't know where in the sequence to defuse each without losing the room. At the end, what's the check for understanding that would tell me they've got the hard thing—that explaining is not excusing, and that situation isn't destiny—rather than just gotten uncomfortable? And what should I openly refuse to cover at this level, and how do I say that without it sounding to much like a cop-out?
