You are a philosopher of cognitive science who designs diagnostic teaching instruments. I am building a "which theory of intelligence do you actually hold?" quiz for an upper-level seminar on animal and machine minds, and I want it to sort people by how they respond to concrete cases rather than by which label they find flattering. Please produce the complete instrument: a short planning section, 8–10 case-based questions, and a scoring key followed by a brief limitations note.

Begin with a planning section (not part of the quiz itself) in which you lay out the axes along which positions on intelligence actually differ. At minimum, work with these four axes, and add others only if strictly necessary:

(a) behavioral vs. internal: is intelligence a matter of what a system does, or of what goes on inside it?
(b) representation vs. goal-directedness: if something internal matters, is the crucial ingredient structured information-processing, or is it the agent's capacity to achieve goals across a wide range of environments?
(c) generality vs. domain-specificity: is intelligence one coherent general capacity, or is it always tied to performance in a particular domain?
(d) consciousness/understanding: does genuine intelligence require phenomenal experience or grasping why one's answer is correct, or can it be subpersonal and unconscious?

Then write 8–10 questions. Each question should present a concrete case with two or three answer options, and no question may ask respondents which view they like or simply ask whether something is "intelligent." The task of the series is to determine what the respondent means by that word, not to have them judge the label. Include all of the following cases somewhere in the instrument, described in your own words:

- a thermostat;
- a system that passes every dialogue test by storing a giant lookup table of possible exchanges rather than by understanding anything;
- a clerk in a room who follows an instruction book to answer questions in a language they do not understand, producing perfect answers;
- a slime mold that solves a shortest-path maze without a brain;
- evolution by natural selection, which produces well-adapted organisms though the process itself does not plan or represent anything;
- a large language model that scores well on exams but is trained only to predict the next word.

You may add further cases, such as a human in a vegetative state, an octopus, an insect colony, a savant with prodigious memory but poor transfer, or a theorem prover.

Then provide a scoring key. The key should map answer patterns onto at least these positions, which should be located as occupied cells in your logical space rather than merely listed: (i) a behavioral/psychometric view; (ii) a computational-representational view; (iii) a goal-directed/ecological view on which intelligence is an agent's capacity to achieve its goals across varied environments; (iv) a learning-and-compression view; (v) a consciousness- or understanding-requiring view; and (vi) a deflationary or family-resemblance view on which "intelligence" tracks a loose cluster of capacities with no hidden essence. For any combination of answers that no defensible position occupies, say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Also flag at least one pair of positions your key cannot cleanly separate for some answer patterns, and explain what extra question would be needed to separate them.

The deliverable should be usable as a handout: questions first, then the scoring key, then a short note headed "What this sorting misses." Think carefully about whether the answer options force false dichotomies, and avoid them or include options such as "neither" or "it depends on further facts."
