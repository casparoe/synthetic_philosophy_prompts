I'm presenting a paper next month at my anthropology department's weekly seminar. The thesis is that a single large, non-domestic structure at a non-literate site cannot by itself discriminate among three institutions that site reports routinely assign to such buildings: (1) a hereditary chief's residence and court, (2) a feasting hall whose hosting rotates among households or lineages, and (3) a council house for an egalitarian assembly. My argument is that what separates these institutions is not the shell of the building but the rhythm of use — who gathers, how often, what is consumed, whether surplus is stored inside or distributed out — and that the decisive evidence must therefore come from household-level patterns of storage, refuse, craft production, and mortuary treatment, most of which archaeologists already collect without connecting to the institutional question. The audience includes a political anthropologist who thinks architectural scale and labor investment track hierarchy reliably enough cross-culturally; an environmental archaeologist who will say subsistence and storage data already settle these cases in practice; a colleague who will object that I am treating as mutually exclusive institutions that can coexist, since a building can be all three at once for different people or in different seasons; and a senior faculty member who will say the paper is purely negative and gives excavators no usable rules of inference. Prepare my question-period prep sheet: the objections in the order they are actually likely to come, phrased the way these particular colleagues would phrase them, an honest ranking of which objections really hurt, what I should concede up front and what I should hold, each objection with the short reply I should give and where that reply is softest, and the one question I should most hope nobody asks.
