Write a pantoum from the point of view of a field linguist who's slowly realizing that gavagai might be untranslatable in a deeper sense than they'd thought. Use the pantoum's repetitions to do the actual philosophical work: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain come back as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza recycles the poem's opening lines, so each time a line returns it's the same string of words with the same behvioral evidence behind it, but its meaning shifts with its new context — rabbit, undetached rabbit part, rabbit stage, the whole rabbit-situation. By the end, the same utterances should support at least two incompatible translation manuals, and the poem should make the reader feel why no ammount of pointing, assent, or clever fieldwork can settle which one is correct. Don't state the theory in the poem; let the form manufacture the indeterminacy. Try to make it an actual good poem too, not just philosophy with line breaks.
