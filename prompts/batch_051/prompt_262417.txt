I'm trying to work out what I actually think about a question that keeps surfacing in a philosophy of psychiatry course I'm auditing: whether the statements people make in the grip of delusions are, in the exact sense, beliefs. I've been told that the two-factor theory of delusion distinguishes (i) an anomalous experience — the strange feeling of unfamiliarity in Capgras, or the visceral sense of one's own death in Cotard — from (ii) a separate deficit in the system that would normally reject the wild explanation forming around that experience, while a one-factor view holds that the anomalous experience plus ordinary reasoning is enough. But I'm less interested in adjudicating those theories in the abstract than in figuring out whether there is a principled account of belief behind my own verdicts on cases. Here they are, as faithfully as I can record them.

First, a severely depressed patient with Cotard's syndrome repeatedly says the sentence "I am dead" and insists that her organs have rotted away, yet she eats normally, watches television with interest, and steps out of the path of an oncoming car in the hospital driveway. I don't think she believes she is dead in the way she believes her own name; but I also don't think she is lying or speaking metaphorically. Second, a man with Capgras syndrome says his wife has been replaced by an identical impostor, though he grants that the woman in front of him looks exactly like her; when he speaks to his wife on the telephone from another room, he says the caller is his real wife and the woman before him is the duplicate. I can't see how to describe him as anything other than sincerely believing the impostor claim. Third, a stroke patient insists that her left arm belongs to her doctor; she agrees that it is attached to her shoulder, and then immediately reattributes ownership to him. I lean toward saying she believes it, but on the model of a belief formed from misleading evidence rather than the way the Capgras man believes his wife has been replaced — yet I cannot state the difference. Fourth, a man with schizophrenia is convinced that a television presenter is sending him encoded messages and that his apartment is wired; he covers the television, hides the radio, and refuses to say certain words aloud. I think these are plainly beliefs, merely false and badly supported. Fifth and most troublesome, a woman with anorexia at a dangerously low weight insists that she is fat. Part of me says this is an evaluative attitude rather than a factual belief; but she acts in just the ways someone would act if she believed the factual claim, and I can't locate the non-doxastic part.

I'd like you to tell me whether there is one account — or a small set of candidate accounts — of what it takes for a verbally endorsed, action-guiding state to be a genuine belief that captures my verdicts, or most of them, and to state exactly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. In particular, I want to know what the two-factor theory predicts about each of the five cases, and whether my feeling that the Cotard patient is less of a believer than the Capgras patient can be defended or must be abandoned. Please also say what your favored account implies for a case I have not listed, such as a person who has recovered and now describes the delusion as having been more like a vivid imagining than a belief. Finally, mark the points where your verdict would change: which argument, or which empirical finding about these patients, would lead you to reverse the conclusions you give, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
