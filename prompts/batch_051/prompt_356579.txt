I’ve been going down a rabit hole in Berkeley’s question-book on money, the one where every sentance is a question and he never quite comes out and argues. The view I keep landing on is something like: money is just a sort of ticket or tally for moving purchasing power around among people; the actual wealth of a nation is the industry and goods of its people; and Ireland was poor back then not because it lacked gold but because its labor was idle and there weren’t enough circulating tickets to put that labor to work. The kind of proposal this points toward is a public bank issuing paper notes that cannot be redeemed for gold or silver, backed by the general credit and future industry of the nation rather than by metal in a vault.

Here’s what I want from you, and please don’t tell me it depends on your monetary theory: give me your actual credence that a disciplined public bank issuing irredeemable paper tickets could have sustained trade, employment, and stable-enough prices in a small, open, depressed economy of the 1730s at least as well as a specie-coin system. By “worked as well” I mean as a transaction mechanism and a spur to employment over, say, a decade, not as a get-rich scheme. Decompose the number: what probability do you put on (a) money’s value being fundamentally a coordination convention rather than something that inheres in the metal, (b) an irredeemable paper currency holding its value absent the threat of conversion into gold or silver given the institutions actually available then, (c) the real constraint on that economy being a shortage of circulating medium rather than land tenure, trade barriers, or the political situation, and (d) the bank’s governors staying disciplined under fiscal and political pressure rather than over-issuing. Give a number and one line of justification for each, show how you combined them, and then tell me which single sub-question is doing the most work in your final number and what evidence would move it most.
