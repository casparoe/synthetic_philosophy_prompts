Right, so after a three-hour argument with my mate last night I still can’t shake this thing. We were arguing about whether 2 is an element of 4. I said that’s not a real arithmetic fact, it’s just an artefact of how you build numbers out of sets. In the standard construction, sure, 2 is a member of 4 because 4 is {0,1,2,3}. But you can also do the construction where each number is the singleton of the previous one, and then 2 isn’t a member of 4 at all. Both constructions give you the same arithmetic — 2+2=4, every number has a successor, induction works — but they disagree on a whole load of set-theoretic side effects: which numbers are members of which, whether 1 is a subset of 2, things like that. My friend kept saying that’s just because one encoding is the correct one, but I couldn’t see what makes the standard one better except habit. I definately think there’s something going on here, but I don’t know what to call it.

What I’m after is whether this general situation has a name. Not just ‘structuralism’ because that’s the positive view. I mean the specific problem that multiple set-theoretic reductions of arithmetic work equally well but assign different truth-values to statements like ‘2 ∈ 4’, so if you insist numbers literally are sets you end up with a load of arbitrary extra truths. Is there an established term for that, and how does it differ from the general idea that structural properties are all that matter? If there’s no name, I’d like a coined one with a definition and a couple of boundary cases (e.g. does it also affect constructions of ordered pairs, or the natural numbers as Frege’s cardinals?). I’m not looking for a bare label — I want the edges of it, where it stops applying.
