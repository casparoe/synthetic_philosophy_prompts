I'm a handling editor at a general science journal, handling maybe six papers a month, and I keep seeing referee reports where "elegant" or "ugly" does the work of an argument: a beautiful unification gets recommended despite thin data, and an ad hoc-looking model gets panned despite fitting better. I do it too, and I've started to think we're just laundering aesthetic preference as evidence. I'd like a reusable procedure for our reviewers and editors for deciding when elegance may count as evidence for taking a theory seriously, and when it should be set aside entirely. It has to be usable by a busy working scientist with about two hours per report and no philosophy background, and the final call still rests with two handling editors. One thing I want nailed down: reviewers use "elegance" to mean several different things (few free parameters, symmetry, unifying previously separate phenomena, or just "it feels right"). Should the procedure treat those separately or bundle them? Ground the procedure in whatever the philosophy of science actually knows about beauty or elegance as evidence, but don't cite anything; I want the rationale, not a reading list. Include worked applications: one where a mathematically lovely model fits slightly worse than an ugly ensemble of mechanisms, one where elegance is the only thing going for a paper, and at least one case where the procedure forces me to do something I'll dislike. Flag the points where judgement can't be eliminated. Please write in British English, and make the very last line a one-sentence summary of the procedure.
