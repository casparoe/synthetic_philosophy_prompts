So I've just had a long argument with a mate about Maimonides on animal sacrifice, and it's left me genuinly unsure. He reckons Maimonides thought the whole sacrificial system was a concession to the Israelites' weakness: God didn't really want it, but he couldn't take the people straight from paganism to purely spiritual worship, so he redirected their existing sacrificial habits towards Himself instead. In the Guide for the Perplexed, part III, chapter 32, Maimonides says something to the effect that it is impossible for a man to go suddenly from one extreme to the other, and that God therefore did not command Israel to abolish all these modes of worship but allowed them to continue, transferred to God's name; he compares it to a physician who does not suddenly withdraw everything a patient is used to. I pushed back with the same book, part III, chapter 46, where Maimonides goes through the various offerings and gives detailed reasons for them, saying they were instituted to counteract specific idolatrous beliefs and to teach true opinions about God. That sounds like he takes them to have real positive value, not just tolerated second-best. Are these two chapters actually compatible? If they are, what exactly is the relation between the concession and the detailed rationales: is Maimonides just making the best of a bad job, or does he think the sacrifices become good once redirected? And if they aren't compatible, which one is his real view? I want a verdict on which reading does less violence to the text.
