I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am working on a piece about what happens when rules written for one strategic era are forced to govern another. I keep returning to a declassified 1972 directive from the small, now-dissolved Republic of Valara, which governed its nuclear forces for two decades. Its controlling clause reads:

"No officer of the Republic may order or recommend the release of any nuclear weapon except upon receipt of an authenticated report, confirmed by two independent national technical means, that a hostile nuclear detonation has occurred on the territory of the Republic, on its armed forces, or on the territory of a state under the Republic's nuclear protection. Nothing in this section shall be read as authorizing release whenever such a report is received."

The clause was drafted to solve two problems: an accidental launch after a false warning, and a leader who might threaten nuclear use too early in a conventional crisis. Tying release to a confirmed detonation seemed to close both.

The unforeseen case is this. The successor state now relies on a small submarine-based deterrent whose release orders travel through a handful of geostationary satellites, and both of the independent national technical means specified in the clause depend on those same satellites for their data. An adversary has demonstrated a non-nuclear, cyber-enabled ability to blind or destroy those satellites, and has deployed undersea drones that shadow every submarine. War games suggest that in a crisis the successor state could lose the capacity to receive or authenticate any report of a hostile detonation—and with it the legal precondition for any nuclear release—before a single detonation has occurred. Its leaders would be left with weapons they are forbidden to use, because the event that would make their use lawful can no longer be confirmed. Some officials argue that to preserve the clause's purpose a massive non-nuclear strike on the state's warning and command architecture should be treated as satisfying the clause; others argue that the text cannot be stretched that far.

I am less interested in a policy recommendation than in the interpretive problem: does the 1972 clause permit, forbid, or simply fall silent in this scenario, when read by its text, by its purpose, and by what its drafters would likely have said if asked? Work through all three approaches and tell me which should control, and whether the clause's letter is a bulwark or a trap.
