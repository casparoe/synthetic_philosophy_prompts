I have no formal training in philosophy or the history of science, but I have been thinking on my own about how scientists choose between competing theories, and I keep running into the same argument. A friend of mine, an engineer, puts it roughly like this: for any finite collection of points on a graph, infinitely many different curves can be drawn through them; in the same way, for any finite body of observations, infinitely many different theories can be made to accommodate them. From this he concludes that evidence never compels a theory on its own. Whatever extra consideration tips the balance, such as simplicity, elegance, or naturalness, is doing the real work, and therefore our accepted theories are closer to convenient fits than to truths about the world. I gather this is the idea often called the underdetermination of theory by evidence. I cannot find the flaw in his argument, but the conclusion feels much stronger than the analogy can support, and I suspect the curve-fitting picture is quietly doing more work than it is entitled to.

I would like an audit of this analogy. Please spell out the full mapping first: what in real scientific theory choice corresponds to the data points, the curve, the axes or coordinates, the residual errors, and the preference for a simpler curve. Then identify the places where the mapping fails, and distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely matter for my friend's skeptical conclusion from those that are merely superficial. Be explicit about what the curve-fitting picture quietly imports, about what observations are like, what a theory is for, and how much competition among theories really resembles competition among curves, that real scientific inference does not automatically include. Finally, judge whether the conclusion survives the disanalogies: is it still true that any finite body of evidence is compatible with indefinitely many theories and that non-evidential considerations therefore carry real weight, or does the analogy overstate how arbitrary the choice between theories is? If there is a better analogy that preserves the genuine insight about underdetermination without the overstatement, offer it as well.
