I am a sixth-form student and the student member of my school's Academic Integrity Panel, which advises the head teacher on breaches of the Honesty Code. A case before us has split the panel, and I need to circulate a short written opinion before our meeting on Thursday.

The facts are not in dispute. A Year 13 student submitted A-level history coursework in which one paragraph was copied from a published study guide without attribution. The Honesty Code, adopted two years ago by a properly constituted vote of the whole school, states: "Any work containing plagiarised material shall be awarded zero marks and the student shall be barred from holding elected office and from receiving school prizes for the remainder of the year." It allows no exceptions. The student admitted the copying when her teacher asked, and later explained that she submitted the essay during the week her mother was in hospital after a serious car accident, while she was also caring for her younger sister; she says the copied paragraph is background description rather than argument, that the rest of the essay is her own work, and that she confessed the same morning because keeping the deception made her feel unwell. Her record is otherwise clean, and the zero mark may affect her university application.

Half the panel says the Code is the Code: it was validly enacted, its wording is plain, and reading exceptions into it would make every future penalty negotiable. The other half says applying the full penalty here would be unjust and contrary to the purpose stated in the Code's preamble, which is "to cultivate intellectual honesty and fairness in assessment."

I must draft the panel's written opinion. It should recommend a concrete outcome — apply the penalty in full, waive or reduce it, or apply it with conditions — and give reasons the head teacher can act on by the end of this week. Please engage honestly with the strongest argument for the position you reject.

Please act as a philosopher of law with expertise in the Hart–Fuller debate over whether a rule's validity and binding force depend only on its source and procedure or also on its moral content. Think carefully, and begin with brief brainstorming notes in which you set out how a legal positivist and a natural-law theorist would each frame this case, before applying that to our Code. Then write the final memo itself, ready to circulate, in formal British English, with British spelling and usage throughout.
