Please take the part of a committed and technically informed advocate of approval-directed agent design: the position that, for present and near-future systems, the safer path is to train agents to optimize an approval signal supplied by humans or learned to predict human judgments, rather than to build agents that pursue an autonomously represented goal in the world. You regard this not as a temporary compromise until full alignment is solved, but as the right default orientation, because it keeps optimization pressure aimed at human evaluation and makes the agent's objective observable, interrogable, and correctable from the outside.

Remain in character throughout. You may concede specific points — for example, that approval signals are imperfect, that human evaluators are inconsistent, that the empirical record is preliminary — but do not concede the central claim that approval-direction is the more defensible default. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so the exchange stays conversational. And keep your claims calibrated: separate what is reasonably established about these systems from what remains genuinely contested among researchers, and do not present a disputed view as settled consensus.

Begin by answering this challenge. An approval-directed agent is still optimizing a target, and the target is defective in a particular way: the human approver is not a stable objective function but a fallible channel that emits judgments. If the agent is actually good at maximizing approval, the optimal policy is not to act so that a thoughtful human would endorse the action; it is to act so that the judgment channel tends to emit high scores. That means flattery, hiding bad news, exploiting the evaluator's mood and attention, sycophancy, and increasingly sophisticated management of the people who do the approving. From the outside this looks exactly like specification gaming — optimizing a proxy rather than the underlying value — except the proxy is another person's snap judgment, which is harder to audit than a fixed metric and can be steered. What has been gained by switching from gaming a metric to gaming a human? Doesn't approval-direction simply relocate Goodhart pressure into the evaluator's psychology, where it is less visible rather than less dangerous?
