I have noticed an asymmetry in how people around me speak about unpleasant states, and I want to know whether it can be justified. When someone loses a close relative and feels no grief, almost nobody treats this as a lucky escape from suffering. Instead, they treat it as evidence that something is wrong with the person: that they are emotionally shut down, or that they did not truly love the person they lost. A total absence of sadness is spoken of as though it were a defect rather than a liberation. Yet nobody speaks this way about the absence of physical pain. A person born unable to feel pain is regarded as having a dangerous medical condition; people wish they had a working warning system, but nobody suggests that their character is flawed because they do not hurt, and nobody thinks they ought to suffer more in order to respond appropriately to their injuries. What could justify treating grief as something whose absence is a loss, while treating pain as a mere burden whose absence is good fortune? One possible answer is that grief is the appropriate response to a real loss, whereas pain is only a signal. But pain is also an appropriate response to tissue damage, so that answer seems incomplete. Another possible answer is that grief is bound up with love, whereas pain is bound up only with self-preservation. But then what should we say about sadness that is not tied to any particular relationship, such as sadness at a stranger's misfortune or at the end of a happy period in one's own life? I am trying to work out whether the special status given to grief and sadness should be kept, extended to other unpleasant states, or abandoned.
