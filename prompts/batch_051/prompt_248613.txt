I've been trying to figure out when an author's intentions should control what a text means, and my verdicts on individual cases are pulling in different directions. I'd like a principle, if there is one, that captures them — not just a label and not a reading list, I want the rule stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases.

Heres the cases:

1. A poem has a line that is genuinely ambiguous between two readings. The poet later says in an interview she meant reading A. I think that settles it: the line means A. I don't see why we would ignore the one person who knows what she was trying to do.

2. A novelist's private notebook says the villain was meant to be sympathetic, but in the published novel the narrator sneers at him and the plot punishes him. I don't think the notebook changes what the novel says. The text presents him as contemptible, and that's what he is in the novel.

3. A maintenance manual says in step 3 to tighten the red valve, but the diagram and every other step make clear the blue valve is the one under discussion. The writer later confirms it was a typo. I think the manual means the blue valve, and the writer's intention is doing real work here, not just the diagram.

4. An AI is prompted to write a poem about grief and produces a decent sonnet. There is no single human intention behind those specific words, but I still think the text means something and can be read well or badly. The prompt doesn't settle whether the poem is about genuine grief or performed grief.

5. A novel from the 1920s has a minor character who now reads as an antisemitic caricature. Letters show the author meant him as an affectionate portrait of a friend. I think the modern reading is legitimate — the book does carry that meaning now — but I also don't want to say the letters are irrelevant to what it meant. Both seem real.

6. A novel is published anonymously; after the author dies a letter surfaces saying the whole thing was meant as an allegory of his marriage. I don't think that letter changes the novel's meaning, though I would use it as one piece of evidence among others.

I know philosophers disagree about whether the author's intention controls the meaning of a text; I'm not asking for the name of a position. I want the principle behind my verdicts — or if no single principle can hold them, the best one or two candidates, stated precisely, plus which of my verdicts each forces me to drop. And I'd like you to test your preferred principle on a new case: the message "I'm sorry you feel that way" sent after an argument. Does it mean what the sender intended, what a reasonable recipient would take it to mean, or something else, and why?
