I've been reading two kinds of material about numbers in scripture, side by side, and I can't shake the sense that the scholars involved are applying a double standard. On one side, the history of interpretation treats the surface numbers of the Bible as a real layer of meaning: the forty days of rain in the flood narrative, the twelve tribes and twelve apostles, the seven seals, and the one hundred fifty-three fish in John's account of the post-resurrection catch are all read as deliberate figural or theological patterning rather than as incidental counting. On the other side, when modern Bible-code researchers claim that equidistant letter sequences in the Hebrew Bible encode significant names and dates, much of the same scholarly culture dismisses the work as apophenia: data-dredging over an enormous combinatorial space and then retroactively declaring the hits meaningful. The two enterprises are not identical, but their epistemic structure looks uncomfortably similar. Each selects, from a huge range of possible numerical or spatial arrangements, the subset that appears significant, and then treats that selection as evidence of design rather than coincidence.

So I want to know whether the asymmetry in how we receive those two practices can actually be justified. Is there a principled difference — in the constraints used, the prior hypotheses in play, the role of tradition, or the kind of claim being made — that makes the ancient symbolic reading worthy of intellectual respect and the modern computational version unworthy of it? Or is our different treatment mostly a bias in favor of old patterns and against new ones? If there is a line, I would like it drawn precisely enough that it does not also license the modern code-hunter; if there is no line, then I would like to know which side of the practice should be revised as a result.
