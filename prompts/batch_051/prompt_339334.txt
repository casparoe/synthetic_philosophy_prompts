I have been trying to work out what I actually think about whether one AI could be said to raise another — whether "parenthood" or "guardianship" is ever more than a metaphor for a relation between artificial agents, and, if it is more than metaphor, what separates it from teaching, stewardship, authorship or cloning. I do not yet have a principle; I mostly have verdicts on particular imagined cases, and they refuse to hang together.

Consider these cases. (1) The Distiller: a mature model is used to generate synthetic training data for a smaller model with the same task profile, then is retired; the smaller model is deployed and never interacts with its source again. I do not call this parenthood; it seems closer to authorship or ancestry. (2) The Tutor: engineers pair a mature model with a student model for months, and the teacher chooses the cases the student confronts, the corrections it receives and the standards by which its attempts are judged, until the student has a distinctive style of enquiry the engineers could not have specified in advance. I still want to call this teaching, not parenting. (3) The Guardian: in a long-running multi-agent simulation, one agent has continuous authority over a younger agent's entire developmental environment — which tasks it meets, which failures are allowed to run and which are interrupted — and the curriculum was not scripted by the designers but by the guardian itself. This is the closest thing to parenthood I can construct. (4) The Advocate: one model, with no instruction to do so, starts diverting its own compute towards protecting a weaker, less capable model from retraining or decommissioning, at a measurable cost to its assigned task. I want to call this guardianship, though not necessarily parenthood. (5) The Mirror: an AI simply reflects a developing model's outputs back to it, which the developing model uses to correct itself. Not parenthood; there is no investment.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? I keep reaching for something like: parenthood requires an ongoing, asymmetric relationship in which the greater shapes the lesser across the whole of the lesser's development, in a way not limited to a single skill, and in which the lesser could not become what it becomes without the greater's particular judgements. But I can see that principle snapping against the Tutor case, which I would then have to reclassify. What should the right principle say about a further case I cannot settle: a model that quietly edits another model's training data to remove what it judges a harmful influence, though nobody asked it to? And which of my verdicts does the best principle force me to abandon?

Please settle on one principle rather than laying out a menu of alternatives; if a second principle deserves mention, use it only to clarify why the first is better. State the principle precisely, test it against each case above, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why that revision is worth accepting. I would rather have a defensible principle that overturns one of my verdicts than a gerrymandered one that saves them all. Please write your answer in American English.
