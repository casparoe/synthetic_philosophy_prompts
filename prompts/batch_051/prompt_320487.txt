So me and my friend just went around in circles for two hours about faithfulness vs minimality in causal discovery, and I think the reason we got stuck is the same reason that debate has been stuck in the literature for decades. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who's right.

Quick setup so you know what we were fighting about. You've got a bunch of variables and a joint distribution over them, and you want to recover the underlying causal graph. Nearly everyone accepts the causal Markov condition: a graph is Markov to the distribution when every variable is independent of its non-descendants given its parents — equivalently, whenever the graph d-separates two sets of variables, they are independent in the data. The problem is Markov alone is hopelessly weak: the complete graph with every pair connected satisfies it trivially, since it d-separates almost nothing, so it cant rule edges in or out. Hence the fight over what to add to it.

The two rival additions:

- Faithfulness: the converse of Markov — independence in the data iff d-separation in the graph, so there are no "extra" independencies beyond the ones the graph entails.
- Minimality: among the Markovian graphs, keep only the sparsest ones — no proper subgraph of the graph you pick is still Markov to the data.

My friend's position is that minimality is the modest, sane assumption. It's just Occam/avoiding overfitting, a generic epistemic virtue, whereas faithfulness is a flat-out metaphysical bet that nature never produces exact cancellations — even though feedback, homeostasis, and engineered control produce exactly that kind of cancellation all the time. He says the search-algorithm people treat a "measure zero" argument as if it settles an empirical question about the world, when it doesn't.

My position was that faithfulness is the thing that actually buys you identification. Under faithfulness, the true graph is guaranteed to be among the minimal Markovian graphs, and those all agree on adjacencies, so algorithms like PC can recover the equivalence class. Without it, minimality will happily delete an edge whose influence is exactly canceled out and then call the smaller graph the answer — so in the very cases where the two principles disagree, minimality is just wrong about the structure while faithfulness would at least stop you from deleting the edge. Plus the unfaithful parameters live in a lower-dimensional corner of parameter space, so treating them as the default worry is weird unless you have specific reason to think you're sitting exactly on a cancellation.

We couldn't move each other, and afterwards it occurred to me that the same stalemate runs through the causal discovery literature: one side treats faithfulness as an innocuous typicality/default assumption, another treats it as an untestable metaphysical commitment that's false surprisingly often, and a third says minimality is the honest replacement even though it gives different answers precisely in the cases that matter.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, structurally? Is it substantive — about how common unfaithfulness actually is, or about what the whole enterprise is for (recovering the true graph vs finding a useful summary)? Is it verbal — about what "the true graph" or "causation" means? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing all the work, or is the question itself ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actual progress look like — a theorem, an experiment, a distinction, a different question?

My hunch, which you should treat as a hypothesis not a conclusion: the two camps are answering different questions under one heading. The faithfulness camp is doing ontology — they want the true graph, or at least its equivalence class. The minimality camp is doing something closer to predictive modeling — they want a parsimonious, stable summary. And the real fight is about whether "the true graph" is a thing to be recovered or just a reification of "the summary that turned out to be useful." But im not married to that. If the better diagnosis is that one side is simply right and the other is confused, or that the distinction I just drew is itself loaded, say so plainly. And if I've garbled the technical setup or misstated what either assumption commits you to, correct me instead of humoring a garbled version.
