I have been trying to pin down what it means to see through a picture, and I have arrived at a criterion I call the covariance criterion. An image I is see-through with respect to a scene S, I want to say, just in case three conditions hold. First, there is a continuous physical channel from the light leaving S to the surface of I, or to the light emitted by the screen that displays I. Second, for any sufficiently small region of I, some visible property of that region — brightness, hue, texture, or the like — would have been different if the corresponding local property of S had been different at the time the image was made. Third, no link in the channel is one at which a person's beliefs or pictorial conventions decide which visible property goes where. I call the third condition the no-interpretation condition.

On this criterion, photographs are see-through. In a portrait photograph, the brightness at each point on the print is counterfactually controlled by light from the corresponding point on the sitter's face, and the process runs through lens, sensor, and print without anyone's judgement placing the tones. A photorealist painting is not see-through: even if it is visually indistinguishable from a photograph of the same scene, the artist's beliefs about the scene determine where every mark goes, so the image is a report made from the scene rather than a sample of its light. I find this appealing because it explains why I say I can see my grandfather in a photograph of him but not in a charcoal portrait of him, and why photographs function as evidence in ways that paintings typically do not.

So far I have accepted these consequences. A heavily edited photograph is only partly see-through: it ceases to be see-through wherever local features no longer covary with the scene. A photocopy of a photograph is see-through with respect to the original scene, just degraded by noise. A live security-camera feed is see-through. An AI-upscaled image is not fully see-through, because the upscaler inserts detail that merely looks typical and does not covary with the scene — and this bothers me, because that inserted detail is not put there by a person's beliefs at all. A camera obscura tracing is the case I cannot settle: the local marks do covary with the scene, but the artist's hand seems to introduce exactly the mediation the no-interpretation condition is meant to exclude.

I am aware that I may have wandered into a familiar debate about whether photographs are transparent in a way paintings are not, and that my criterion may be a cruder version of something already on the table. Give me an honest assessment: restate the criterion in its strongest form, tell me which existing ideas it is closest to and where it genuinely differs, and present the three strongest objections. In particular, say whether any objection is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and whether the no-interpretation condition can be repaired or should be abandoned.
