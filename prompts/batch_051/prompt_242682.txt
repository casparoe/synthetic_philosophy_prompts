I am a retired physical chemist, and I convene a monthly reading group of twelve retired scientists and engineers — mostly chemists and physicists, with one economist — who have lately turned to philosophy of economics and social epistemology. We have been working through the idea that markets perform epistemic work: prices aggregate dispersed private information. But the no-trade theorem appears to pull in the opposite direction, suggesting that purely information-based trade should unravel, because a rational counterparty's willingness to trade at a given price is itself information about what they know. I would like the group to feel this tension in a structured way rather than simply discuss it.

Please design a 90-minute game for our next meeting, for twelve participants (divisible into pairs or small groups as needed). Each participant should receive a private signal about the terminal value of a single asset, then trade claims on that asset through an explicit procedure, with a point-based payoff scheme that needs no real money and is tied to the asset's terminal value. The rules should be arranged so that the group first experiences a burst of eager trade and then, ideally, watches the market freeze as they learn to read one another's willingness to trade as information; the theorem itself should not be named until the debrief. The debrief should then map specific moments in the activity onto the theorem's assumptions — common priors, common knowledge of rationality, and strict risk aversion — and should say which of these the game genuinely enforced and which it merely approximated. I would also like an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: actual markets contain noise traders and liquidity needs, and the theorem predicts zero trade among the idealized agents, not that real traders are irrational when they trade.

Give me a definite design, not a menu of options: the exact rules and materials, the payoff formula, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief questions in order, and your best prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it. Where the design forces you to choose among mechanisms, commit to one and explain why you chose it.
