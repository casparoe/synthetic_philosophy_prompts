so i jsut spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether Rousseau's forced-to-be-free business is actually coherent or just an elegantly dressed-up apology for the state crushing people. We kept going in circles: every time i raised the totalitarian worry, my friend would say no, you're misunderstanding what the general will is, it's not the will of all. Which mostly convinced me that the interesting questions are the ones that push past the slogan.

Anyway, I host a small political theory podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a strong reading of Rousseau's social contract: he holds that the general will is a real standard, not just an ideal fiction or aggregative shortcut, and that the forcing-to-be-free passage is a serious claim about what freedom requires rather than an embarrassment to be explained away. I want eight to ten interview questions that don't let him just restate the strong reading — they should press where the view actually creaks. For each question, include the follow-up you'd ask after the answer he's most likely to give.

Make sure to include: a question on how the general will is supposed to differ from the will of all in practice — he'll probably say the general will is what remains once particular interests that cancel each other out get subtracted, so gear the follow-up toward why that formal remainder should count as what I actually will, and what we're supposed to do when the majority is wrong about it; a question on the Lawgiver's role (and the civil religion stuff) in shaping the very wills that are then supposed to be free, and whether that's education or manufacture; a question on whether the general will smuggles in a substantive, contested conception of the good, so forcing someone to be free ends up being forcing someone into someone else's thick idea of a good life; and a closing question about what evidence could ever show that a state is enforcing the general will rather than a faction's will. Dont give me generic questions about why he got interested in Rousseau.
