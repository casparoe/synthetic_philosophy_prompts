I am a doctoral student in media law, and I have been asked to contribute a satirical piece to a workshop on the First Amendment's treatment of ratings, forecasts, and the fact/opinion distinction. Please write the piece. It should be an Onion-style news article — headline plus roughly 450 words — reporting that a startup has launched a browser extension called OpinionShield that automatically rewrites a user's restaurant reviews, contractor complaints, and social-media posts so that every fact-like accusation is converted into a star rating, a forward-looking forecast, or a sentence prefaced with phrases such as "in my opinion," "it seems to me," or "allegedly." The company's pitch is that these changes turn defamatory assertions of fact into constitutionally protected opinion. Keep the article deadpan, in the voice of The Onion's technology desk, and aim the satire at the broader practice of treating the words "opinion," "rating," and "forecast" as a talismanic shield against defamation liability.

The jokes should come from getting the actual doctrine right rather than from generic anti-lawyer or anti-tech-bro gags. In particular, the piece should work with — and exaggerate without misstating — the following: that there is no magic-words test, so prefatory phrases do not by themselves immunize an underlying factual claim; that an otherwise evaluative statement, including a star rating or a prediction, can still be actionable when it implies an undisclosed defamatory fact; that liability turns on whether the statement is provably false rather than merely unpleasant; and that the fact/opinion question depends on how a reasonable reader would understand the statement in its full context. A strong version might show a user typing "the owner has hepatitis, I saw the lab report" and receiving "1/5 stars — in my opinion, the owner allegedly has hepatitis, and I forecast he will remain a public-health risk," after which an invented First Amendment attorney or media law professor quoted in the story explains, with a straight face, exactly why the software has changed nothing while the company's terms of service disclaim liability for everything. Please include at least one such quoted expert. The result should read as satire rather than a doctrinal summary, but the legal details should be the engine of the humor.
