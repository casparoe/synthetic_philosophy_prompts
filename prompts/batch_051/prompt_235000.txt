I have recently joined a historic preservation commission, and we are weighing a proposal to demolish a 1920s municipal auditorium in order to build affordable housing and a transit hub. In our debates, people keep reaching for Burkean conservatism and Chesterton’s fence, but the arguments are looser than I would like. I want to think through a real case to make the theory precise. In Reflections on the Revolution in France, Burke argues that political institutions are an entailed inheritance, to be held as a trust for those not yet born, and that the statesman’s default is to repair rather than demolish. Chesterton’s fence image in The Thing pushes the same burden of proof onto the reformer: before removing an existing thing, you must be able to say what it was for. Take the demolition of Pennsylvania Station in New York: the above-ground station designed by McKim, Mead & White opened in 1910 and was torn down in 1963 to make way for Madison Square Garden. Analyze that episode through this combined Burke–Chesterton lens. I do not need the architectural history; I need the mapping. Who were the relevant actors, what options did the lens say they faced, and what should they have done by its lights? Then turn the lens around: which features of the Penn Station case does it explain, and where does it fail or misdescribe? What does the case reveal about the limits of Chesterton’s fence as a decision principle—does it collapse into a bias for whatever exists, and can it say when removal is genuinely justified rather than merely convenient? Keep the analysis disciplined about what the theory is silent on rather than forcing it to speak.
