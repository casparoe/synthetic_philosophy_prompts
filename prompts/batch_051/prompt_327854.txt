I’ve been covering science and ideas long enough to have a pet theory I keep reaching for, and I want it checked. I call it etymological laundering. The idea is that when a field coins a new term from Greek or Latin roots, it isn’t just naming a concept — it’s laundering a rough everyday idea so it comes out looking precise, settled, and objective. The dead roots do the work by cutting the word off from any living language community that could complain about how it’s being used. So ‘idiopathic’ turns an honest admission of ignorance about cause into a condition the patient has; ‘heuristic’ turns a decent rule of thumb into an authoritative-sounding property of a method; ‘comorbidity’ turns the fact that conditions often show up together into a quasi-diagnosis. My stronger claim is that this is often the point: coining from a dead langauge lets a field legislate a definition without having to win an argument, because nobody is alive to say the word means something else. I know ordinary jargon does some of this too, and I know some of these terms later get operationalized until they earn their precision. But I keep thinking the dead-language part adds something distinctive. Give me your bottom line first — is this a coherent distinction or just a metaphor? — and then walk me through what it gets right, whether it already exists under another name, and where it breaks or overclaims.
