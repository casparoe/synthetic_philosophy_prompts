I work on the policy team at a company that hosts griefbots — chatbots that simulate someone who has died, built from their texts, voice notes and social media. We're considering a rule where the living user has exactly one control: every thirty days the system asks whether the bot has made the last two weeks easier or harder to get through. Answering "harder" permanently lowers the bots fidelity one notch (how closely it mimics the dead person), and that lowering cant be undone by anyone, including us. After three "harder" answers, the bot is archived and cannot be rebuilt from the same data for a year. There is no way to raise fidelity, ever. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral judgement. What does it incentivise among grieving users, among family members who share access, among the engineers tuning the model, and among us as a company that charges a subscription? Where does it get gamed first? What second-order effects should I expect? And could the failure modes be patched without the rule becoming something else entirely? Answer in British English.
