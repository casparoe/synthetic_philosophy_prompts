I am an editor at a university press preparing a new annotated translation of a classic work in the history of science — for concreteness, imagine a seventeenth-century natural-philosophy text whose vocabulary includes force, body, and proportion, though the procedure should be reusable for earlier and later material. My job is to review the annotations, glosses, and modernization decisions proposed by a team of subject experts and copyeditors. These decisions recur on nearly every page, they will be quoted by historians, and a wrong gloss can systematically distort how students read the work. I need a written procedure I can hand to a new editor — someone with modern scientific training but little history of science — to decide whether a proposed note or gloss is anachronistic.

Please turn the conceptual work on anachronism in the history of science into a concrete decision procedure. It should: ask its questions in a fixed order; say what counts as sufficient evidence for each; mark the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a specialist; distinguish different kinds of anachronism rather than treating anachronism as one thing — at minimum, substituting a later concept for the author's own, attributing a later standard of rigor to the author, and using later mathematics to reconstruct what the author may have been groping toward; and be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a proposed note replacing a text's word for speed with velocity so readers will not confuse the two; (2) a proposed note explaining a geometrically argued proof by rendering the argument as a modern algebraic equation; (3) a proposed note that criticizes the author for failing to state a conservation principle that was not formulated until much later. At least one of these should come out as do not print in a way that a modern-trained editor is likely to resist. End by saying what the procedure cannot settle.
