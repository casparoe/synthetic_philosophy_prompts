I keep seeing this summary of strong longtermism float around — a friend sent me a magazine article, and now its the version everyone in my group chat cites. It goes roughly:

Strong longtermism says that if humanity survives, there could be trillions and trillions of future people, while there are only eight billion of us alive now. Future people therefore massively outnumber us, and the expected value of nearly any choice we make is dominated by its effects on them rather than anyone alive today. Since we can't predict what will actually matter centuries from now, the practical upshot is to focus on preventing human extinction, and to care about other things only insofar as they lower extinction risk; present-day problems like global poverty or climate change are, in expectation, morally negligable compared to the far future.

I'm inclined to accept the starting thought — the numbers really do seem lopsided — but the claim that present-day stuff is "morally negligable" feels like something got lost along the way, and I don't see why extinction would be the only lever worth pulling. Is this a fair distillation of the actual view? Could you separate what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what a defender of strong longtermism would say is just wrong, and then say how much of the practical upshot actually survives once the correction is made?
