I've become pretty convinced by the epiphenomenalist line: the physical world is causally closed, every bit of behaviour seems to have a sufficient neural cause, and phenomenal qualities never show up in that causal story. But I also can't shake the conviction that when I say "that toothache is sharp, on the left, and bad," the felt toothache is part of why I say exactly that — and not just part of what makes the sentence true. If qualia are truly inert, then my phenomenal judgments get produced entirely by neural machinery that would have produced the very same judgments even if the feelings had been absent or different. That leaves me with a physical system reliably emitting accurate reports about a domain that never once feeds back into the system, which feels like an enormous coincidence and makes my knowledge of my own experience look like a fluke. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict: what would it take to debunk and drop the intuition without changing the subject, what would it take to amend or abandon epiphenomenalism (and would that force giving up on causal closure), and is there a reading on which the pain explains my judgment without causing it — and if so, what notion of explanation is doing the work? Spell out the cost of each option.
