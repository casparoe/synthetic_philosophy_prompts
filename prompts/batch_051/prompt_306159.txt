I keep getting stuck on the deplatforming/cancel-culture debates because the two sides seem to be running different unstated theories of what the practice even is. One side says it's not punishment at all, just private association — nobody owes anybody a platform. The same people then describe it as accountability, consequences for bad behavior, making an example of someone. The other side calls it a mob, but then complains about the lack of due process, which is a weird complaint if it's "just" employers and platforms declining to associate. I'd like you to take the actually-existing practice as the object: pile-ons, platform bans, withdrawn invitations and book deals, demanded apologies, the fact that it has no fixed procedure, no proportionality check, and no one says when its over. What theory of punishment or censure does this practice presuppose? I mean that precisely: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Go through the candidate theories — retributive, deterrent/incapacitation, expressive, restorative, and maybe a Becker-style social-sanction account — and say what each explains and what each can't explain about the practice as it actually operates. Then say whether the practice is internally consistent, and what would have to change operationally if we committed to one candidate theory for real (if it's deterrence, you'd need some notion of proportionality and of when the threat is over; if it's expressive, you'd need to say what message survives a pile-on with no procedure).

Act as someone who has done serious work in philosophy of punishment and the social/free-speech literature. Think carefully and brainstorm two or three candidate reconstructions before settling on the best one. Write it up as prose, then after the writeup give me a JSON object with this schema: candidate_theories, an array of objects with fields theory (short name), status (whether the practice strictly presupposes it or is merely consistent with it), features_explained (which observed features it makes sense of), features_it_cant_explain (which observed features it fails on), operational_changes (what would change if the practice were run consistently on this theory); and verdict, an object with fields most_consistent_single_theory (best single reconstruction or null), main_internal_inconsistency (the biggest tension), and most_underdetermined_question (where the practice doesn't pick a theory).
