I am drawn to a strict view of promising. On this view, to promise to meet at seven is to bind yourself to the arrival itself, not merely to a good-faith effort or to compensation for any inconvenience; if a delayed train makes you ten minutes late, the promise has already been broken, and the delay bears only on blame and repair, not on whether there was a breach. This seems right to me because it honors what it is to commit to a determinate act, and because weakening the principle to track fault threatens to turn every small broken promise into a dispute over excuses.

But I also have a firm intuition I cannot shake: a friend who is briefly late because of a subway stoppage has kept faith with me in a way a friend who leaves late out of indifference has not, and I would not say the first friend broke a promise at all. I don't want a verdict. Give me the strongest version of each side, and then say what would justify each of these moves: debunking the intuition and dropping it; amending or abandoning the principle; or keeping both by distinguishing breach from blameworthiness, or by treating the content of the promise as already including the ordinary background of unavoidable delay. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first item on the strongest case for the principle, the second on the strongest case for the intuition, and one item on each of the three moves.
