I co-chair a working group inside a frontier artificial-intelligence laboratory whose task is to give our board a single document explaining why, on our current trajectory, otherwise unsentimental actors may end up in costly conflict — military conflict, reciprocal sabotage, or the breakdown of safety coordination — over the development and deployment of general-purpose AI systems. The group contains two factions that both accept Fearon's rationalist account of war: the baseline position that fighting is ex post inefficient, and that even rivals with opposed interests share an incentive to locate some Pareto-superior bargain rather than fight, provided information is symmetric and promises are credible. They have nonetheless argued for a year about which departure from that baseline does the work when states or laboratories race.

The first faction holds that the race is at root an information problem. Players conceal capabilities, training progress, and the military usefulness of their systems; each has incentives to misrepresent its strength and its willingness to deploy; and because they cannot locate a mutually acceptable bargain on restrictions or de-escalation, they choose costly competition that no one privately prefers to a workable agreement.

The second faction holds that an information problem would be bargainable away, but an AI race presents a deeper commitment problem. Even a player who sincerely signs an agreement not to weaponize, not to train past some threshold, or not to deploy in ways that could trigger conflict cannot credibly bind itself, its successors, or its contractors once it has acquired a decisive capability. The capability itself changes the bargaining range, and no outside party can verify or enforce compliance over model weights and their copies. The two factions also disagree about whether the apparent indivisibility of a decisive algorithm — a capability that cannot usefully be split between competitors — is a genuine third mechanism or merely a special case of commitment failure.

I have become personally involved because my own team is now deciding whether to publish a training method that would be impossible to claw back, and the question of which mechanism best explains our situation is no longer academic for me.

Please draft the joint statement this working group should be able to sign. I want it to include: the claims both factions can accept about AI races under Fearon's framework; the precise points where they part company, each position phrased as its own proponents would accept it; the conceptual or empirical cruxes such that settling one would move at least one faction; and for each crux, a concrete case, historical analogy, or future observation both factions would accept as probative, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please attend especially to whether the information/commitment dispute is substantive or partly verbal — for example, whether information about a player's type is merely the mirror image of a commitment about its future choice — and say so if some portion of the disagreement dissolves under closer wording. The final text should be written so that neither faction feels translated into a straw man, and the residual disagreement stated exactly enough that the board can see what evidence would close it. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
