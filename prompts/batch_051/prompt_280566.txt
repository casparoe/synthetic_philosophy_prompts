You are an expert philosopher of action and decision theory with a strong practical interest in pedagogy, especially in designing classroom experiences that force students to confront conceptual tensions rather than merely hear about them. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar, about 12 students, most of whom have had one prior course in formal epistemology or decision theory. We have a 75-minute session. I want to spend it on a live version of a puzzle about practical deliberation and self-prediction.

The puzzle is this. When you are genuinely deliberating about whether to do A or B, there seems to be something odd about simultaneously assigning a probability to the proposition that you will do A. A high probability seems to undermine your sense that B is a still-live option, and a probability of 0.5 seems to describe you as if you were watching yourself from outside rather than deciding. More strongly, one might think the stance of predicting your own choice and the stance of making the choice cannot both be fully occupied at the same time: deliberation crowds out prediction, or at least makes certain sorts of self-assessments rationally unavailable. Some philosophers think this shows that rational agents cannot have well-defined credences about their own future free actions while they are in the business of choosing them; others think the tension is only pragmatic or psychological.

Please design a 75-minute in-class activity for that session that makes the students feel this tension directly, rather than first hearing the puzzle named. I want them to run into the problem themselves: they should actually try to predict their own choice, with real (small) stakes, and then actually choose, under rules arranged so that the prediction and the choice collide. You may use small stakes like candy, coffee vouchers, or points on an upcoming quiz, but no real money is necessary. Include the following:

1. A short opening setup: materials, roles, and the exact instructions given to students. The instructions should not mention deliberation, prediction, or probability as philosophical topics; they should just set up the task.
2. A timeline, with rough minute allocations, including a prediction phase, a choice phase, and a debrief.
3. The payoff structure, explained precisely enough that a stranger could run it, and designed so that the tension between treating one's own choice as evidence and treating it as up to oneself becomes salient.
4. A debrief section that maps what happened in the activity onto the conceptual distinction between the deliberative stance and the predictive stance, and onto the idea that assigning probabilities to one's own choices while deliberating may be rationally unstable.
5. A frank note on what will predictably go wrong on a first run, and how to handle it—for example, students refusing to take the prediction task seriously, students gaming the payoff scheme, or the tension failing to register because the stakes are too low.
6. An honest statement of which feature of the underlying idea the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator in the debrief.

Before you write the full activity, please do some preliminary brainstorming: think through two or three candidate designs, evaluate which one most reliably produces the collision between prediction and choice, and then commit to the strongest one. Then present the activity in a clean, runnable form. Aim for a design that would work in a real classroom, not a merely illustrative sketch.
