I'm a PhD student in legal philosophy, mostly writting on desuetude — the idea that a law can effectively lapse when nobody enforces it for ages. My supervisor has a really specific track record of verdicts on hypotheticals about old laws, and I'm trying to work out what principle she's actually running. I'd be keen to get your read.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A 40-year-old by-law making it an offence to tell fortunes for money, never enforced in living memory and treated by everyone as a dead letter. Police suddenly prosecute a fortune teller, with no warning. She says: improper.
2. A fire-safety inspection by-law for guesthouses that had gone unenforced for years. After a fatal fire, the council runs a three-month publicity campaign and tells owners inspections are restarting, then enforces the rules evenly. She says: permissible.
3. A Sunday trading law dormant for 25 years, suddenly enforced against one corner shop after a rival reports it. She says: improper.
4. A permit requirement for importing certain birds, unenforced for 20 years. The importer had demonstrably built their business around the belief it wouldn't be enforced. No warning, then prosecution. She says: improper.

Next week's case is this: a 1920s by-law making it an offence to tell fortunes for money, never enforced in living memory. The council decides to resurrect it, not because of any new harm or complaints, but as part of a 'tidy up the statute book' project. It gives six months' notice, runs a public information campaign, and promises to enforce uniformly. A fortune teller is prosecuted after the notice period.

Predict her verdict. I can see at least two principles she might be running: (a) a dead-letter law can be revived by officials as long as they give fair prospective notice and don't pick on people, or (b) fair notice is necessary but not sufficient, because a law that has genuinely lapsed through non-enforcement and public indifference needs the legislature, or at least some real new public interest, to bring it back to life — executive say-so isn't enough. Say which fits her track record better and why, and describe one further case, different from this one, that would cleanly settle which of the two rules she is actually using. Also say whether you think the new case is really about desuetude proper or about something adjacent like abuse of discretion or fair notice.

Please write the answer in British English, by the way — its for my notes and I'd like the spelling to match.
