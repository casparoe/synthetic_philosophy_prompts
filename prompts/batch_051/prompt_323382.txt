I convene a working group on collective epistemology, and we are trying to settle — or at least state cleanly — a disagreement that has split us into two camps over the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation. The first camp favors premise-based aggregation: members vote on the underlying premises, and the collective conclusion is whatever follows from the majority-supported premises, so that the group remains consistent and its decision stays tied to reasons. The second camp favors conclusion-based aggregation, at least for courts and similarly tasked bodies: aggregate only on the conclusion, because premise-based voting can commit a group to an outcome that no member individually supports and that the body was not asked to decide. Both sides accept the canonical three-judge example, in which a majority accepts each of two premises while a majority rejects the conclusion that follows from them because only one judge accepts both; both sides also accept the broad impossibility results and agree that majority voting on an entire agenda need not produce a consistent collective judgment. They remain stuck on whether a collective body should be modeled as a reasoner or as a decider, and on what propositionwise majority should have to respect.

Please draft the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two camps would eventually produce. Structure it as follows: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about individual rationality, the paradox itself, what the impossibility results rule out, and the minimum constraints any admissible aggregation procedure must meet; (2) a disagreements table giving each side's positive view in wording that side would accept, on at least three points where they genuinely diverge; (3) a crux section identifying the subquestions such that settling them would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or formal result both sides would accept as bearing on it — for example, a modified court with an abstention or a premise whose truth is private information — with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Keep the agreed language precise enough that neither side is signing a strawman, and flag any part of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the claims you put in each camp's mouth, and single out the one part of the document you are least sure about.
