Here's something I keep tripping over. A doctor treating a patient in the ordinary way is expected—ethically and legally—to give the treatment she judges best on the evidence. If she prescribes something she honestly thinks is probably worse, we call that practising badly. Yet put the same doctor in a randomised controlled trial and we let her toss a coin between the treatment she thinks is probably better and the one she thinks is probably worse, and we call it ethical, so long as the expert community is genuinely divided about which is better. That, as I understand it, is the clinical equipoise story: the relevant uncertainty belongs to the community of expert clinicians, not to the individual doctor at the bedside.

I can't see wether the community's uncertainty should give an individual doctor permission to act against her own best clinical judgement. Is there a principle that genuinely separates these two cases, or is clinical equipoise just a compromise we've been dressing up as an ethical insight? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case where the doctor is 70/30 convinced drug A is better and still randomises the next patient she sees. If you can't defend it, tell me what rule for enrolling patients in trials you would put in its place.
