At a reading group on anti-realism, two members got into an argument about Fitch's paradox, and neither would concede. (1) Elena set out the standard result. Take any truth q that no one ever knows. The conjunction 'q and no one ever knows q' is then also a truth. The knowability principle says every truth can in principle be known, so that conjunction can be known. Now suppose someone knows it: she then knows q, and she knows that no one ever knows q; by the factivity of knowledge, if she knows that no one ever knows q, then no one ever knows q — yet she herself knows q. Contradiction. So the conjunction cannot be known after all; hence, Elena concluded, the knowability principle collapses into the claim that every truth is known. (2) Marcus replied that the whole argument is a trick with a Moorean construction: 'q and no one ever knows q' cannot coherently be asserted or verified, so a verificationist was never committed to its being knowable in the first place, and the principle was never meant to range over such sentences. (3) Elena answered that the principle is stated over all truths, and if q really is an unknown truth, the conjunction really is a truth; to exclude it Marcus needs a principled criterion for which truths fall within the principle's scope, and until he gives one his restriction is ad hoc. (4) Marcus then said the proof leans on classical logic — specifically the move from 'suppose it is known, then contradiction' to 'so it cannot be known.' A verificationist who works in intuitionistic logic can block the result there. Elena replied that the contradiction uses only the distribution of knowledge over conjunction and the factivity of knowledge, and that deriving a negation from a contradiction is intuitionistically acceptable, so the classical-logic escape fails.
