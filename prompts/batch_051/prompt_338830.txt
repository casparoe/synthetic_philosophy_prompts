I was up late last night arguing with a friend who has strong consequentialist instincts, and the conversation kept circling around the treatment of virtue in chapter 4 of Mill's Utilitarianism. He insisted that Mill gives the right shape of the answer: virtues such as honesty, courage, and loyalty begin as means to happiness, become desired for themselves through association, and thereby become part of happiness, but they never acquire an independent authority that could outweigh the general happiness. I found myself neither convinced nor sure where I disagree, because my own reactions to cases pull in both directions. I would like you to help me work out what principle, if any, captures my verdicts.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) A colleague's grant proposal has a weak section, but I know from years of collaboration that she is punctiliously honest; I trust her on that section more than the written evidence alone would justify, and I think this is reasonable. (2) A mid-level employee discovers systematic fraud at his company. Reporting it will likely get him fired and may not, on balance, produce more good than harm once his own life and his family's are counted; I still think he has a duty to report. (3) I tell a friend a small falsehood to keep a surprise party secret; I think this is fine, and that truthfulness does not have absolute force. (4) A protester knowingly charges at a line of riot police in a demonstration that will be immediately suppressed; her courage is admirable even though it has no prospect of changing anything. (5) A judge refuses a bribe to acquit a guilty defendant in a case where the bribe would pay for life-saving surgery for the judge's child and the acquittal would not materially increase public corruption beyond what already exists. I think the judge should refuse, but I am not sure whether this is because honesty is independently demanded or because of institutional consequences I cannot fully specify.

My friend says I am being inconsistent: I treat truthfulness as merely instrumental in the surprise-party case but as independent in the whistleblower case, and I treat courage as valuable apart from consequences in the protester case but, he claims, I would not extend the same reasoning to a reckless gambler's nerve. I do not know whether one stable principle lies behind these verdicts or whether I must give some of them up. Please do not just gesture at virtue ethics or pluralism; I want a precise candidate principle, or a small set of candidates, stated so that it actually yields verdicts on the cases, including which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Also apply the Millian passage to my cases: does the move of saying that virtue becomes part of happiness survive a distinction between something's being valued for its own sake and something's being a source of reasons independent of consequences, or does it collapse once I insist on that distinction? I would like your answer as a JSON object with the following fields: candidate_principles (an array, each element with label, statement, motivating_case, verdicts_on_my_cases (an object mapping case numbers 1 through 5 to match, overturns, or does_not_speak), and further_case_verdict (a verdict on a sixth case of your own devising that tests the principle)); best_candidate (the label you would defend); least_secure_of_my_verdicts (which of my verdicts is hardest to keep and why); and mill_verdict (whether the Millian account can or cannot accommodate the best principle, in two or three sentences).
