I have been trying to work out how a speaker can communicate something without its being part of the sentence's literal meaning, and I have reached firm verdicts about the following cases. My question is whether any single principle captures all of them, and if not, which of my verdicts I should abandon and what the right account says about a further case.

(1) A teacher asked whether everyone passed the exam replies, "Some of my students passed." I take her to convey that not all of them passed, even though the sentence would be literally true if all had. But if she were asked, "Did anyone pass?", I would not take the same sentence to convey that not all passed.

(2) In the middle of a downpour, a friend says, "Excellent weather for a picnic." I take him to convey that the weather is terrible, and I would not call him a liar, because we both know he means the opposite of what the words say.

(3) When I ask a friend whether he is coming to my party and he answers, "I have an exam tomorrow," I take him to convey that he is not coming.

(4) A teacher asked to recommend a student for a lab position writes only, "Jordan is punctual and always dresses neatly." I take the teacher to convey that Jordan is not qualified for the position, although nothing said is false.

(5) The exact-number implication of "Marcus ate four slices" can be canceled: "Marcus ate four slices—maybe five" does not convey four exactly. But the contrast conveyed by "The car is old but reliable" seems resistant to cancellation, since adding "and I do not mean to suggest any connection between age and reliability" barely removes it.

The asymmetry in (5) and the question-sensitivity in (1) trouble me. Is there one principle behind all of these, or should I stop expecting one? If the answer is that these fall into different kinds of conveyed content, what exactly is the dividing line, and which of my verdicts would be overturned? Please also say what your account predicts about this further case: someone asked "Are you coming to the party?" replies, "I have an exam tomorrow—but I will be there anyway." Does the not-coming signal arise and then get canceled, or does it never arise?
