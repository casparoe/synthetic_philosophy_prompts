Last night a friend and I spent the evening arguing about whether the inside/outside-view distinction can actually tell you how modest to be, and by the end I had talked myself into a construct I want pressure-tested. I call it the Principle of Recursive Modesty. The usual advice is: when your case-specific estimate is more optimistic than the base rate for similar cases, revise toward the base rate. But the advice is silent on the two places the real work happens — which reference class to use, and how far to move — because any answer to those is itself a case-specific judgment. My principle says that an outside view is binding on an inside estimate only if the judgment that places the case in that reference class would itself survive the same outside-view treatment. Modesty, on this account, is not a one-time discount applied to self-confidence; it is the requirement to audit recursively each local judgment, including the judgment to defer, until the audit reaches a stable fixed point rather than oscillating or regressing. I think this gives a principled stop condition for how much outside correction is enough, and it explains why planning-fallacy cases are compelling (the membership judgment, "my project is like the reference class of similar projects," is robust under recursive audit) without forcing a blanket deference that would make every case-specific insight inadmissible.

My friend thinks the principle is either a confused restatement of Bayesian priors or a regress that never discharges the original inside judgment; I think it is neither, but I cannot yet say exactly why. Is the principle coherent enough to evaluate, or does it fail at the definition? If it is coherent, give me the three strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. And if I have reinvented a familiar idea under a new name, I would rather know that than keep calling it mine.
