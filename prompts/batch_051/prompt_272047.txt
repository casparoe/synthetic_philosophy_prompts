A graduate reading group I lead is working through self-locating belief and the Sleeping Beauty problem, and I want to find out what each member actually believes before the labels come out. Construct a ten-question sorting quiz, with a scoring key, that infers people's positions entirely from concrete choices about credences, announcements, and bets in variations of the protocol. No question may ask anyone to identify with a named view.

Every question should state its variant in full, so a person who has read only the original problem can answer without recognizing a named variant. Work through at least these cases: the standard Monday–Tuesday protocol with memory erasure; a truthful announcement that it is now Monday; a complementary case in which it is announced that it is now Tuesday (as a consistency check); a generalized protocol in which Tails produces k consecutive waking days while Heads produces one; a bet on Heads offered at each waking and paid at each waking; and a single bet on Heads offered before the experiment and paid only after it ends. For each case, ask for a committed credence or a specific accept-or-reject decision at stated odds. Provide two or three concrete answer options, with an additional "no single number; it depends on the goal or reference class" option where the deflationary position needs room to show.

The scoring key should map each answer pattern onto at least these positions: (i) the thirder, who gives P(Heads) = 1/3 on waking and P(Heads) = 1/2 after the Monday announcement; (ii) the standard halfer, who gives P(Heads) = 1/2 on waking and P(Heads) = 2/3 after the Monday announcement; (iii) the double-halfer, who keeps P(Heads) = 1/2 both on waking and after the Monday announcement; (iv) the hybrid or compartmentalized position, which separates the doxastic credence in the coin toss from the value that governs repeated per-awakening betting; and (v) a deflationary position, which denies that there is a unique rational credence independent of a specified question, goal, or reference class.

Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from the list of names. Work out the axes on which these leaves come apart — what the Monday announcement and the k-awakening generalization do to one's answer, and whether the two betting cases pull toward different numbers — and choose questions that place a respondent along those axes. In the scoring key, identify answer combinations that correspond to no stable position and briefly say why the cell is empty: because the pattern is incoherent, unattractive, or merely an unexplored compromise. Finally, flag any pair of positions the ten questions cannot fully separate, and name the additional case that would separate them if the quiz had an eleventh slot. The quiz is a diagnostic instrument, not an argument for any one camp.
