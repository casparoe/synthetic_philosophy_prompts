I have been defending a broadly deflationary account of thought experiments, and I want help adjudicating a clash between that account and an intuition I cannot shake. On the deflationary view, a thought experiment is an argument in narrative dress: whatever it seems to reveal is already latent in the principles, classifications, and inferential habits one brings to the imagined case. The case can make those commitments vivid, expose equivocations, or flush out an unnoticed premise, but it cannot serve as an independent source of evidence. I find this view compelling for the usual systematic reasons: it demystifies the armchair, it predicts that people with different background theories will report different verdicts, and it spares us the picture of philosophers collecting data in a private mental theatre.

Against it stands a recurring first-person experience, which I can now make precise. Consider two philosophers, N and R, who have argued about the ethics of confidentiality for years and who accept, by every available test, exactly the same explicit principles: the same account of promising, the same account of justified disclosure, the same account of how consequences weigh. They are both given the following case. A counselor must decide whether to break a client's confidence after the client reveals a settled intention to sell a forgery to a buyer who will be financially ruined by the purchase; the client has forbidden disclosure, and the counselor knows the ruin is certain. N's only acquaintance with broken trust is a history of small, ordinary social lies. R was once deceived for years by a close collaborator in a way that led her to distrust her own sense of whom she could rely on, though she has long since recovered. After reflecting, N judges that the disclosure is probably wrong on their shared principles. R judges that it is obviously right on those same principles, and neither can cite any consideration the other rejects or lacks. My intuition is that R's history does not merely bias her: it gives her judgment of this imagined case an epistemic edge, rather as an experienced clinician's sense of a described patient can carry weight over a novice's even when both can recite the same textbook chapter. But that intuition sits badly with the deflationary view. If the imagined case only applies principles both philosophers already hold, then two people with identical principles should have identical reasons for judging it; R's history should be irrelevant once the principles coincide, and my lingering sense that she is better placed should be dismissed as sentimentality about biography.

I am not asking you to settle the first-order question about the counselor. I am asking you to settle the methodological one: what should yield here, the deflationary account or the intuition that R's experience has evidential weight? I can see three families of resolution, and I ask you to develop the strongest version of each before giving your own verdict. Perhaps the intuition should be debunked: R's history is a source of distortion, and the point of a thought experiment is precisely to strip such history away and force both parties back onto their shared principles. Perhaps the deflationary view should be amended: a thought experiment can be a device through which experiences too fine-grained to be formulated as principles are legitimately brought to bear on a question, and that is evidence in an ordinary and respectable sense. Perhaps both survive under a sharper reading of evidence: the case gives R no new propositional evidence, but it makes salient something she already inchoately knew, and I am mistaking that new salience for evidence. I would like a negotiated verdict. What would justify each of these moves, what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and is the third reading stable or merely evasive? Please reply in continuous prose, as you would write a thoughtful letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
