I've got a little theory I've been kicking around about which jobs survive automation with their meaning intact, and I want it stress-tested. I call it the grip theory of meaningful work. The core idea: work is meaningful to the degree it gives you grip over the process, where grip is a package of three things — (1) legibility (you can tell, in real time, whether it's going well), (2) leverage (the next move you make can actually make it go better), and (3) revisability (you can see how your moves changed things and adjust). Meaning, on this theory, doesn't come from the output mattering, or from effort, or from being paid, though those might correlate with it. Automation destroys meaning exactly when it takes away the grip, not when it takes away the toil.

A radiologist who reads a scan has grip; a radiologist who just co-signs an AI read and couldn't say where the AI tends to go wrong doesn't, even if patients do just as well. A barista pulling shots has grip; a person jabbing a button on a superauto machine doesn't, even if the coffee is more consistent. The theory makes a further prediction: automation that leaves you with second-order grip — tweaking, overriding, tuning, auditing the machine's failures — will preserve meaning even when the first-order task disappears, while automation that quietly does the thing for you will gut the meaning even if it keeps flattering you that you're still "in the loop."

Tell me honestly whether this holds up. What are the three strongest objections, and is any of them fatal rather than just uncomfortable? I know about self-determination theory and Marx on alienation; is my grip thing actually just autonomy plus competence with a tool metaphor, or does the real-time coupling part do new work? And give me the clearest counterexample: either something with lots of grip that's still miserable, or something with almost no grip that people find deeply meaningfull. Also say what would actually change your mind about the theory overall — which objection, if it landed, would flip your assessment, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
