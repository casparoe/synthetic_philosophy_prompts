I'm reading part of Aristotle's Politics, and there's a bit in Book I where he says that some people are naturally suited to be ruled rather than to rule, and he grounds it in a comparison with the soul ruling the body. The translation I've got puts it like this: he says the soul rules the body with the sway of a master, while the intellect rules the appetites with a statesman's or king's rule, and then he says the same must apply in the case of mankind generally, so that those who differ as widely as the soul differs from the body and the human from the lower animal "are by nature slaves, for whom to be governed by this kind of authority is advantageous." Then he adds that a natural slave is someone who "participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it." I want to know how much work these lines can really do for him, because my lecturer keeps saying this isn't just Aristotle being a man of his time but follows from his actual metaphysics, and that modern defenders of natural hierarchy have to smuggle in something like this too. Is that fair? Specifically, does the passage imply that if a person *is* capable of full deliberative reasoning then it would be unnatural for them to be enslaved, and would Aristotle's own criteria, applied consistently, have ruled out most of the chattel slavery that later thinkers attributed to his authority? Or is the "apprehend but not possess" line vague enough that a society can just *claim* the people it enslaves are like that, and the theory has no resources to check them? I'm less interested in whether Aristotle was a bad guy by modern standards and more in whether the argument he actually gives has an internal standard that could be turned against the institution he's defending. And please write the answer in British English.
