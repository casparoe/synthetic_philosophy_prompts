Two of my colleagues — I will call them Emerson and Kaur — have been arguing, on and off for months, about whether a destructive act inside a shared virtual world can be a genuine harm, and whether the objects destroyed are real enough to be the subjects of that harm. The argument resurfaced at our last colloquium, and I left unable to referee it. I am writing it up here as faithfully as I can, because I would like a committed adjudication of what was actually said.

The case they keep returning to is this. A group of players spent several years building a settlement inside a long-running simulation: houses, a working economy, a library of player-written texts, and a memorial garden dedicated to a member of the group who had died. A newcomer exploited a permissions flaw and deleted the entire settlement in an afternoon. The operators restored a six-month-old backup, so most of the settlement returned, but six months of additions and the memorial in its final form were gone. No real-world money changed hands, and none of the players had met offline.

Emerson says the deletion was a real harm to the players, and the settlement was a real object whose loss that harm consists in. As Emerson put it: "The fact that the settlement was stored on a server no more makes it unreal than the fact that a village's boundaries are lines on a map makes the village unreal. The players did not treat the settlement as a fiction or a revocable license; they treated it as a place they had made together, a repository of mutual recognition and memory. When someone destroys the thing a community has built its ongoing agency around, the community is harmed by the loss of that thing, not merely by the feelings the loss happens to produce. The memorial had exactly the normative function a physical grave marker has: it was where the group went to remember their dead friend. Vandalizing it is wrong in the way desecrating a grave is wrong, even if the materials happen to be a stored pattern rather than stone."

Kaur is unpersuaded. "Those analogies borrow their force from features the virtual case lacks. A grave is a site of a body and of a community's rites; obligations to the dead and to the mourning attach to that site in ways that are not reproduced by a set of records the operators can roll back with a patch. And within a game, what a player 'owns' is at every moment a license inside the operator's rules, revocable and alterable by someone else's code. So the newcomer's act is a nasty breach of the platform's terms and of the group's own house rules — a wrong of disregard and disrespect toward the other players — but it is not the destruction of a thing. And the players' distress, while real as an emotion, is not yet the same as being harmed."

Emerson replies: "Revocability by the platform shows only who holds power over the object, not whether the object exists. A sovereign can revoke a law, but the law is real while it stands; the players relied on the settlement in exactly the practical ways we rely on laws and neighborhoods. What made it a real object was the network of standing expectations, joint intentions, and recognition in which it figured. A harm need not be felt in order to occur: a writer whose only manuscript has been deleted is harmed before she learns of it, because a capacity she had reason to value has been destroyed. These players lost their capacity to return to, add to, and be recognized in a place they had made. That is a real loss, and calling it 'distress' scants it."

Kaur responds: "The manuscript case is not the same. Its loss destroys the capacity ever to reconstruct the work, whereas the backup restored the settlement, and the group retained both the memory and the skill to rebuild the missing six months. If there is a real loss here, I want to know what exactly its object is supposed to be — the missing increments? The final arrangement of the memorial? That is the wrong vocabulary. The newcomer did wrong, but the wrong is fully captured by saying he callously disregarded the players' projects and the rules of a community he had voluntarily joined. We do not need a brute 'virtual harm' or a 'virtual object' to earn the condemnation; adding them confuses a perfectly adequate story with ontology we have no reason to accept."

Emerson closes with a rejoinder: "The claim that the wrong is 'fully captured' by disregard is precisely what is in dispute. If every loss of a made thing can be redescribed as an interruption to someone's projects, then we could say the same of a fire that destroys a city archive: the harm was never the archive, only the interrupted practice. But at some point the lost thing is the harm — the archive mattered to those who made and used it, and that is the texture of moral life everywhere else. The settlement was that kind of made thing. The database entry is not the object; the maintained, jointly recognized settlement was, and deleting it was vandalism."

I see substance in both positions, and I honestly cannot say which of them is more right overall. Please adjudicate this disagreement as it was actually made: identify which of Emerson's claims are right, which of Kaur's claims are right, what both of them are missing, and state who is more right overall. I want a committed verdict — take a definite position, give the reasons for it, and do not settle for merely laying the options out.
