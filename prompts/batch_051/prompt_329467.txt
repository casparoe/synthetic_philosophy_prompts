Give me a working definition of emergence—or, if you think no single definition will do, give me two sharply distinguished senses of the term and say which cases each one covers. By a working definition I mean one explicit enough that a newcomer could apply it to a ninth case without first having to guess your philosophical sympathies. The definition should separate emergence from the mere fact that a whole has a property its parts lack, and it should not quietly reduce to unpredictability in practice unless you are prepared to defend that reduction.

The cases: (1) the temperature of a gas; (2) the total mass of a pile of sand; (3) the wetness of liquid water; (4) the backward-propagating wave in a highway traffic jam, where every car is moving forward; (5) the hexagonal convection cells that appear in a thin layer of liquid heated uniformly from below, known as Bénard cells; (6) the glider that moves across the grid in Conway's Game of Life; (7) the salty taste of table salt, given that neither a sodium atom nor a chlorine atom is salty; (8) conscious experience in a drowsy person.

My starting judgments, which you are free to overturn if the definition justifies it: I take (1) and (5) as clear cases of emergence and (2) as clearly not emergence. I am unsure about (4), (6), and (7). I decline to commit on (8), because I cannot tell whether it shows that a strong notion of emergence is needed or merely how far I am from understanding how experience arises.

Run your definition through all eight cases. Say where it surprises you, revise it once if any verdict looks ad hoc, and then identify which of the eight final verdicts you are least confident in and explain why.
