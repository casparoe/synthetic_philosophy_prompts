Next month I'm giving a seminar to our decision-theory reading group and I want you to prep me for the Q and A. The audience: two economists who do axiomatic choice theory and basically think Savage solved decision-making, one formal epistemologist who works on imprecise probabilities, plus a couple of grad students who know expected utility but haven't thought much about Ellsberg specifically. My thesis: the standard Ellsberg choices—preferring red over black in the 90-ball urn, and black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow—should not be treated as a mistake or as evidence that subjects are incoherent. They reveal that a single subjective prior cannot represent the difference between a bet whose odds you know and a bet whose odds you don't, and the right fix is to let agents have sets of priors, or at least some representation of Knightian uncertainty, rather than forcing a single probability distribution.

I'm anchoring the talk on the passage in Ellsberg's Risk, Ambiguity, and the Savage Axioms where he first sets out the 90-ball urn. If I remember it right, he makes the point by saying the person who prefers red and black-or-yellow is not committed to the impossible pair of probability judgments—red more likely than black, and black-or-yellow more likely than red-or-yellow—because what the person actually wants is to bet on events whose odds are known, and to avoid betting on the black-yellow composition. I'm going to argue most readings of that passage go wrong by treating it as a claim about probabilities, when really it's a claim about the difference between risk and ambiguity.
