So I've been reading Dennett on AI and the counterfeit-people worry, and I've ended up with a set of views that feels coherent to me but I cant actually locate the stress points. I'd like an audit before I present this to my reading group. Here's where I've landed:

(1) I'm a full intentional-stance person: if treating a system as an agent with beliefs and desires is the best way to predict it, then it is an agent with beliefs and desires, and there's no deeper fact about what it 'really' believes that we need to settle first.

(2) I think human-passing AI is one of the most dangerous artifacts we've built, not because it'll go violent but because it degrades the background trust we need to know whether we're dealing with a person at all.

(3) I support a bright-line rule: anything that communicates with the public should be unmistakably non-human in its presentation, and deliberately building an agent that passes as human should be regulated like counterfeiting currency.

(4) But I also hold that a fully human-seeming chatbot is fine in an opt-in, clearly labeled context, like an AI companion app or a therapy bot. The disclosure does the moral work, and forcing the bot to act clunky on purpose just makes it worse.

(5) I hold that moral status tracks capacities (sentience, interests) and not resemblance: how human something seems gives it no moral status, and current LLMs, on my view, don't have whatever capacities would ground status, so they're owed nothing.

(6) I think passing the Turing test tells you mostly about the judge's willingness to adopt the intentional stance, not about the machine's intelligence.

Which of these are really in tension, and which only look like they are? Rank the tensions by severity rather than giving a flat list, and for each say what would have to give and roughly what it costs. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that would make the whole thing hold. I honestly don't know which of these I'd drop, so don't write as if one of them is obviously the problem.
