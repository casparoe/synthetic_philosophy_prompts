Our local history society has a bylaw, written in 1961, that says: "No statement about the town's past shall be entered into the official history unless it is supported by two independent witnesses, or by one witness who was in a position to know and had no reason to misrepresent." The rule was meant for when history meant letters, diaries, and talking to people's grandparents. But now a member used a computer program to reconstruct the old brick chapel that burned in 1934, feeding it a single blurry 1911 photograph and the measurements of the surviving wall. The program says the chapel almost certainly had two east doors, not one, and that if a second door had been there, a beam above it would have shown up in the photo. Nobody alive saw the chapel, and no document mentions its doors either way. Under the bylaw, can we put "the chapel had two east doors" in the official history? Argue it three ways: from the plain words of the bylaw, from the purpose of the rule, and from what the people who wrote it in 1961 would have said if theyd been asked about a machine that can see what isnt in a photo. Then tell me which approach should win and why, and whether it matters that the program is only "almost certain."
