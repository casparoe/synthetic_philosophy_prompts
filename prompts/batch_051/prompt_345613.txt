I'm a conservation ecologist comfortable with models of habitat fragmentation, source–sink dynamics, density-dependent regulation, and ecosystem services, but I have only a passing familiarity with animal-welfare philosophy. Here is a passage from Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species, Chapter III, "Struggle for Existence":

"We behold the face of nature bright with gladness, we often see superabundance of food; we do not see, or we forget, that the birds which are idly singing round us mostly live on insects or seeds, and are thus constantly destroying life; or we forget how largely these songsters, or their eggs, or their nestlings, are destroyed by birds and beasts of prey; we do not always bear in mind, that though food may be now superabundant, it is not so at all seasons of each recurring year."

Restate the view of nature in this passage in the vocabulary I work in—habitat quality, carrying capacity, trophic interactions, r/K strategies, ecosystem services, restoration baselines—as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts of Darwin's picture map cleanly onto my ecological categories, which parts have to be distorted to fit, and which have no counterpart at all. In particular, when people in the welfare-biology debate claim that destroying habitat can reduce wild animal suffering because it prevents the existence of animals that would otherwise die quickly, starve, or be eaten, is that a claim my vocabulary can state, or does it require importing a welfare framework that ecology does not contain? I want to know whether the disagreement between conservation's default view that intact habitats are good and the welfare-biological view that they are full of suffering is a scientific disagreement about the facts or an evaluative disagreement that ecology alone cannot adjudicate.
