A friend and I argued late into the evening about a point in Galois theory, and I still cannot state cleanly where we disagreed. He kept saying that the labels don't matter. The case we kept returning to was a polynomial with rational coefficients, say x² − 2, and the two roots it has over Q. I objected that when one writes √2 and −√2, the notation appears to name the two solutions, but the field Q(√2) cannot itself tell them apart: the two roots satisfy exactly the same equations, and there is a field automorphism exchanging them. He replied that this was precisely his point. The labeling is arbitrary; either choice gives an isomorphic field; and any true statement about the elements of one labeled version can be translated into a true statement about the other, so nothing mathematical changes.

I pressed him with cases where something does seem to change. The notion of the positive square root is available only after one has imported an ordering from the reals or fixed one of the two embeddings into R. Newton's method started near one root converges to one root rather than the other. And if one numbers the roots r1 through rn and forms the product of the differences ri − rj taken over all pairs with i < j, permuting the numbering can flip the sign of that product. He called all of these artifacts of notation and said that whatever is not invariant under every permutation of the roots is not a real feature of the situation. By then we were unsure whether we were still disagreeing or whether the word matter had shifted its meaning.

I would like you to disassemble the slogan that the labels don't matter as it operates in this kind of example. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making: about definability within the field, about symmetry and automorphisms, about transfer of truth between isomorphic models, about what extra structure is required before a label picks out one element rather than another, and about whether label-dependent constructions can be useful rather than merely conventional. For each claim, say whether it is trivial, defensibly true, or false, and identify which one my friend's argument actually needs. I am especially interested in whether there is a defensible reading on which the choice of labels is never mathematically significant, and whether that reading survives once one moves from the field itself to objects built over it, such as embeddings, ordered bases, or algorithms that must process the roots in some order.
