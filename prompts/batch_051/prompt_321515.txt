I have been thinking about Minneapolis 2040, the plan that ended single-family zoning citywide so that lots formerly restricted to one detached house could instead hold duplexes and triplexes. Supporters argued that single-family zoning had been a tool of racial and economic exclusion, and that allowing more homes in high-opportunity neighborhoods would give more people access to the city. Opponents, including many long-time homeowners and some neighborhood associations, argued that this would destroy the character of their neighborhoods, strain schools and utilities, and hand power to developers; some also warned that market-rate upzoning would speed up displacement in lower-income areas rather than help them.

I kept coming back to Henri Lefebvre while listening to both sides. In his book The Right to the City, as I understand him, Lefebvre argues that the right to the city is not just a right to housing or to occupy a dwelling. It is a demand to take part in urban life as someone who actively makes the city, rather than being treated as a consumer who merely buys a piece of it. He contrasts the city as an ongoing work created by its inhabitants through their daily use and activity with the city treated as a product, where space is dominated by exchange value and the need to extract profit. On his account, being pushed to the periphery is not only a geographic fact; it is a kind of exclusion from the full life of the city, and that exclusion is what the right to the city is meant to oppose.

The strange thing is that both sides of the Minneapolis fight sound as if they are invoking something like this idea. Supporters speak as if they are extending the right to the city to people who have been kept out, while opponents speak as if they are defending the right of existing inhabitants to keep the city they have made. But the two sides do not map cleanly onto Lefebvre’s categories, because the developers who would build many of the new units are hardly inhabitants trying to create the city as a shared work, and existing residents can be displaced even by policies framed as inclusion.

I want you to analyze the Minneapolis 2040 episode through the lens of Lefebvre’s idea as I have reconstructed it. What would his framework say the real conflict is, which group’s claim comes closer to the right to the city, and where does the theory fail to capture what actually happened? Does the episode show that Lefebvre’s concept is a useful tool for sorting out housing arguments, or does it show that the concept is too ambiguous because both sides can claim it?
