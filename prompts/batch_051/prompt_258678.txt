I have a policy about which mathematical existence claims I actually believe, and I suspect it is either principled constructivism or a bias wearing principle's clothes. The policy is this: when an existence proof is nonconstructive — when it derives an object from the assumption that none exists, or runs through a choice principle — I discount the claim that the object exists nearly to zero. I still accept that a sentence has been proved in a formal system, and I will happily use the result instrumentally; I just withhold the existential belief. By contrast, I grant existence readily to anything I can build or specify by an explicit rule, and I am willing to stretch construction to include a point tracing a curve in time. This matters because I read mathematics from the outside and often cannot audit proofs step by step, so I need a standing rule for where to grant the word exists.

What unsettles me is that my line keeps looking like a historical taste: the Greeks made compass-and-straightedge constructibility the test of legitimacy, early modern geometers admitted algebraic curves into the canon, later generations admitted transcendental ones, and the contemporary profession often seems willing to admit whatever a sufficiently strong formal system certifies. I do not want any era's consensus as my standard, but I also cannot do without some threshold. So: can I defend this habit as a policy for allocating belief rather than a rationalized discomfort? When does discounting nonconstructive existence claims serve me, and when does it fail? Is there a restated policy that keeps the benefits — resistance to objects conjured from a contradiction — without freezing my ontology at the intuitions of some earlier century? And what would indicate, from the inside, that I am doing philosophy of mathematics rather than protecting my gut feelings from challenge?
