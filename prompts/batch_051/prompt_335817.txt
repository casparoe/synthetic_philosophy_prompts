I'm a philosophy PhD student, and I've been banging my head against a teaching handout for an upper-level undergraduate section on moral particularism versus generalism, so I need a thought experiment constructed to a precise spec rather than another muddled adaptation of someone else's example. By "generalism" I don't just mean the boring claim that there are some true moral principles. I mean the invariant-valence view: a feature that counts in favor of an act in one situation keeps counting in favor of it in every situation, though it can be silenced or outweighed by defeaters. By "particularism" I mean the Dancy-style holist view: a feature can count in favor in one context and against in another, and no feature has a fixed moral direction across all contexts. My problem is that every time I try to write a case, it either collapses into simple weighing (the generalist just says "fine, it's outweighed") or it begs the question by redescribing the feature under dispute.

Here's the spec. Give me a minimal pair of cases, call them A and B, set in ordinary social life: no trolleys, no life-or-death stakes, no high-stakes legal or institutional settings, nothing where a rule is already doing the moral work. Pick a single feature F, spelled out exactly as both sides would describe it before the argument starts, such that in A, F counts in favor of acting, and in B, F counts against acting. I mean that literally: not that F is merely outweighed in B, but that its contribution reverses. The only difference between A and B should be a single background feature G, and G should not itself be an obvious moral consideration; otherwise the whole thing is just "an extra reason showed up." The generalist is allowed to posit defeaters, enablers, intensity modifiers, whatever, but they cannot simply redescribe F as some other feature F* unless the redescription can be motivated independently of these two cases. The particularist, for their part, should say that G changes what F contributes, not that G adds a separate reason. And don't just stipulate that F reverses; the setup should let each side's verdict follow from the view I described.

Once the pair is on the table, walk me through the reasoning for each theory: what each side says one should do in A and in B, and why each side thinks F counts as it does. Then diagnose the structure: say which feature of the pair is doing the philosophical work, what happens if you remove G—does it collapse back into a standard outweighed case?—and how a smart generalist would attack the example. The handout is meant to make undergrads feel the pull of both views, so the case cant lean on them already being sympathetic to holism.
