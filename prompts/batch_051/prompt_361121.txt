Two people in my reading group got stuck on a Lockean property hypothetical and I want a referee. The setup: a vacant lot on their block had been used for years as an informal dog run and shared garden, owned by nobody. Bev clears and fences off the flat, sunny half, tills it, and says the plot is now hers because she mixed her labour with unowned land and left the rest for the neighbours. Here's the back-and-forth as best I remember it:

(1) Ravi: Under Locke's labour theory, Bev is fine. The proviso only requires her to leave enough land, of no worse quality, for others, and she did—there's still plenty of lot left.

(2) Lena: The proviso isn't that easy to apply, because you've already built your preferred reading into it. Bev took the only flat sunny part; what's left is sloped and shaded. On a strict reading, she fails the proviso. On a looser reading, she passes. Your argument is just an assertion that we should use the looser reading.

(3) Ravi: The strict reading can't be right, because it would make any first appropriation impossible—however good the remaining land is, the piece you just took was the best of a previously common pool, so the remainder is always a little worse. Since Locke clearly wanted property to be possible, the only workable reading is weak: you can take so long as you don't leave people worse off than they were before the taking.

(4) Lena: Before her fence, everyone could use the whole lot; now they can't use the fenced half. On that baseline, they are worse of. If your baseline is instead something like 'still enough for their projects' or 'no worse off than in a world without Bev,' then you've stopped deriving the verdict from the proviso and started choosing whatever baseline gives you the answer you wanted.

(5) Ravi: Any reasonable weak reading has to pick a baseline that allows some exclusion, since unrestricted access to everything would make every appropriation a harm to everyone else. And notice you've quietly changed the subject from the labour theory to the proviso. Labour grounds Bev's claim; the proviso is only a side condition. Even if the proviso is ambiguous, that doesn't touch the basic labour claim.

(6) Lena: I haven't changed the subject—I'm showing that 'labour plus a weak proviso' isn't the view you started with. Also the labour claim itself is doing less than you think: the fertility and the sun weren't Bev's labour, so at most she owns the improvements, not the pre-existing land. If either reading of the proviso is up for grabs, then you can't say the labour theory alone gives Bev the plot.

You are a political philosopher who works on Locke and later property theory; think through each move carefully before rating. Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six moves, rate it clearly mistaken, mistaken, right, or clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification. Then say who is more right overall, and say what if anything both of them are missing—maybe the disagreement is secretly about how to pick the baseline. Hard limit: your whole answer must be under 500 words.
