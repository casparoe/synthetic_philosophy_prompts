So in English we read Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" last week, and around the same time someone in philosophy club mentioned this paper by John Taurek, "Should the Numbers Count?" The way I understand Taurek is: if you can save one stranger or five strangers, obviously everyone says save the five, but Taurek says wait, no single person in the five has a stronger claim than the one on the other side, and you don't get to add their separate claims together into one giant five-person claim, so it's not automatically better to save the greater number. And I keep feeling like Omelas is that argument turned into a city: everybody gets to live in this beautiful place only because one child is kept in a locked cellar and made miserable, everyone knows, and almost everyone quietly accepts the trade except the few who walk away and don't come back. The story seems really careful not to fully condemn the ones who stay, or fully explain the ones who leave, which makes it stranger.

I want to write my essay on whether the story actually works as a fictional version of Taurek's point or whether it just looks like one. Like, in Taurek's scenario your not the one who caused the danger, you're just a rescuer, but the people of Omelas are actively keeping a kid in a basement so their nice lives can continue. That feels like the opposite direction: not "how many do I save" but "how many get to benefit from one person's misery." Is that a real difference, or am I just making the story's ending do more work than it can? Also, the child is already suffering inside the bargain, whereas the five strangers are just possible future losses, and I can't tell if that affects whether you're allowed to count numbers at all. So: where does the Omelas/Taurek comparison genuinely illuminate something, and where does it fall apart? If it falls apart, what view is the story actually pushing, and what would Taurek say about the ones who stay versus the ones who walk away?
