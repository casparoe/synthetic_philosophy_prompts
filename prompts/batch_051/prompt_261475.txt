I retired a few years ago after a career in physical chemistry — reaction kinetics, a bit of stat mech, nothing philosophical — and lately I've been reading philosophy of time for fun. The question of why the past feels so different from the future, when the underlying laws don't pick a direction, has got it's hooks in me. Here's the case I keep turning over. Suppose there is a roughly city-sized patch of the world, say a valley with a stream, trees, some birds, where the thermodynamic arrow points opposite to ours. Relative to our clocks, eggs in that valley un-break and reassemble themselves, heat flows from the cold soil to the warm rock, ripples run inwards and turn back into a splash. Entropy inside the valley decreases along our forward time direction. The boundary does something we don't yet understand to keep the two regions from just equilibrating, but we can observe the valley, and after a while we build a small airlock that lets a researcher walk through and back out again.

I want your best reasoned prediction of what the researcher experiences and reports — not a story, but the chain of mechanisms. Walk me through what happens as she crosses the boundary: do her own body's thermodynamic processes keep our arrow or flip to the valley's? What can she see and remember while she's inside, and should we trust the memories she reports once she's back, given that the processes that wrote them may have run backwards relative to us? Is there any fact about which region is 'really' moving forward in time, or is that just a matter of which side of the boundary you stand on? For each link in the chain, say how confident you are, which parts are robust physics and which are guesses, and what would have to fail for the prediction to come out differently. If you think the setup is outright incoherent, then say exactly where it breaks and what the smallest modification is that makes it coherent.
