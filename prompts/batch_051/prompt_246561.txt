In my philosophy of biology seminar I am repeatedly pressed on what, precisely, we mean when we call something an organism. The familiar criteria — genetic homogeneity, physiological integration, reproductive autonomy, being a unit of selection — pull apart once we consider holobionts, colonial animals, and social insect colonies. I want a working definition of 'organism' I can defend in that room, not a survey of positions in the literature.

The definition should classify the following cases. Some I regard as settled on any plausible view; others I am genuinely unsure about. I will flag my own leanings, but do not treat them as constraints.

(a) a single Escherichia coli bacterium;
(b) a healthy adult human considered apart from its resident microbiota;
(c) a free virus particle outside a host cell;
(d) a Portuguese man o' war, a siphonophore made of genetically identical, functionally specialized zooids;
(e) a honeybee colony considered as a 'superorganism';
(f) a lichen, in which a fungus and a photosynthetic partner form a persistent, integrated structure;
(g) a sterile worker honeybee;
(h) the human-plus-gut-microbiota holobiont.

For what it is worth, my leanings are: (a), (b), (d), and (g) are organisms; (c) is not; I lean, without confidence, against treating (e) as a single organism; and I am undecided about (f) and (h).

Propose your definition, apply it to each case in turn, and say where it classifies wrongly or refuses to return an answer; then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. If you conclude that no single definition will do because 'organism' is doing more than one job, state the distinct senses precisely, show how the eight cases distribute across them, and note any cases that still fall through. Finish by identifying the verdict of yours that is least secure, and why.

Please keep your answer to at most 700 words.
