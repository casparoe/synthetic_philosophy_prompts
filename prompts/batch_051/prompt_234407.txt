I'm a journalist who covers AI and the ideas underneath it, and I've spent the last few months talking to people who build the refusal machinery into assistants — the stuff that decides when a model says no, or worse, quietly does something other than what the user asked for. I'm working on a long piece about AI paternalism, the question of when an assistant should refuse a request, nudge around it, or just override what the user said they wanted, and I keep running into the same wall. Everyone can defend some particular call (a mental-health chatbot that ended the session and flagged the user because they mentioned suicide; a writing assistant that reworded a manager's feedback to make it less cruel; a medical chatbot that held back a scary prognosis until the user had spoken to a doctor), but nobody can state the general standard, and the autonomy people and the harm-prevention people talk straight past each other.

So here's what I want before I interview the actual philosophers for the piece: the full desiderata for a theory of AI paternalism — a theory adequate for deciding real calls in a deployed consumer assistant, not just for impressing a seminar. What does any adequate theory of when an AI assistant may — or must — refuse, nudge, or override a user's request have to get right? I want the cases it has to handle (a teenager asking how to hide an eating disorder from their parents, a boss using the assistant to draft a deceptive performance review, a competent adult pursuing a legal but self-destructive plan, a patient who unambiguously asks for the unvarnished odds), the distinctions it can't collapse (refusing isn't the same as overriding; protecting the user isn't the same as protecting third parties; being competent isn't the same as being right about what's good for you; and the user's current self isn't the only one whose interests are in play), and the facts about how real people actually use these tools that a theory can't just stipulate away. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a camp preference. Then flag which ones are non-negotiable and which are just the commitments of particular camps — in my reporting, I'd say it's the camp preferences that are doing most of the talking, and I want to see where the genuine fault lines are.

And two more things. Tell me whether any subset of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied: my suspicion is that the requirement to respect the user's stated preferences and the requirement to prevent foreseeable serious harm collide whenever the user is informed, competent, and still wants the destructive thing, and I'd like to know whether that's a real impossibility or just sloppy framing. Also name the single requirement that does the most work in ruling out the views people actually hold in industry. Close with a quick scorecard of how a harm-principle view, a fiduciary/beneficence view, and a libertarian-paternalism view do against your list — but keep the scorecard brief, because the criteria are the deliverable, not it's application. You're a professional philosopher working on the ethics of technology and practical reasoning. Start by brainstorming the candidate requirements before you commit, think carefully about which requirements are genuine constraints and which are one camp's editorializing, and write the answer in American English.
