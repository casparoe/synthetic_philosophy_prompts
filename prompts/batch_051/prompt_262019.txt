I've been working through the General Scholium to Newton's Principia in Andrew Motte's 1729 translation, and there is a passage I cannot parse to my own satisfaction:

"He is not Eternity and Infinity, but Eternal and Infinite; he is not Duration and Space, but he endures and is present. He endures forever, and is every where present; and, by existing always and every where, he constitutes Duration and Space."

What exactly is the relation Newton is asserting here between God, duration, and space? He plainly denies that God is duration or space, yet he immediately says that God "constitutes" both by existing always and everywhere. Is "constitutes" best read as causal production, as metaphysical grounding, as something closer to identity, or as some fourth relation I am not tracking? I want a careful reconstruction of the position he is trying to occupy between these alternatives, including what work the phrase "by existing always and every where" is doing in the claim, and what in the passage would decide between the available readings. Please work from the passage itself rather than bringing in secondary sources or historical philology, and keep the answer to no more than 300 words.
