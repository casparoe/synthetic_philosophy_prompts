I have recently been appointed to a small committee charged with revising the disciplinary procedures at my university, and one member — who describes his own position as following Caruso rather than Dennett on the question of just deserts — circulated the following argument. He takes it to show that no one can ever deserve punishment, so that our committee should eliminate every retributive element from the code and retain only measures aimed at rehabilitation, deterrence, and the protection of others. I find the conclusion hard to accept, but I cannot see exactly where the argument goes wrong, and I do not want to reject it merely because its conclusion is unwelcome.

The argument, with the steps numbered, is this:

(1) An agent deserves punishment for an act only if the act was under the agent's control in the sense that desert requires.

(2) If determinism is true, then no act is under the agent's control in the sense that desert requires: every act has a complete explanation in heredity, environment, and earlier events, none of which the agent chose or made.

(3) If determinism is false, then some acts are not fixed by prior causes. But to the extent that an act is not fixed by prior causes, the agent's own character, values, and reasoning do not fully explain it, and what is left unexplained is a matter of chance. To the extent that an act is fixed by prior causes, the consideration in step (2) applies to it. So on either supposition, no act is under the agent's control in the sense that desert requires.

(4) Determinism is either true or false.

(5) Therefore, no act is under the agent's control in the sense that desert requires.

(6) Therefore, no one ever satisfies the control condition on deserving punishment.

(7) Retributive punishment is justified only when the person punished satisfies that control condition.

(8) Therefore, retributive punishment is never justified.

I half suspect that the phrase "control in the sense that desert requires" shifts its meaning between step (2) and step (3), but I have trouble saying exactly what the two readings are. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, which premise should a defender of deserved punishment deny, and what is the strongest ground for denying it? If it is not valid, at which step or steps does the inference fail? Please test the argument by giving each disputed premise its most plausible interpretation and then seeing whether the conclusion still follows on that interpretation.
