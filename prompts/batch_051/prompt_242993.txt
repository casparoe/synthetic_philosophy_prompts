I retired a few years ago from a career in physical oceanography, and I have since been working through Bayesian confirmation theory with more patience than I ever had for it as a working scientist. I would like you to write a short research proposal for a one-semester paper, roughly six thousand words, on the problem of old evidence. The proposal should state a determinate question, explain why the standard responses leave it open, describe an approach that could make progress and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives, say what a successful resolution and a failure would each look like, and give an honest assessment of what might make the project too large for a semester. Please do not include a bibliography or citations; I will supply those myself.

The problem, as I understand it, is that if evidence E is already known when hypothesis H is formulated, then the probability of E is 1 for the agent in question, and E cannot raise the probability of H on the standard Bayesian picture. Yet the history of science seems full of cases in which a new theory is counted as strongly confirmed by observations made long before the theory existed. I want the proposal to separate three situations that are often run together: evidence that was known but had not been brought to bear on the phenomenon in question; evidence that was known and had already been absorbed into an older theory; and evidence that was, in effect, used in constructing the hypothesis itself. My suspicion is that only the first is a genuine problem for Bayesian confirmation theory, while the second is a problem about how background knowledge should be represented, and the third is better treated as a constraint on hypothesis construction than as confirming evidence at all.

The central question should therefore be something like this: is the problem of old evidence a defect in the Bayesian formalism, or a defect in how we individuate evidence and hypotheses when the formalism is applied to historical cases? The usual repairs tend either to replace the agent's actual probabilities with counterfactual ones or to add the logical fact that H entails E as a new piece of evidence, and I am not convinced either move captures what scientists are doing when they take old data to support a new theory. I would like the approach to be comparative rather than another attempt to fix the formalism wholesale: take two or three episodes, such as the advance of Mercury's perihelion and general relativity or the Michelson-Morley result and special relativity, and model all of them with the same resources, rather than tailoring a new device to each case. Please say explicitly what a result would have to show to count as resolving the problem for that range of cases, what would count as failure, and which part of the project is most likely to expand beyond a semester. I am less interested in a survey of existing positions than in a plan that exhibits the structure of the difficulty and a feasible route into it.
