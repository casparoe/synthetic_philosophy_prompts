Lately I've been arguing with my roommate about what it actually is to *intend* to do something, as opposed to just wanting it or expecting it to happen. I've more or less settled on four criteria for genuine intention: (i) you've stopped deliberating and treat the action as settled; (ii) you believe doing it is at least partly up to you and that you will do it, or at least that doing it is a live possibility; (iii) you treat it as fixed in your planning, so you'd turn down conflicting invitations and make arrangements around it; and (iv) it involves a kind of commitment that a mere wish or daydream lacks. My roommate thinks (ii) is doing too much work, but I'm not convinced.

Now here's the case I can't stop thinking about. An eccentric billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at exactly midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow at noon. The toxin will make you feel awful for about a day but has no lasting effects. The catch is that you don't actually have to drink it: a perfectly reliable brain scanner checks at midnight whether you've formed the intention, and if you have, the money is yours on the spot, no further conditions. So by tomorrow morning you'd already have the money and no reason whatsoever to poison yourself, and you know all of this from the beginning.

So my question is this: suppose you could get yourself into some mental state at midnight—does that state count as intending to drink the toxin, according to my four criteria? Go through them one by one. If the verdict is no, say whether that's because the criteria are wrong about what an intention is, or because the setup makes the intention impossible. And does this case show that intentions can't just be a matter of deciding what it would be rational to do later?
