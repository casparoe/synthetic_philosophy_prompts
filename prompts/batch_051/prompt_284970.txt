I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have spent a good while thinking on my own about the sea-battle problem and its surrounding issues. I facilitate a small, non-academic discussion group, and I would like to set its members a take-home exercise that separates people who can name the usual positions from people who can reason through the issue themselves.

Please write a single essay question, intended to be answered in 90 minutes, on future contingents. The question itself should not mention the sea battle, any named thinkers, texts, or technical labels; instead, it should present a short, unfamiliar scenario — for instance, a lottery draw whose numbers have already been generated but not yet revealed, or a coin that will be flipped tomorrow in a sealed room — and use it to force candidates to distinguish at least these four claims:
- Bivalence: every meaningful declarative sentence is already true or false.
- Settledness: there already exists a fact of the matter about the future outcome.
- Determinism: the present state of the world together with its laws settles a unique outcome.
- Knowability: some ideal agent could in principle know now what will happen.

The question should require more than defining these claims. It should ask candidates to show which of them entails which, and to construct at least two internally consistent packages that combine them in different ways — for example, accepting bivalence while denying knowability, or accepting settledness while denying determinism. It should also force them to take seriously the thought that truth might be relativized to times, so that a sentence such as “the coin will land heads” may have no truth value now yet come to have one later, without any physical change in the coin or the world.

Then provide a model answer of about 500–700 words, followed by a marking scheme with a total of 20 marks. The marking scheme should identify five must-hit points, state how many marks each is worth, and give a one-line note on how candidates typically mishandle each point. The model answer and marking scheme should reward clear separation of bivalence, settledness, determinism, and knowability, and should penalize answers that merely summarize the sea-battle discussion or treat the four claims as interchangeable.
