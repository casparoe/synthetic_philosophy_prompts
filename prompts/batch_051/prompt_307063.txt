I have been reading Blaise Pascal’s Pensées on my own, and one fragment in the section usually headed justice and the reason of effects has been nagging at me. As I understand it, Pascal says that just commands deserve obedience, but only strong commands reliably receive it; that a just principle without power behind it is ineffective, whilst a powerful principle without justice becomes tyrannical; and that, because people have not been able to give justice the backing of force, they have instead given the backing of force the name of justice. I am not academically trained, so I may have turned his compressed remark into a bad argument. But when I spell it out, it runs like this:

(1) A principle can only regulate a society if it is effectively enforced; unenforced principles are disobeyed.
(2) There is no agreed method for identifying a truly just principle, since what counts as justice seems to vary across countries and centuries.
(3) So no determinate standard of justice can be enforced as such; enforcement attaches to whatever group happens to have superior strength.
(4) Once a powerful group establishes its rules, most people come to regard those rules as just, because they have no experience of alternatives and because the rules carry the prestige of authority.
(5) So in the only sense available to us, justice just is the set of rules that the prevailing power successfully enforces.
(6) Consequently, it is a confusion to condemn an existing legal order as unjust simply because it originated in or relies on force.

I find each step by itself plausible, but the conclusion seems to make moral criticism of laws impossible, which I cannot quite accept. Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and how should that step be corrected? If it is valid, does the conclusion really follow from Pascal’s paragraph, or is there another reading of it?
