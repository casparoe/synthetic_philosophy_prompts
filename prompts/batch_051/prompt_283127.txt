Last night I had a long argument with a friend about the dichotomy of control, and it has left me genuinely unsettled. His case was that the distinction collapses once you press on the word control. If control means complete voluntary control, then the only things up to us are certain acts of judgment and intention, and even those are not fully reliable, since lapses of attention, habit, and bias can capture them without our choosing. If control instead means having some causal influence, then health, reputation, and other people's responses all count as up to us, because our actions can shift them. On neither reading does the sharp line Epictetus draws survive. The longer we argued, the harder I found it to resist this. I care because I have been trying to practice the version of Stoicism now popular in books and podcasts, and that practice seems to rest almost entirely on this very distinction. My question is whether there is a defensible reading of the dichotomy of control that escapes this dilemma, and if so, what that reading is; or whether an honest modern Stoic should treat the dichotomy as useful rhetoric rather than a distinction that can carry real philosophical weight. Please write your answer in American English.
