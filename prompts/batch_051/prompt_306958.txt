My brother is getting married in the fall and asked me to give the toast. He and his fiancee both work in finance – he does compliance at a big bank, she does something with municipal bonds. I'm the philosophy one in the family, and when we were kids he used to tease me for being the impractical one. I'd like to return the favor a little, but with love.

I want to build the toast around a passage from Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter I, the part where he's arguing that the state should fund public education. He writes:

In the progress of the division of labour, the employment of the far greater part of those who live by labour, that is, of the great body of the people, comes to be confined to a few very simple operations, frequently to one or two. But the understandings of the greater part of men are necessarily formed by their ordinary employments. The man whose whole life is spent in performing a few simple operations, of which the effects too are, perhaps, always the same, or very nearly the same, has no occasion to exert his understanding, or to exercise his invention in finding out expedients for removing difficulties which never occur. He naturally loses, therefore, the habit of such exertion, and generally becomes as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become.

I'm not going to quote "as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become" at a wedding. But the passage got me thinking. The toast needs to land for a mixed room: my family and the couple's friends, some of whom know Smith, most of whom don't. I want the toast to use this passage as a pivot. The idea would be something like: Smith worried the market, left to itself, will make people's minds narrow and dull – and marriage, done well, is the opposite of repetitive simple operations. It drags you into another person's complicated particularity, constantly presents you with difficulties that do occur, and gives you occasions to exercise your understanding about something other than your own portfolio. But that's treacly if I just assert it.

Can you write the toast? It should be funny at the start, warm by the end, around four minutes spoken. It should not lecture, but it should make the Smith worry structurally load-bearing – like the toast only works because the audience glimpses that the brother, of all people, is the one who escaped the division of labour's stupid-making, or is at least now hedged against it. And it has to be true to Smith without making the couple sound like an economic development policy. Also, give me a line near the beginning that acknowledges the weirdness of citing an eighteenth-century economist at a wedding, so no one thinks I forgot where I am.
