I've gradually come to hold the view that the adaptationist programme—in roughly the sense Gould and Lewontin meant, treating every trait as shaped by selection and then inventing a plausible selection story for it—isn't merely often wrong but methodologically self-insulating: for any trait, a sufficently clever person can find some selection story to fit, and the programme never announces in advance which traits should resist that treatment. I want to know what would count against that anti-adaptationist critique itself, not against adaptationism. What would have to be true for it to be false that the programme is unfalsifiable in this global sense? Are there forms of adaptationist inference that do stick their necks out—comparative, quantitative, phylogenetic, whatever—and would be abandoned under identifiable circumstances? What findings, in evo-devo or elsewhere, would show that the programme is in better epistemic shape than I claim? Which of my claims would a Gould-and-Lewontin-style pluralist concede as counting against them, and which would they wave off as attacking a strawman? And if I patch my view by saying 'local adaptationist hypotheses are testable, only the global programme isn't,' have I insulated my own position into triviality—and is that a problem for a methodological critique?
