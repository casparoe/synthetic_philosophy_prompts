I'm taking a course on equality law, and it's made me realize I don't actually know what my own principle is for when discrimination is suspect. I've been collecting my gut reactions to cases, and I'd like someone to help me reverse-engineer the rule, if there is one.

A landlord refuses to rent to a Black family: wrong, no question. A company rejects a Muslim applicant because the owner says customers would be uncomfortable: wrong, still wrong. A school fires a teacher for being gay: wrong. An airline won't hire pilots over 65: fine, the safety risk is real. A gym won't hire someone who's quite overweight as a fitness instructor: I mostly think fine, since the job is literally to model the thing they'd be coaching, though I feel a bit uneasy about it. A modeling agency only hiring conventionally beautiful people: fine. A bar won't hire a bartender with prominent face tattoos because it would ruin the "vibe": fine, and I can't imagine wanting to forbid that. A company refuses to promote a woman because she's pregnant and will need leave: wrong. So far the pattern feels easy. But here's where it gets muddy. A warehouse won't hire someone with a 20-year-old drug possession conviction: I think that's wrong — it punishes them twice, and whatever the intent, it ends up hitting Black applicants harder. An accounting firm won't hire someone with an embezzlement conviction: I think that's fine, the conviction is plainly relevant to the job. A landlord won't rent to someone whose income is mostly a housing voucher: I genuinely don't know; it doesn't feel as bad as the race case, but it doesn't sit right either.

I used to think the principle was immutability: suspect grounds are the ones you can't change. But religion isn't immutable and I called that suspect, while tattoos are chosen and I called those fine, so immutability can't be the whole story. Then I thought maybe suspect grounds are ones tied to a history of group subordination. But being overweight has that kind of history and I basically accepted the gym case; and the voucher case is connected to poverty, which is about as tied to subordination as you get, but I'm ambivalent rather than outraged. Then I thought maybe it's just job-relevance: suspect when the trait has nothing to do with the task. But the embezzlement case (fine) and the old drug conviction (wrong) are both chosen traits, and relevance can't easily explain why I split them — unless relevance to the present rather than to the past is doing the work. Is there a single principle that gets all or nearly all of these verdicts, and which ones does it force me to give up? And what would that principle say about political party registration, a low credit score, being a heavy smoker, having young children, or speaking with a strong regional accent?
