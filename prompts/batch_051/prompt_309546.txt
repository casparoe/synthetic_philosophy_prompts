A friend and I spent an evening arguing about whether the law should recognize mental privacy or cognitive liberty as brain–computer interfaces become ordinary, and we ended up disagreeing about which principle survives contact with concrete cases. I would like to know whether my own verdicts hang together, or whether I am just reacting case by case. Here are the cases and what I actually think about each.

1. An implanted closed-loop device continuously senses the neural pattern that precedes a depressive episode in a patient and automatically adjusts stimulation to avert it; the data stay inside the device and are seen by no one. I do not regard this as a privacy violation at all.

2. Long-haul truck drivers must wear an EEG headband that detects the neural signature of drowsiness or microsleep and vibrates to wake them; the signal is discarded immediately and the company sees only aggregate compliance statistics. This strikes me as permissible, perhaps positively desirable.

3. An office requires employees to wear a headset that continuously scores their attention and engagement, and the scores go into their personnel file for promotion decisions. This strikes me as impermissible, even though the hardware is essentially the same as the truck drivers'.

4. A marketing firm measures viewers' neural reward responses to advertisements and sells rankings of which ads capture attention; the method reads no beliefs or propositional thoughts, only valence and salience. This also bothers me, and I would regulate it, though I cannot say exactly why if I approve of the truck-driver case.

5. A defendant in a murder trial is ordered, over his objection, to wear a cap of sensors while being shown crime-scene and control photographs; an algorithm infers from his recognition response which scenes he has seen before. I think this should be prohibited outright, even if it were accurate, because it forces him to supply evidence from inside his own head.

6. A person with locked-in syndrome uses an implanted interface that decodes intended hand movements from motor cortex and turns them into text; everything it produces is communication he has chosen, and he controls when it is sent. This seems to me clearly permissible and liberating, although it externalizes inner states in much the way the marketing and courtroom devices do.

I can feel a principle somewhere in these verdicts, something about who benefits, who consents, and how close the reading comes to the content of a person's thoughts, but I cannot state it in a way that keeps all six without becoming ad hoc. What principle best captures these verdicts? What does it predict about a case I have not listed, for example a school using attention-sensing headsets to help children with ADHD, with parental consent and no stored records? And which of my six verdicts, if any, does the cleanest principle force me to abandon?
