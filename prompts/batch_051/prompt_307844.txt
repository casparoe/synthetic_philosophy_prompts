I am a PhD student working on the political philosophy of cultural property, and I keep running up against a conceptual question that I have not managed to settle to my own satisfaction. I would be grateful for your help with it.

The term "wrongfully held" does a great deal of work in debates about museum restitution, but its extension is contested. I would like you to propose a working definition of when a cultural object in a museum collection is wrongfully held — that is, held in a way that would normally ground a moral claim to its return, even where no legal claim is presently available — and then test it against the cases below. I have chosen the cases because my own view is clear on some and genuinely opaque on others. For what it is worth: I would be surprised if the first two cases did not come out wrongfully held and if the third did; I have no stable view about the rest, and I would rather you follow the definition than my hunches.

The cases:

1. A plaque from the royal palace of Benin, part of the group known as the Benin Bronzes, removed by British troops during the punitive expedition of 1897 and now held in a European museum.

2. A painting seized from a Jewish family under the Nazi regime, later bought in good faith by a museum, and now claimed by the heirs.

3. A contemporary sculpture purchased directly from the living artist at a fair price and later donated to the museum.

4. The Parthenon Marbles, removed from Athens in the early nineteenth century under an Ottoman-issued document whose scope is disputed, and claimed by Greece.

5. A carved ancestor figure sold in the 1920s by a local chief to a colonial administrator. The sale was formally recorded and a price was paid, but the chief had been given to understand that refusal would bring punitive consequences, and the price was a token one.

6. Human remains collected in the late nineteenth century for racial-science research, claimed by a recognised descendant community but still used in ongoing scientific study.

7. A sacred object from a community that was dispersed during a colonial-era famine and no longer exists as a distinct group; a neighbouring group claiming partial cultural descent seeks its return.

8. A high-resolution 3D-printed replica made by a museum and sent to a source community while the original remains in the museum; the community argues that the original remains wrongfully held, while the museum replies that the essential cultural information has been returned.

For each case, state whether your definition classifies the object as wrongfully held or not, and flag any case where the definition yields no clear answer, yields an answer you find surprising, or tempts you to revise the definition. The deliverable is the definition itself, refined in light of the cases, rather than a survey of positions or legal instruments, so please do not supply citations or a reading list.

I have a further suspicion, which you are welcome to reject: that "wrongfully held" conceals two questions — whether the original acquisition was wrongful, and whether present-day possession wrongs anyone now. If your definition separates these, please say how; if it treats them as inseparable, please say why.

Please write in British English. Throughout, please distinguish what is broadly settled among specialists from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
