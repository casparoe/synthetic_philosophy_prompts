I got into an argument on a forum about whether Goodhart's law and Campbell's law are really the same point, and I've realised I can't sharpen the distinction without some concrete cases. My rough grasp, which may be a bit off: Goodhart's original claim was that a statistical pattern in the world tends to stop being a pattern once some authority starts leaning on it to steer policy. Campbell's claim was more sociological: once a quantitative social indicator is used to make real decisions, it starts to invite manipulation, and the very activity its supposed to measure gets distorted in the process. The objects feel different — Goodhart is about a regularity breaking; Campbell is about an indicator corrupting a practice — but in the standard examples (exam targets, hospital waiting time targets) they always seem to happen together.

Construct me two thought experiments that pull them apart. In the first, Campbell applies but Goodhart doesn't: an indicator is used in decisions and quietly twists the practice it monitors, while the underlying statistical regularity stays more or less intact. In the second, Goodhart applies but Campbell doesn't: a statistical regularity comes apart the moment it's used for control, but there's no quantitative social indicator corrupting a practice and no one is gaming — the break is a structural or mechanical effect of the control itself. Keep them realistic, set the first in a university and the second in a hospital or local council, avoid the usual cobra-effect/rat-tail examples, and keep each scenario to one paragraph.

Then tell me the minimum alteration that would flip each case (turning the Campbell-only case into a Goodhart case, and the reverse).
