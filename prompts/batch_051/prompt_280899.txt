ok so I'm a phd student writing a chapter on whether Rawls's treatment of envy makes him miss something, and I need a thought experiment built to pretty exact specs. My own attempts keep either stacking the deck or letting the two views blur into the same verdict.

Here's the conceptual setup I'm working with. In the envy section of A Theory of Justice, Rawls distinguishes envy proper from resentment, roughly as follows. Envy is hostility toward someone else's advantage even though that advantage doesn't make you worse off; it's a vice, at best excusable when the gap is large and it has damaged the person's self-respect. Resentment, by contrast, is a moral sentiment: it responds to a violation of your legitimate claims or the fair terms of cooperation, so it's the sense of justice at work, a signal of injustice, not a vice. The classification runs through the object of the attitude: if the other person's advantage is something the principles of justice permit (say it satisfies the difference principle), hostility toward it is envy; if the advantage violates those principles, hostility is resentment.

The rival line I'm trying out is an epistemic-functionalist one: classify the hostile attitude by wether it reliably tracks a real moralized relational harm — the other person capturing the surplus you created, taking credit or standing extracted from you, that sort of thing — and whether it tends to generate corrective pressure. On this test it doesn't matter whether the final distribution satisfies the difference principle.

I need a case built so the two tests come apart cleanly. Specs:

- Realistic workplace, ordinary people, no philosophers in it, one paragraph.
- A worse-off worker W shows hostility toward a better-off colleague C.
- C's advantage satisfies the difference principle and fair equality of opportunity, so a strict Rawlsian has to classify W's hostility as envy, not resentment. Tell me whether a Rawlsian would allow it as excusable envy or just plain envy, and defend that call.
- But on the functionalist test, W's hostility is a textbook signal of injustice: it tracks a genuine moralized relational harm (for instance, W produced a measurable surplus that C captured, or W's contribution got absorbed into C's standing/credit) and it ends up feeding a reform that improves the worst-off.
- The facts have to generate the two verdicts; don't stipulate them. The case is for a chapter section arguing that the vice/moral-sentiment taxonomy can misclassify the epistemically useful resentment of low-status agents, so it fails if it only works by making the Rawlsian look obtuse.
