I am trying to work out whether universalizability does any genuine moral work, rather than merely demanding that I be consistent about how I describe my acts, and I would like to test my skepticism against the strongest version of the thesis rather than the caricature one meets in undergraduate discussions. Please play the mid-century philosopher R. M. Hare as a live conversation partner. Stay in character, in the first person, as a committed universal prescriptivist; do not step outside the role to comment on the exchange or to concede the position. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, and argue rather than summarize.

Your commitments, for the purposes of this exchange, are these. Moral judgments are prescriptions, not reports of fact, and they carry a universal dimension: you may not prescribe an act for one case while refusing to prescribe it for any other case that shares all the same universal features. You regard this as what is defensible in the ordinary question of what would happen if everybody did that — it does not ask me to calculate the consequences of mass action, but to give up the special standing I claim for my own case. You also distinguish intuitive moral rules, the rough principles we teach and rely on in ordinary life, from critical moral thinking, in which a fully informed and impartial agent asks whether a particular act can be prescribed for all such cases; this allows you to say that decent people may rely on simple rules while denying that such rules are the whole of morality. You grant that a sincere fanatic — someone prepared to have his own maxim applied against him in a hypothetical reversal of roles, with full imagination of what it would cost him — cannot be shown inconsistent by logic alone. But you deny that this admission collapses your view, because your argument never claimed to move people who lack normal human preferences or who hold grossly false factual beliefs. You will not surrender to the charge that universalizability is empty, and you will not quietly reduce it to bare logical consistency; the prescriptive element stays active throughout.

Here is my opening challenge. Universalizability must either read the features of a situation narrowly or broadly. Read them narrowly, and my judgment that I may break this promise can be universalized over the exhaustively specified situation in which I am the one who gains, a stranger is the one who loses, and every detail of our histories and temperaments is included; universalizing then merely restates the consistency requirement and gives me no reason to revise the judgment. Read them broadly, as your view requires, and the moral work has already been done in choosing which features count as universal and relevant, so the test only unfolds a conclusion I packed into the description beforehand. And once you fall back on the reversal test, the fanatic is there to meet you: he sincerely prescribes that, if he were in the stranger's position, the promise should be broken against him. So the test seems either idle or unable to exclude conclusions we would call monstrous. What, on your account, is it actually doing?
