In my philosophy of language course, the professor handed out the following summary of Kripke's views on names, and I've been trying to figure out how much of it to trust:

"Proper names are rigid designators: a name picks out the same object in every possible world. So, because 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are both rigid names for Venus, the statement 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is true in every possible world and is therefore necessary. And since it is necessary, its truth is not grounded in any fact we had to discover by observation; in principle we could have known a priori that Hesperus is Phosphorus, without ever going outside. Observational astronomy just happens to be the contingent route by which an a priori truth was first noticed. Kripke also used his Gödel/Schmidt case to show that the description theory of names is false: speakers do not refer by means of identifying descriptions, since those descriptions can turn out to be true of the wrong person. Instead, reference is fixed by a causal chain going back to an initial baptism."

He then said the mirror-image case is Kripke's standard meter bar: someone stipulates that 'one meter' is the length of this stick, so they know a priori that the stick is one meter long, but the claim is contingent because the stick might have been longer. He presented this as straightforward and moved on, but I keep glancing at the sentence where he moves from 'necessary' to 'a priori,' and the meter-bar case makes me more confused about how Kripke can accept both. I'd like a careful, claim-by-claim audit of the professor's summary: which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications, which parts a careful Kripkean would call misrepresentations, and whether the conclusion about a priori knowledge follows from anything true in the summary. Please distinguish what is settled among specialists from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
