By 'causal-role account' I mean roughly: a trait's function is whatever it currently does that contributes to the organism's (or some subsystem's) capacities. By 'etiological' (selected-effects) account I mean roughly: a trait's function is the effect it was historically selected for.

Can you design a pair of biologically credible cases — invented species are fine, but no lab-made organisms, no humans, no sci-fi — such that:

1. In the first case, the causal-role account says a trait has a particular function, while the selected-effects account says it either lacks that function or has a different one.
2. In the second case, it goes the other way: the selected-effects account says a trait has a particular function, while the causal-role account says it doesn't (or at least doesn't have that one).

The cases should involve specific structures or behaviours, not placeholders like 'trait T with effect E', and the scenario descriptions should be under about 300 words all told. Also, don't build the verdicts into the description — give enough natural history that the verdicts fall out from the two theories rather than just stipulating them.

Then, after the scenarios, please do the following seperately for each case: say exactly what verdict each account gives and why; identify the structural feature doing the real work (I'm guessing it's a mismatch between selection history and current role, but be precise); show what happens if that feature is removed — does the disagreement collapse?; and give the best objection a defender of the account that comes out badly in that case could make. No references or reading list needed — I just want the reasoning.

The handout is meant to show students the two accounts aren't just notational variants, so the specs genuinely need to be met — no fudging allowed.

Oh, and could you write the whole answer in British English? It's for a UK university.
