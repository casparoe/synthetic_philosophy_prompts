I am taking an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of cognitive science this term, and I keep returning to the long-running standoff between representationalist and enactivist—or more broadly embodied—approaches to cognition. On one side, defenders of computational-representational accounts hold that cognition is, at its explanatory core, the processing of internal representations: content-bearing states that carry information about the environment and are manipulated by rule-governed processes. On the other side, enactivists and their allies argue that basic cognition is constituted by the history of sensorimotor coupling between an organism and its environment, that much intelligent behavior is produced by organism–environment structure rather than internal inference, and that the appeal to content-bearing internal representations is at best a misleading redescription and at worst an explanatory dead end. The exchange has now run for several decades and, from what I can tell, both sides have sophisticated answers to the obvious objections, yet the center of gravity barely shifts: the same arguments recur about which capacities are meant to be representation-hungry, whether the notion of representation can be deflated rather than eliminated, and whether anti-representationalists are simply changing the subject. The two camps also seem to prefer different showcases—catching a fly ball, navigating a cluttered room, or skilled tool use on one side; counterfactual reasoning, planning, and offline concept use on the other—which makes me suspect they may not be trying to explain exactly the same thing.

My question is not which side is right, but what kind of disagreement this is and why it has stalled. Is it a substantive dispute about the architecture of minds, a partly verbal dispute about how much theoretical work the term 'representation' is being asked to do, or two research programs answering different questions—one about subpersonal mechanisms that produce behavior, the other about the capacities of a whole embedded organism? Or is the impasse driven by different standards: which phenomena count as the data, whether phenomenological or dynamical considerations are admissible as evidence, when a formal model counts as explanatory? My working hypothesis is that the two camps have drifted toward different criteria of explanatory success and now mostly talk past one another, but I am not confident. Diagnose the disagreement by engaging the positions each side actually defends, and tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis—a sharper distinction, a change of question, an experimental or formal result, an agreed-upon test case—or why no progress should be expected and that is fine.
