I'm a novelist working on a death-row novel and I keep circling back to The Green Mile — I mostly know the Darabont film, so feel free to use that version. I want to read it through the three big death-penalty debates: retribution, deterrence, and the risk of executing someone who didn't do it. The obvious move is John Coffey's innocence, but I'm more interested in whether the story has anything real to say about retribution and deterrence, or whether innocence is doing so much of the work that the harder question (whether the state may ever execute the guilty) just never gets asked. Also, does Coffey's healing gift carry any philosophical weight, or is it just a supernatural way to make his execution feel worse than everyone else's? I'd like a reading that's honest about where the film is being philosophically serious and where its stacking the deck.
