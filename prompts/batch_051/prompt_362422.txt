You are a professional philosopher working in moral psychology and the philosophy of evaluation, especially theories of love, veneration, and the emotions. I teach an upper-level seminar called "The Ethics of Devotion." Early in the term we take up the question of idolatry understood very broadly: not merely religious images, but the more general phenomenon of valuing something the wrong way, such that a partner, a political cause, a work of art, a nation, or a philosophical method is made to carry more than it can bear. I would like you to write a first-day sorting instrument for the seminar: a "which account of misplaced valuation do you actually hold?" quiz of eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete case with two or three answer options. The questions must sort students by what they say about cases, not by which label they like; no question should ask directly what they think idolatry is.

Before you draft the questions, think carefully about the diagnostic distinctions, because that is where the instrument will succeed or fail. The most important one is this: the wrong-making feature of an idolatrous life may lie in what is valued (the object is false, merely finite, or projected) or in how it is valued (the object is genuine but valued in the wrong order, degree, or key). Many rough treatments collapse these, and the quiz must visibly keep them apart. Please compose the cases around the following or similar situations: a partner who needs the beloved to be flawless and experiences every ordinary fault as catastrophe; a political movement that treats its leader as above evidence and criticism; a grieving spouse who leaves a room untouched for decades; a researcher whose pride in a paradigm makes an anomaly invisible to her; an environmentalist who treats any intervention in a landscape as desecration; and a fan whose devotion reorganizes their finances, friendships, and identity around a performer.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto the following five positions, named in plain language rather than by theorist, and kept genuinely distinct: (1) the ultimacy account, on which valuing goes wrong when a finite thing is made the ultimate source of a person's meaning and orientation; (2) the disordered-love account, on which the object may be good but is loved in the wrong rank or measure relative to other goods; (3) the fitting-attitude account, on which the defect is a mismatch between attitude and object, such as veneration where only appreciation is due or faith where only tentative trust is due; (4) the vulnerability account, on which idolatry is a practical mistake about exposure, resting one's flourishing in something that can be lost, withheld, or destroyed; and (5) the projection account, on which the devotee deposits her own powers in the object and then worships them as though they were alien. Please translate these into diagnostic criteria rather than using my glosses as answer options. For each position in the scoring key, add one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Also note which combinations of the diagnostic questions are unoccupied and say briefly whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of the five positions the instrument cannot reliably separate. The quiz is for students with no prior reading in the area, so do not attach names, citations, or reading lists; the instrument should stand on its cases alone. Take a breath, work step by step, and begin by mapping the diagnostic questions and the space of answer combinations before you write the quiz itself.
