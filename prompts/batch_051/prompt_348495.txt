Two friends of mine, Maya and Dan, have been going back and forth for weeks about Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and its started to make book club a bit tense. We're right at the point in Part III where Smith switches from judging other people to judging yourself, and the argument is over this passage:

"Whatever judgment we can form concerning them, accordingly, must always bear some secret reference, either to what are, or to what, upon a certain condition, would be, or to what, we imagine, ought to be the judgment of others. We endeavour to examine our own conduct as we imagine any other fair and impartial spectator would examine it. If, upon placing ourselves in his situation, we thoroughly enter into all the passions and motives which influenced it, we approve of it, by sympathy with the approbation of this supposed equitable judge. If otherwise, we enter into his disapprobation, and condemn it.

Were it possible that a human creature could grow up to manhood in some solitary place, without any communication with his own species, he could no more think of his own character, of the propriety or demerit of his own sentiments and conduct, of the beauty or deformity of his own mind, than of the beauty or deformity of his own face. All these are objects which he cannot easily see, which naturally he does not look at, and with regard to which he is provided with no mirror which can present them to his view. Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before. It is placed in the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with, which always mark when they enter into, and when they disapprove of his sentiments; and it is here that he first views the propriety and impropriety of his own passions, the beauty and deformity of his own mind."

Maya reads it as Smith being basically a social constructivist about conscience. Her point is the mirror passage: a person raised alone literally cannot think about the beauty or deformity of his own mind, and the only mirror he ever gets is "the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with." So when Smith says we examine our conduct as a "fair and impartial spectator" would, Maya says that spectator is just the crowd we've internalized — a generalization of actual onlookers, not something independent of them. On her reading, Smith cannot ground a moral judgment that runs against what the people around you would approve of, because the imagined judge is built entirely out of materials supplied by real audiences. Dan says Maya is turning a genetic claim about how we acquire self-consciousness into a claim about what the standard is. The passage, he points out, says our judgments carry a "secret reference" not only to what are the judgments of others but to what "upon a certain condition, would be, or ... ought to be" their judgments; and the spectator is specifically "fair and impartial" and a "supposed equitable judge." So the standard is explicitly idealized and corrective — what an informed, unbiased observer would approve — not a poll of the neighbors. Maya shot back that all those idealizing adjectives are still adjectives applied to an imagined other person: Smith doesn't appeal to a moral law or reason, he appeals to a point of view, and there's no way to fill in "fair" and "equitable" except from the social practice you happen to inherit. Dan replied that origin and content are different: society hands you the mirror, but the whole point of imagining an equitable judge is that it lets you correct what actual society reflects back, and the "ought to be" in Smith's sentence is doing that normative work. Maya answered that "ought to be the judgment of others" is just "what a better-informed member of the same community would think"; for people who break with their whole community's standards, the model has nothing left to stand on, since every "supposed" judge is supposed out of the very reactions that community taught them to expect. Dan said that only shows the mechanism is embodied and social, not that its content reduces to local approval — and that if Maya were right, the lone person brought into a corrupt society could never come to condemn it, which is an outcome she seems more bothered by than Smith would be.

I honestly couldn't referee it, and I'd like something more precise than "a bit of both." Could you adjudicate the disagreement point by point: who is right about the passage on which specific claims, where does each of them overread or underread what Smith actually says, and what is the most defensible account of the relation between the mirror of society and the impartial spectator in the passage?
