I keep being drawn to a version of Han Feizi's Legalism as political theory, and I also keep colliding with an intuition I cannot abandon. I want to know which side should yield, and on what terms.

The theory, as I am attracted to it, runs like this. A well-run state should be built on general, public standards backed by determinate rewards and punishments, applied impersonally and without any assessment of anyone's inner virtue. The point is not that human beings are especially selfish; it is that the state should not need them to be otherwise. When the incentives of law are clear, compliance becomes the low-cost option for the selfish and the virtuous alike. And whenever government depends on the moral character of a ruler or on recruiting upright officials, it has staked its survival on the one resource that cannot be reliably produced. The view has the virtues a theory should have: it gives officials a decision procedure, it unifies many cases under a single regularity, and it explains why even benevolent dynasties decay, namely, that they governed through the wrong kind of dependence.

But I have a firm intuition that something is missing. A code of rules does not apply itself. Someone must decide what a rule means in the case at hand, whether a particular action counts as compliance, and what to do when rules run out or conflict. It is hard to see how every one of those applicative decisions could itself be settled by a further fixed rule without generating a regress, because applying that further rule would raise the same question again. So the system seems forced, at some point, to rely on an official's judgment exercised under particular conditions; and judgment of that sort, where the rules are silent, is not obviously different from the virtue or practical wisdom the theory set out to avoid needing. If the Legalist claims that the code will settle every case, that looks like an article of faith; if it admits that discretion must operate somewhere, then it has reintroduced a reliance on character through the back door.

I want the strongest version of each side and a negotiated verdict. What would have to be true about legal interpretation for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, and am I conflating law with a fixed code? What would have to be true about the design and administration of a legal order for the Legalist theory to give way, and is showing that some discretion remains enough to refute it? Or is there a reading on which bounded, rule-governed discretion is not a betrayal of the theory but a consequence of it? Which side should yield, and why?
