Here's an asymmetry in ordinary talk about language that I keep noticing. People will happily say that languages carve up color space differently, that Turkish's evidential markers or Mandarin's aspect system slice things differently from English, and nobody calls that unscientific. But if the next step is "so maybe Mandarin speakers actually experience time differently" or "maybe evidentiality markers make you track sources of information more carefully," the same people suddenly act like you've started talking about crystal healing. So the asymmetry is: radical diversity in linguistic form gets a pass, but radical diversity in the minds behind that form gets treated as obviously bogus.

Sapir himself says in Language (1921):

Speech is a human activity that varies without assignable limit as we pass from social group to social group, because it is a purely historical heritage of the group, the product of long-continued social usage.

That's a really strong claim about the variability of speech. My question is: is there a principled line between saying the code varies without assignable limit and saying the users of the code vary in some corresponding way? Or is our comfort with the first and discomfort with the second just an intuition we've never had to defend? Don't just tell me what Sapir did or didn't personally believe; I want a verdict on the asymmetry. And tell me what would change your mind — what evidence or argument would make you switch sides on this.
