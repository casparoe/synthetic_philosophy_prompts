Explain the nonidentity problem and discuss whether it shows that person-affecting moral principles are untenable.

Here is the student's answer:

Parfit's nonidentity problem is usually introduced through the case of a fourteen-year-old who decides to have a child now rather than waiting several years. The crucial point is that if she waits, a different child will come to exist, because which particular child results depends on the exact circumstances and timing of conception. The child she has now has a life worth living, though a much worse life than the later child would have had. Since this child's life is worth living, and since this child would never have existed if she had waited, the child is not harmed by the decision. Yet the decision still seems wrong. That is the problem: the act seems wrong even though no identifiable person is harmed or wronged. Person-affecting principles, which hold that an act can be wrong only through its effects on particular persons, cannot accommodate this judgment. The problem therefore refutes person-affecting theories, and the only workable alternative is an impersonal total view on which the choice is wrong because it produces less total well-being. Parfit himself drew this conclusion, and it is essentially the same point as his Repugnant Conclusion: both show that only the total amount of well-being matters, not who has it.
