Most of us hold two firm judgments about favoring our own. A person who donates a kidney to her sister rather than to a stranger with a better tissue match and greater need is admired for it. A health official who moves her sister up a public organ-recipient list, ahead of strangers with better matches, is condemned as corrupt—and would be condemned even if she genuinely believed her sister's life mattered more to her. The act of favoring is the same; only the setting differs. Is there a single principle that explains and justifies the difference, or are we simply inconsistent about partiality?

The pressure behind the question is old. The Confucian doctrine of graded love holds that care should be strongest for family and weaken with distance, and that loving one's own is the foundation of a well-ordered moral life rather than a compromise of it. Mozi's impartial care holds that a humane person's concern should not distinguish her own from others, and that what the Confucians treat as filial virtue is a clan selfishness that, carried into public life, becomes exactly the corruption we punish. Our ordinary judgments seem to want both at once: Confucian partiality in private acts and a Mozi-like impartiality from officeholders. Is that a defensible division of moral labor, or only a comfortable contradiction?

Find the strongest principle you can that draws the line between the donor and the official. Candidate lines include: you may be partial with what is unambiguously yours but not with a common resource held in trust; special obligations to family are real, but public office imposes a fiduciary duty that overrides them; or the difference is only enforceability, scale, or a convention we happen to approve. Test your preferred principle against nearby cases—a teacher grading her own child's exam; a surgeon who moves her mother to the front of her own operating schedule; a judge who rules for her sister in a case where the law clearly favors the other side; a parent who spends her savings on her children while strangers go hungry. If a principle survives, state it precisely and say how much partiality it permits. If none does, say which of the two original judgments you would give up and why.
