Quasi-Empirical in the Wrong Way: Lakatos Between Method and Metaphysics

Proofs and Refutations is usually summarized as showing that mathematics is quasi-empirical: informal proofs face potential falsifiers, theorems are conjectures, and mathematical knowledge grows by a process of conjecture and refutation rather than by deduction from self-evident axioms. I want to argue that this familiar summary runs together two different claims.

The first claim is methodological. Mathematical knowledge grows through a dialectic of conjecture, proof-analysis, and attempted refutation. The proof is not a mere certificate of validity; it is an instrument for uncovering the theorem's hidden lemmas and for discovering the domain over which the theorem holds. Consider the dialogue's treatment of Euler's formula V − E + F = 2. A counterexample such as the picture frame is not a simple falsification. It forces the community to make a choice: bar the offending object as a monster, quarantine the counterexample as an exception, or incorporate the refuted lemma into the theorem as an explicit condition. Each choice changes the content of the theorem while the theorem appears to become more secure. This is a convincing picture of mathematics as a fallible, historical, community-level activity, and the dialogue supports it well.

The second claim is stronger and, I think, unsupported: that mathematics is quasi-empirical in a sense parallel to natural science, tested against an independent mathematical reality. The parallel fails because the observations in the mathematical case are not independent of the theory in the right way. In natural science, a falsifying observation is standardly taken to be produced by a world not under the scientist's control; auxiliary hypotheses can be revised, but the observation exerts pressure from outside. In the mathematical case, what counts as a counterexample is partly constituted by the definitions and the proof-analysis under revision. The picture frame becomes a counterexample only relative to a provisional definition of polyhedron and the hidden lemmas of the proof. When the community responds, it is not accommodating pressure from an independent mathematical world; it is renegotiating the meanings of its terms and the scope of its theorem. That is conceptual revision, not empirical testing.

If this is right, the proper conclusion is that Proofs and Refutations establishes a methodological quasi-empiricism — mathematics as a fallible, historically situated practice of conjectures and refutations — while giving no reason to accept an ontological quasi-empiricism, the view that mathematics has an independent subject matter that pushes back the way nature does. The slogan that Lakatos showed mathematics to be empirical is therefore not merely loose but misleading in a philosophically important way.
