I've been trying to defend Rawls's maximin reasoning in the original position with the following argument, and people keep telling me it's too quick but I can't find the exact crack. (1) Behind the veil of ignorance, you choose principles of justice without any objective probabilities over the positions you might occupy. (2) If you have no probabilities, then no social arrangement has a well-defined expected utility for you. (3) If a choice has no well-defined expected utility, then expected-utility maximization cannot be the rational rule for making it. (4) The only thing left to compare objectively is the worst-case position each society leaves you in. (5) The rule that compares only worst-case positions is maximin. (6) So maximin is the only rational decision rule behind the veil. (7) And since the difference principle says, roughly, pick the society with the best worst-off position, maximin gives you the difference principle. So Rawls comes out right, and Harsanyi's average-utilitarian objection is just pretending there are probabilities where there aren't any. I'm fairly sure the slide from (3) to (4) is doing real work, but maybe I'm wrong and all the other rules — minimax regret, Hurwicz optimism-pessimism, whatever — just smuggle in probabilities or arbitrary parameters anyway. Tell me whether the argument valid or not; if it's not, say exactly which step fails, what the strongest version of that step would be, and whether minimax regret or Harsanyi-style expected utility actually escapes the dilemma or just relocates it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of the question.
