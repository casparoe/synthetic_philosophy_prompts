In my graduate epistemology seminar, I find that students reliably run together three distinct commitments when we reach the material on luminosity and the KK principle. They treat the standard anti-luminosity argument as though it were simply the sorites paradox replayed with an epistemic operator, and they infer that rejecting the luminosity of a condition such as feeling cold is tantamount to rejecting the principle that if you know that you feel cold then you know that you know that you feel cold. I would like a careful, self-contained explanation of the conceptual issue that separates these things, precise enough to serve as the basis of a lecture but free of scholarly apparatus and attribution. In particular, please explain how the anti-luminosity argument's force depends on a margin-for-error or reliability premise about second-order judgment rather than on a tolerance premise about a vague predicate. Use this running example throughout: a subject whose sensitivity to ambient temperature is excellent but not perfect is asked, through a series of very small temperature decrements, whether she feels cold and whether she knows that she feels cold. The explanation should make explicit the precise point at which the argument would collapse if the reliability premise were weakened to something even a defender of luminosity would have to grant.

Structure the response in four sections:

1. "The three claims" (about 150 words): distinguish the luminosity of feeling cold, the KK principle as applied to that belief, and the margin-for-error principle the argument actually needs.
2. "The argument, step by step" (about 300 words): reconstruct the anti-luminosity argument on the temperature case, making the reliability premise explicit and showing how it licenses the backward induction.
3. "Why it is not a sorites" (about 200 words): contrast the margin-for-error premise with a tolerance premise and explain why the argument's conclusion is not merely that "feels cold" is vague.
4. "What it establishes" (about 200 words): spell out what a successful anti-luminosity argument would show about the KK principle and where a defender of KK retains room to maneuver.
