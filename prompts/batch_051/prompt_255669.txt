I work as a health psychologist in a cardiology clinic, mostly seeing patients after a first heart attack. We get about twenty minutes every few months to persuade them to take statins, stop smoking, actually walk. The same thing comes up constantly: a patient will agree with every fact — yes, the statin lowers my risk, yes, I would rather not have another heart attack — and then say something like "I just dont have a reason to do it," or "I know I should, but that's not the kind of life I care about." I genuinely never know whether this is a motivational problem (find the right hook, fix the barriers) or an autonomy-respecting signal that the person really has no reason, at least none that matters from their side.

This reminds me of a philosophy unit I once took on internal versus external reasons. Roughly, the internalist line (Williams, if I'm remembering the name right) was that a person has a reason to do something only if you could get there by sound deliberation from the set of things they already want, value, care about, fear, or are attached to. The externalist line says some reasons apply to you regardless of what you currently care about. I want you to turn that distinction into a decision procedure I can actually run in the room: a sequence of questions about the patient's motivational setup and the deliberation that could get from there to, say, taking the statin, with a clear answer at each step for whether I keep pushing, back off, or switch to a different goal. It has to survive contact with a busy clinic, so make the steps concrete and quick, and mark the points where I am making a value judgment rather than applying the theory. Then run the procedure on three of my patients: (1) a man who wants to walk his daughter down the aisle next year but refuses statins because his brother got muscle pain on them and he is convinced they will cripple him; (2) a woman who says she doesnt care whether she lives to old age, no longer enjoys anything since her husband died, and will take the pills only while a nurse is watching; and (3) a man with plenty of plans and energy who just values rich food and cigarettes more than an extra decade, and says he would be miserable if he changed. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to stop pushing even though the medical facts say otherwise, and say clearly what the procedure cannot settle — for example, whether the clinic is allowed to treat external reasons as reasons at all, or when the clinic's own values get to override a competent patient's motivational set.
