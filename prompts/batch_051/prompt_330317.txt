I'm in a history-of-science reading group this term, and we've been asked to each write down where we stand on how old scientific and mathematical texts should be read, so the rest of the group can push on it. Here's what I'd put down. (1) I think the main sin in this area is whiggish history: treating the past as a slow ladder up to what we now believe and grading everyone by how close they got. (2) But I also think that when you teach this stuff, it's fine to recast an old argument in modern notation or vocabulary to show what was going on, as long as you flag that you're doing it. (3) I think real historical reconstruction means trying to get at an author's concepts in their own terms, not ours, so it bugs me when people say Newton "really" had the concept of a limit, or that Aristotle's physics "really" anticipated inertia. (4) But I don't want to swing all the way to the view that we can never call an old theory false: phlogiston theory was false, and I'm comfortable saying so. (5) I also think theres no neutral standpoint from which to read these texts—we're always reading from our own time and categories, so reconstruction without any anachronism is impossible in principle. (6) Still, I think some anticipations are real and worth pointing out: later ideas sometimes genuinely grow out of earlier work, and noticing that isn't always whiggish. (7) And I think the rationality of past science matters: systems like Aristotle's or Ptolemy's weren't stupid or irrational—they were sensible given the evidence and constraints their authors had—which makes "wrong" feel too blunt when applied to a whole program, even if specific claims inside it are wrong.

Go through these and tell me which commitments are in tension and how badly; which pairs only seem like they're in tension and why; for each real tension, what would have to give and what the cheapest fix is; and then say which single revision would do the most to make the whole set hang together.
