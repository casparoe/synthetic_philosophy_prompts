Exam question:

In chapter 5 of On Crimes and Punishments (1764), Cesare Beccaria offers a critique of obscure criminal laws. Paraphrase his central argument in that chapter — being careful to state the connection he draws between legal obscurity, interpretation, and political power. Then answer the following two questions. (a) Would the rule of lenity, as it operates in American criminal law, be sufficient to meet Beccaria's objection? Why or why not? (b) Consider a modern ordinance making it a misdemeanor to "annoy, abuse, or threaten" another person in public. Does this ordinance exhibit the defect Beccaria identifies? Apply his reasoning directly to the statutory language, and state what your application reveals about the difference between his complaint and a modern void-for-vagueness challenge.

Student's answer:

Beccaria's chapter on the obscurity of laws argues that when a penal code is written in a language that ordinary citizens cannot understand, the law no longer guides conduct. Citizens become dependent on a small group of legal interpreters, because they cannot read the rule for themselves. This transfers effective legislative power from the sovereign or the public text to judges and jurists, who announce what the law "really" means. Beccaria thinks this is a serious political harm: the public loses the ability to foresee the legal consequences of its actions, and punishment becomes a function of private opinion rather than public law. A clear, widely comprehensible criminal code is therefore not merely a convenience but a safeguard of liberty and deterrence.

On (a), the rule of lenity is the principle that ambiguous criminal statutes should be construed in favor of the defendant. This seems responsive to Beccaria's worry, and in fact Beccaria himself endorses something like it when he says that judges should resolve doubts in favor of the accused. But lenity is not sufficient. Lenity operates only after a court has already determined that the statute is ambiguous, and judges can disagree about whether the language is ambiguous at all. Moreover, lenity is a constitutional mandate in American law, grounded in the due-process requirement of fair warning, so it protects the defendant at the point of adjudication but does not make the law ex ante knowable to the person deciding whether to act. A citizen who cannot tell whether her conduct falls within the statute has already been failed by the law, even if a court later rules that the statute was ambiguous and resolves that ambiguity in her favor.

On (b), the ordinance making it a misdemeanor to "annoy, abuse, or threaten" another person in public does not exhibit Beccaria's defect. The terms are drawn from ordinary language, so they are at least comprehensible to the ordinary citizen; the problem Beccaria identifies is specifically about legal Latin and technical jargon that excludes the public. Moreover, courts have upheld similar language against constitutional challenge, which shows that such language does not hand judges an unconstrained power to define crimes. A void-for-vagueness challenge would indeed fail here for the same reason: the ordinance gives fair notice because people of ordinary intelligence can understand what it prohibits.
