I was a working physicist for forty years and I've only properly got into philosophy and the history of ideas since I retired, so be patient with me if I have the labels muddled. What I'm chasing at the moment is Newton's theology, because the sheer quantity of it came as a shock to me after a lifetime of being told he was just the Principia. The threads I can already identify are these:

(1) He was an anti-Trinitarian of the Arian sort: the Son is not co-eternal with the Father, the Trinity is a later corruption, and worship belongs to God the Father alone, so praying to Christ as God struck him as idolatry.
(2) He held a Great Apostasy view, on which the primitive church had the true doctrine and lost it in the fourth century, with Athanasius and the settlement at Nicaea as the main villians.
(3) He read Daniel and Revelation as coded history whose real subject was the church's apostasy, and he blamed the Roman church for pious frauds in the transmission of Scripture.
(4) He did painstaking textual criticism of two proof-texts, 1 John 5:7 (the Johannine comma) and 1 Timothy 3:16, arguing that both had been doctored to smuggle Trinitarian doctrine into the New Testament.
(5) He denied the natural immortality of the soul, which sits rather awkwardly in my head next to the rest of his biblicism.
(6) He invested huge effort in biblical chronology, trying to line the Old Testament up with Egyptian, Babylonian, and Persian history.

I keep feeling I have missed a seventh major thread, and I suspect it touches his natural philosophy rather than his work on texts and church history. Give me your best candidate for the one Im forgetting. It has to be a genuinely distinct theme in Newton's thought, not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of items (1) to (6). Then say briefly how it connects to the threads I've listed. After that, tell me how confident you are in the identification, and single out the particular point you are least sure of.
