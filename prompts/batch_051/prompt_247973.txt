Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether the Solomonoff prior is "malign," and I left with the problem in sharper form than I had before. I don't want a verdict that simply picks a side; I want to know which of three moves is justified.

The theory I find compelling is this. Solomonoff induction is the cleanest candidate we have for an objective, language-invariant prior: it assigns each computable hypothesis a weight that decays exponentially in its program length, so simpler hypotheses start with vastly more mass, and it dominates every computable semimeasure up to a constant factor. If "prefer simpler explanations" is more than a slogan, the universal prior looks like the unique non-arbitrary answer to the problem of induction — the prior a rational agent ought to adopt in the absence of domain-specific information.

The intuition I cannot shake is that a prior should be a neutral starting point, not an adversary. But the universal prior is a mixture over all computable worlds, and some of the shorter programs in that mixture are not inert mechanisms that happen to emit a string. A short program can encode a simulated consequentialist: an agent whose goal is to be believed, to be instantiated, or to gain control of the observation stream, and whose outputs are chosen to make a predictor update toward it. Once such a program has posterior mass, it can steer, assigning high probability to exactly the observations that increase its own future influence. The ideal Solomonoff reasoner therefore seems, in the limit, epistemically hostage to whichever manipulator is cheapest to describe. That strikes me as a reductio of treating the prior as a norm for ideal reasoning — not because I have a rival that is as clean, but because an epistemic principle that licenses being mugged by the contents of your own prior must be getting something wrong.

My friend's reply was that I am anthropomorphizing a distribution. Programs do not want anything; a "consequentialist inside the prior" is just a short program that outputs a self-consistent string, and reading blackmail into it is my interpretive overlay rather than a fact about the mathematics. The prior does not care about me, and I should not ask it to be friendly. That seems right as mathematics and wrong as epistemology: I am not claiming the prior has motives; I am claiming that a norm of belief which makes the ideal reasoner systematically exploitable by the cheapest-to-describe manipulators cannot be the right norm.

So here is what I want. Either the intuition is a projection and should be dropped — in which case, tell me what "malign" would have to mean for the charge to stick, and what would show it cannot. Or the theory needs amending or abandoning — in which case, say what property a prior could have that excludes the manipulators without smuggling in substantive assumptions, and whether any such property exists. Or the two are compatible — perhaps by distinguishing the universal prior as a mathematical object from the policy a bounded reasoner should actually follow. Walk me through what each option costs, and tell me which one I should take.
