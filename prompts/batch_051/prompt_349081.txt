I am a high school student and an officer of my school's philosophy club, and I have been asked to produce the final joint statement for a year-long exchange between two of our members on the problem of dirty hands in politics. The exchange has been between two positions. One member defends what I will call the dirty-hands view: there are cases in which a public official ought, all things considered, to take some action—deceive the public, break a promise to an ally, authorize a lethal operation—and yet that action remains genuinely wrong, not merely regrettable; the official should afterwards feel something stronger than regret, something closer to guilt, and should try to make amends. The other member defends what I will call the lesser-evil view: in those same cases, the official faces a situation in which every available option violates some normally important moral requirement, and the option the official ought to take is the lesser evil; because it is what they ought to do, it cannot also be wrong, and the proper response is sorrow or regret at the value sacrificed rather than guilt for a wrongful action.

The two members agree that public officials sometimes face agonizing choices, that feeling terrible after choosing well is often appropriate, and that a good outcome does not by itself sanitize a decision. But after a year they have realized they may partly be talking past each other, so they have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration.

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in ethics and political philosophy. Think carefully, start by brainstorming the distinctions and test cases you would need, and then draft the document these two members should have been able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Do not write a speech for either side; the document should be one both parties can put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. Format it as a numbered Agreed Claims section followed by a Disagreements table.

The agreed claims section should list the substantive claims about political action, wrongdoing, justification, regret, guilt, and role obligations that both sides can sign, phrased so that they are not vacuously broad. The disagreements table should state the remaining disagreements precisely, each formulated so that a reasonable advocate of that side would accept the wording as an accurate statement of their own view; it should also flag any disagreements that are at least partly verbal and say exactly what is verbal about them. Then identify the genuine cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux, give one or two concrete cases or kinds of evidence both sides agree bear directly on it, and record each side's prediction about the case in advance. I suspect one crux is whether being all-things-considered what one ought to do is compatible with the act's still being wrong, and another is whether holding public office can make an otherwise impermissible act permissible, but do not simply adopt my guesses.
