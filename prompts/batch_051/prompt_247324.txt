I'm giving the five-minute opening talk at a workshop called something like Ethics in the First Five Seconds. The room is half moral philosophers who could reconstruct Bernard Williams's drowning-wife-and-stranger example from memory, and half fire officers and paramedics who will notice immediately if I'm romanticizing their jobs. I want to build the whole talk around Williams's one-thought-too-many idea: the husband who cannot act until he has formulated a justification for saving his wife rather than the stranger has already let something go wrong. I want that idea to be the load-bearing beam of the talk, not a decoration. It should explain why the emergency responders' habit of acting first and articulating later is not a cognitive defect, and also why the philosophers' demand for a prior justification can sound like asking the responder to have exactly the extra thought Williams warned about. But it has to land as respect for both halves of the room, and the practitioners should feel the philosophers need them there rather than feeling like specimens. No jargon until the idea has earned it. End on an unsettled question, not a thesis.
