I'm a retired natural scientist and I've been reading a lot of philosophy lately, mostly the debate between Humean and anti-Humean views about laws of nature. Maybe you can tell me whether a parallel I've been chewing on is real or just me pattern-matching.

I spent my career fitting models to data, and the thing you learn early is that the best-fitting model is never the one you want: it overfits, it memorizes noise, and it breaks when you try to use it on anything new. So you trade off fit against complexity. There's real machinery for this — AIC, cross-validation, minimum description length, the bias-variance tradeoff. The model you pick isn't the one that fits perfectly; it's the one that balances simplicity and strength, and you choose among models by that balance.

Now I've been reading philosophy of science, and the best-system account of laws (David Lewis's, roughly) says something that sounds nearly identical: the laws are the theorems of whichever true systematization of all the particular facts does best at combining simplicity and strength. It's as if nature's laws are just the model with the best AIC for the whole mosaic of events.

So: is this a deep structural identity — is selecting laws just model selection applied to all of history — or a loose analogy that falls apart under inspection? If it's deep, tell me what has to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and which results actually do; for instance, do the usual overfitting arguments tell us anything about why a "system" that lists every fact as its own law would be a bad theory of laws? If it's superficial, show me where the mapping breaks. One place I can already see trouble: in model selection you're picking something to predict data you havn't seen, but the best system is supposed to be the true description of everything, so there's no held-out test set. Does that kill the analogy, or does it just force you to define "overfitting" differently? And is the simplicity in model selection the same sort of simplicity Lewis needs, or is that where my parallel quietly changes meaning?

I'd rather be told the analogy is bad than have it flattered.
