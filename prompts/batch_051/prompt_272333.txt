Take Frank Jackson's Mary thought experiment—the one where she grows up in a black-and-white room but learns all the physical facts about color. Now modify it: Mary isn't totally color-deprived. From birth she has seen pure red, pure green, and pure blue patches, exactly one at a time, but never any mixture, never white, yellow, magenta, cyan, and so on. She still knows all the physical facts about color vision, including opponent-process and mixing facts. One day she's shown a patch that is a red-green equiluminant mixture, which normal observers see as yellow. Does she learn something new?

Here's the twist: John Stuart Mill drew a distinction between what he called homopathic laws, where the joint effect of several causes is just the sum of their separate effects, and what he called heteropathic laws, where the combination produces a genuinely new effect, like a chemical compound. So the real question is whether color qualia are homopathic or heteropathic in Mill's sense. Does the standard knowledge-argument get stronger, weaker, or just change shape in this variant?
