I am not a philosopher or a mathematician; I have been working through something on my own and want a sharp verdict rather than a survey. I have been wondering whether the question "is 2 a member of 4?"—using "member of" in the set-theoretic sense, as in "a is a member of {a}"—has an answer before any particular way of building the numbers is chosen. I arrived at the following argument.

(1) The natural numbers are whatever plays the role of the sequence 0, 1, 2, 3, ... with the right successor relation; a number is its place in that pattern, not the material filling the place.
(2) There is more than one way to fill the pattern with sets, and both ways do the arithmetic. In one way, each number is the set of all earlier numbers: 0 is the empty set, 1 is {0}, 2 is {0,1}, 3 is {0,1,2}, and 4 is {0,1,2,3}. In the other way, each number is the set whose only member is the previous number: 0 is the empty set, 1 is {0}, 2 is {1}, 3 is {2}, and 4 is {3}.
(3) These two ways agree on everything a number has to do—order, addition, multiplication, and so on—and differ only in what the items at the positions look like internally.
(4) In the first way, 2 is a member of 4, because 4 = {0,1,2,3}. In the second way, 2 is not a member of 4, because 4 = {3}, and 2 is not 3.
(5) If there were a fact of the matter, prior to any choice of filling, about whether 2 is a member of 4, then two equally correct fillings could not give opposite answers.
(6) So the question "is 2 a member of 4?" has no answer about the numbers themselves; it is like asking whether the letter "a" is serif or sans-serif, which is only a question about a chosen printed version of the letter, not about the letter as such.

I half-suspect the trick is in how the word "is" behaves between steps (1) and (5), but I cannot articulate it. Is this argument valid? If not, exactly which numbered step fails, and under what reading? Please work through the steps rather than giving a general essay.
