I have been trying to pin down what exactly makes an evolutionary-psychology explanation a "just-so story," and I have converged on a position I want checked before I keep using it. I call it the monopoly criterion, and it has a practical corollary I call the two-story rule. Here is the theory.

Start with a few definitions. A rival to an adaptationist explanation E is another explanation of the same trait, specified precisely enough that some observation or other — a cross-species pattern, a developmental trajectory, a design detail, a neural signature, a behavioral measurement — would be more probable under the rival than under E. The rival need not be equally plausible; it only needs to be stated well enough that evidence could tell between the two. An explanation is monopolized when no such rival is on the table, either because none has been proposed or because alternatives get dismissed only after the story has already been fitted.

The central claim is this. The just-so quality of an evolutionary story is not primarily a matter of falsifiability. Almost any story can be rewritten so that something would falsify it; what such stories are missing is not logical risk but competitive pressure. A monopolized story can fit the facts beautifully, but the fit does no evidential work, because the story was fitted against nothing — there is no specific alternative whose failure would matter. So a just-so story is, I want to say, simply a monopolized explanation. This explains why so many adaptationist narratives feel empty even though they are technically falsifiable: the emptiness is not a property of the theory's logic but of its position in an argumentative market with no competitors.

The corollary is the two-story rule: no one should assert that a trait is an adaptation unless they can state at least one rival hypothesis precise enough to be beaten.

An example. Consider the claim that first-trimester nausea is an adaptation that protects the embryo by making the mother avoid plant toxins. As usually presented, this is monopolized: a rival — say, nausea as a side effect of hormonal shifts in early pregnancy, or as the sort of byproduct Gould and Lewontin called a spandrel — is either absent or stated so loosely that no observation would favor it over the adaptation story. On my criterion, then, the story is a just-so story: not necessarily false, but scientifically idle. Put the same story next to a precise rival, and it would be forced to specify discriminating predictions, and would start doing genuine evidential work.

I know this will sound like warmed-over Popper, and like the old injunction to hold several working hypotheses at once. But I think the monopoly criterion is different: Popperian falsifiability is about what a theory in principle rules out, whereas my criterion is about whether the theory is actually positioned so that its failures would benefit a specific alternative.

I want an honest assessment. First, is this already a worked-out position under another name, and if so where does my version diverge from it? Second, give me the three strongest objections to the monopoly criterion and say which, if any, are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable — in particular, can a monopolized story still be good science, and can two bad stories competing with each other become good science merely by competing? Third, is the two-story rule defensible as a methodological norm, or is it too strong, and what does it get wrong about how evidence actually accumulates?
