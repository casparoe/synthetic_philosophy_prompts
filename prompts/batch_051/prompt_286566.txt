I was recently asked to join a working group on cultural heritage policy in my city after a bruising public dispute about a midsummer festival: one side claimed it had been celebrated for centuries, the other showed it was largely stitched together from a Victorian pageant and a 1970s tourism campaign, and each side left convinced the other was arguing in bad faith. The dispute pushed me into reading about how many apparently ancient traditions turn out to have been assembled or reworked rather suddenly, and how the sense of an unbroken communal past is often as much a retrospective construction by institutions as an inheritance from earlier generations. Our group is now considering a proposal that I am not sure how to evaluate.

The proposal would create a Certified Traditions Register. Any community could apply to have a practice certified as an authentic tradition of the region. Certification would require documentary or oral-historical evidence that the practice had been observed continuously for at least seventy-five years before the application date, a named community body authorized to speak for the practice, and a fixed written description of its canonical form and calendar. Certified traditions would receive collective legal protection against commercial use by outsiders, priority access to public grants, a place in the school curriculum, and a formal role in planning decisions affecting associated sites. Applications would be decided by a board of historians, anthropologists, and community representatives.

Please analyze how this institution would actually behave, not whether it sounds defensible in principle. Structure your response in the following sections, in this order. "First-order incentives" (roughly 350 words): the behaviors the scheme would predictably encourage among communities, applicants, and local officials. "Winners and losers" (roughly 300 words): which kinds of traditions and communities would tend to be certified, and which would be excluded, rewritten, or frozen in the process. "Failure modes and gaming" (roughly 400 words): fabricated or backdated archives, strategic splitting or merging of communities, capture by well-organized groups, the board quietly becoming the author of the very traditions it claims only to recognize, and any other failure modes you see. "Fixes and bottom line" (roughly 250 words): which failure modes could be fixed without gutting the scheme, and your overall judgment about what the register would do to the living practice of tradition.
