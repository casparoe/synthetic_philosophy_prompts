I am a novelist at work on a book set around a dynastic succession crisis in a small early modern principality. The plot turns on the thought that the entire succession could have gone otherwise had one trivial mishap not occurred: a courier’s horse throws a shoe, a letter arrives a day late, a marriage alliance is never finalized, and a kingdom that had seemed stable comes apart. A historian friend who read the draft told me I was leaning too hard on what he called the “for want of a nail” view of history. He did not deny that such sequences happen; he used the proverb as though it named an entire philosophy of historical causation, and he plainly considered that philosophy suspect. When I pressed him to say exactly what the proverb asserts, he gave me three formulations in ten minutes — one about small causes having large effects, one about every link in a chain being necessary, and one about history being more fragile than we like to think.

Before I revise, I want to know what I am actually being accused of. Take the proverb that runs from a lost nail to a lost shoe, horse, rider, battle, and kingdom, and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making — about counterfactual dependence, sensitivity to initial conditions, overdetermination, retrospective narrative selection, and whatever else you find in it. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false as a general claim about how history works. Then tell me which reading my friend’s objection needs if it is to stick, and whether that reading holds up. Please separate what is settled among people who seriously study historical causation from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
