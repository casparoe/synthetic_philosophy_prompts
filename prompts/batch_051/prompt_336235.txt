Consider two things a state can do at a single border crossing: refuse to let a foreigner enter, and refuse to let one of its own citizens leave. Ordinary moral and political opinion treats these as radically different. A visa denial, a migrant turned away at a checkpoint, a wall along the boundary — these are widely accepted as normal exercises of sovereign self-governance. An exit permit denied, a border closed against the state's own people — these are treated as something far more serious, a hallmark of an unfree regime rather than an ordinary policy choice. The asymmetry is even written into modern human-rights law, which recognizes a right to leave any country, including one's own, but no corresponding right to enter any particular country. Yet the two acts are structurally the same exercise of power over the same threshold, run in opposite directions. And the standard justifications for the right to exclude entrants — preserving a distinctive political community, protecting institutions and a welfare state, collective self-determination — look as if they would license keeping citizens in just as readily. If a people may decide who joins it, why may it not decide who leaves it? Your task is to determine whether anything justifies this asymmetry. Find the strongest candidate principle, test it against hard cases (for example, a state that prevents doctors and engineers from emigrating in order to protect the very institutions that entry restrictions are supposed to protect), and give an honest verdict: does the principle vindicate the asymmetry, or should one of the two ordinary judgments be revised, and in which direction?
