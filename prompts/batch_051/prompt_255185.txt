I got pulled into a proper row between two mates of mine last week, and it's been nagging at me since, partly because a similar thing came up at work with a text from my boss that I completely misread. Anyway, here's the argument.

Our friend Dan was asked in the group chat whether he could cover someone's Saturday shift. He replied: 'I'm at the in-laws' on Saturday.' That bit was true — he really was at the in-laws' that day. But the shift was an evening shift, and Dan could have made it if he'd wanted to; he just didn't fancy it. Sam, the one asking, read the message as a no and stopped asking.

In the pub later, Ali said Dan didn't lie. What Dan literally said was true, and he didn't assert anything false. The thing Sam took away — that Dan couldn't cover the shift — wasn't said at all; it was inferred. Ali said lying has to attach to what a sentence actually says, not to whatever the hearer happens to read into it, otherwise every polite excuse becomes a lie.

Bea came back hard. She said Ali was collapsing what a person means into what the words mean. What Dan did, she said, was intend Sam to believe Dan couldn't take the shift, by getting Sam to recognise the in-laws remark as a refusal. That's what a speaker means in the philosophical sense — and since the intended belief was false, Dan lied about being unable to work, whatever his words literally stated.

Ali said that's just the distinction between what is said and what is implicated. Lying attaches to what is said, not to what is implicated. Implicatures are cancellable: Dan could have added 'but I can still make the shift' and the message would not contradict itself. If the false bit can be taken back without contradiction, it wasn't part of what he said, so no lie.

Bea replied that cancellability only shows the inference isn't guaranteed by the sentence; it doesn't show Dan didn't intend it on this particular occasion. She said if a person only means what they mean when they couldn't add a retraction afterwards, then speaker meaning is useless for holding people responsible. She also pointed out that Dan had used the in-laws line to dodge shifts three weeks running, so by now the line was a known way of saying no in that group — more like a convention than a one-off hint.

I had to leave before they settled it, and I genuinly couldn't pick a side. I think they both knew the same bits of Grice and were using them in different ways. Can you go through the exchange and adjudicate each move — clearly right, half right, or wrong — then say who comes out ahead overall and what, if anything, both are missing?
