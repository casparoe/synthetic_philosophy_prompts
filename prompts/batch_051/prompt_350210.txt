I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am writing a long-form piece about why the deliberate killing of civilians in the Allied air campaigns of the Second World War has been so easily normalized in public memory. I keep coming back to Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet Mr. Truman's Degree. As I read her, she argues that Truman's decision to use atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki cannot be morally laundered by the distinction between intended and merely foreseen civilian deaths, because the civilian deaths were not a side effect of some independently legitimate military aim. They were, she thinks, the very instrument by which surrender was to be produced. For that reason she treats the choice as murder, and she treats Oxford's decision to confer an honorary degree on Truman as an occasion for scandal rather than celebration.

I have limited space, and I want to spend it where the argumentative pressure is highest. Rank the following unresolved questions by importance, where importance means both how much turns on the answer for contemporary judgments about war and political leaders, and how underexamined the question remains in serious public argument.

1. Whether the Allied firebombing of cities such as Dresden and Tokyo is vulnerable to the same analysis, and whether this should materially change the moral standing of leaders we otherwise remember more favorably than Truman.
2. Whether Anscombe's argument extends to the policy of nuclear deterrence, since deterrence involves the conditional threat to kill the innocent, and whether that extension, if it succeeds, should most trouble defenders of the postwar nuclear order.
3. Whether her use of the intended/foreseen distinction can be made stable enough to handle modern cases of dual-use infrastructure and attacks aimed at reducing enemy military capacity while still reaching her verdict about Hiroshima and Nagasaki.
4. Whether the most durable part of her case is not the strategic analysis but the idea that certain acts must be unconditionally excluded from practical reasoning, and whether that framing matters more for how we evaluate contemporary leaders who order mass killing under extreme pressure.
5. Whether the least outdated part of her argument is the institutional one: the propriety of universities and comparable bodies conferring honors on political leaders who have ordered such attacks.

For each item, defend its rank briefly, state the criteria you used, and say what would most change your mind: which historical counterexample, empirical finding, or philosophical distinction would move it up or down. If you think the most important line of work is missing from this list, put it first and explain why it outranks the rest. If you believe one of these questions is genuinely settled, say so, and say what would unsettle it.
