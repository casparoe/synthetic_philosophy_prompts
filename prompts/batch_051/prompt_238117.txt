For the past two years, our state public utilities commission has watched the same standoff recur in nearly every rate case. A utility or an intervenor submits an economic model; the other side moves to reject it because one of its assumptions — perfectly informed consumers, costless entry, cost-minimizing firms — is false on its face; staff reply that the assumption is only an idealization and that the model should be judged by its predictions; and the hearing judge is left without a principled way to settle the dispute. The decisions that turn on these models include rate orders, merger conditions, and infrastructure procurement approvals, so the stakes are large. I have been asked to write a standing decision procedure for staff that determines, model by model, when an unrealistic assumption is a legitimate ground for rejecting or heavily discounting a model and when it is not.

Ground the procedure in the Friedmanite position often called the F-twist: roughly, that the descriptive realism of a model's assumptions is not by itself a legitimate test of the model, and that predictive success is the standard that matters. A stronger reading of the position also treats sufficiently unrealistic assumptions as a virtue; the procedure should either adopt or reject that stronger reading and say what difference the choice makes in practice. Do not pretend the F-twist is an algorithm. Most of what the commission uses models for is counterfactual policy analysis, and the models seldom come with a clean, agreed predictive track record. The procedure must therefore preserve the distinctions the F-twist actually commits us to, and must say explicitly where the F-twist runs out and some other standard — a legal burden of proof, an economic judgment, an engineering benchmark — has to do the work.

The analysts who will use the procedure have public policy degrees and are comfortable with spreadsheets but have no formal training in philosophy of science or econometrics. The procedure must be usable by one of them in a single working day per model without that training, and it must be precise enough that our general counsel and chief economist, who will both have to sign off on it, can see where it stops being the F-twist and starts being something else.

The procedure must handle at least these hard cases:

- an acknowledged idealization, such as zero transaction costs, versus an assumption that is really a substantive claim about the causal mechanism at work;
- a model that fits historical data well but in which the false assumption is doing most of the predictive work, so that success on the data does not transfer to the policy counterfactual;
- a model used to answer a counterfactual question for which no directly relevant outcome data can exist, so that predictive success cannot be checked at all;
- a model in which one of the assumptions is exactly the proposition the model is brought in to establish, so that treating it as an assumption would make the exercise circular.

Then apply the procedure to these two worked cases, showing what an analyst would write down at each step.

Case A. A retail electricity competition model is submitted to support new consumer-protection rules. It assumes that all consumers are perfectly informed and switch immediately to the cheapest tariff. The model has forecast average retail prices in the state with small error over a decade. In the current docket, however, the question is whether vulnerable customers in practice capture the advertised savings from switching. Show how the procedure treats the switching assumption differently depending on which question the model is being used to answer, and explain why a good historical forecasting record is not decisive for the second question.

Case B. A water utility submits a cost-benchmarking model to argue that its operating costs are efficient. The model assumes that the utility behaves as if it already faced competitive pressure to minimize costs. There is no competitive market for water service in the state, and the model can offer no track record of predictions about this utility's costs under competition, because that counterfactual has never been observed. Show how the procedure handles a case in which the F-twist's own test cannot be run, and identify at least one step at which the procedure forces a conclusion the commission will not like.

Conclude by stating plainly what the procedure cannot decide, exactly where an analyst's judgment or a burden of proof is doing the work, and which part of the procedure is most likely to let a model through over the objection of someone who simply finds its assumptions absurd.
