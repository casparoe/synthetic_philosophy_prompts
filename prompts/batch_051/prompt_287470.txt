My friend's a clerk for a state-court judge who hears a lot of appeals from a city with a Riverwalk. We've been collecting her rulings to figure out whether she's actually a textualist or a purposivist, because the decisions don't sort cleanly. The ordinance in every case is the same one: "No person shall operate a motorized vehicle on the Riverwalk." Her track record, as best we can reconstruct it from slip opinions:

- A delivery moped driven at about 20 mph: violation. She said it was a straightforward motor vehicle.
- A man in an electric wheelchair moving at walking speed: no violation. She said wheelchairs are mobility aids, not "motorized vehicles" in the relevant ordinary sense.
- A city groundskeeper driving a gasoline cart down the path to carry pruning tools from one planter to the next: no violation. Her opinion leaned on the idea that he wasn't operating it as traffic, just using it as a rolling tool chest.
- A kid's ride-on electric Jeep moving at 2 mph with a parent walking beside it: no violation. She said toy cars aren't "vehicles" in the sense the ordinance uses.
- A motorized skateboard weaving through a crowd at 15 mph: violation. She called it the paradigm case.

I can read the record as strict textualism with some tight definitional moves, or as a quiet purposivism where the law is really about fast, dangerous traffic and everything harmless falls out. I can't tell weather the definitional moves are doing real work or are just fig leaves for a result she already likes. Next week's case might split those two readings. For the Veterans Day parade, the committee drove a restored WWII jeep down the Riverwalk at walking speed, with barriers up and marshals walking alongside. The committee says it was ceremonial and no one was endangered; the city says a jeep is a jeep and the text has no parade exception.

Which way would she most likely rule on the jeep? I'd like the best competing accounts of her record, which one you'd bet on, and a further case that would cleanly separate them.
