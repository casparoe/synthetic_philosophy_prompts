I've ended up as the mediator between two people in my seminar who've been argueing for months about idealization in scientific models, and they've finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration. I want you to draft the document they'd produce: the joint statement both of them could sign, not a brief for one side.

The two positions, roughly. The instrumentalist — call her the Friedman/F-twist person — holds that a model's assumptions don't need to be realistic as long as its predictions are good. It's fine to model a billiard player as though she were solving the physics equations, a firm as though it were maximizing profit, or a foraging bird as though it were computing an optimal patch-leaving rule. The assumptions are just a compact way to generate predictions, and asking whether the agent 'really' does the computation is beside the point. The realist about assumptions holds the opposite: if the internal machinery is false — point masses, non-interacting molecules, full information, unlimited computational power — then matching the data isn't explanation; it's curve fitting, and the model's success either is luck or comes from something the model doesn't actually represent.

They've both staked positions on a shared pile of cases, and the document has to engage with them: (a) planets treated as point masses; (b) the ideal gas law treating molecules as non-interacting point particles; (c) firms modeled as literal profit-maximizers with full information; (d) the foraging-bird model above, which predicts patch-leaving times pretty well though the bird isn't doing math; (e) a climate model that represents clouds through a crude tunable parameter no one thinks is physically correct, included mainly to make the model fit observed temperatures; and (f) a huge agent-based traffic simulation that represents each car and driver fairly realistically but is too unwieldy to predict or explain jam formation better than a toy model does.

Please write it with these sections in this order:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list, ten items or so (about 350 words). Claims both can sign about idealization, prediction, explanation, what 'as if' talk is doing, and when false assumptions do and don't matter; at least three of cases (a)–(f) should show up. At some point address the distinction between idealizations whose error is controlled and regime-limited and assumptions that are just false in a way that could be doing hidden predictive work. Don't make these lowest-common-denominator mush; they should be substantive enough that it's slightly surprising both camps sign them.

2. Disagreements. A table (about 450 words). Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by this disagreements table, then the rest. Columns: The issue; The instrumentalist's own wording of her view; The realist's own wording of his view; What would count as evidence against her/his own view. Four or five rows. Each side must accept the exact wording in their column — no strawmen.

3. Cruxes. Three or four sub-questions (about 250 words) such that settling them would move at least one side. For each, say why both sides agree it's a crux, and phrase it so both accept the wording of the question.

4. Tests and advance predictions. For at least three of the cruxes, describe a concrete case, data pattern, or modeling exercise that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance (about 400 words). Pick tests where the side that's wrong would actually have to eat crow; avoid tests where both predict the same thing.

5. Verbal disputes. A short section (about 150 words) saying which parts of the fight, if any, are really about the words 'explanation' or 'approximately true' rather than about the world, and which parts are genuine.
