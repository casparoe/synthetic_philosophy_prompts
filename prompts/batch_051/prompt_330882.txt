Me and a few friends were complaining about how bad we all are at the small stuff—saying "I'll be there at 7" and strolling in at 7:45, or agreeing to help someone move and then bailing the morning of. Nobody thinks it's a huge deal, but it erodes something over time. I've been sketching an app to fix it mechanically. The core is an escrow pool: you link a payment method, and whenever you accept an in-person event from someone in your circle, the app pulls, say, $10 and holds it for you. Show up within 15 minutes of the start time (GPS geofence around the location), and the money goes back to you. Cancel at least three hours ahead, same thing. If you're later than 15 minutes, or you no-show, the $10 gets split among the other people who actually showed up (if everyone flakes, it rolls into the next event). I know how I'd build the geofencing, the payments, and a public on-time scoreboard; that part's straightforward.

What I actually want to think through is how the thing would behave once real people are using it. Who does it actually discipline, and who just eats the $10 as a fee? What obvious games open up—location spoofing, fake events, invites sent to people you know will be late, refund arbitrage? Do people respond by making fewer small promises, or by getting sneaky? And what does it do to the relationship if my being on time is now partly a payment to you rather than something I did because I said I would? Could you patch the worst failure modes (an appeal for genuine emergencies, some kind of cap) without the patches making it all toothless, or is the whole premise of pricing lateness the problem?
