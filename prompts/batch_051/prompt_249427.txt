Suppose that in 1860 the British Army, rather than letting dueling among officers wither under informal social disapproval and occasional legal pressure, establishes a standing Court of Honor. Any officer who claims to have been insulted may bring a complaint; the court may extract a public retraction or apology, impose monetary reparation, publish its findings in the service gazette, and effectively end the career of any officer who refuses its jurisdiction or defies its verdict. It has no power to punish physically. Pistols, the press, and civilian law remain as they were. Predict, with reasoning, how dueling among British officers changes over the next fifty years: does it collapse faster, persist as ritual, migrate underground, or survive only in certain regiments? Make explicit the mechanisms by which the duel actually served officers—restoring honor, displaying courage, maintaining equality among them—and assess whether this court can substitute for each. Indicate which links in your reasoning are robust and which are guesses; state what would have to differ for your prediction to fail; and conclude with what the outcome implies about whether such practices end through moral argument, interest, or the redirection of honor into new institutions.
