In my AP Gov class two kids got into a whole argument about a judge and a made up case, and neither would back down, so I told them I'd find someone to go through it point by point. The setup they agreed on: a city has a boardwalk rule banning any motorized vehicle on the boardwalk between 6 am and 10 pm. The city copied the rule from a state trail law, but the state law had an exception for motorized wheelchairs and other mobility aids, and the city left that exception out when it wrote its own rule. A guy gets a $250 ticket for using his electric wheelchair on the boardwalk, and a judge is deciding whether to throw it out. Marcus said the judge has to enforce the text: a motorized wheelchair is obviously a motorized vehicle, and the fact that the city had the exception sitting right there and deleted it shows the omission was on purpose, so adding it back would just be the judge writing the law. Dana said this is exactly what the absurdity doctrine is for: the whole point of the rule was to keep fast or loud stuff from endangering walkers, and a wheelchair going four miles an hour doesn't threaten anyone, so applying the rule here defeats the reason for having it. Marcus said "absurd" can't just mean "bad policy"; maybe the council wanted the boardwalk kept totally clear of all wheels since its super narrow, and a judge can't read their minds. Dana said the doctrine isn't about just any harsh result, it's about results so clearly at odds with the law's own purpose, or so contradictory with the city's other rules like disability access, that no reasonable person could think the council meant it, and the deleted exception was probably a drafting slip, not a real decision. Marcus came back that if there's a later access law the judge should say that law limits the boardwalk rule, but she shouldn't pretend the words "motorized vehicle" don't cover a wheelchair, because that lets judges pretend they're just interpreting whenever they actually change the law. Dana said the outcome is the same either way, and some drafting accidents have to be fixable even without a second law, because otherwise every typo becomes law. Marcus said if deleting an exception that was sitting right there isn't evidence of intent, nothing is, and you're basically calling the council too dumb to know what it was doing. Dana said that's sort of what the doctrine asks: is it plausible the drafters actually pictured this case and wanted this result. Who's right about which specific points, and what are they both missing?
