I was talking to some friends in the math department about the four-colour theorem, and I keep coming back to what looks like a weird double standard. Nobody blinks at using a computer to multiply two hundred-digit numbers or to run a numerical simulation, and nobody asks to see those steps. And nobody blinks at accepting a forty-page proof on a single human referee's say-so, even though referees are sleepy, biased, and miss serious errors all the time. But when a proof rests on a computer exhaustively checking thousands of cases — the four-colour theorem, and the same unease came back with the Kepler conjecture — a lot of mathematicians get uncomfortable. They'll say 'its probably true, but I don't feel I understand it' or 'that's not mathematics, that's just computation.' Yet the computer's check is, if anything, more reliable than the referee's: computers don't drift off halfway through the five-thousandth case, and the code can be checked once and rerun by anyone. So the practice looks asymmetric: we demand surveyability, insight, a human narrative from machine-checked proofs, while holding human-checked proofs to a lower standard of actual reliability.

I'd like you to find the strongest justification you can for that asymmetry — maybe it has to do with what proofs are for, or explanation versus mere checking, or with trusting a person rather than a process — test it against some awkward nearby cases, and then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, given up, or reversed.
