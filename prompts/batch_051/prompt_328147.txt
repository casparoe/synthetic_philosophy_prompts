i keep coming back to the surprise exam paradox after it resurfaced in a rationalist-adjacent forum thread, and i'd love to get a poem out of it rather than another argumentative comment. The setup, in case: a teacher announces on friday that there will be an exam on exactly one day the following week, and that it will be a surprise — meaning the students won't be able to know in advance which day. The students reason backward: it can't be friday, because if it hasn't happened by thursday evening then friday would be the only remaining day and so it wouldn't be a surprise; with friday gone, it can't be thursday either, because wednesday night would then force thursday; and the same move eats its way back through wednesday, tuesday, and monday. So they conclude there can be no surprise exam at all. Then the exam comes on wednesday, and they're surprised anyway. What gets me is that the backward induction feels completely valid while your doing it and totally irrelevant once the exam actually lands.

Could you write me a pantoum about this? It feels like the right form because a pantoum keeps handing its own lines back to itself — the second and fourth lines of one stanza become the first and third of the next, and the poem finally loops around to its opening lines — which seems to track the way each day's elimination becomes the premise for the next day's elimination and the argument ends up back where it started. I'd like the repeated lines to carry the two competing threads: the announcement that the exam will come and will be a surprise, and the induction step that the last possible day can't be the day, so it's out. By the end they should be tangled the way they are in the paradox, where the very reasoning that rules the exam out seems to be what lets it arrive as a surprise. It should work as a poem for someone who's never met the paradox, and for someone who knows the logic it should be legible enough that they can see the friday-to-monday elimination and the wednesday punchline. And please no headings, no bold or italics, no commentary before or after — just the poem with its line breaks and stanza breaks, plain, the way it would look in a plain text email.
