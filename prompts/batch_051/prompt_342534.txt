I have been turning over Newcomb's problem after a friend introduced me to it, and I want to settle a variant I have not seen laid out anywhere. In the standard version, an opaque box contains £1,000,000 if a predictor has predicted that you will take only that box, and nothing if it has predicted that you will take both boxes; a transparent box always contains £1,000. I want to weaken the predictor and add a choice about information. Suppose the predictor is right 70% of the time in both directions: on occasions when you end up taking only the opaque box, it was filled 70% of the time, and on occasions when you end up taking both boxes, it was empty 70% of the time. Now add the following: before you choose, you are offered a free, optional, perfectly reliable opportunity to learn what is actually in the opaque box. The predictor makes its prediction before the boxes are sealed but, as in the original problem, it is good at anticipating what someone in your exact situation will do, including whether you will take the opportunity to look.

Please work through this carefully for the two familiar ways of thinking about the problem. The first says that once the boxes are fixed, taking both boxes is always £1,000 better, because your choice cannot change what is already inside. The second says that your choice is evidence about what the predictor did, so it can be right to take only the opaque box even though, at the moment of choice, the contents are fixed. For each of these two outlooks, I would like to know: what should you do if you are not offered the peek, and should you take the peek if it is free? Then find the predictor accuracy at which the second outlook is exactly indifferent between choosing in ignorance and taking the peek, and show how the £1,000 in the transparent box enters that threshold. Finally, I once read that Good's theorem says free information can never be expected to harm a rational agent. Does the theorem survive in exactly this variant — for each of the two outlooks — and if it fails for one of them, which of the theorem's usual assumptions is being quietly broken by the Newcomb situation? Please write your answer in British English.
