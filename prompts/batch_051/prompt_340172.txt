I have been teaching Schopenhauer to graduate students and keep worrying about a tension in The World as Will and Representation that my students are too quick to dissolve. In the second book, Schopenhauer describes the intellect as, in its normal functioning, a mere instrument of the will: its business is to survey the environment, construct representations, and present motives, all in the service of the will's unceasing striving. In the third book, he describes aesthetic experience as a state in which one becomes a pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge, momentarily released from willing and therefore from the suffering that willing brings. The apparent conflict is this: if the intellect is essentially a tool of the will, how can it ever bring about, or even survive, a state in which the will is no longer in charge? I want you to decide whether these two descriptions can be reconciled without weakening one of them into a mere façon de parler. Lay out the strongest candidate reconciliations: perhaps the will-less state is not an achievement of the intellect but rather the result of a special kind of object or relation; perhaps the servitude claim concerns only the intellect's ordinary function rather than its whole nature; perhaps Schopenhauer is speaking at different explanatory levels. Then test each reconciliation against what he actually needs: the intellect must still know something in aesthetic experience, but not in a way that reinserts willing. Which reconciliation is most faithful to the text, and which ones are special pleading? If none works, say what Schopenhauer should have said instead, and whether the failure damages only his aesthetics or also his broader pessimism about the possibility of any genuine release from the will.
