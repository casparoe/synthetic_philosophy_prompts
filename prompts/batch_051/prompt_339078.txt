I lead the control-systems group at a small company that builds autonomous ground vehicles for warehouse and yard logistics. Two years ago we adopted an unusual navigation architecture, and it has since become both our main performance advantage and the source of our most persistent internal dispute. The architecture is, in effect, the careenium thought experiment implemented in software: a swarm of several thousand simple elements — simmballs — that interact locally with one another and with the sensor and actuator interfaces, while the behavior of the whole vehicle is routinely described in high-level terms such as goals, plans, obstacles, hesitations, and confusions. The question that divides my engineers is whether those high-level descriptions are causally real: whether the symbols actually push the simmballs around, or whether the simmballs do all the real causal work and the symbols are only a convenient summary we impose after the fact. This has stopped being a seminar question, because we triage fixes and feature requests roughly once a week, each triage gets at most a few hours before I have to sign off, and choosing the wrong level of intervention either changes nothing or degrades behavior that surfaces later in field tests.

I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run on any proposed intervention to decide whether we should intervene at the symbolic level (editing the planner's rules, the goal registry, or the interpreter's labels), intervene at the substrate level (retuning simmball parameters, altering local interaction rules, or adjusting interface gains), or reject the proposal as a no-op. The procedure should lay out the ordered questions, the evidence that would let us answer each question and move on, the conditions under which it should stop with a refusal, and the conditions under which it must escalate to a person because the remaining question is not empirical. It should also mark the steps that look like tests but are really judgment calls. Then run the procedure on the three proposals currently on our board: (1) the vehicle circles a fixed point in the test yard and the high-level log says 'planner stuck in a loop'; one engineer proposes adding a symbolic rule that detects repeated plan cycles and breaks symmetry; (2) another engineer proposes increasing the global coupling strength among all simmballs to reduce indecision, over the objection that this will destroy the obstacle-avoidance behavior visible at the high level; (3) a product manager wants to add a new high-level goal, 'avoid gravel surfaces,' by writing it into the goal registry with no simmball-level changes, on the grounds that the goal will then push the simmballs to avoid gravel.
