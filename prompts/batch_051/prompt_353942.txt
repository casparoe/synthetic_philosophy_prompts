I'm a PhD student in clinical psychology studying self-referential thought, and I keep running into a frustrating exchange in our lab's reading group. On the empirical side, I work with findings suggesting that self-focused attention is not a single thing: reflective, curious self-examination often looks adaptive, but rumination — repetitive, passive, negatively valenced brooding — reliably predicts the onset, severity, and recurrence of depressive episodes and appears to impair problem-solving. On the philosophical side, one of my more philosophically minded colleagues always ends the discussion by appealing to the old injunction "know thyself." His move, as best I can reconstruct it, is that inward-looking self-scrutiny is the surest route to self-knowledge, so more of it cannot be the real problem; if some inward-looking states happen to feel bad, that is a feature of accurate self-knowledge, not a harm. I suspect this trades on an ambiguity in the slogan, but I am not sure how to make that precise.

Please act as an expert in philosophy of psychology and moral psychology, and think carefully about the question before answering — ideally beginning by brainstorming the distinct things someone might mean by "know thyself." I would like a menu of the precise claims that injunction could express, ranging from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is defensible in light of what is known about rumination and self-focused attention, and whether it actually licenses my colleague's inference that self-examination cannot be harmful in the relevant way. Then say which reading or readings he would need to make the slogan do that argumentative work, and whether any plausible reading does. Finally, sketch what a well-motivated clinician should actually say about when self-examination becomes harmful. Please write in American English.
