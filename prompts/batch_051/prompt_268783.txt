I'm putting together a handout for an upper-level metaethics seminar, and I want to make sure I have genuinely distinct strategies by which an expressivist can answer the Frege–Geach problem. As I'll frame it for the class: if an atomic moral claim such as 'Stealing is wrong' primarily expresses a noncognitive attitude — say, disapproval of stealing — rather than a belief, it becomes puzzling how that sentence embeds under negation, conditionals, disjunction, and the other logical operators. A speaker who endorses 'If stealing is wrong, then encouraging your little brother to steal is wrong' need not disapprove of stealing itself, yet the antecedent still seems to contribute its content to the conditional in the standard compositional way.

So far my handout lists five families of response. First, Blackburn's quasi-realist solution, on which the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude toward combinations of lower-order attitudes: roughly, approval of having a package of attitudes that pairs disapproval of the antecedent with something less than disapproval of the consequent. Second, Gibbard's norm-expressivist semantics, which models normative states of mind as plans or hyperplans and assigns to normative sentences sets of factual-normative worlds — world-plan pairs — so that embedding is handled by relations of plan-consistency rather than by belief-content alone. Third, Schroeder's being-for solution, which gives up on disapproval as the core attitude and posits a single, maximally general noncognitive attitude of being for, directed at represented properties of actions, so that negation and disjunction can compose over the object of the attitude. Fourth, hybrid or ecumenical expressivism, according to which a moral judgment expresses both a belief-like state and a desire-like state, with the belief component doing the compositional work in embedded contexts while the desire-like component carries the practical or normative punch. Fifth, deflationist or minimalist assimilation, which treats truth and propositionhood as cheap, low-cost notions, so the expressivist stops resisting the idea that moral sentences are truth-apt, borrows ordinary truth-conditional composition for embedded contexts, and then locates the expressivism in the conative state involved in accepting even those truth-apt moral claims.

My question is whether there is a sixth answer that is genuinely different from these five — not a restatement of one of them, not a special case, and not a mere combination of them. If you can sketch one, I would want a sentence or two on the mechanism by which it secures composition, a sentence on how it differs from the five families above, and your honest assessment of whether it actually solves the Frege–Geach problem or merely relocates it. I care about the idea itself, not about whether it already has a name in the literature.
