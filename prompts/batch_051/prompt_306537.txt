(1) mental images are depictive not descriptive/propositional — they represent the way maps or pictures do rather than the way sentences do, and that's a fact about representational format, not just a manner of speaking.

(2) the classic behavioral results (scanning time scaling with distance, rotation time scaling with angle) are robust, and they're my main positive reason for (1).

(3) but i also think Pylyshyn's tacit-knowledge/demand-characteristics critique is basically right: subjects in these experiments are trying to do what the situation seems to ask for, so the reaction-time data on their own don't force the depictive reading.

(4) i still think the neuroimaging evidence saves depictivism anyway: early visual cortex lighting up topographically during imagery is what you'd expect if a genuinely picture-like representation is being used, and not what you'd expect if it's just a description being interpreted.

(5) i'm a functionalist about the mind. if two systems are functionally equivalent, there's no further fact about what their internal vehicles "really are."

(6) i think whether imagery is depictive or propositional is a substantive empirical question about architecture, not something a conceptual argument can dissolve.

(7) i also suspect the conscious image is usually explanatorily idle — the real work is done by nonconscious representations and the felt image is a byproduct.
