Okay so here's where I'm coming from: I'm a backend engineer, I've spent years building distributed systems, so stuff like consensus, leader election, replication, two-phase commit, gossip protocols, split brain, quorums, vector clocks — that's all native vocabulary for me. I have no formal philosophy background, but I picked up somewhere that philosophers have a whole debate about what makes two people's action genuinely *joint*, as apposed to just two individual actions happening at the same time. Like the classic walking-together thing: two people walking side by side by agreement vs two strangers who happen to be walking in the same direction at the same speed. I want to see whether my distributed-systems mental model actually covers what the philosophers are on about, or whether it misses something real.

The way I'd naively translate it: a joint action is just a bunch of agents running a shared protocol. They each have a local copy of a plan, they did some handshake to align on it, they execute their own part while monitoring each other (heartbeats, acks, maybe a gossip protocol to converge on common knowledge of what everyone's doing), and their subplans have to be compatible in the same way two services' API contracts have to be compatible. When you exit, you send a leave message or time out. Feels pretty complete to me.

But I gather there are at least three positions in the philosophy literature I should try to map:
1. The reductionist one: a shared intention just is individual intentions plus common knowledge plus subplans that mesh (or something like that).
2. The "we-intention is primitive" one: each person has an irreducible 'we intend to do X' attitude that can't be decomposed into 'I intend' plus beliefs about the other person.
3. The "joint commitment" one: when we agree to do something, we create a commitment that is *ours*, not mine-and-yours, so neither of us can just dissolve it unilaterally — if I bail mid-walk, I owe you something, I can't just stop as if I'd been walking alone.

Here's what I want. Restate each of those three positions in distributed-systems terms, as faithfully as you can — a mapping table would be great. For each one, identify which claims translate cleanly (and what they translate to), which claims you had to fudge or distort to make them fit, and which claims have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Then give me the residue report. In particular, I'm suspicious of two places where my model may be cheating:
- Common knowledge. I know from distributed systems that literal common knowledge isn't achievable once messages can be delayed or lost — there are impossibility results around that. But the reductionist account seems to assume it. So is that account loading in something my world says you can't even implement?
- The obligation part. A protocol violation in my world is a bug or a crash, not a wrong against another party. When the theory says I can't unilaterally dissolve 'our' commitment, is that just a liveness/safety property, or is there a normative residue with no distributed-systems translation?

And then the actual thing I want to settle: is my "joint action is just nodes running a shared protocol" take basically right, or is the stuff that doesn't survive the translation exactly the stuff the philosophers care about? In other words, is the residue a bug in my vocabulary, or is it evidence that some of those philosophical claims are less crisp than they look?
