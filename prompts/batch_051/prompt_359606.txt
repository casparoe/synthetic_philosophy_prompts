I've been building my own Bongard problems as a way to think about how concepts are learned from very few examples, and I've worked out an account of what is going on in them that I'd like checked. (Bongard problems are the puzzles where you see six figures on the left and six on the right and must find the rule separating them.) I call my view the shadow account of concept induction. For an intended rule R, define R's shadow as the set of alternative rules R′ such that (1) R′ classifies all twelve displayed figures exactly as R does, (2) R′ would classify some unseen figure differently, and (3) R′ is at least as simple, or at least as natural to a typical human solver, as R. My first claim is that the felt difficulty of a Bongard problem tracks the size and salience of R's shadow, not the complexity of R itself: a complicated rule with an empty shadow is easy, while a simple rule with a crowded shadow is maddening. My second, stronger claim is that solving a Bongard problem is never pure induction from the twelve examples, because the examples always underdetermine the rule. What actually happens is that the solver's prior over rules suppresses most of the shadow, and the problem is solvable only to the extent that the solver's prior overlaps with the rule-maker's. In short, I think a Bongard problem is a test of shared salience, not of induction.

Here is the kind of case that convinced me. Suppose the left panel contains six large circles and the right panel contains six small squares. The intended rule might be 'circles on the left,' but 'large figures on the left' and 'curved outlines on the left' also fit all twelve displayed examples; the rules sit in one another's shadows because a small circle or a large square would sort differently under each. I suspect this is not a defect of the example but the normal condition of concept induction from a few cases. I'd like an honest assessment. Is the shadow a real construct, or a relabeling of older ideas about underdetermination, simplicity, grue-like predicates, or Bayesian concept learning? Where does it break as an account of difficulty—can a problem with a crowded shadow be instantly solved, and can a problem with a near-empty shadow be impossible? And is my second claim too strong? Once prior overlap is factored out, is there anything left that deserves the name induction, or does the distinction between prior overlap and induction collapse?
