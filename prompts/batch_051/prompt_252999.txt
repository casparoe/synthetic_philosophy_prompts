I've been helping run a restorative justice circle at my school, and last week the group got genuinely stuck on whether it still makes sense to resent someone for the harm they caused when their whole life has been one long chain of bad breaks, neglect, and untreated mental illness. One facilitator kept saying hurt people hurt people as if that dissolved the question of blame, and I kept wanting to push back without being able to say exactly why. A friend later sent me an episode of a philosophy podcast, and the host summarized P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" somethign like this.

The host's gloss was roughly: Strawson says the entire free will debate is beside the point, because no matter what we decide about determinism, we cannot actually stop feeling resentment, gratitude, guilt, and the like. These "reactive attitudes" are what it means to treat someone as a responsible person rather than an object, and since even hard determinists go on feeling them in daily life, determinism is no real threat to moral responsibility. The question whether we have free will turns out to be irrelevant to whether we may hold people responsible. According to the host, Strawson thinks we suspend these attitudes only when we take "the objective stance," treating the person the way we would treat a patient with a mental illness, and since that stance is reserved for people we regard as abnormal or incapacitated, the normal case of blaming is fully justified by our natural emotional responses. So Strawson supposedly proved that if holding someone responsible feels natural, it is justified, and a hard determinist who tries to train himself out of resentment is just being sentimental.

I know enough to suspect there's a slippage between "we can't help feeling it" and "it's justified," and I have a vague memory that Strawson distinguishes excusing someone from taking an objective, managing stance toward them, which the host maybe runs together. But I can't reconstruct the argument, and my friend insists the host got it exactly right. Could you walk me through the summary piece by piece and tell me which parts a careful Strawson defender would accept, which parts they'd wince at or call a popularized distortion, and whether the closing claim about blame being justified actually follows from the accurate parts?
