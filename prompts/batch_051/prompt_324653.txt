I retired twelve years ago from a research career in physical chemistry and have lately been reading moral philosophy with a small discussion group. Last week two of the regulars—I will call them A and B—argued about a small, deliberately ordinary case, and I kept out of it. I compressed my notes of the exchange below. I do not have a settled view, and I would value a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general impression.

The case they used was this. A man habitually pours spent motor oil down the storm drain behind his house. He then reproaches his neighbour for applying far more fertilizer to her lawn than the local watershed guidance allows, so that the excess runs off into the same stream. Everyone granted that the fertilizer runoff is a genuine environmental wrong, that the oil disposal is a wrong of roughly the same kind and seriousness, and that the oil disposal neither causes nor excuses the runoff. The disagreement was over whether the neighbour may properly resist his reproach.

A: That man has no standing to reproach her. The runoff is wrong, and his oil dumping does not cancel her wrong. But a person who dumps oil into the same drain system cannot set himself up as an enforcer of the very norm he flouts. Her "clean up your own mess first" is not an excuse; it is a refusal to be called to account by someone who exempts himself from the standard while invoking it against her. His blame is not a contribution to a shared practice; it is the exercise of a private privilege.

B: That is a version of the ad hominem fallacy. The proposition that excess fertilizer runoff is wrong is true or false independently of who utters it, and you have granted that it is true. The neighbour's first question should be whether the charge against her is accurate, not whether the man's own record is clean. If we adopt your principle, wrongs will go unchallenged whenever the person who happens to notice them is imperfect, which is nearly always.

A: You are treating blame as though it were only an assertion. Blame is an interpersonal act: not merely "this is wrong," but "answer for this." The standing to make that demand depends on the relation between the one who blames and the one who is blamed. A man who has never submitted himself to a norm has no authority to summon another person to answer for violating it. The truth of the description survives; the authority to act as its accuser does not.

B: Then you have already conceded the main point, because you say the truth of the charge survives. It follows that the neighbour cannot conclude she did nothing wrong merely because the man is a hypocrite. As for the demand aspect, where is this authority supposed to come from? You seem to be requiring that a critic be blameless before he may criticize. That would silence nearly everyone. A recovering alcoholic is often the best-placed person to tell a friend not to drive drunk; his past makes the warning more credible, not less.

A: I did not say the critic must be blameless, and a past wrong is not what removes standing. The recovered alcoholic has standing because he now applies the norm to himself. What removes standing in the storm-drain case is the man's present exemption: he treats the rule about watershed harm as binding on her but not on him. That is a way of claiming a higher rank, and it displays contempt both for the neighbour and for the rule. It is that continuing exemption, not some earlier lapse, that licenses her refusal to be admonished by him.

B: Even if that is right, what the neighbour gains is only a complaint about the manner of the criticism. She gains no reason to reject its content. She knows the fertilizer runs off, she knows it is wrong, and none of that changes when the man reveals himself to be a hypocrite. I am prepared to say there are two wrongs here: her runoff and his sanctimony. You want the second wrong to nullify the first blame, and that is too strong.

A: That is exactly where we part company. Blame is not a proposition that can be detached from the person who delivers it. It arrives in a particular voice, within a particular relationship, and what it does is summon the addressee to account to the speaker. If the speaker lacks the standing to issue that summons, the blame fails as blame, and the addressee is under no second-personal reason to answer him. She may still have reason to change her practice out of concern for the stream, but not because his reproach supplies one.

B: Then your conclusion has become narrower than your opening suggested. The neighbour still cannot treat the charge as false, and she still has reason to change what she does. You may have shown that the blame is defective as an act, assuming that notion can be made good, but you have not shown that she may set the whole criticism aside. Whether a blame-act is defective and whether its target may reject it are two different questions, and I think you have begun to run them together.

Here the meeting broke up. I would welcome an adjudication of this exchange. In particular, I would like to know which specific claims each speaker is right about, which moves overstate their conclusions, whether the distinction between the truth of a criticism and the standing to make it can be drawn as cleanly as B supposes, and what, if anything, the two of them are jointly missing.
