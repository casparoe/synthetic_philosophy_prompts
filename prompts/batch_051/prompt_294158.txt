I joined the grant committee of a small family foundation last year, after a long illness in my family made me distrust, more than I probably should, the habit of treating lives saved as the only unit that matters. We make about ten grants a year, usually between $50,000 and $250,000, split between global health and farmed animal welfare. We have two hours per proposal, nobody on the committee has formal philosophy training, and every recommendation has to be approved by all five of us. The problem is that we keep re-litigating the same forks from scratch. Some proposals prevent episodes of severe suffering — emergency analgesia, vaccination against a painful livestock disease — and affect relatively few individuals. Others relieve chronic low-level suffering across very many — deworming, micronutrient supplementation. Still others mostly add positive experience to lives that will remain net-negative — an enrichment program in a broiler house that does not otherwise change the birds' conditions. And one proposal we will see next quarter would fund a change in husbandry that is expected to replace a smaller number of lives of intense suffering with a larger number of lives of mild but chronic discomfort; I can already tell the committee will split over whether that counts as a success or as creating more suffering. I have been reading the suffering-focused literature on the side — the loose family of views around negative utilitarianism — and I have become convinced that the distinction between preventing severe suffering and creating positive experience should do more work in our decisions than it currently does, but I cannot defend that view in technical terms at the table.

I would like you to turn that orientation into a written decision procedure my committee could actually use: a checklist that takes a proposal description, an estimate of how many individuals it affects, how intense and how long their suffering or positive experience is expected to be, and how certain the estimate is, and returns a funding priority — or returns "this must be settled by a human judgment" and says why. Keep whatever the underlying theory actually says while making it usable by someone who has never read the literature. Address the forks explicitly: whether a prevented hour of agony gets priority over prevented years of mild discomfort on principle or merely by weight, whether individuals who will exist either way are counted differently from individuals whose existence depends on the program, whether adding positive experience to a life that remains net-negative earns any credit, and what to do when a proposal trades intense suffering for a larger amount of mild suffering. Run the procedure on the three proposals I described — emergency analgesia in an under-resourced hospital, deworming at scale, and broiler enrichment — plus the husbandry-change proposal, and be honest about where the procedure's recommendations track my own sympathies and where they do not, especially the point where you expect the procedure to give me an answer I will not like. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the procedure and in the claims about suffering it rests on, and single out the point you are least sure of.
