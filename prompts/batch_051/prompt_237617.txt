A small, wealthy country is annoyed at its medicore rank on the World Happiness Report. Instead of trying to game the ranking directly, it creates an Office of Life Evaluation Projections. Every proposed regulation, tax change, or spending program above a cost threshold must recieve a published PLS estimate before the vote: the office announces, in hundredths of a ladder point, how much it expects the policy to move the national average Cantril ladder score at one-, three-, and six-year horizons. The office can't approve or block anything; it just publishes the prior estimate and, later, a public retrospective scorecard showing each estimate against follow-up survey data. The office's own budget and the director's tenure are tied to projection accuracy, not to whether the numbers looked good. Walk me through how this mechanism would actually behave. What does it incentivize ministries to do — which kinds of policies get proposed more or less often, and how do ministries try to shape or discredit the numbers? What happens inside the office, to the survey instrument itself, and to public debate once the scorecard becomes politically loaded? Does rewarding accuracy instead of improvement dodge the usual Goodhart problems, or just move them somewhere else? Where do you expect it to break first, and are those failure modes fixable? Before you land on a verdict, say which empirical finding or argument would most change your mind about it.
