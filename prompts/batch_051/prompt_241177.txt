In my upper-level metaphysics course, the instructor introduced the view that possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated universes, and motivated it with the familiar comparison to parallel universes: just as the observable universe might be one among many physically real universes, the world we call actual is one among many concrete worlds, and calling it actual functions like calling a place here. A classmate has been leaning on this analogy to argue that this kind of modal realism cannot be as extravagant as its critics claim, because physicists already take parallel universes seriously. I would like an audit of how far that analogy actually holds. Please give a correspondence table—what plays the role of a possible world, of actuality, of possibility and necessity, of cross-world sameness, and of accessibility—and then identify where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the classmate's argument from those that do not. In particular, say which features of the physical parallel-universe picture the analogy quietly imports that concrete modal realism either rejects or does not include. Finally, say whether the classmate's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy would be if there is one.
