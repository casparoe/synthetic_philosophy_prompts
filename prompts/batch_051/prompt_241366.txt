I read a long-ish article about "accuracy-first epistemology" the other day and I keep wanting to bring it up, but I don't know if I actually understood it or if the author was over-simplifying. The article summarized the program like this:

"The accuracy-firsters start with one idea: the only thing that matters, epistemically, is being accurate. A belief is rational exactly when it maximizes expected accuracy, where accuracy just means closeness to the truth. From that one idea you can derive all the usual epistemic norms. Joyce showed that if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there is some other set of degrees of belief that is more accurate no matter which possible world is actual — so incoherent credences are guaranteed to be worse than a coherent alternative. Greaves and Wallace showed that conditionalization is the only update rule that maximizes expected accuracy from your current point of view, so if you update in any other way you're being irrational by your own lights. The upshot is supposed to be that traditional epistemology's talk of justification, coherence, and other internal notions was a detour: once you have the scoring rules, you don't need any of it. And one weird side effect is that the view forces you to think your own credences are better than everyone else's, which they call immodesty."

The broad picture sounds plausable, but the bits at the end feel like strong conclusions coming out of nowhere. Could you go through this summary claim by claim and tell me which parts are fair, which parts are compressed in a misleading way, and which parts a person who actually works in this area would reject? In particular, is the move from "accuracy is the fundamental epistemic value" to "we don't need justification or coherence" something accuracy-firsters actually accept, and does the framework really force you to think your own credences are the best? I'm trying to figure out which of these sentences I can safely reuse and which I'd be embarassing myself by repeating.
