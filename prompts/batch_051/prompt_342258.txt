A friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about what it even means for money to corrupt politics. He insisted that, absent a straight quid pro quo — an officeholder changing a vote or an official act because of a particular check — the word corruption is just a pejorative for spending one dislikes. I resisted that, but I found it harder than I expected to say what the wrong consists in when there is no deal on the table. I want to use Citizens United v. FEC (2010) as a way of working through that disagreement, and I would rather you not restate the history except where the analysis needs it.

Here is the theoretical frame. Distinguish two conceptions of corruption that are doing different work in the debate over money in politics. On the narrow conception, corruption is quid pro quo exchange: money corrupts only where it purchases an official act. On the broad conception, corruption also includes distortions of the political process itself: large concentrations of wealth can buy access, set agendas, and crowd out other voices even without an explicit agreement, and that is itself a corruption of democratic government rather than merely a pre-existing inequality.

The episode, in the form relevant here, is this. The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 barred corporations and unions from spending general treasury funds on electioneering communications — broadcast ads naming a federal candidate shortly before an election. Citizens United, a nonprofit corporation, wanted to distribute and advertise a film attacking Hillary Clinton during the 2008 presidential primaries and was blocked. In 2010 the Supreme Court held that the ban on corporate and union independent expenditures violated the First Amendment; it separately upheld the statute's disclosure and disclaimer rules. The majority reasoned that independent spending, because it is not coordinated with a candidate, does not corrupt or appear to corrupt, and it rejected the idea that the state may limit some speakers' spending in order to equalize the relative influence of different voices. The dissent argued that corporate independent spending corrupts politics in a broader sense even without a direct exchange.

Analyze the episode through those two conceptions, and then run the analysis in the reverse direction. Specifically:

1. Map the dispute onto the two conceptions. For each, identify who the relevant actors are, what the harm is supposed to be, and how both the 2002 ban and the Court's ruling look from inside that conception. Locate the precise point at which the two conceptions diverge and force different legal conclusions.

2. Say what each conception explains about the case and what it misdescribes or leaves awkward. Pay particular attention to the Court's distinction between direct contributions and independent expenditures, its rejection of influence-equalization as a permissible governmental purpose, and the fact that it nonetheless kept disclosure and disclaimer rules. If independent spending really cannot corrupt, why retain transparency requirements; and which conception makes best sense of the work disclosure is left doing?

3. Now turn the case against the theory. Consider the aftermath: lower-court decisions applying Citizens United led to Super PACs that can accept unlimited contributions for independent spending and are often run by people closely tied to the candidates they support, coordinating in every practical sense but not formally; much election spending now also passes through organizations whose donors are difficult to trace. Does this aftermath show that the narrow conception was empirically wrong, or does it show that critics were using corruption to mean something else — inequality of influence — which the First Amendment may not let the state cure directly?

4. Finish by saying where each conception is silent or overreaches: where the narrow conception cannot name a wrong it clearly senses, where the broad conception fails to supply a limiting principle, and what, if anything, Citizens United actually settles about whether the problem is corruption, inequality of influence, or both.
