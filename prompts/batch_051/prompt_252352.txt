For the past several decades, philosophers of mind and cognitive psychologists have argued about whether introspection deserves to be treated as a reliable source of evidence about conscious experience, and the debate seems to me to have reached one of those standoffs in which both sides repeat familiar moves and no one changes position. One side treats introspective reports as broadly usable and often indispensable: they point to protocols in which trained subjects are prompted at random moments to describe their ongoing experience, and to the difficulty of making sense of the idea that a person could be systematically wrong about whether they are currently in pain or currently visualising a face. The other side treats introspection as systematically untrustworthy, pointing to experiments in which people confidently give demonstrably false explanations of why they acted as they did, to change blindness and choice blindness, and to the way experimental demands can shape what subjects say they experienced.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is at least partly a disagreement about which cognitive act the word 'introspection' is supposed to name. One side is mostly talking about concurrent attention to, and reporting of, one's present experience; the other is mostly talking about retrospective causal storytelling about one's own behaviour and mental processes. If that is right, then the two sides are not always contradicting each other, and some of the apparent disagreement should dissolve once the two activities are separated. But I suspect something remains even after that distinction is drawn: a genuine dispute about whether concurrent introspective reports can be checked against anything other than further reports, and about how reliable a source must be before it earns the title 'evidence'.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly — verbal, factual, methodological, or a difference about bedrock cases that neither side can argue for? Is my distinction between concurrent reporting and retrospective explanation doing the real diagnostic work, or is the live disagreement elsewhere? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an experimental design, a changed question, or the conclusion that the question as framed is ill-posed and should be retired?
