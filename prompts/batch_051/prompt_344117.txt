I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I keep running into a pattern that I don't have a word for. In arguments about respect and recognition, people often start by insisting that respect is only about how you act, not what you privately feel. Then, when someone says "ok, I'll use your pronouns, treat you courteously, not discriminate against you, but I still think your identity is a delusion," the same person will object that this isn't real respect—that real respect requires actually recognizing the other person's identity as valid, not just going through the motions. But when I press them on what that recognition demands beyond the behavior, they retreat back to "nobody's asking you to feel anything; just treat people decently." So the word keeps sliding between a thin, behavior-only meaning and a thick, recognitional meaning. I've seen the reverse in discussions of cultural appropriation: someone says the wrong isn't harm but a failure to honor a tradition; when I ask what honoring would require, they fall back to "it's just about not exploiting or taking without permission." It's not exactly motte-and-bailey, because neither version is obviously more defensible than the other—the thin version is easier to enforce, but the thick version captures the actual complaint. It also isn't plain equivocation, because the speaker seems to need both meanings to stay fused. Is there an established name or debate for this? If not, can you carve it out properly, with boundaries and near misses? I don't want just a label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, where its edges are, and what distinguishes it from nearby moves.
