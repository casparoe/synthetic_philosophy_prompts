You are a political philosopher working in social choice theory. A colleague presents a small, stylised family decision together with a set of verdicts they find intuitive, and asks whether any single coherent principle captures all of them.

Amara and Ben, adult siblings, jointly inherit a single unpublished memoir by their late mother. The options come down to three: Amara reads it, Ben reads it, or neither reads it. Amara is strait-laced: she would prefer that no one read it, but if someone is to read it she would rather it were herself, because she would treat it with discretion. Ben has the opposite sensibility: he wants it read and would prefer that Amara read it, thinking it would do her good, but he will read it himself if she will not.

The colleague's verdicts are these:

1. Amara should not be made to read the memoir. Whether she reads it is her own concern, so her refusal settles the comparison between Amara reading and neither reading in favour of neither.
2. Ben is free to read the memoir. Whether he reads it is his own concern, so his choosing to read it settles the comparison between Ben reading and neither reading in favour of Ben reading.
3. Because both siblings agree that Amara's reading it is better than Ben's reading it, the group should prefer Amara reading to Ben reading.

The colleague observes that taken together these three verdicts yield no final choice: each of the three options is ranked below another. They ask you to work through the structure carefully and explain: is there a coherent principle that yields all three verdicts, or nearly all of them? If one must give way, which is weakest, and how should it be revised? State the strongest defensible version of the principle you would keep, test it against the three cases, and say plainly which of the colleague's verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. Then state what your preferred principle predicts for a fourth, simpler case: a family in which both siblings strongly prefer that Ben read the memoir, Amara strongly prefers not to read it herself, and Ben is glad to read it. Before settling on your answer, give some thought to whether the fault lies in the first or second verdict, in the third, or in the assumption that the three can be combined at all.
