I've been puzzling over a disagreement with my friend that neither of us knows how to resolve, and I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics to help locate where our disagreement actually lives. We agree that trade-offs within a single life are usually unremarkable: it can be reasonable for me to accept a painful procedure now to avoid a much worse pain later, because the later benefit compensates the earlier cost to the same person. We split sharply when the same arithmetic runs across persons. My friend thinks refusing to impose a small harm on one person to prevent a much larger harm to others is just a refusal to do the numbers. I think something changes when the one who bears the cost is not the one who receives the benefit: the cost is never made good to the person who paid it. That has led me to the view that the separateness of persons objection — the idea that utilitarianism wrongly treats a group of distinct persons as if it were a single person whose gains and losses can simply be netted out — is a decisive, self-standing objection to maximizing act-utilitarianism, the view that an act is right exactly when no alternative produces a greater total sum of well-being. For this exercise, treat decisive as meaning: the objection on its own, setting aside demandingness, the repugnant conclusion, informational limits, and other familiar complaints, would make it unreasonable for a fully informed person to accept maximizing act-utilitarianism as a correct criterion of rightness.

I want a real number, not an "it depends" — I've pinned the target down; my own credence is about 0.75, and my friend's is about 0.25. Please start by brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side, then give me your own credence in that proposition, decomposed. State the three or four sub-questions your credence really turns on — for example: whether the analogy between trade-offs within one life and trade-offs across lives is actually doing structural work in defenses of utilitarianism or is just a vivid gloss; whether the intuition that one person's loss cannot be cancelled by another's gain survives being pressed against cases where refusing the trade-off looks callous; what exact scope the objection would have if it succeeded; and how credible the strongest utilitarian reply is. Assign each sub-question a rough probability, explain how they combine into your overall number, and tell me which single sub-question the number is most sensitive to. Then use the same decomposition to say where a 0.75 and a 0.25 person most likely diverge, and name the one evidence, argument, or thought experiment that would most move your own credence.
