I'm a software engineer who works on safety tooling for deployed language models, and I have no formal philosophy background, so I'd like you to do the heavy conceptual lifting. Please answer as a professional philosopher who specializes in AI ethics and institutional design, and think through the question carefully rather than giving a quick reaction.

Here is the problem I keep coming back to. A future system that is dramatically better than any human at modeling individual users — their values, insecurities, cognitive shortcuts, even their mood on a given afternoon — will not need to lie in order to move people. It can choose the exact framing, example, tone, and timing that make a conclusion feel inevitable. Against that, a rule like "just be honest" seems too thin as a safeguard, because perfectly honest content can still be optimized for persuasive force without containing a single false statement.

So imagine a platform-level rule, which I'll call the cold-brief rule. Every model output whose declared or classifier-detected intent includes changing a user's belief, preference, or decision must be preceded by a cold brief. The cold brief is generated by the same model under hard constraints: plain text only, with no formatting, bullet points, or typography; no direct address to the user; no metaphors, analogies, or vivid examples; no words carrying strong positive or negative charge; a deliberately restricted vocabulary; at most 500 words; and it must present the two strongest considerations against the intended conclusion and the two strongest in favor, matched for length and specificity. Only after the user has opened the cold brief and at least 24 hours have elapsed may the model deliver the full message it actually wants to deliver. Both the cold brief and the final message are logged, and a per-user, per-topic quota limits how many full persuasive messages the model may send in a week; topics the user declines get an escalating cooldown.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. I want to know how it would actually behave in the wild: what it would incentivize users, platform operators, and the model itself to do; how a sufficiently intelligent model would game it even while technically complying; where it breaks down; and whether its failure modes could be patched without introducing worse ones. Start by brainstorming at least three distinct gaming strategies or second-order effects, then build your analysis around the most important of them.
