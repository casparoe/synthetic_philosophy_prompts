I am a sixth-form student preparing an essay for a school philosophy competition, and I have chosen to read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through the language of thought hypothesis. My understanding of the hypothesis, which I owe largely to Jerry Fodor, is this: thinking is not carried out in one's natural language but in an inner, language-like system of representations — often called mentalese — whose combinatorial structure explains the systematicity of thought. If I can think that the creature is lonely, I can equally think that Victor is lonely, because the relevant concepts can simply be recombined. Learning a natural language is then largely a matter of attaching public words to concepts already present in that inner medium; in Fodor's stronger version, even concept learning becomes puzzling, since acquiring a genuinely new concept would seem to require already possessing it.

This clashes, on the face of it, with the novel's account of the creature's mind. Shelley presents him as beginning from bare sensation and gradually building up language, moral understanding and self-consciousness through observation — above all at the De Lacey cottage — and through the books he later finds. His narration suggests that language does not merely label thoughts he already had, but transforms, and perhaps even produces, his sense of who he is. That sounds closer to Lockean empiricism than to Fodor.

I would like a reading of Frankenstein through the language of thought hypothesis that is faithful in both directions. Where does the novel dramatise or anticipate something the hypothesis gets right — for instance, the creature's evident ability, before he is fully fluent, to form structured desires, make inferences and recombine what he has learned? Which episodes should I examine most closely, and what in them supports or complicates the Fodorian picture? Where does the novel genuinely resist the reading, so that an honest critic would have to say Shelley is committed to an empiricist account of the mind that Fodor would rule out? And finally, what does Shelley's treatment add to the philosophical debate that the bare hypothesis cannot show — perhaps something about what it is like to think before one has words, or about the role language plays in turning a creature into a self?
