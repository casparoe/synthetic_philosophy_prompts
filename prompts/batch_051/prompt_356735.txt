I am a novelist, and while plotting my current novel I have been leaning on a familiar way of talking about characters' values that a book on craft pushes further than most. The familiar form is monetary: people speak of moral currency, of spending one's integrity, of paying for a choice with something else. The book codifies this into a full analogy: treat each of a character's values as a currency, and treat every dramatic choice as a foreign-exchange transaction. When a character trades fidelity for ambition, or mercy for order, they are selling one value-currency to purchase another, at a personal exchange rate that reveals who they are; the writer's task is to know each character's exchange rates and to hold them steady enough that the character's choices feel inevitable in retrospect. So far, so useful. But the book then draws a stronger conclusion: beneath this surface of many local currencies, every well-constructed character must actually operate with a single underlying currency in which all of their values can be denominated, since otherwise their choices would be arbitrary and no reader could understand them. It even recommends constructing a value budget for each character on the model of a household or national budget.

I would like you to audit this analogy in detail, because I suspect it may be smuggling in more than its author realises. Lay out explicitly what maps onto what in the currency picture; identify the points where the mapping breaks down; and separate the disanalogies that are merely decorative from those that matter for the intended conclusion. In particular, the analogy seems to import quietly from real money markets the ideas of fungibility, a common numeraire, exchange rates that converge on a single scale, transaction costs, arbitrage, inflation, reserve currencies, and even black markets. Which of those imported features do genuinely illuminating work in understanding a person's commitments, and which of them assume exactly what the book is trying to establish? Most importantly: does the book's conclusion survive the audit—that human values, however many they appear to be, must in the end be one thing in disguise, priceable on a single scale? If not, what would a better analogy be, and what would it correctly refuse to import from the world of money?

I ask partly for the sake of the novel, but partly because this craft question seems to me a miniature of a real philosophical one: whether human values are many, or one thing in disguise, and whether the imagery of exchange can ever be separated from the assumption of a common measure.
