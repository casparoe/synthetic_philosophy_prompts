I have been trying to work out what the nonidentity problem implies for our duties to future people, and I keep arriving at a conclusion that feels too strong, so I suspect I have gone wrong somewhere.

As I understand it, the nonidentity problem shows that a decision can change not merely how well off people are, but which people exist. The person created by such a decision cannot be harmed by it, because the alternative for them was never to have existed; provided their life is worth living, they are not worse off than they would otherwise have been. The standard examples are procreative, but the structure seems to apply far more widely. Large policy choices — about carbon emissions, public debt, infrastructure, waste disposal, which research to fund — will predictably alter the course of enough lives that somewhat different people will be conceived and born under each policy. If the people who actually live under a depleted, unstable climate would not have existed under a more careful policy, then they are not, as individuals, worse off than they would otherwise have been; they simply would never have been. And if no particular future person is worse off, it is hard to see how any particular future person could have been wronged. In what sense, then, do we owe anything to future generations? Does the nonidentity problem show that so-called duties to future generations are really duties to no one, and that ordinary talk of harming posterity rests on a fiction?
