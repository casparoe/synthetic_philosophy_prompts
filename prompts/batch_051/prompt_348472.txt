A friend of mine is building a mid-size discussion forum and asked me to sanity-check the moderation design they've settled on, but I honestly can't tell if its brilliant or a disaster. The core idea is to do away with permanent, moderator-imposed bans entirely, and replace deplatforming with a formalized social-sanction system. Any member above a certain account age and activity threshold can file a 'sanction charge' against another member, but they have to stake some of their own reputation in proportion to how severe the sanction they're asking for is. The charge goes to a panel of twelve randomly selected users, who see anonymized evidence and don't know who the parties are. The panel picks from a menu: no action, a public content label, a thirty-day reach cut, a six-month demotion, or a year at near-zero reach with automatic full restoration. If the panel chooses no action the accuser loses their stake; if it imposes any sanction at all, the accuser gets a fraction of the stake back as a reward. And the accused can, before the verdict, accept a sanction one rung below the one charged and short-circuit the whole process.

I want to know how this thing actually behaves if people are roughly self-interested and a bit vindictive. Who gets charged, and for what kind of conduct? How do accusers choose how high to charge, given the plea option? What do panelists do, given that they're anonymous and unaccountable? Where does this get gamed first, and are the failure modes patchable without gutting the mechanism? And what happens over time to the tone of the forum, the distribution of who's willing to say controversial things, and the whole idea of punishment when the community, rather than a mod, is the one imposing it?
