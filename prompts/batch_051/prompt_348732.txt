I'm leading a discussion on East of Eden in a couple weeks, and I want to press on the book's theology rather than just the family drama. My sense is the novel is genuinely torn about original sin and inherited guilt. It sets up the Cain and Abel pattern as recurring across the Trasks — Charles and Adam, then Cal and Aron — and the way it's repeated makes it feel like a curse that gets handed down the bloodline. But then the famous timshel scene pushes the opposite line: the point of the Cain story is that God tells him he may master sin, not that he shall or must, so the sons are free. I don't think the plot actually backs up the scene. Read the novel through the lens of inherited guilt for me: does it end up closer to an Augustinian view that the children are guilty from the start, closer to a free-will view where they only inherit a temptation, or is it trying to hold both and not quite succeeding? Point me to places where the book treats the pattern as inherited destiny and places where it treats it as a choice, and say whether it's tracking a real distinction between inheriting a disposition and inheriting guilt. I care less about the usual Steinbeck critcism and more about what the book actually shows about whether guilt can be passed down.
