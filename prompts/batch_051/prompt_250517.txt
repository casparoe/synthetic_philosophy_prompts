I am a novelist, and I have been turning over a parallel between how a reader infers a character's desires from her actions and the problem of inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior. In my craft, a character's choices are all the reader gets, but what the reader actually reconstructs is a pair: what the character wanted, and what the character took to be the space of live options. The same act can be read two ways. A woman who accepts a humiliating marriage might be read as valuing security over dignity, or as valuing dignity no less but not seeing any course of action that would preserve it; the two readings differ in the perceived alternatives, not in the desires. As a novelist, I control that inference by deciding which alternatives the narrative makes salient to the character and to the reader — what she considers, what she nearly chooses, what never occurs to her at all.

This strikes me as close to the problem of inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from behavior. Given only the actions an agent takes, one must recover both the objective function being optimized and the search space over which the optimization runs, because an observed choice that looks like indifference to some value may instead be a limitation of search: the option that would have served that value was never generated or evaluated. Behavior alone underdetermines the pair. Any finite sequence of choices can be rationalized by many combinations of objective and search space — for example, by an objective that values exactly what was chosen, paired with a search space containing nothing else. Disentangling the two requires assumptions about the search process: what was considered, in what order, when search stopped.

I want to know whether this is a deep structural parallel or a loose analogy. On one side, both seem to be inverse problems with the same identifiability limit: from outside, one observes choices and must jointly recover an evaluation and a horizon of considered possibilities. On the other side, a character is not literally an optimizer; her desires need not form a real-valued function, she need not be consistent across contexts, and her choices are authored rather than generated by a search procedure. So I am asking: is there a genuine correspondence here, and if so, what would have to be true of the literary side for results about inferring objectives from behavior to transfer? Or does the analogy break, and where exactly does it come apart?
