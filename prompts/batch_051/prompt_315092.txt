I'm a philosophy professor who somehow ended up chairing our university's academic standing committee. Twice a year we review students flagged for not making "satisfactory academic progress." The policy never defines "satisfactory," and the university's lawyer says we can't just replace it with a GPA cutoff without effectively legislating. Each case gets about fifteen minutes, the committee is mostly non-philosophers, the dean signs off on our procedure but won't read more than a page, and the decision can get a student dismissed, so it matters.

At this point I see committee meetings as the sorites paradox with consequences: no single file ever seems to cross from satisfactory to unsatisfactory, but by the end of the pile we've dismissed four people. I want you to take the theory of vagueness I actually believe — supervaluationism, where a vague claim is true just in case it's true on every admissible precisification — and turn it into a one-page proceedure my committee can actually use. Don't give me a generic rubric. I need a checklist, two or three worked examples (including one where the procedure gives us an answer we won't like), and a clear statement of where the procedure stops and judgment takes over. Also tell me which philosophical distinctions are worth keeping when I explain this to a dean and which ones to leave out entirely.
