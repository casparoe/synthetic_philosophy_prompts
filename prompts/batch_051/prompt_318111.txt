I keep circling around the following argument and cannot locate the flaw. (1) To attribute a character to a people is to attribute some trait or tendency whose distribution differs enough from other peoples' to count as distinctive. (2) The same criterion applies to any group: if national character merely requires a real distributional difference, then blood-type character and zodiacal character are equally well supported wherever such differences exist. (3) Since we treat zodiacal character as empty, the difference must lie elsewhere — presumably in the fact that nations, unlike birth months, are constituted by shared institutions, history, language, and self-understanding that causally shape their members. (4) But those cultural forces are nested and cross-cutting: a person is shaped simultaneously by profession, neighborhood, religion, class, and family, each of which is also constituted by shared practices and history. (5) Whatever one says to privilege the nation among these overlapping cultures appears to beg the question, because there are as many real differences between classes or neighborhoods as between nations. (6) Therefore either national character is as empty as zodiacal character, or we must admit that every stable subculture has a character and that the nation is just one arbitrary slice among many. The first horn denies something I find almost undeniable in ordinary speech and social science; the second flattens the nation into just another demographic. Which premise would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, what principle picks out the nation without committing to zodiacs?
