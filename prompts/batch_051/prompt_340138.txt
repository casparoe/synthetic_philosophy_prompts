I am looking for the established term, if there is one, for a recurring two-step I keep meeting in reductive theories. The first step introduces some feature F as a by-product of an underlying mechanism, and insists — often explicitly — that F exerts no causal influence on anything at all: it does not affect what people say, choose, notice, or write down. The second step, later in the same argument, appeals to people's ability to notice F, to discriminate among instances of F, and to report those discriminations, as evidence for claims about F. That strikes me as an odd combination, because if F really is causally inert, it is not clear how it could leave any trace in the very acts of noticing, comparing, and reporting on which the second step depends.

The clearest case I know concerns the felt qualities of conscious experience. A view holds that the hurtfulness of a burn or the specific look of a red wall is a causally idle accompaniment to brain activity: the brain's states do all the causal work through ordinary physical processes, while the felt quality is carried along without influencing even the reporting of it. Yet the same view treats our introspective reports — 'this burn hurts more than the pressure I felt a moment ago', 'that wall looks redder than this one' — as data about those very felt qualities, and sometimes as the main evidence that the qualities differ. I have also met the pattern outside philosophy of mind, in a theory of artistic value: the value of a work is said to be a by-product of certain social mechanisms and to exert no causal force over anyone's judgements, but the theory then relies on people's stable, fairly fine-grained judgements of value to identify which works are the ones to be explained. In both cases a phenomenon is introduced as inert and then quietly put to epistemic work.

Is there a name for that specific two-step — the dialectical configuration itself, not the underlying metaphysical view about felt qualities — and if not, what would be a good name and definition? I am not after the generic idea of a self-defeating or self-undermining argument, where the content of a claim undercuts the conditions for asserting it; the theories here are not obviously incoherent, and the difficulty is rather that the phenomenon is assigned two roles, one causal and one evidential, that seem hard to hold together. I also do not mean a debunking explanation, because in these cases the appeal to noticing is offered in support of the theory, not as an account of why people believe it. I want the boundaries drawn sharply: what must be true of a case for it to count; which cases merely resemble it without counting (for example, a by-product that is unpredicted but still measurable; a report that is caused by the underlying mechanism and merely correlated with the by-product; or an appeal to someone's report used as an objection to their own view); and which distinctions among these matter. If the mind case is the standard home of this pattern, does the pattern travel to the aesthetic case cleanly, or are the two versions importantly different?
