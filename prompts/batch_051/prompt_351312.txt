I am a student currently taking an upper-level undergraduate course in Bayesian epistemology, and I am preparing for the final by drafting mock exam materials. Please play the role of an exacting but fair professor of philosophy of science who sets questions that cannot be answered by reciting the lecture and that reward precise, structural understanding. Take your time, and before composing anything, briefly brainstorm the common confusions students have about the problem of old evidence and how a well-designed question can expose them; only then write the materials.

I would like you to produce three things, in this order.

First, one essay question for a 90-minute final exam, aimed at students who have already covered Bayesian confirmation theory, the problem of old evidence, and the two standard families of response: the counterfactual strategy and the logical-learning strategy. The question should be self-contained, define any technical notation it introduces, and require no outside reading, no named papers, and no quotations. Rather than asking for a general summary, the question should use a targeted evaluative task, such as a short fictional case, so that students cannot pass by reproducing the lecture outline.

Second, a model answer of roughly 800 to 1200 words, in continuous prose, that an excellent candidate could give.

Third, a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, built around five must-hit points. For each point, give the marks it is worth and a one-line note on the specific error or omission weaker candidates typically make at that point.

The crux the question should force candidates through is this. Let E be a proposition an agent already knew before hypothesis H was formulated, so their probability for E is 1. On the standard account, E confirms H only if the probability of H conditional on E is greater than the unconditional probability of H; but when the probability of E is 1, these are equal, so E cannot confirm H. Yet in actual scientific practice, long-known facts do get invoked as evidence for new theories. The counterfactual strategy responds that we should evaluate support relative to a probability function in which E has been subtracted or has not yet been learned. The logical-learning strategy responds that what is genuinely learned is a new logical fact: that H entails E, or renders E antecedently much more probable. The question should require candidates to set out both strategies and then take a reasoned stand on whether either solves the original problem or merely relocates it: for instance, by making support relative to a fictional probability function that no actual agent held, or by adding controversial structure to the probability calculus to represent logical information. Strong answers should also distinguish the problem of old evidence from the general subjectivity of priors and from the catch-all problem, and should say something precise about what must be true of the counterfactual probability function for that strategy to work.
