I have been working through an argument in constitutional theory that a colleague presented at a workshop, and I keep finding that each step looks plausible while the conclusion is one I cannot accept. I want to know where the chain actually breaks.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) An individual can make it the case that her own future self has reasons she would not otherwise have had: Ulysses has himself bound to the mast, a problem gambler signs a binding casino self-exclusion order, a smoker gives away her cigarettes for the evening. What does the justificatory work in such cases is not the content of the restriction but the fact that the same agent imposed it on herself, at a clearer moment, in service of ends she already held.

(2) A political community is a temporally extended agent in the same relevant sense. A people, like a person, persists through time and can act at one moment to constrain itself at later moments; a constitutional founding is precisely such an act, carried out at a moment of unusually considered collective judgment.

(3) Therefore, constitutional precommitment is justified on the same ground as individual self-binding: a democratic people may entrench fundamental rights, supermajority requirements, and structural provisions in order to bind its own future majorities.

(4) But if the ground of justification is self-imposition itself, then there is no principled limit of content or time. The founding generation may bind not only its own later self but all successor generations, and it may entrench whatever it likes.

(5) That yields conclusions we are not prepared to accept: a bare founding majority may permanently entrench a minority veto, place core provisions beyond amendment forever, or even entrench an unamendable autocracy; and by the same individual-level logic, a person may irrevocably bind herself into servitude.

(6) Since those conclusions are unacceptable, at least one step in this chain must be rejected. But I find I most want to keep (1), and I also want to keep enough of (3) to make constitutional government intelligible.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I want a diagnosis: which step should be rejected, why that step nonetheless looked compelling, and what the cost of rejecting it is. In particular, if we cannot help ourselves to the full self-imposition story, what residual justification for constitutional precommitment remains, and does it still carry any weight against a current majority that genuinely wants to unbind itself?
