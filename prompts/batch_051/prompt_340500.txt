I'm drafting a short essay for a general-audience philosophy blog and I'd like some comments before I expand it into the full thing. The working claim is that the sunk-cost fallacy gets taught as one mistake when it's actually a tangle of at least three seperable things, and only one of them is clearly a mistake. Here's the idea in note form:

1. The clear mistake: treating past losses as if they make a bad remaining option better on its merits. If continuing and quitting would otherwise look equally good except that I've already sunk time or money into continuing, then the ledger should start fresh and the past cost is no reason to continue.

2. Sunk costs as evidence: having invested heavily in a project can be evidence that I at some point expected it to pay off, or that I have a taste or aptitude for it that current frustration is obscuring. Persisting then isn't honoring the cost; it's just reading information off of it.

3. Sunk costs as part of the good: for some projects the value of the outcome is partly historical. A degree I hated finishing is not the same good as the same degree bought instantly; part of its value is that I persisted through the sunk costs. If I hold an antecedent second-order preference to be the kind of person who doesn't abandon what they've invested in, then continuing changes what my present act means, not just what happens afterward.

The punchline would be that the usual always-ignore-sunk-costs advice smuggles in a purely forward-looking, end-state-only picture of value, and that (3) is the strongest counterexample to that picture. But I'm not sure (3) survives scrutiny.

Could you comment on whether (3) is doing work genuinely distinct from (2), or whether it's just loss aversion with nicer vocabulary; whether my implied test for legitimate persistence (the second-order preference has to be held antecedently and be revisable if the costs grow high enough) is too weak or too strict; and whether I can state the clear mistake in (1) without already begging the question against (3). If you think the framing is off, say how you'd reorganize it instead.
