I've been persuaded by the fitting-attitudes analysis of value — roughly, that to call something good, admirable, or desirable is not to attribute an extra property too it, but to say that valuing, admiring, or desiring it is fitting, where fitting is cashed out in terms of the reasons bearing on the attitude. It's the only account I know that doesn't leave value as a mysterious non-natural add-on, and it makes the tie between value and reasons much tidier. But the demon case keeps bothering me: if a credible demon threatens to torture me unless I admire him, I plainly have a reason to admire him, yet something seems wrong in saying he is admirable, or that admiring him is fitting, or that this reason shows my admiration would be correct. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would cost to debunk or explain away the intuition, what it would cost to amend or give up the analysis, and whether there's a reading of a reason to admire on which the theory and the intuition can both survive.
