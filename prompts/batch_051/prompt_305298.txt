I am a doctoral student working between philosophy of language and family law, and I want to understand what a familiar legal practice is implicitly committed to. In most European systems, given names are chosen by parents within broad limits set by the child's welfare and are easily changed by adults; the surname is not freely chosen at all — it is inherited, usually patrilineally, and may be changed only for serious grounds such as marriage, divorce, or danger — and the same surname then serves as a stable identifier in civil registers, property records, and inheritance. Iceland is different: the second name is a patronymic or matronymic formed from a parent's given name and recomputed each generation, so no family name is transmitted. What conception of what a name is, what it refers to, and what naming is for does the inherited, hard-to-change surname presuppose? Does the practice presuppose that a surname is partly a genealogical description, that it is a merely conventional tag whose value is administrative continuity, or something else? Is the Icelandic arrangement a rival conception of naming or only a different record-keeping convention? And what in the surrounding legal concepts of filiation, identity, property, and inheritance would have to change if inherited surnames were replaced by freely chosen or per-generation names?
