I've lived in four countries over the last decade and I keep noticing that my own moral reactions to everyday conventions don't line up, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one. Some cases, with my gut verdicts. In the US I tip 20% even for mediocre service, and I'd call a visitor who knows the custom and refuses a freeloader, not merely clueless, because the server's pay depends on it; but in Japan I don't tip, and a visitor who insists on tipping is doing something embarrassing yet not wrong. In Britain I'd say someone who pushes in at a bus stop is doing something wrong, full stop, and I'd say the same of a tourist, though I'd blame them less if they genuinly didn't know. But jaywalking: in a city where everyone crosses against the light I do it without a twinge, and in a city where everyone waits I wait, yet only because it feels awkward, not because I think crossing would wrong anyone. I cover my shoulders in Italian churches and think a tourist in beachwear is being disrespectful in a way that's wrong even if they're just oblivious; I take my shoes off at the door in Japanese homes and would call a refusal rude, maybe wrong. But I don't think a visitor to Japan who never bows is doing anything wrong at all, just missing the texture of the place.

My working idea is that a local convention binds a visitor when it is doing one of two jobs: either it keeps a cooperative practice fair, so that breaking it free-rides on everyone else's compliance (queueing, tipping where wages depend on it), or it protects something locals treat as sacred or solemn (dress in churches, shoes in homes and temples). Conventions that do neither are optional for a visitor, even if following them is graceful (bowing, anthems, jaywalking). But I can already see trouble: a Brit would say queueing is as much about respect as fairness, and I have no idea why an Italian church counts as sacred to me while a Japanese bow counts as mere texture. Is there a principle that gets all my verdicts, or close, and which verdicts does it force me to drop? What does it say about the cases I keep wobbling on, like a visitor eating on the street during Ramadan in a majority-Muslim country, or haggling in a market where fixed prices are the norm?

If the whole framing is off—if 'convention' is doing too much work, or my cases are secretly two different questions—tell me that rather than forcing a tidy principle. And please write the answer in British English.
