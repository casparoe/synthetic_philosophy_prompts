I am trying to get a working definition of the word "undeserving" in the phrase "the undeserving poor"—or, if you think that phrase is beyond repair, of the simpler predicate "deserves public income support." The definition should be usable: for any recipient of means-tested state support, it should tell us whether they count as deserving, and why.

Here are the cases the definition has to handle.

(a) A 60-year-old former care worker whose back was ruined lifting a patient and who can no longer do paid work: clearly deserving.

(b) A 31-year-old, fit, childless man who turns down a warehouse job 45 minutes away because the work is dull and he would rather not move: clearly undeserving.

(c) A single mother on unemployment benefit who works ten hours a week cleaning houses under the table, because declaring the income would trigger a clawback that leaves her worse off: I lean toward deserving, but the fraud bothers me.

(d) A 27-year-old whose own psychiatrist says he is incapacitated by depression, while the state's assessor says he is fit for work and stops his payment: I have no stable view.

(e) A 23-year-old who has never worked, meets the formal job-search requirement by applying for the required number of positions each week but deliberately sabotages the interviews, and lives on the benefit while gaming: I lean undeserving, but I cannot say exactly why, since he follows the rules.

(f) A self-employed builder who takes mostly cash jobs, reports about half his income, and on paper qualifies for a full housing benefit even though his real household income is near the national median: undeserving.

(g) A woman who cares full-time for her elderly mother without pay instead of taking a job; if she took paid employment, the state would have to spend more on professional care for the mother than it now spends on her benefits: deserving, in my view.

I suspect the word is doing two jobs at once: one tracking whether a person's need arose through no fault of their own, and another tracking effort, contribution, or good faith.
