I keep returning to a view about conditionals that I would like stress-tested before I commit to it in writing. The view is that the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals is real but shallow: a single variably strict semantics covers both, so that a conditional of the form "if A then B" is true at a world just in case B holds throughout the closest A-worlds, and the felt gap between "If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did" and "If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have" comes entirely from the ordering of worlds — epistemic possibilities for indicatives, historical or metaphysical possibilities for counterfactuals. I like the view because it posits one lexical entry for "if," explains the shared inferential behavior of the two forms, and treats the difference as a matter of context rather than of meaning.

What I want is a genuine map of what would count against this. I already know the usual suspects: the fact that the acceptability of an indicative seems to track the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent rather than the probability of any proposition, together with triviality results that appear to rule out such a proposition; Gibbard-style cases in which two speakers with different partial information each reasonably accept an indicative while its contrary seems equally acceptable to the other; the way conditionals behave under negation, "might," "even if," and quantifiers; and the asymmetry that indicatives are infelicitous when the antecedent is known to be false while counterfactuals are not. Sort these, and add anything else you think is doing the real work. Which would force a committed defender of the unified view to give ground, and which can be absorbed as a tweak to the ordering or to context? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and end with an honest verdict on whether the view has been insulated by qualifications — and whether that insulation is a problem or just what a mature semantic theory looks like. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
