A friend of mine, who read a short introduction to Christian ethics for a discussion group, offered me what he presented as a fair summary of Augustine's view on lying, and I have been turning it over since. I am not a scholar of Augustine, but I know enough to suspect the summary flattens something, though I could not say exactly where the distortion lies. His summary went roughly as follows: "Augustine's view is that lying is always wrong, without exception. He defines a lie simply as saying what is false, so on his account even an honest mistake counts as lying. And because all lies are equally sinful, a lie told to save an innocent person's life is no better than a lie told to swindle someone. That last consequence shows how absurd the view is: no sensible person would hand someone over to be killed rather than tell a falsehood, so the position falls apart as soon as you test it against a single hard case."

I would like an accuracy audit of this summary rather than a general defense or attack. Please separate the claims that are accurate from those that are simplifications or distortions; say which of them Augustine actually held, and where the friend has conflated distinct ideas — for instance about what makes an utterance a lie, and about whether all lies are on a par. Then assess whether the closing objection succeeds, or whether it assumes the very point at issue. Finally, state which parts of the summary a careful Augustinian could accept, and what the corrected version of the position would look like once the conflations are undone.
