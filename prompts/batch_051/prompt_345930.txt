In Berlin in the first years of the twentieth century, a horse known as Clever Hans, trained by Wilhelm von Osten, appeared to solve arithmetic problems and answer questions by tapping his hoof. The psychologist Oskar Pfungst later showed that Hans failed whenever no one present knew the correct answer or the horse could not see the people who did: Hans was not calculating, but was responding to subtle, involuntary changes in the questioner's posture and tension as the tap count approached the right number.

Analyze this episode as a test of C. Lloyd Morgan's canon, the methodological rule that we should prefer a lower process in the scale of psychological evolution and development over a higher psychological process whenever the lower one adequately explains an animal's action. Begin with the careful mapping: what were the competing explanations of Hans, where did each sit on the lower/higher scale, and how did each of Pfungst's conditions, the ignorant questioner and the hidden questioner, function as a controlled test between them? Give the canon its due, then press it. The received verdict is that the canon won and anthropomorphism lost, but the explanation that replaced arithmetic was itself a sophisticated social-cognitive capacity: reading unconscious human cues is not obviously lower in any principled sense. What, then, did the episode actually demonstrate about the canonical preference for parsimony, and where does the canon fall silent about the positive question of what Hans did know rather than what he did not? Finally, reverse directions. What does Clever Hans teach about Morgan's canon and its legacy, including the fact that the Clever Hans effect was later found in human experimental subjects, whose experimenters unintentionally leaked expectations through behavior? Does the canon remain a defensible methodological rule, or has it become partly a bias against attributing cognition to nonhuman animals? If a version is worth keeping, state the version and its limits.
