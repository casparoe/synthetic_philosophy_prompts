I work in people-ops at a company of about 200, fully remote, so all of our resignations happen in Slack or email. I'm the person who has to decide, usually the next morning, whether a resignation actualy happened: the words set the clock for final pay, access cutoffs, rehire status, and what we tell the rest of the team. I make this call maybe twice a month, I get about fifteen minutes per case, and I have to explain my call to our ops director afterwards without any philosophy jargon.

The problem is people quit in all sorts of ways. I've seen "I'm done," sent to a teammate in a group channel during a fight; "consider this my notice"; "I quit unless you take me off this account"; an essay about burnout at 2am that never says the word resign; and once a very clear "I hereby resign, effective Friday" followed eight minutes later by "that came out wrong, can we pretend I didn't send it."

What I want from you is a reusable decision procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "not a resignation," and where it hands the call to a human because the step is a judgment call, not a fact. Ground it in what philosophy of language actually knows about performative utterances — the idea that some utterances don't just report a change, they make the change — but translated for someone who isn't going to read the theory. Keep whatever distinctions actually earn their keep and drop the rest, and tell me honestly where the procedure will misfire. Dont give me a bibliography or citations; I want the reasoning, not the reading list.

Then run the procedure on these three:

1. A developer DMs her manager at 11:42pm, "I hereby resign, effective Friday," and eight minutes later follows up with "sorry, that came out wrong, can we talk tomorrow?" In the morning she says she was exhausted and did not mean it.
2. In a heated group-channel argument about on-call load, an engineer posts "that's it, I'm out." The next day he keeps working and says he meant he was out of the argument, not out of the job.
3. A project manager tells her manager in a 1:1, "If you don't take me off the Terrence account I'll have to resign." A week later, when she wasn't taken off it, she sends a note saying her earlier statement had been conditional notice and she now considers herself resigned.

I suspect at least one of these will give me an answer I don't like. Keep the procedure honest anyway.

One more thing on honesty: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this stuff, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. For any point where you had to pick a side (say, how much it matters whether the hearer actually saw the message, or whether someone's private intention can un-do the words), tell me it's contested, which side you picked, and why.

After the main answer, give me a compact JSON summary — nothing after it — with this schema:

- "steps": an array of objects, each with "number" (integer), "question" (the question to ask at that step), "passes_if" (what counts as a sufficient yes), and "action_if_fails" (what to do if it fails, including whether to stop or hand off).
- "worked_cases": an array of objects, each with "case" (string label), "verdict" (one of "resigned", "not_resigned", or "human_decision"), "first_failed_step" (integer or null), and "judgment_calls" (array of strings naming steps where a human had to decide).
- "human_decision_points": an array of strings naming the kinds of cases the procedure cannot settle by itself.
- "contested_claims": an array of objects, each with "claim" (string) and "status" (one of "settled" or "contested").
