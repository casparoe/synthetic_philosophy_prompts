You’re an expert on democratic theory and political philosophy. I want you to think carefully and, before you jump to a conclusion, spend some time brainstorming what each side of an analogy actually commits you to.

I’ve been arguing with a friend about whether voting is a duty or just a personal choice. My friend keeps reaching for an analogy from John Stuart Mill: she says that when you vote, you’re not acting as a private person expressing a personal preference; you’re acting as a kind of public trustee or juror. Her point is that a vote is not something you use to advance your own interests or even to express your own convictions in the way you might express a taste. It’s a task you perform for others, and you’re supposed to perform it in the way you think best promotes the common good.

I remember reading Mill on this, but I don’t have the book in front of me. I think he says something like: a man’s vote is not a thing in which he has an option; it is a matter of duty, like the verdict of a juryman on a trial. Something close to that, anyway. The idea is that the voter is under an obligation to vote for what he judges to be the public interest, not for what he happens to want. In that way, voting is less like choosing a meal and more like serving on a jury.

I want to know how far this analogy really goes. Take the juror analogy seriously for a moment. A juror has a specific role, clear procedures, evidence presented to them, and a legal standard to apply. They don’t decide the outcome because they personally like one party. They swear an oath. They deliberate with others. They can be punished for ignoring their instructions. They’re assigned a role rather than choosing it. A voter, by contrast, has no oath, no evidence, no training, no formal deliberation, no one checking whether they voted for the common good, and no penalty for voting purely selfishly.

So here’s what I want you to do. Audit the analogy of the voter as juror. What maps onto what, where does the mapping break, which features of jury service does the analogy quietly import that voting lacks, and which conclusion, if any, about the ethics of voting can it actually support? Does it show that voting is a duty in the way a juror has a duty? Or does it show something weaker: that if you vote, you should treat the decision as something other than a private preference, but without any of the structure that makes a juror’s role coherent? And if the analogy breaks down under pressure, what better analogy—if any—captures what voting is like for an ordinary citizen?
