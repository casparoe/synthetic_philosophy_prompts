I'm a student in a seminar on paternalism and the ethics of nudging, and I'm putting together a handout for undergraduates with no background in the literature: a "what do you actually think about paternalism?" sorting quiz. The quiz should have eight to ten concrete policy cases, each with two or three answer options, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The quiz is meant to sort people by how they answer cases, not by which label they like, so no question should simply ask whether the respondent is a paternalist.

I want the battery built around one particular fault line: what to do when a choice-preserving intervention seems to improve people's welfare by routing around their biases, but it is unclear whether the intervention is respecting their real preferences or, instead, creating or consolidating the very preferences it then claims to satisfy. The following cases must appear: automatic enrollment in a retirement savings plan with an opt-out and automatic escalation of the contribution rate; a cafeteria that places fruit at eye level and makes dessert require extra steps; plain packaging plus a substantial tax on cigarettes; a mandatory cooling-off period before taking out high-cost short-term loans; a home-energy report that compares a household's consumption with its neighbors'; and a default on student accounts that shares anonymized data with campus mental health outreach unless the student opts out. For each case, give enough concrete detail for a respondent to answer without knowing the philosophical vocabulary, and add a one-line note on which positions the case should separate.

The scoring key should sort respondents into at least these six leaves: (1) a harm-principle anti-paternalism on which self-regarding choices by informed adults are sovereign; (2) a soft paternalism that permits intervention only when a choice is non-autonomous or materially misinformed; (3) a hard paternalism that permits bans and substantial costs to protect welfare even against informed adult choices; (4) a libertarian paternalism that permits defaults and framing but rejects bans and material costs; (5) an ends-oriented or perfectionist paternalism that may promote objectively worthwhile activities even when people knowingly decline them; and (6) a preference-endogeneity view, perhaps unnamed, that denies there is a clean baseline of true preferences against which a nudge can be measured. Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from the labels: first set out the separating commitments (whether non-voluntariness must be shown, whether choice must be preserved, whether the welfare standard is the agent's considered preferences or some objective good, and whether preferences are treated as exogenous), then locate each leaf, and mark empty cells of the space with a brief diagnosis of whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or probably just underexplored. Flag any two positions the battery cannot separate and say which further case would separate them. Finally, add a short self-assessment indicating how confident you are in the scoring key and singling out the point you are least sure of.
