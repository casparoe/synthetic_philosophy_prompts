I host a small philosophy of language podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends indexical eliminativism: the claim that a language could in principle function without any indexical expressions — no personal pronouns, demonstratives, or context-sensitive temporal or spatial terms such as now and here — and that their apparent indispensability stems from conflating what a sentence means with how speakers use it. Her standing reply to the classic action cases is that they succeed only because the candidate descriptions are deliberately impoverished, and that once the agent's full descriptive relation to herself, the object, the place, and the time is supplied, no irreducibly indexical content remains. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press this view where the literature presses, rather than asking her to restate it. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for each such question include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: (1) action explanation — whether replacing a first-person belief with a fully descriptive belief preserves the explanation of why the agent acts; (2) immunity to error through misidentification — how she explains the datum that certain first-person and present-tense judgments cannot be wrong in the way an ordinary identification can be; (3) de se belief reports — whether another person's essentially first-person belief can be reported without smuggling indexicality back in; and (4) the self-statement challenge — whether she can state her own thesis in an indexical-free language without weakening it. End with one question about what evidence or counterexample would lead her to abandon or significantly revise the view.
