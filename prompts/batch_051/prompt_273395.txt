I've been mulling over a Buddhist argument (I think from Dharmakīrti) that everything real is momentary, and I can't tell whether it's sound or just sneaky. It runs like this:

(1) To be real just is to have causal power — to be able to make some difference. Something with zero causal power is as good as nothing.
(2) Suppose a permanent thing exists: say an unchanging seed that sprouts only once it gets water and warmth.
(3) If the seed itself is the cause of the sprout, then since the seed before watering and the seed after watering are exactly alike, the sprout should arise at every moment the seed exists, or at none. You cant point to the water to break the tie: if the seed only sprouts because the water is there, then the real cause is the seed-plus-water-plus-warmth complex, and that complex isnt permanent; but if the seed does it all by itself, the sprout should be there as long as the seed is.
(4) Sprouts happen at definite times — they aren't always there and they aren't never there.
(5) So a permanent thing can't cause anything.
(6) So nothing permanent is real: every real thing is a momentary flash, and what look like enduring objects are just rows of these flashes stitched together.

I keep feeling the sleight of hand is in (3), but I can't isolate exactly what's wrong, and maybe nothing is. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails — and how should a philosopher who believes in enduring things best resist it?
