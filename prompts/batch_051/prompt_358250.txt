A colleague working on Renaissance political thought claims that in The Prince, Machiavelli’s distinction between fortune and virtù is not merely a piece of rhetorical coloring or a folk metaphor. She argues that it is best read as an explanatory thesis: political success or failure is a function of the interaction between external circumstances, which Machiavelli groups under fortune, and a special kind of adaptive agency, which he calls virtù, where virtù is not conventional moral goodness but the capacity to know when to change one’s conduct as conditions shift. She wants to defend this as a claim that can be brought into contact with evidence, not just as an interpretive proposal. Design the strongest empirical test you can of the thesis that rulers who exhibit greater Machiavellian virtù—understood as adaptive prudence rather than ordinary virtue—tend to succeed more often than otherwise comparable rulers who lack it, holding fortune as constant as possible. Be concrete about what population or comparison you would use, how you would measure both fortune and virtù without building the conclusion into the measures, and what pattern of results would count as confirmation, disconfirmation, or mere noise. Then say exactly what a positive result would and would not establish about the claim as an interpretation of Machiavelli.
