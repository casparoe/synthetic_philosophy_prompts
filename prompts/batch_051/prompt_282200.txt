I help run a small giving circle, and I have been unable to settle a disagreement between two bodies of advice I respect. The first, which I absorbed from effective-altruism-influenced writing, is that one should reason at the margin. For any additional dollar or hour, ask what the next unit of resource would actually change, not how good the organization or cause looks on average; then move resources toward whichever option has the highest marginal impact, taking diminishing returns into account. Because most interventions exhibit diminishing returns at some point, the next dollar matters more than the average dollar, and a series of locally best choices was supposed to add up to the globally best allocation.

The second body of advice comes from friends who work on complex systems in ecology and public health. They insist that the social and biological systems we actually care about are governed by feedback loops, delays, thresholds, and tipping points. Around a threshold, the local effect of a small intervention can look negligible right up until it is decisive; several interventions may produce almost nothing separately but a great deal in combination; and a small nudge can set off self-reinforcing dynamics whose eventual effect is many times larger than anything a marginal estimate would suggest. They say the marginal-impact picture assumes an impact surface that is smooth enough and locally decomposable enough for the "next unit" to be a meaningful, stable quantity, and that this assumption fails exactly where the most consequential decisions are made.

I would like an honest technical assessment rather than a compromise. Does the marginal-impact argument survive this development, and if so in what form? Which specific premise of the argument does systems thinking put under pressure, and is that premise load-bearing or merely illustrative? If the argument needs repair, state the repaired version sharply and say what it still licenses and what it no longer licenses. If it is dead as a general prioritization tool, say what should replace it when someone is deciding where to put a marginal donation.
