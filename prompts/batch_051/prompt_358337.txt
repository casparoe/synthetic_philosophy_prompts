A magazine article summarized what it called the transparency view in the philosophy of photography as follows. On this view, a photograph is not really a representation but a window: when you look at a photograph, you are in direct visual contact with the thing photographed, exactly as it was, much as you see through a telescope or a mirror. The article says the view rests on the fact that photography is a mechanical, belief-independent process, whereas drawing and painting depend on the artist's beliefs about the scene. It then offers the standard objection that photographs are framed, selected, and often manipulated, and that a photograph does not locate the scene in any spatial relation to the viewer; from this the article concludes that the transparency view is refuted, since the camera obviously mediates between you and the scene. I suspect this summary is mostly fair but subtly wrong in places, and I want to know what to trust. Which parts accurately capture the transparency view, where does the summary overstate or distort the view or a defender's likely replies, and does the objection show what the article says it shows?
