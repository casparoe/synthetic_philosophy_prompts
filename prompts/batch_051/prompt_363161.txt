In my graduate seminar on the metaphysics of time I regularly end up defending a definite resolution of the grandfather paradox, and I would like the case against it stated at its strongest rather than the polite version I usually get from students. My view is this. The grandfather paradox does not refute the possibility of time travel. There is a single actual history; a time traveler who departs for the past was always part of that history; and the contradiction at the heart of the paradox — killing one's own grandfather before one's parent is conceived — cannot arise, because any attempt that would erase the time traveler's existence will be defeated by ordinary causal failures. The gun misfires, the shot misses, a bystander intervenes, the traveler loses his nerve, or, if a killing does occur, the victim turns out not to have been the traveler's grandfather after all. I accept the familiar distinction between changing the past (replacing one history with another) and merely affecting it (being among the causes of things that were, all along, going to happen), and I hold that time travel can involve only the second. I am deliberately agnostic about whether the constraint that keeps history self-consistent is best understood as a law of nature, a restriction on admissible boundary conditions, or a matter of logic; my commitment is to the single fixed timeline and to the impossibility of self-inconsistent histories, not to any one explanation of why the constraint holds.

Now I want you to play the position straight, as a serious claim about the world rather than a stipulation, and tell me what would count against it. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, thought experiments, or formal results would force me to abandon it, and which would merely cost me? Please sort the defeaters from fatal to awkward rather than listing them all as equal, and be explicit about which of them a committed defender of the fixed-timeline view should themselves concede count against them, as opposed to what only its critics say. The rivals I want it tested against include at least these: the branching-timeline account, on which the traveler kills a counterpart of his grandfather in a diverging history; the view that the paradox is simply a reductio of time travel simpliciter; and the view that the past can be changed outright, but that when it is changed a history is erased or overwritten along something like a second temporal dimension. I would personally be moved most by a demonstration that the fixed timeline can be made consistent only by fine-tuning, or by a gerrymandered notion of ability, and by any argument that the view collapses into the claim that time travel never actually happens. Finally, tell me honestly whether the position, as I have stated it, risks anything at all, or whether its qualifications have insulated it — and, if they have, whether that insulation is itself a decisive reason to reject a view of this kind.
