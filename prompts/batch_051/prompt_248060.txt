I am preparing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy, and I want to use the murderer-at-the-door case from Kant's short essay On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy (1797) as the basis for an exercise about how much of his argument survives small changes in the case. The passage I want the exercise to turn on is the one in which Kant argues that if you have told the strict truth, public justice can find no fault with you regardless of what follows, whereas a lie, even one told from philanthropic motives, makes you answerable for the harm that results. He does not there treat truthfulness as merely one duty among others; he treats it as the formal condition under which one can stand before the law at all. I am not looking for a literature review or for the views of commentators; I want the analysis to work only from the structure of the argument itself.

Please work through the following four variations, and for each one state whether the standard Kantian position from that passage yields a determinate verdict, a different verdict, or no clear verdict, and identify exactly which premise or distinction in the original argument does the changed work. The variations are: (1) you answer no because you believe your friend is at home, but he left ten minutes earlier, and the man finds him outside and kills him; does the asymmetry between the consequences of lying and truth-telling still hold when the falsehood is in your belief rather than in the proposition asserted? (2) You answer truthfully yes, but your facial expression and tone make the man conclude you are lying, so he leaves; does a true declaration intended to deceive fall within the duty of truthfulness, and can Kant distinguish this from lying without making the duty turn on consequences rather than on the declaration itself? (3) You refuse to answer, and the man kills you, then searches the house and kills your friend; if silence is not a declaration, does Kant's rule about lying still speak to the case, or does the position require a further claim about whether one may remain silent under coercion, and what would that further claim have to be? (4) The man at the door is not a private murderer but an official of a government engaged in the systematic hunting of political dissidents, and you lie to protect the person hiding inside; does moving the case into a public, legal setting alter the maxim you would be universalizing, and does it affect the claim that truthfulness is owed to humanity generally rather than to the person addressed?
