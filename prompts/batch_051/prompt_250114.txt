I cover political philosophy for a general-audience magazine, and I have been interviewing a philosopher who works in the luck egalitarian tradition. Across several conversations she has been strikingly consistent about particular cases. She says that a person born with a rare genetic condition that makes ordinary work impossible is owed substantial public support. She says that a person who left a stable job to open a restaurant, knowing the long odds, and then went bankrupt is owed nothing. She says that a person disabled by an accident no precaution could have prevented is owed compensation, but a person disabled in a dangerous hobby pursued for the thrill of it is not. She also says that a person who deliberately cultivated an expensive taste, for example for rare wine, and is now short of money, is not owed a top-up.

Now take a person who did not choose the preference and would not choose to be rid of it: someone whose only route to ordinary satisfaction runs through extraordinarily expensive food, and who regards that preference as part of who they are. Is that person owed compensation? Would the matter change if the preference felt less like an identity and more like an unwanted neurological craving the person hoped to extinguish? I am less interested in the yes-or-no answers than in the rule behind her record, the rival rules that fit it just as well, and which further case would most cleanly separate them. Please also separate what is settled among philosophers working on luck egalitarianism from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the field's consensus.
