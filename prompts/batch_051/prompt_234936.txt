As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I keep thinking about self-deception in terms of database consistency violations: a process updates a committed belief in a way that serves a concurrent desire, but the update is not visible to the part of the system that does explicit reasoning. Suppose we altered human cognitive architecture, starting tomorrow, so that whenever a person forms or revises a belief in a direction that a simultaneous desire, fear, or identity commitment favors, an involuntary internal check fires. The person experiences a distinct, undeniable sensation, something like a compile-time warning about circular reasoning, before the belief is fully committed. The check does not prevent the update; the person remains free to accept, reject, or postpone the belief. Nothing else changes: memory, perception, social incentives, institutions, and emotional life operate exactly as before, except that this one signal is always available.

I would like a reasoned prediction of how this plays out over thirty years in the lives of individuals and in institutions. Pay particular attention to romantic relationships, political ideology, workplace rationalization, addiction, religious faith, marketing, and the stability of self-esteem. I want the chain of reasoning made visible: which downstream effects follow almost mechanically from the new signal, which depend on how people learn to reinterpret or blunt the warning, and which depend on the assumption that people actually want consistency with their own explicit reasoning. Also address whether the intervention would reduce self-deception globally, or whether people would simply route around the check by fragmenting their goals, renaming the desires, or delegating belief formation to groups. Which of your predictions are you most confident in, and what would have to be true for the overall outcome to be worse than the status quo?
