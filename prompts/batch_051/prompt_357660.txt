I've been reporting a piece on blame and forgiveness, and I keep bumping into the same problem. Strawson's basic move, as I understand it, is to stop asking whether people are free in some deep metaphysical sense and look instead at the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, guilt, that sort of thing — that we actually hold one another to in everyday life. The trouble is that nobody gives a clean defintion of what makes an attitude a reactive one, and I'd like one I can actually use.

So here's the ask. Propose a working definition of a reactive attitude that gets these cases right:

- resenting a colleague who deliberately takes credit for your work;
- feeling grateful to a stranger who stops to help you change a tire;
- being furious at a thunderstorm for ruining a picnic you'd planned for weeks;
- feeling indignant on behalf of a stranger you've only read about in the news;
- feeling guilty after breaking a promise to a friend;
- being annoyed at a two-year-old who keeps biting you;
- feeling hurt by something cruel said by a relative with advanced dementia;
- feeling wronged by a pricing algorithm that suddenly jacked up the cost of a drug you need;
- briefly resenting a chatbot that gives you a curt, dismissive answer.

I'm confident the colleague and the helpful stranger are in, and the thunderstorm is out. The toddler, the relative with dementia, the algorithm, and the chatbot are the ones I honestly can't sort. Run the definition through all nine, tell me where it surprises you, and if it gives the wrong answer somewhere, either revise it or defend the surprising verdict.

One more thing, since I'd be leaning on this in print: say how confident you are in the definition overall, and single out the point or case you're least sure about.
