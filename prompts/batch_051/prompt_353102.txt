I'm a psych PhD student and my dissertation has turned into a statistics proxy war, and by now it's personal. My advisor is a strict frequentist: p-values, confidence intervals, preregistration, control your Type I error rate, and she visibly winces if someone reads p = .08 as weak evidence for the null. My other committee member is a Bayesian who wants me to report Bayes factors and credible intervals, and keeps saying the replication crisis is partly what you get when a whole field builds its epistemology around p < .05. What gets me is that neither one is crazy, and neither one moves. I've read enough methods stuff now to see this fight goes back decades and the replication crisis just reheated it. So my question is: what kind of disagreement are these two actually having? I can think of a bunch of possibilities and I want a diagnosis, not a scorecard. Is it a factual dispute about which statistical practices actually caused the replication mess? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "probability" — long-run frequencies versus degrees of belief? Is it that they're answering seperate questions, one about error control over hypothetical repeated samples and the other about what I should believe after seeing this one dataset? Or is it just unarguable bedrock intuitions about whether letting a prior into the analysis is a feature or a bug? The optional-stopping thing confuses me too: my advisor treats peeking at the data as a sin, and my committee member acts like it's obvious that stopping rules are irrelevant to what the data support. I have a working theory that most of the heat comes from the two of them solving different problems under one heading, and that once you see that, the debate stops looking like something a simulation study could settle. But I definately want that pressure-tested. Tell me what shape the disagreement has, and tell me what would actually count as progress given that diagnosis — a theorem, a different question, a decision-theoretic argument, a change in how we train people — or whether I should just accept that the two of them will never converge and stop expecting a single right way to do stats to exist. I don't need a reading list; I need to understand the impasse well enough to defend an analysis plan to both of them at my defense.
