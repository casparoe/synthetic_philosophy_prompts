I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and over the past year I have repeatedly watched empirical disputes — over how much weight to give a clinical trial, over the assumptions packed into a climate model, over the statistics behind a forensic match — slide into a more abstract quarrel about whether anyone's chain of reasons ever really bottoms out. At one such event, a philosopher of science I know walked the group through what she called the Münchhausen trilemma, attributed to Hans Albert, and concluded that all justification is ultimately arbitrary and that every disagreement therefore ends in something like a leap of faith. I admit I have seen this move used to dismiss lines of evidence I took seriously, so I want to check whether the underlying argument actually holds. This is my best reconstruction of what she said:

(1) For a belief to be rationally justified, the person holding it must be able to offer some reason in its support.
(2) That reason can support the belief only if the reason is itself rationally justified.
(3) Justification therefore runs along a chain of reasons: the original belief is supported by R1, R1 by R2, R2 by R3, and so on.
(4) Any such chain must have one of three shapes. It may run on forever; it may eventually circle back to something already assumed; or it may stop at a point where no further reason is given.
(5) An endless chain cannot confer justification, because at any finite stage the chain is still incomplete and the original belief's warrant has not been secured.
(6) A circular chain cannot confer justification, because it presupposes one of the beliefs it is trying to establish.
(7) A chain that merely stops cannot confer justification, because the final reason is asserted without support — dogmatically.
(8) Therefore no belief is rationally justified, and every claim to justification bottoms out in infinite deferral, circularity, or dogmatism.

If this argument is sound, much of what I do professionally — pressing sources for their reasons, ranking bodies of evidence, conceding to a stronger case — would seem to be pointless in principle, so I want to know exactly where it might go wrong. Please act as a professional epistemologist with a working knowledge of the later literature on this dilemma. Begin by restating the structure of the argument in your own words and identifying any assumptions hidden in the image of justification as a chain. Then go premise by premise: is the deduction valid, and if it is, which premise or premises would a foundationalist, a coherentist, and an infinitist each reject, and on what reading? Finally, say whether the skeptical conclusion follows only if we adopt a needlessly demanding standard of justification, and whether someone doing ordinary scientific or journalistic reasoning is obliged to adopt that standard.
