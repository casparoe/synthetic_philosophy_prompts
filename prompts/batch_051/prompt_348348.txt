You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory and institutional design. Think through the following carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate views before settling on the one that best explains the evidence. I am not looking for a quick label; distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with, and mark where they leave the matter underdetermined.

Here is a puzzle about three features of actually existing democracies. First, legislators are chosen by competitive election: candidates advertise their experience and qualifications, and voters are expected to select the best person available. Second, some of these same systems impose term limits on legislators, forcibly retiring them after a fixed number of terms even when they remain popular and seemingly competent; the usual rationale is to prevent a permanent political class, keep long-serving politicians from becoming corrupt or out of touch, and rotate ordinary citizens through office. Third, when ordinary citizens are asked to decide a particular case or to deliberate on a policy, the system often selects them by lot: juries are chosen by lottery, and in recent years some countries have convened randomly selected citizens' assemblies to deliberate on constitutional or policy questions such as abortion law or climate policy. Yet nobody proposes choosing surgeons, trial judges, or air-traffic controllers by lot, and nobody argues that an experienced surgeon should be term-limited out of the profession.

At first glance these practices embody conflicting pictures of political judgment. Election seems to presuppose that governing rewards acquired skill, knowledge, and voter discernment. Term limits seem to presuppose that long service is more dangerous than valuable, and that an ordinary citizen rotating through office is at least acceptable. Lottery selection seems to presuppose that ordinary citizens can be trusted with serious decisions without campaigning or professional training — even though the same society would not trust an ordinary citizen to perform surgery or direct air traffic.

What theory or theories of political representation, civic competence, and political judgment do these practices jointly presuppose? Is there one coherent theory that makes sense of all three, or does the package rest on inconsistent commitments? If the package is inconsistent, which practices would have to change under the main rival theories — for example, a theory on which governing is a skilled profession like surgery, a theory on which representation ought to mirror the citizenry rather than select the unusually able, or a theory on which the point of democratic selection is to prevent the rule of a permanent political class? Be explicit about exactly what each practice presupposes, as opposed to what it is merely compatible with.
