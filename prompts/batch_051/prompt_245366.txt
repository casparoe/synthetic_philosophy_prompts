I am drafting a novel in which a former professional coffee taster, after decades of writing precise tasting notes, becomes convinced that his notes cannot be about anything private: he starts saying there is no 'what it is like' to taste coffee, only judgments, standards, memories, and reactive preferences. To keep his position from collapsing into vague skepticism, I need a rigorous working definition of 'qualia' — the very thing he is rejecting.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle. Clear positives: the vivid redness of a ripe tomato as you look at it in ordinary light; the throbbing pain of a stubbed toe. Clear negatives: your standing belief that Paris is the capital of France; your automatic tendency to flinch when an object moves quickly toward your face. The harder cases: a mental image of a red tomato, called up voluntarily and held before the mind's eye; a wine taster's acquired ability to detect tannin structure, where she insists the taste itself now seems different rather than merely more interpretively available; the difference between two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who after the blend changes make opposing reports — one says the coffee tastes the same but he no longer likes it, while the other says it now tastes different and he likes it no less, and each insists the other's report is confused; blindsight, in which a person denies seeing but can guess a light's location at above-chance levels; and the 'what it is like' of echolocation for a blind person navigating a room.

Propose a definition, run it through all seven cases, and tell me which of your verdicts is least secure. If the definition forces surprising judgments — for example, that some ordinary first-person reports of felt experience are not really about qualia, or that two of the cases differ less than their surface grammar suggests — defend those judgments rather than quietly revising around them.
