Ok so Adam Smith's line is that we feel for other people by imagining ourselves in their place, and that we eventually build up this impartial spectator inside us that corrects for the fact that we're naturally way more worked up about our own stuff than other people's. He even used the earthquake-in-China example to show how distance cools our sympathy: we'd lose sleep over the prospect of losing our little finger but sleep fine after hearing about a huge far-off disaster. Fair enough. But what do you do with the inverse case, which is pretty much the main thing that happens on modern social media? The algorithm feeds us snippets about strangers' conflicts from thousands of miles away, we perform our indignation for an audience, and suddenly the target is getting a pile-on that seems way out of proportion to what anyone close to the actual situation feels. Its like distance makes the reaction bigger, not smaller.

I want someone to actually run this through Smith's machinery. If Smith could watch this play out over a few months, where exactly do you think he'd say the breakdown happens: in the spectators' information, in their sympathy (maybe they're not imagining the person's situation so much as a cleaned-up, shareable story), or in their self-command? And in a pile-on, would Smith's impartial spectator mostly ratify the crowd's resentment, or mostly tell the crowd to sit down? Give me a fairly precise answer, not just he'd be worried about mobs.
