I'm on a compliance team at a bank, arguing with my manager about our model-governance manual. The clause that matters says:

'No covered model may be used to make a decision materially affecting a customer unless at least one human employee is able, at the time of the decision, to state the principal grounds on which the model reached it, and to say why those grounds are sound.'

When that was drafted, "model" mostly meant a linear scorecard, and the idea was that someone could point to the coefficients. Now we approve or reject fraud appeals with a large language model, and no one can read the weights. So we trained a second model, an interpreter, that takes the first model's activations and outputs a short plain-English rationale: "party type and amount plus the timing of the account opening put this in a high-risk cluster," that kind of thing. The interpreter agrees with the base model very well in backtests, but no one can explain the interpreter either; if you ask it twice about the same decision, it sometimes gives contradictory stories. The human reviewer just reads the interpreter's note aloud and signs.

My manager says we are obviously compliant: an employee can state the grounds, because the grounds are just whatever the interpreter's note says. I think we are laundering one opaque model through another. How would you argue it from the text, from the purpose of the clause, and from what the drafters would have said if they had known where this would go? Which approach should win here, and why? And doesn't the recursive point stand regardless: the interpreter is itself a model we cannot explain, so either the policy is violated or it has to bottom out at something like trust in the interpreter?
