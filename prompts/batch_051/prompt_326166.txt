In the subantarctic Macquarie Island, feral cats introduced by nineteenth-century sealers had long preyed on burrowing seabirds. Rabbits, introduced later, also supplied the cats with food and were kept partly in check by them. In 1985, managers began a cat eradication program to protect the seabirds, and the last cat was removed in 2000. Within a few years, rabbit numbers exploded, grazing stripped much of the island's vegetation, and severe erosion followed. Managers then carried out a large-scale baiting program against rabbits, rats, and mice, and by 2014 the island was declared free of those introduced mammals.

Analyze this sequence through the lens of the debate about whether habitat destruction reduces wild-animal suffering and how to assess the net welfare of ecosystems. The question is not whether the program succeeded in its conservation goals, but what theory of value the sequence presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit welfare theory of the eradication program: did it treat individual cats, rabbits, rats, mice, and seabirds as units of welfare at all, or did it treat species, vegetation, and ecosystem integrity as the relevant locus of value? Then tell me what a view that counts individual wild-animal welfare as genuinely important would say about each step—the initial cat eradication, the rabbit outbreak, the poisoning campaign, and the eventual revegetation. Does the case show that habitat restoration and wild-animal welfare diverge, or can a net-welfare view endorse the program after all? And what does the episode reveal about the theory itself: does it expose a blind spot in ecosystem-health framing, or does it show that wild-animal welfare theory still lacks a tractable alternative to conservation as usual?
