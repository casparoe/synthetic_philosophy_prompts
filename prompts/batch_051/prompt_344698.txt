In the philosophy-of-mind reading group I attend, one member, M., has become my touchstone on the personal-identity cases we discuss, because her verdicts are quick, confident, and apparently consistent. Her track record over the past term runs as follows. (1) A working matter transporter that disintegrates a person here and assembles a molecule-for-molecule duplicate elsewhere, with intact memories: M. says the person who steps in is destroyed, and the arrival is a new person whose memories are merely copied. (2) A program that gradually replaces neurons one at a time with silicon equivalents, spread over years, with the patient awake and subjectively unchanged at every step: M. says the patient plainly survives; there is no step at which the self is replaced, only a long series of small exchanges. (3) A scanner that records every detail of a brain while destroying the tissue, then boots up a whole-brain emulation on a computer: M. says the patient dies in the scanner and the emulation is a duplicate inheriting that patient's memories, because the continuous chain of mental life is broken before it resumes elsewhere. (4) A total and irreversible loss of episodic memory, in which the person cannot recognize any event from her past but retains language, skills, temperament, and habits: M. says it is still the same person, badly injured rather than replaced. (5) A hemispherectomy performed to control intractable epilepsy, after which the patient wakes with much of her personality intact: M. says the same person survives.

We have just encountered a new case, and I have a small bet riding on what M. will say. A neurosurgical proposal, call it forebrain transfer, moves a person's entire forebrain — the seat of her memories, character, and stream of thought — into a second body whose own forebrain has been destroyed, preserving the transferred tissue's blood supply throughout. The original body is kept alive by its brainstem and lies in a coma. M. has not yet heard this case. What should I predict she will say about whether the person survives and, if so, in which body? More importantly, what rule is she actually running? I can see at least two candidate rules that fit all five of her prior verdicts: a biological-continuity view, on which a person is the living organism and survives just insofar as that organism continues to live, and a psychological-continuity view, on which survival consists in an uninterrupted overlap of mental life — memories, habits, temperament, the stream of thought — carried by whatever parts sustain it from step to step. Do her verdicts and her stated reasons favor one candidate over the other? Would forebrain transfer force them apart? And if you think two candidates remain standing afterward, give me one further hypothetical, different from forebrain transfer, that would settle between them.
