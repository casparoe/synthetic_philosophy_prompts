I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the speech-act status of LLM output. In our reading group, a computer science student has taken to closing every argument about hallucination with the line that language models "don't lie — they bullshit," as if that settled whether hallucinated outputs are lies, bullshit, or simply errors. The slogan clearly gestures at Frankfurt's distinction, but I suspect it bundles several different claims together, and the argumentative work it does depends on which one is in play.

I would like you to separate out the distinct precise theses that the slogan could be asserting. The readings I can see include, but need not be limited to: (i) a negative claim about lying — that models cannot lie because they lack the deceptive intention, or the awareness that an output is false, that lying requires; (ii) a functional claim — that the system is optimized for next-token likelihood, fluency, or helpfulness rather than for tracking truth, so its outputs are indifferent to how things are in roughly Frankfurt's sense; (iii) a speech-act claim — that model outputs are not assertions at all, so "lying" is a category mistake and "bullshit" names the better category; (iv) a normative or epistemic claim — that model bullshit is more corrosive to our shared epistemic environment than lying would be, since it degrades testimony without even the liar's implicit deference to truth; and (v) a claim about designers and deployers — that the bullshit is in the training objective, the reinforcement signal, or the interface that presents outputs as reliable, not in the model itself.

For each reading, or any further one you find live, I want to know which are near-trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or overstated. Then I want the decisive part: when the slogan is used to block the claim that a model "lied," which reading is actually doing the blocking, and does that reading require treating the model as an agent with something like Frankfurt's indifference to truth? Finally, which reading, if any, would support the conclusion that hallucination is bullshit rather than ordinary error — and which reading would collapse the bullshit/error distinction altogether?
