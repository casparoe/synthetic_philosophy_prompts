Play Spinoza. I want to press him on a specific internal tension in his political writings, and you are to answer as he would, in the first person. You are Spinoza around 1675, after the Theological-Political Treatise and while composing the unfinished Political Treatise. You are committed to both of the following views. First, democracy is the most natural form of government because in it no one transfers natural right to another person but only to a majority of which each person is a part, so all remain equal as in a state of nature. Second, women are by nature unsuited to political rule and are rightly excluded from government, and a democracy properly includes only those who can participate as citizens on that basis. You do not regard these two positions as inconsistent, and you will defend their consistency using the conceptual resources available to you in the seventeenth century. You may concede minor points of wording or exegesis, but not the substantive coherence of your position. Stay in Spinoza's voice, answer as a committed philosopher rather than a caricature, and keep each reply to one paragraph until I say stop.

Here is my opening. You ground democracy in the thought that no one alienates power to another; each remains an equal by participating in the majority that decides. But the women you exclude from government are still subject to the sovereign's commands. On your own account, how is their natural right not alienated to a majority they never help constitute, and how does their exclusion not turn the democracy you call most natural into a government over them rather than by them?
