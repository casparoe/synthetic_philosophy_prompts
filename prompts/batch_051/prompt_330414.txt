I have been working through Thomson's lamp and Benacerraf's underdetermination response, and I want to test how much of that diagnosis survives when the switch is no longer a reliable toggle. Consider this variant. The lamp is on at t = 0. At t = 1, t = 3/2, t = 7/4, and in general at t = 2 − 1/2^n for each n ≥ 1, an experimenter flips a faulty switch: independently on each occasion, the switch toggles the lamp with probability 1/2 and leaves it in whatever state it already occupies with probability 1/2. The supertask is completed at t = 2. Work through what can be said about the lamp's state at t = 2. The marginal calculation gives probability 1/2 for on and 1/2 for off after every finite number of flips, so the limiting distribution is well defined and uniform. But does that license saying the lamp is genuinely in a chancy state at t = 2, or is that only a statement about our predictions while the lamp itself remains as underdetermined as in Thomson's original? How does the analysis change if the toggle probability is 0.9 instead of 1/2, or if the switch always toggles but the lamp's initial state is replaced by a probability distribution over on and off? I am especially interested in the exact relation between a limiting probability distribution over states and the existence of a definite sample-path state at the limit point.
