After a long argument with a friend about religion in politics, I keep coming back to a passage in John Rawls’s *The Idea of Public Reason Revisited*. He writes, in my paraphrase, that reasonable comprehensive doctrines—religious views included—may be introduced into public political discussion at any time, provided that in due course proper political reasons, and not reasons given solely by comprehensive doctrines, are presented sufficient to support whatever the comprehensive doctrines were introduced to support. What struck me is an asymmetry in how we apply this. A legislator may say in a hearing that he opposes a policy because his faith teaches something, and then later or even on the spot give a secular justification—consequentialist, constitutional, whatever—and we treat the religious opening as perfectly acceptable, even as evidence of candor. But the reverse sequence does not seem to work. If a legislator first gives a fully secular argument and then adds that his faith also commands the same conclusion, the religious addition is inert; if anything, it makes people wonder whether the secular argument was sincere. And a judge who offered a legal argument but was later shown to have been actually motivated by a religious precept would be accused of something much worse than the legislator who openly began from faith. Is this asymmetry—that a religious reason can be redeemed by a later political reason, while a political reason cannot be redeemed or enhanced by a later religious reason—actually justified? Or does it show that we only pretend to care about the public reason while really caring about the order in which people present their reasons? If you had to keep or reject this asymmetry, which would you do?
