I am writing a paper for a seminar on moral philosophy and twentieth-century history, and I want to test Kant's murderer-at-the-door account of lying against a real historical episode. Between 1942 and 1944 the Frank family was hidden in a secret annex in occupied Amsterdam by a small network of friends and employees; when questioned by police or suspicious callers, those helpers denied that they had anyone in hiding. On its surface this is exactly Kant's case: someone with homicidal intent asks a question, and a truthful answer would likely lead to the deaths of the people being sought. I would like an analysis that runs in both directions—what Kant's position implies about what the helpers did, and what the historical case, with its complicating features, shows about the position itself.

In particular, please address the following. First, in Kant's example the speaker knows who is at the door and what he intends; in the historical case, a helper answering a knock often knew only that the caller was an officer or that a raid was likely, not that these specific people would certainly be found and killed. Does the difference between knowledge and high probability matter for the duty Kant describes? Second, is the rule best read as a ban on false assertion, or as a ban on making oneself an instrument of the questioner's lethal plan by supplying information? Those descriptions come apart in cases where silence or a true-but-misleading statement would still guide the officer. Third, how should evasive denials—refusing to answer, saying something true that implies the falsehood without asserting it, or directing the questioner elsewhere—be classified on Kant's view, and does the view have the resources to permit evasion while condemning only the lie? Finally, what does this case show about the claim that truthfulness is an unconditional duty? Does it suggest that the position has been misread, that it generates an unacceptable conclusion, or that it was never meant to settle cases with this much uncertainty?

I am less interested in a moral verdict than in a precise mapping of the thought experiment onto the historical case, including the places where the analogy breaks.
