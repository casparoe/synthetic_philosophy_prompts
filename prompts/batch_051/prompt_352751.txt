I'm putting together a unit on presupposition and accommodation for a philosophy of language course, and I've been trying to think of a film that actually dramatises what's at stake when a speaker quietly adds propositions to the common ground. The Lewis scorekeeping idea, roughly, is that conversation is a kind of game with a scoreboard: every utterance updates the score, and sometimes an utterance presupposes a proposition that wasn't already there, and the hearers accommodate it rather than stopping to challenge it. Normally that reads as friendly repair — someone mentions their sister and you just take on board that they have a sister, no fuss. But I keep coming back to Gaslight, the Hollywood film where a husband slowly convinces his wife she's losing her mind. It seems to me he rarely asserts outright that she is going mad, which she could flatly deny; instead he asks after the brooch as if she is always misplacing things, comments on how tired she looks, and generally talks as if her unreliability is already on the scoreboard, so she — and sometimes the household — accommodates all of it. So accommodation can't be a neutral repair mechanism, can it?

Could you read the film through that lens for me? I'd like to know where the scorekeeping machinary does work that bald lying or assertion couldn't, and where the model breaks down or misses something about what the film shows concerning power and the construction of shared reality. Also, which scenes would you actually show a class, and what would you say beforehand to stop them reading it as simply a film about a husband lying to his wife? I'd like a reading that takes both the language-game picture and the film seriously, and that says at the end what the film adds to the philosophical story. I'm in the UK, so please write in British English.
