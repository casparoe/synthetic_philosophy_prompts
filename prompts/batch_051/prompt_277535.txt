I am a longtime reader of LessWrong, the EA Forum, and related rationalist-adjacent blogs, and I have internalized the habit of asking for precise cruxes and preregistered predictions. For months my reading group has been stuck on the question of whether social facts are anything over and above facts about individuals, with one member defending a strong methodological individualism and another defending a strong methodological holism. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of social science and draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, with their disagreement made exact. They have agreed to organize the collaboration around the following passage from John Stuart Mill, A System of Logic, Book VI, Chapter I, Section 1:

If on matters so much the most important with which human intellect can occupy itself a more general agreement is ever to exist among thinkers; if what has been pronounced "the proper study of mankind" is not destined to remain the only subject which Philosophy can not succeed in rescuing from Empiricism; the same process through which the laws of many simpler phenomena have by general acknowledgment been placed beyond dispute, must be consciously and deliberately applied to those more difficult inquiries.

The passage is not a neutral data point for them: each side reads it as partial support for their own view, and one of their agreed cruxes is exactly how to interpret it. The deliverable should be a document they could both sign. Format it as follows: first, a numbered list of claims about social-scientific explanation, emergence, and the relationship between individual-level and aggregate-level facts that both parties accept, stated in language each would accept; second, a disagreements table with columns for (a) the issue, (b) the methodological individualist's own wording of their position, (c) the methodological holist's own wording of their position, (d) the precise crux separating them, (e) a concrete case, experiment, or evidence both agree would bear on that crux, and (f) each side's preregistered prediction about what that case or evidence will show. The statement should include at least one crux directly about how to read the Mill passage, and at least one crux about a concrete social phenomenon, such as ideological polarization, market prices, or the persistence of institutions. Before writing the final document, brainstorm the interpretive options for the Mill passage and consider which apparent disagreements are merely verbal; flag any such verbal disputes explicitly, and say which part, if any, of the debate both parties agree is not worth continuing.
