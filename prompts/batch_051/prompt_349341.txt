Here's an asymmetry I can't shake. In classical mathematics you can prove that something exists just by assuming it doesn't and deriving a contradiction: "there is an n with property P" goes in the books, even if the proof gives no witness, no procedure for finding n, and not even a bound on how big it might be. But try the same move in ordinary life and people treat you as having done nothing. A customs officer who proved that a shipment must contain contraband because the assumption that it's clean leads to absurdity — but who couldn't say which crate — hasn't actually found contraband, and wouldn't be allowed to seize anything. A detective who proves the killer is in the room but can't point at a person has solved nothing. So the same logical shape is treated as a discovery in math and a non-result everywhere else. Is the asymmetry defensible? The constructivist says no: classical math has been letting a rule that intuitionistic logic rejects (from not-not-P infer P, or the full force of excluded middle) do the work, and nobody would accept that rule when a shipment or a conviction is on the line. The classical mathematician has to say the ordinary cases are disanalogous, or that ordinary life is just being picky. I want the strongest principle you can find that either licenses the mathematical move, condemns the ordinary counterpart, or explains the split — and then an honest verdict on wether we should keep the asymmetry, bring ordinary reasoning up to classical standards, or make mathematics constructive.
