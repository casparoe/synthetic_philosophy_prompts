I've been reading about the empirical challenge to virtue ethics, and I keep coming back to a passage from a popular psychology magazine that I cannot fully evaluate on my own. The article introduces "situationism" as follows:

"Virtue ethics counsels us to become honest, courageous, and compassionate people and to act from these stable traits of character. But social psychology has shown that this picture rests on a myth. In the famous Good Samaritan experiment, Princeton seminary students who had been told they were running late almost all walked straight past a man slumped and groaning in a doorway, even when the talk they were about to give was on the parable of the Good Samaritan. In a similar study, people who found a dime in a payphone helped a stranger who had dropped her papers, whereas hardly anyone who found no dime helped. The lesson, according to social psychologists, is that helping behavior is driven almost entirely by trivial features of the situation rather than by anything stable inside the person. Studies of honesty tell the same story: a child who cheats on one task is barely more likely to cheat on another than a child who did not, which shows that behavior has no meaningful cross-situational consistency; the correlations are effectively zero. Psychologists call the mistake of inferring stable character traits from such behavior the fundamental attribution error, and it is exactly this mistake that virtue ethicists make when they posit global virtues. Since virtue ethics requires virtue to be a global disposition that reliably manifests itself across different kinds of situations, and since global traits of that kind do not exist, virtue ethics rests on a false empirical psychology and has been refuted. At most, we are left with local, situation-specific habits, but nothing like what Aristotle meant by virtue."

I find the article persuasive, because it seems to have real experiments behind it, but I also suspect it is moving too quickly from laboratory results to a sweeping philosophical conclusion. I do not know the empirical literature or the virtue-ethical replies well enough to say where. Please audit this passage: take it claim by claim and tell me (a) which parts a careful social psychologist would accept as accurate summaries of the studies, (b) which parts are simplifications or overstatements, and (c) whether the final conclusion that virtue ethics has been refuted actually follows from the accurate parts. On that last point, say whether a virtue ethicist could reasonably reject one of the premises, and what the strongest version of the situationist argument would still establish once the overstatements are corrected.
