I've been turning over an argument I built after a friend claimed that no scientific theory is really more true than another, since every theory eventually gets replaced. The argument seems to prove something I don't believe, and I can't find the weak step.

(1) A theory is a bundle of claims, and a false theory is simply a bundle that contains at least one false claim.
(2) To say that one false theory is closer to the truth than another, you would have to compare how much truth each bundle contains.
(3) But the amount of truth in a bundle is not fixed by the theory or by the world. Any theory can be restated as one enormous claim joined by the word "and" — which then counts as a single false claim — or as many small claims, most of them true and one of them false, so the ratio of true to false parts depends on how you slice the bundle.
(4) If the score depends on how you slice the bundle, then closeness to the truth is not a real feature of the theory but an artifact of phrasing.
(5) Therefore no false theory is genuinely closer to the truth than any other; saying one is "less wrong" than another is just a manner of speaking.
(6) But this conclusion is absurd. The old belief that disease comes from an imbalance of four bodily humors is much farther from the truth than the early idea that microscopic organisms cause infectious disease, even though that early idea got many details wrong. Newton's physics is also plainly closer to the truth than Aristotle's, though both are strictly false.

Where does the argument go wrong? Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I would rather keep the verdict that Newton's physics is closer to the truth than Aristotle's than keep the picture of truth as a count of true and false parts.
