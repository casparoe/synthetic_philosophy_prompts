I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been turning over, on my own, a family of everyday inferences: the move from how things look, or from what an instrument or another person tells one, to a stronger conclusion—including the conclusion that one is not being misled in some particular way. My verdicts on individual cases are firm, yet I cannot find a single principle that makes sense of them all. I would like you to work out what principle, if any, best captures my verdicts; I also want to know which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and what the principle says about the unsettled cases at the end. Where not all of my verdicts can be kept, I want an honest account of which ones go and why.

1. The red table. I walk into a room and see a table that looks red, in lighting that seems ordinary. I judge that the table is red. If someone then asks whether I know that the table is not a white table lit by red light, I want to answer yes: the table's being red rules out that possibility, and I know the table is red.

2. The waiting-room clock. I am in an unfamiliar waiting room. The wall clock reads 3:00. I have no watch, no phone, and no other way to check the time. The clock is running and looks in good repair, but I know nothing about its history. I form the judgment that it is 3:00. I want to say I know that it is 3:00. But if someone asks whether I know the clock is not wrong, I want to say no—not yet. Yet these two verdicts collide: if I know it is 3:00 and the clock reads 3:00, it follows that the clock is not wrong, at least about the present reading.

3. The rental-car gauge. I start an unfamiliar rental car. The fuel gauge reads full. It looks normal, but I have never driven the car and know nothing about the gauge's history. Do I know the tank is full? My verdict: no, not yet. The gauge could be broken or badly calibrated, and I would want some independent check before I would claim to know.

4. The familiar gauge. I have driven my own car for years. Over that time the fuel gauge has been dependable: when it read empty, the tank took a full tank when filled, and its readings have matched the distances I have driven. Today it reads three-quarters full. Do I know the tank is about three-quarters full? Yes. Do I know the gauge is not wrong on this occasion? I want to say yes.

5. The gauge vouching for itself. I try to see whether a single reading can establish the gauge's correctness. I reason: the gauge reads full; so the tank is full; so the gauge read correctly this time. Does that reasoning put me in a position to know that the gauge read correctly? I want to say no: as a way of establishing the gauge's reliability, it is circular, even though each step is valid. But this makes me uneasy, because it now seems close to the red-table case. There too, my only evidence for the color of the table came from my eyes, and I used that very evidence to rule out one way my eyes could have been misleading.

6. The weather-wise friend. A friend has been right about whether it will rain on the great majority of past occasions; she has a practiced eye and no special theory. She tells me it will rain this afternoon. Do I know it will rain? Yes. Do I know that she is telling the truth this time rather than making an uncharacteristic mistake? I want to say yes, in any ordinary sense.

7. The brand-new thermometer. I buy a new digital thermometer from a drugstore and use it as directed on my child. It reads 39.0°C. Do I know my child has a fever? I want to say yes, even though I have never used this particular thermometer before and have no second thermometer to confirm. This sits uneasily with my verdict on the rental-car gauge: both are unfamiliar instruments read for the first time.

I have not been able to state the difference myself. I feel as though there is a difference between a source that earns its credentials through independent checks and a source that is left to speak for itself, but I cannot say precisely what counts as independent enough, or why the red-table case seems to fall on the acceptable side of the line while the gauge vouching for itself does not.

Two further cases, where I have no settled verdict, seem to turn on whatever principle is right here. First, a stranger who looks like an ordinary, unhurried passer-by tells me that the bus I am waiting for has been rerouted and will not stop here. I have no independent confirmation and no particular reason to think he is deceiving me, but I know nothing about him. Do I know the bus will not stop? Sometimes I think yes, because I would act on the information; sometimes I think no. Second, I open a newly printed official timetable for a train line I have never used, and it says the last train leaves at 11:40. Do I know the last train leaves at 11:40? Is that more like the thermometer, the rental-car gauge, or the weather-wise friend?

I am asking you to extract the principle behind my judgments, state it as precisely as you can, show how each of my verdicts either follows from it or must be revised, and then apply it to the two unsettled cases. If no single principled way exists and the choice is between a complicated principle that saves every verdict and a simpler one that overturns some, I want you to say so plainly.
