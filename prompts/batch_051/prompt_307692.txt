I am a doctoral student working on Machiavelli's political thought, and I have a standing interpretive rule that I would like you to test. Whenever I have to explain a political success or failure in the texts I study, I treat fortune—timing, circumstances, the accidents of others' conduct—as the default explanation, and I only credit virtù when I can point to a specific moment at which the agent changed his whole mode of proceeding, such as his instruments, alliances, or characteristic conduct, in response to changed circumstances. My supervisor says this is too restrictive: Machiavellian virtù also includes courage, decisiveness, and the audacity to force circumstances to conform to a fixed temperament, none of which requires changing. I reply that without the requirement of observable change, I cannot tell genuine skill at responding to circumstances from a fixed temperament that happened to suit the times. Is my rule a defensible epistemic policy? If it goes wrong, where exactly, and what is the corrected version that neither credits fortune with the name of virtù nor credits virtù to what was only luck?
