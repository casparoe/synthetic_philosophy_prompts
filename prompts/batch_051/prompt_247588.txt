Last month a colleague ran the Allais problem past a few of us at lunch — the one where most people prefer a guaranteed £1m over an 89/10/1 gamble, but then prefer a 10% shot at £5m over an 11% shot at £1m — and I came out with the supposedly incoherent pair of choices. I'd have shrugged it off, except I'm about to choose between two annuity options and I keep noticing my gut does the same thing: I overpay for certainity in one comparison and then chase the long shot in another. So this has stopped being a puzzle and started being about my actual money.

Since then I've been trying to sort out the main ways people respond. The ones I keep meeting are roughly: (a) the hardline view that Allais-type choices are simply a mistake and the independence axiom should be kept as a requirement of rationality; (b) rank-dependent or prospect-theory style probability weighting, where probabilities get transformed before they're weighted, so certainty gets special treatment; (c) regret theory, where what matters isn't just the outcome but the comparison with what you'd have got in the unchosen branch; (d) disappointment-aversion accounts, where an outcome stings if it falls short of an expectation you'd formed; and (e) the smoother generalised-expected-utility line, where independence only holds locally, so small violations are unremarkable and only large ones threaten the model.

Could you put these into tiers by how much you'd trust them as an account of a sensible chooser — someone whose Allais preferences might be reasonable rather than a bias to be corrected? Rank them and say a sentence or two about why each lands where it does. Please be decisive: no putting all five in a cautious middle tier. And don't bother with citations — I want your actual assessment, not a reading list.
