I've been going back and forth on LessWrong about the utility monster again, and I want a case designed to order, not another rehash of the original. Construct a thought experiment where a total utilitarian has to bite the bullet in a way that can't be waved away by the usual empirical move — that real people don't have steep enough utility functions, that diminishing marginal utility and status comparisons make such a monster practically impossible. The trick I want: the monster isn't found, it's made. The policy itself warps one person's utility function, so the monster is endogenous to the choice rather than a weird input.

Constrainsts: realistic (an NGO program, a local government scheme, a school — no wireheading, no drugs, no sci-fi), one paragraph, and the person's new utility function has to be plausibly shaped by the intervention rather than just being a preference-satisfaction pump that happens to exist. I want the case to come out so that total utilitarianism approves the policy, a basic prioritarian weighting rejects it (say, because the monster's gains should be discounted since they accrue to someone already well-off), and a simple respect-for-persons constraint rejects it too.

After the scenario, walk through what each view says and exactly which premise does the work. Then remove the feature that makes the monster endogenous and say what the case collapses into — presumably the standard stalemate over whether such monsters actually exist. Finish with the strongest reply a committed total utilitarian would give to your case.

This is for a blog post arguing that EA defenses of aggregation have an unhandled endogeneity hole, so don't write it as if the monster objection is obviously decisive.
