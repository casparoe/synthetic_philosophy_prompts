I cover literature in translation for a general-interest magazine, and there's a pattern I keep running into that I don't have a word for. Take poems built on an artificial constraint — a sestina with its six rotating end-words, a lipogram that avoids a particular letter, a poem written using only one vowel. A certain kind of translator will keep the exact constraint but change almost everything else: the six end-words get swapped for six that work in the new language, or the banned letter is replaced by a different one that's commonest in the target tongue. The result is, semantically, practically a new poem. Yet critics (me included, I admit) tend call this "faithful to the form," and sometimes treat a version that renders every line accurately but drops the constraint as a "prose crib" or a failure. That's strange, because by the usual standards the line-by-line version is the closer translation, while the constraint-keeping one is the freer one.

Is there an established term for fidelity to a formal rule rather than to the words themselves, and does such a thing actually count as translation, or is it something more like an imitation or a new poem that merely uses the same recipe? I'm familiar with the broad literal/free distinction, but it doesn't capture this: the constraint-keeping version is often the semantically freer of the two. What I want is the carving: where the boundary lies when the constraint is arbitrary, when it's part of the poem's meaning, or when it migrates to a different spot in the poem than where the original had it. And if no established term exists, what would you call it? Please lead with the bottom line — the name, or "no established name exists" — in the first sentence, then give your reasoning without preamble or restating the question.
