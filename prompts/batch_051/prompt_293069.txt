For a piece I'm reporting on consent-tech — apps meant to record sexual consent, dynamic-consent platforms for health data, that kind of thing — I spent an hour with a philosopher who works on the ethics of deception, and he left me with an argument I can't shake. We were talking only about deception, not the other usual consent-vitiators like coercion or incompetence. His line was:

(1) If someone consents to something while being decieved about a feature of it, and they would have refused had they known the truth about that feature, then the consent is invalid. (Think lying about an STI, or lying about what a surgical procedure actually involves.)

(2) The thing doing the work in those cases is just a false belief plus the fact that the false belief made the difference between yes and no.

(3) So the source of the false belief shouldn't matter. Whether the other person lied, omitted, or simply let me walk in with my own mistaken assumption, the mechanism is the same: I'm saying yes to a situation that only exists in my head.

(4) And people consent while holding decision-changing false beliefs all the time — the patient who privately overestimates a drug's success rate, the research subject convinced the trial will help them, the person who says yes to sex assuming it means something the other person never implied, the person who clicks I agree without knowing what data's being collected.

(5) Therefore, by (1)–(3), a huge share of everyday consent — medical, research, sexual, contractual — isn't valid at all.

(6) But that can't be right: if all of that is really non-consent, the concept stops doing the work we need it to do. So the principle in (1) proves too much, and we need a different story about why only some false beliefs invalidate consent.

He said this shows the standard picture of deception and consent has a hole in the middle, and that either deception isn't special or its specialness is hiding in a distinction nobody wants to state out loud. I'd strongly prefer to keep (1) for the clear cases and to keep (6), but I'll hear a case for giving up either. Which step has to go, and what does rejecting it cost?
