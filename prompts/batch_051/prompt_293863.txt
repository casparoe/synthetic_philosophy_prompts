I am a novelist rather than an economist, and I have been working on a book whose protagonist earns a living from speculative bets. While trying to understand when a string of individually attractive bets turns ruinous, I put together the following argument. It seems to prove too much, and I would like a diagnosis.

(1) For a single, isolated decision, the rational choice is the one with the higher expected value. (2) Consider a fair-coin gamble that multiplies your current wealth by 1.5 on heads and 0.6 on tails. The expected multiplier is (1.5 + 0.6)/2 = 1.05, so each play offers a 5 percent expected gain. (3) Since each play is favorable on its own, you should take any single play; by repeating this same reasoning, you should take every play in a long sequence. (4) In a long sequence of fair flips, roughly half will come up heads and half tails, so the typical wealth path after n plays is near 1.5^(n/2) × 0.6^(n/2) = 0.9^(n/2), which tends toward zero. (5) Thus the policy endorsed by step (3) makes ruin almost certain, while simply declining every play would leave the gambler's wealth intact. (6) Therefore expected value cannot be the correct criterion, at least for repeated multiplicative gambles.

I cannot accept (6) across the board, because for a single isolated flip the gamble really does look worth taking, and I want to preserve the ordinary thought that systematically taking favorable bets should make a person richer rather than poorer. Yet I cannot find an arithmetical slip. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the argument? Please also say how confident you are in that diagnosis and which point you are least sure of, and keep the whole answer to no more than 500 words.
