I'm taking an upper-division seminar on Renaissance political thought this term, and I've been turning over a dispute that, to my mind, has reached a dead end. We spend much of our time on The Prince, in particular on the relationship between virtù and fortuna—how much of a ruler's success Machiavelli takes to be within human control, and what kind of excellence he thinks a ruler needs in order to keep power. Three positions have sat in the room all term without anyone moving. One camp reads virtù as roughly instrumental competence: whatever combination of flexibility, boldness, and strategic cruelty lets a prince secure his state, so that virtù is a success category rather than a moral one. A second camp insists that it is a real, if strange, normative ideal; especially when we bring in the Discourses, they argue, Machiavelli seems to be recovering something like civic excellence—public-spiritedness and the capacity to maintain free institutions, qualities that go beyond merely staying in power. A third camp says the essential feature of virtù is its responsiveness to fortuna: on their reading it is a disposition to read a situation and alter one's own methods and character as circumstances shift, not a fixed list of traits at all. We have all read the same passages—the fox and the lion, the image of fortune as a river whose floods can be prepared for with embankments, the claim that fortune governs roughly half of our actions while the rest is left to our control—and yet the argument keeps circling the same test cases: whether Cesare Borgia's deceptions and cruelties display virtù or corrupt it, whether the civic-military vigor praised in the Roman republic is an extension of the prince's virtù or a different concept altogether, and whether the final chapter of The Prince, with its call to liberate and unify Italy, presupposes a more morally serious ideal than the earlier chapters are willing to state.

My working theory is that the disagreement has quietly stopped being about Machiavelli and has become a disagreement about what kind of concept virtù is: a thin descriptive term for whatever works, a thick evaluative term that imports a picture of human excellence, or an essentially modal and relational concept that cannot be spelled out apart from a person's position within a particular situation. It also seems to matter a great deal whether one treats The Prince and the Discourses as one continuous project or as two distinct interventions addressed to different audiences, and our class has never actually settled that prior question. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about the texts that further evidence or closer reading could settle, a verbal disagreement about how we are using the word virtù, or several different questions being pursued under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress—a distinction, a way of relating the two works, a method for separating the descriptive and normative strands—or is this an impasse that no additional close reading should be expected to resolve?
