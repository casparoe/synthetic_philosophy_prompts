I am a philosophy professor, and over the past year I have been keeping track of a colleague's developing view about whether characters in video games are themselves the kinds of things that can be morally wronged, as distinct from being vehicles through which a player wrongs real people. In a series of departmental workshops she has committed herself to the following verdicts:

1. On a dog companion in a farming-simulation game that persists across play sessions, recognizes its owner, and is represented by the game as having hunger, mood, and health: she says the dog genuinely matters. Keeping it hungry for amusement would be wronging it, though only mildly.

2. On a companion character in a story-driven role-playing game who has a detailed backstory, memories, long-term plans, and a tracked bond of trust with the player: she says this companion matters considerably more than the dog. Manipulating her trust simply to see an alternate ending would wrong her in something like the way one can wrong a close friend.

3. On a humanoid enemy soldier in a large-scale shooter who cries out, staggers, and sometimes pleads when wounded, but has no persistence, no memory, and is replaced a moment later by an identical spawn: she says the soldier itself does not matter. Whatever distaste attaches to enjoying the death scene, it is not a wrong against the soldier.

4. On a background pedestrian in a driving game who can be hit and then triggers only a fine and a failed objective, with no injury, distress, or history modeled at all: she says the pedestrian is not a moral patient. If anything is wrong in hitting it, the wrong lies in the player's recklessness.

A game developer has now asked her to comment on a new case: a small welfare-only virtual pet in a minimalist care game. It has no backstory, no dialogue, no memory of events, and no capacity to recognize or bond with anyone. The game tracks exactly three states—satiety, comfort, and health—and the creature emits a distress tone when they fall and eventually dies; when they are kept in an appropriate range it is quiet and content. The developer's own description is that the pet is simply a vector of needs, with no self over and above them. Could the pet itself be wronged by willful neglect?

What should I expect this colleague to say if she is keeping faith with her earlier verdicts? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in (a) the criterion or criteria she seems to be using across the four cases, (b) the alternative criteria that would fit her record just as well, (c) what each of those criteria predicts about the welfare-only pet, and (d) the further case that would best separate whichever candidate criteria are still live after she answers.
