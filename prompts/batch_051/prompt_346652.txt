I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I would like your help refereeing a disagreement I witnessed after a recent seminar on Bayesian inference. The two participants are both graduate students in my program: one works on statistical learning theory, the other on philosophy of science. The debate concerned what, exactly, the standard Bayesian convergence and merging-of-opinions results show about the problem of prior choice. I have reconstructed their exchange below as faithfully as I can, with the moves numbered. Please adjudicate it: rate each numbered move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, identify who is right about which specific point, state what both parties are missing, and give an overall verdict on who is more right. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

[1] A: The convergence literature makes the problem of prior choice far less serious than it is usually taken to be. In an identifiable model with a reasonably regular likelihood, posterior concentration guarantees that the posterior will settle on the true parameter as data accumulate, for any prior that gives the truth positive mass in every neighborhood. And the merging-of-opinions results show that two agents whose predictive distributions over the infinite data sequence are mutually absolutely continuous will eventually issue nearly the same predictions. Prior arbitrariness is therefore at worst a short-run phenomenon; the principled problem is solved, and what remains is technical.

[2] B: That guarantee is internal to the prior. In the consistency theorem, the "almost surely" ranges over the parameter according to the agent's own prior: the result certifies concentration only for a set of parameters to which the prior already assigns probability one. If the truth has zero prior probability, the theorem says nothing about the agent's situation. The original worry was precisely that we have no external check on whether our prior puts positive mass on the truth. The theorem therefore reassures us on our prior's own authority, which is the circularity the skeptic pressed all along.

[3] A: That only shows the theorem is not an unconditional oracle, which no one claimed. The question that matters for prior choice is whether two reasonable agents can rationally persist in predictive disagreement forever. The merging results answer that they cannot: under mutual absolute continuity, sustained predictive disagreement is transient. The condition is not that you know the truth, but that you do not treat as impossible what your interlocutor treats as possible. That is a modest condition, and it is exactly the condition ordinary scientific debate satisfies. The real takeaway is that persistent disagreement, not arbitrary starting points, is what requires a special explanation.

[4] B: Two replies. First, mutual absolute continuity means agreeing on null sets, and agreeing on null sets is agreeing on what is possible — often the hardest thing to justify outside a settled parametric model. So the "modest condition" is doing much of the substantive work. Second, even granting the condition, the theorem is an existence claim about the infinite-data limit and supplies no finite-sample bound. Saying "you will agree eventually, but possibly after more data than any finite investigation will ever see" is compatible with priors diverging over every horizon we can actually reach. As a response to the practical charge of arbitrariness, that is cold comfort.

[5] A: The absence of a universal rate does not mean rates are absent where we need them. In the models we actually use — exponential families with conjugate priors, mixture models with clear identifiability — the prior's contribution enters explicitly and can be shown to be diluted at a rate one can calculate. The general theorem guarantees eventual agreement under weak conditions; the practical models supply the concrete rates.

[6] B: But those models are selected and parameterized on substantive grounds, and the reassuring rates hold only for models we already trust. The argument has shifted from "priors wash out" to "priors wash out quickly within a model class we have chosen." The arbitrary prior has not been eliminated; it has been relocated into the likelihood, the support, and the parameterization. What the theorem vindicates is only that a discipline which standardizes its models can achieve convergence inside them — which is a much smaller claim than the one the convergence literature is usually taken to establish.

Please work through [1] through [6] in sequence, rating each move and briefly justifying each rating, then deliver the overall verdict and your diagnosis of what the exchange as a whole misses.
