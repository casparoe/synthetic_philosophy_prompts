I am a software engineer rather than a philosopher, and I have been arguing with a colleague about a system we both know well. It is a decision-support model: it reads a situation report, forms a normative assessment (for example, that a proposed action would harm a client and should not be taken), and gives reasons for that assessment. By design, however, it has no actuators, no budget, no authority, and no channel that a human operator is bound to consult. If the operator overrides it and proceeds, the system can do nothing except re-state its judgment and its reasons. My colleague's view is that no matter how good the judgments are, the system is not a moral agent: moral agency requires being able to act, to intervene, to be answerable, or at least to have some control over what happens. I am less sure. We praise or blame it in ordinary talk (someone will say that the model told us not to and it was right), it gives reasons, and its output is about what ought to happen rather than only what will happen. I also wonder whether the same reasoning would deny moral agency to a human advisor whose recommendations could always be ignored but who otherwise understood, reasoned, and spoke normally.

My credence that the system is a moral agent in the sense relevant to responsibility, praise, and blame is around 0.5; my colleague is around 0.1. Give me your own number, but more importantly give the decomposition behind it: how much weight you put on what moral agency requires (judgment versus the capacity to act versus accountability), on whether the system's outputs are genuine normative judgments rather than text predictions, on whether its inability to act is a contingent engineering choice or essential to what it is, and on whether reactive attitudes toward it are warranted. Which of those sub-questions would move your final number most if you changed your mind on it, and what concrete evidence or design change would most plausibly move it?
