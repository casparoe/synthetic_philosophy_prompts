I have been reconstructing Descartes's case that nonhuman animals are mindless automata, and I find myself unable to locate the failure in an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. What makes it worse is that, if the argument works, it proves too much: it forces the same conclusion about human infants and about adults who have lost language after a stroke, cases Descartes himself would never have accepted. Since each premise appears to do indispensable work, I want to know which step should be rejected and what rejecting it costs.

Here is the argument.

(1) Thought in another body is wholly hidden. The only outward behavior that gives a certain sign of thought is the flexible use of language: arranging signs to express an indefinite range of contents, rather than emitting natural cries or mechanically triggered utterances.
(2) We may attribute thought to another body only when some outward behavior of that body supplies the certain sign described in (1).
(3) Nonhuman animals never supply this sign. Their most impressive communicative behavior is either an expression of natural passion or a parroted sound, and it is in principle reproducible by an arrangement of material parts with no mind behind it.
(4) Therefore we may not attribute thought to nonhuman animals.
(5) If we may not attribute thought to a being, then the only principled verdict is that the being does not think.
(6) Therefore nonhuman animals do not think; they are automata.

The difficulty is that a prelinguistic infant and an adult with total aphasia also fail the behavioral test in (3). Steps (1), (2), and (5) are general enough to yield the same verdict about them. Descartes cannot dismiss these cases simply by observing that they are human unless he adds a premise that membership in the human kind is by itself sufficient for thought. But that addition seems to make the behavioral test in (1) at best ancillary, since what settles the matter for humans would no longer be the behavior that was supposed to settle it for animals.

Which premise or inference should be rejected, and what does each candidate rejection cost? If (2) is rejected, what alternative ground remains for attributing thought to some bodies and not others, and is the distinction between minds and machines preserved? If (5) is rejected and only agnosticism about animal minds remains, how much of the automaton doctrine survives? If instead the argument is kept for animals while a kind-membership premise is added for human beings, is that exception principled or special pleading?
