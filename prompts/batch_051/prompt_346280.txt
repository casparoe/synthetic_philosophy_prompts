I run the localization QA team at a company that ships a consumer app into about twenty languages, and we keep relitigating the same fight. Some reviewers want to translate into whatever categories the target language naturally carves the world into; others want to preserve the source language's conceptual distinctions even when the English (or German, or whatever) reads a little stiff, on the grounds that flattening the distinction changes how users think about what the app is saying. The usual flashpoints: formal vs informal address, evidential markers (did the speaker witness it or hear about it?), languages with absolute spatial terms, and grammatical gender.

I want a reusable proceedure, not a decision on one ticket: a checklist my reviewers can run in fifteen minutes per string set before the Friday client sign-off call, teachable to people with no philosophy or linguistics background who mostly want to be told which choice is defensible. Ground it in the actual state of linguistic relativity research — the weak, thinking-for-speaking end of the Sapir–Whorf idea, not the strong version where language determines what you can think, which almost nobody in the field defends. Be honest about which steps are actual findings and which are judgment wearing a flowchart costume, and mark the points where the procedure should stop and kick the decision up to a human because it's a values call rather than a facts call.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a German UI string that addresses the user as du rather than Sie, going into English; (2) a sentence from a language with an absolute spatial frame (the Guugu Yimithirr kind of thing, north/south/east/west instead of left/right), where the source says something like "the button north of the photo" and the English wants "the button to the left of the photo"; and (3) a Turkish sentence whose past-tense marker indicates the speaker is reporting or inferring rather than having witnessed the event, going into English where the client style guide says just use plain past tense. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer the client is going to hate, and say whether that's a moment to override the procedure or a moment to show the client exactly where the values call is.
