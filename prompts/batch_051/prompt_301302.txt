In a seminar on documentary photography and its standing as evidence, a classmate defended the claim that photographs are transparent, in the sense that looking at a photograph of a shelled village is not merely looking at a picture of it but somehow seeing the village itself, the way a verbal dispatch about the same scene would not let one do. When I objected with a photograph whose political meaning had been reversed by cropping, she shrugged and said, "the camera never lies; people do." I suspect that proverb is doing more work than its folk-wisdom reputation suggests, but I cannot make the work explicit. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that "the camera never lies" might be used to express, from the trivially true through the substantive and defensible to the radical and false? For each reading, say whether it actually supports her conclusion that looking at a photograph is more like seeing the object itself than like hearing a report about it, and test it against the cropping case and against a carefully staged photograph. Say, finally, which reading she was most plausibly invoking and whether it survives those tests.
