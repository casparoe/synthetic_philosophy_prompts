Modern democratic practice displays a curious asymmetry. We treat election as the democratic mechanism par excellence, and proposals to select legislators by lot—even modest citizens' assemblies with binding power—are usually met as exotic or unserious. Yet we accept random selection from the general population for juries, where ordinary people gain power over a defendant's liberty and sometimes life. The same citizen chosen by lot can be trusted to help decide whether a person goes to prison, but not to help draft a general ordinance; the same principle we call democratic in one setting is treated as disqualifying in another. Is there a principle that actually generates this pattern, or is it institutional path dependence dressed up as theory?

The asymmetry looks even starker against a passage in Aristotle's *Politics*, Book IV, 1294b, where he reports the Greek democratic view:

> I mean, for example, that it is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic, and democratic for them not to have a property-qualification, oligarchic to have one.

There the normative polarity is nearly reversed: lot is democratic and election is oligarchic. That raises the question of whether the modern combination—sortition for juries, election for legislatures—is a defensible middle, or simply a historical compromise we have stopped noticing. Give me the strongest justification you can build for the modern asymmetry, then test it against the hard cases. If accountability or competence justifies election for lawmakers, why does it not also require professional jurors? If descriptive representation or civic equality justifies the jury pool, why does it not also justify a randomly selected legislative chamber? And if the principle cannot survive those cases, should we extend sortition upward, restrict it downward, or abandon the idea that there is a single democratic logic behind either practice?
