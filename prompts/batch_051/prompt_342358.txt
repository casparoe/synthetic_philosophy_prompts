i want to figure out whether the old epicurean argument that death isnt bad for the person who dies survives one specific change, and i dont just want the standard discussion. the argument as i understand it: for a thing to be bad for you, there has to be a you, at some time, for it to be bad for; while you exist your death hasnt happened yet, and once your death has happened there is no you left for it to be bad for; so death is never bad for the dead person, only for the living — fear in advance, grief after.

now suppose continuous backup exists. a device logs your full brain state every few minutes to external hardware; if your body is destroyed, a copy is switched on from the last snapshot. the copy has your memories, your plans, your relationships, your felt sense of being you, and everyone treats it as you minus the few minutes since the last backup. the original body and brain are gone for good, so from the original's side death is still total. but from the outside, the person — the plans, the relationships, the point of view — is back in circulation after a short gap at the cost of maybe five lost minutes.

does the epicurean argument still go through? i can see three verdicts: (a) it survives unchanged, because the original is annihilated and the copy is just a different person; (b) it collapses, because the argument was secretly leaning on death being permanent, and now there is a you on both sides of the gap for the loss to attach to; (c) it needs repair, so it becomes an argument about the lost minutes or the gap rather than about death itself. walk me through which premise the backup tech actually puts pressure on, whether that premise was load-bearing or just scaffolding, and whether the argument can be rebuilt to keep its force or gets demoted to a special case. keep the focus on what happens to the argument, and where you do have to take a stand on whether the copy is you, do it from inside the argument's structure rather than importing a whole metaphysical system.
