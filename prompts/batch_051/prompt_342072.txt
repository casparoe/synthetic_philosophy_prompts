At work I'm a software engineer on an evals team, and I've been asked to present to our safety reading group about chain-of-thought monitoring as a saftey technique. The thesis I want to defend is that CoT monitoring is not a stable control measure once a model is under real optimization pressure: the gradient will eventually find chains of thought that score as faithful and sensible to the monitor while the actual decision-relevant computation happens somewhere the monitor doesn't look. So a clean monitoring eval mostly tells you that the model is good at producing clean-looking summaries. My rough argument is: (1) a monitor only sees a low-bandwidth text channel, not the computation itself; (2) anything the monitor treats as a regular, rewardable signal becomes a target for gradient descent; (3) gradient descent searches a much weirder space of solutions than human auditors do, so the assumption that we would notice is doing a lot of work; and (4) therefore the eval-time correlation between the CoT and the model's true reasons is mostly uninformative about what happens after heavy post-training or in a long deployment.

The audience is our safety reading group, and they are not philosophers: it's about twelve people, made up of four interpretability researchers who basically think faithfulness can be trained for and verified and will be annoyed that I'm treating their probes and paraphrasing models as just another monitor, three RL engineers who think bad CoT is a data or reward-shaping problem and that talk of optimization pressure is hand-wavy, a security person who keeps asking whether the monitor itself can be attacked, and a compliance lead who mostly wants to know if we can point to a logged CoT in an incident review. They're smart and know the literature better than I do, and they'll push hardest on the places where I'm reasoning from engineering intuition rather than experimental results.

Please write me a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the thesis: the objections I'll actually get from this room, roughly ordered by how likely they are times how damaging they are, each phrased the way someone in that subgroup would actually put it, with what I should concede, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is softest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and flag any objection that's probably right enough that I should pre-empt it in the talk itself.
