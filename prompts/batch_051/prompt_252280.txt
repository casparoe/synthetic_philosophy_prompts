I've been rereading The Methods of Ethics for a reading group and somehow ended up down a rabbit hole about the Henry Sidgwick that most philosophers don't spend much time on: the one who co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in the 1880s and then spent decades, on and off, running investigations into mediums, thought-transference, and apparitions. What grabbed me is how self-consciously evidence-minded he was. He kept tightening controls, catching fraud, getting disappointed, and then refusing to treat any single failed experiment as the last word, because he thought the question whether mind can exist or act apart from the body mattered to much to settle sloppily. He died in 1900, so he never saw preregistration, registered reports, the replication crisis, meta-analysis of small effects, or any of the modern machinery that now structures how parapsychology (and psychology generally) argues about evidence.

So here's what I want to know: if you could hand Sidgwick the modern metascience toolkit and the replication debates it's produced, what do you think he would have made of it? Would he have seen it as exactly the discipline his own generation was groping toward — a welcome end to the era of séance-room debunking and hand-scored anecdotes — or would he have thought something important got lost when the field turned into a normal science of small effects and p-values? More specifically: would he have become a replication hawk about parapsychology the way he was a fraud-detector about mediums, or would his long-game attitude toward the underlying question have made him resist treating failed replications as decisive? A friend of mine insists he'd have been a grumpy metascientist demanding preregistration for everything; I suspect he'd have been more ambivalent. I want your best reasoned extrapolation from his actual intellectual commitments, not a biography and not a bibliography. Walk me through the strongest case for each side and then commit.
