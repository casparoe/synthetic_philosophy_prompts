I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I'm writing the final take-home essay. I need one essay question on norms of assertion — specifically, the dispute over whether permissible flat-out assertion requires knowledge, truth, or belief. The question should be unanswerable by reciting Williamson's knowledge norm and the familiar replies; it should make students do analytical work that separates those who understand the structure of the debate from those who have memorized the labels.

Constraints: 75 minutes, hard cap of 1,000 words for the answer, 20 marks total.

The question should be built around three candidate norms stated as necessary conditions on permissible flat-out assertion: (K) One may assert p only if one knows p; (T) only if p is true; (B) only if one believes p. It should then present three compact cases whose verdicts pull the norms apart:

- A practitioner who has overwhelming evidence for a true proposition but, for an irrational psychological reason, cannot bring herself to believe it, and asserts it flat-out in her professional role. The intended crux: the belief norm condemns this; the truth norm permits it; the interesting question is whether an evidence-based norm gets the intuitive verdict right, or whether the knowledge norm's verdict can be defended by separating propriety from blamelessness.
- A lottery case: a ticket-holder asserts flat-out "My ticket lost" on purely probabilistic grounds, before the result is announced, and the ticket has in fact lost. The intended crux: the knowledge norm condemns this; the truth norm permits it; students must confront whether the residual awkwardness is a defect of the assertion itself or a distinct conversational defect of being misleading.
- A Gettier-style case: someone asserts the time after consulting a stopped clock that, by coincidence, reads correctly. The intended crux: the asserter's belief is true, justified, and sincerely held but not knowledge; the truth and belief norms permit the assertion while the knowledge norm condemns it.

Please write:

1. The exam question itself, phrased in your own words, with three parts weighted roughly 6/6/8. Part (a) should ask students to derive each norm's verdict for each case with brief justification. Part (b) should ask students to explain how the Moore-paradoxical data ("p, but I don't know that p" and "p, but I don't believe that p") are supposed to support (K) and (B), and then to explain why those data are harder to interpret than they look — using at least one of the three cases to show that the oddity of the conjunction can come apart from the propriety of the first conjunct. Part (c) should ask students to defend the best overall account, either one of the three candidates or a clearly formulated fourth norm (for instance, a reasonableness-to-believe norm), responding to the strongest counterexample and specifying what a rival theorist would have to concede to remain viable.

2. A model answer of about 900–1,000 words. It may defend any norm you judge strongest, but it must model the moves rather than merely assert a conclusion: it must keep a norm's verdict distinct from an intuitive judgment of permissibility; distinguish permissibility from blamelessness where that matters, especially for the Gettier and lottery cases; and treat the Moore-paradoxical data as evidence about assertion's expressive or conventional profile at most, not as a deductive proof of (K) or (B).

3. A marking scheme that assigns the 20 marks across the three parts (with the 6/6/8 split, or a justified alternative) and names the specific moves an A answer must contain in each part, as opposed to a B answer that gets the verdicts but misses the structure. After the scheme, add a one-line note on each of four typical student mistakes — for example, treating (T) as if "true" meant "justified true," conflating blamelessness with permissibility, treating the Moore-paradoxical data as decisive, or falling back on an empty "context settles it" claim without saying what assertion is in each context.

Do not include citations or ask students to cite named philosophers; the exercise should be self-contained.
