I'm giving a department colloquium next month to a mixed crowd: half the room are value theorists (a couple of Kantian constructivists, a contractualist, one hard-nosed metaethicist who thinks applied ethics is mostly confused), there's a cluster of epistemologists who work on testimony, higher-order evidence, and disagreement, and then two or three computer scientists who actually run RLHF pipelines on big models and usually think philosophy talks are a waste of time. My talk is tentatively called "Alignment Without a Value Criterion," and the thesis is this: for prosaic systems — models roughly like the ones we have now, trained with supervised fine-tuning, RLHF, constitutional AI, etc., just scaled up — the bottleneck is not the absence of a formal specification of human values. The real bottleneck is keeping the training signal trustworthy once the model starts outperforming the humans who supply that signal. I've been calling this the delegation-first view: aligning a prosaic system is best analyzed as a problem in social epistemology, namely supervising an epistemic superior under distribution shift, and demanding a theory of the good before you can make progress is a category mistake rather than an engineering blocker.

In the talk I'm not going to spend much time on first principles. The argument in one line each: (a) RLHF and related methods are, structurally, ways of aggregating noisy human judgments into a reward signal; (b) the failure modes everyone worries about — reward hacking, sycophancy, specification gaming — are better diagnosed as failures of the delegation setup than as failures caused by missing normative concepts; and (c) what gets called the alignment problem for current systems is probaly better diagnosed as a scalability problem in human evaluation, so the practical upshot is that we should be investing in audits, evaluations, and training procedures rather than waiting for normative ethics to be solved.
