I cover science and ideas for a living, and lately I cant stop turning over an asymmetry in how people judge love and suffering — it keeps showing up in both the neuroscience-of-empathy stories I report and the religion ones. In ordinary life, if someone is constitutionally incapable of being pained by a loved one's suffering — not just too defended to feel it, but beyond feeling it — we treat that as a deficiency: their love seems shallow, maybe even fake. But a long tradition of theological reflection says God is impassible (incapable of suffering or being changed by creatures' anguish) and calls that a perfection rather than a defect. So the exact same trait — being unruffled by the beloved's pain — is a failing in a human and a glory in God. I want the strongest principle that could make that difference legitimate, not a list of historical positions but the best case you can actually build. Then stress-test it: a surgeon who stays calm mid-operation, a parent holding it together after a diagnosis, the idea that God becomes incarnate and dies. Does any principle really separate the cases, or should we say one side of the asymmetry is just wrong?
