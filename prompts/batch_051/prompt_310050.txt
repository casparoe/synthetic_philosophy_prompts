A friend and I recently argued late into the night about whether the collapse of the Weimar Republic should be counted as a major piece of evidence in the debate between presidential and parliamentary systems. My friend took the position that Weimar is close to a textbook case of the perils of presidentialism: a directly elected head of state, competing claims to democratic legitimacy between president and Reichstag, fixed terms, emergency powers, and a slow slide into rule by presidential decree that prepared the ground for Hitler. I pushed back that Weimar was not a pure presidential system, that its proportional representation and polarized party system may have mattered more than the presidency, and that the episode may teach us more about semi-presidential design or about the collapse of democratic parties than about the presidential-parliamentary distinction itself. Neither of us could settle it.

I want you to adjudicate that argument by doing a careful case analysis of the Weimar Republic from 1919 to 1933 through the lens of the presidentialism-versus-parliamentarism debate, in particular the perils-of-presidentialism argument. Set out the theory's mechanisms explicitly: dual democratic legitimacy, fixed terms, winner-take-all executive selection, and the relative rigidity of presidential systems as against the confidence vote and coalition flexibility of parliamentary systems. Then map those mechanisms onto Weimar's actual constitutional situation: the directly elected Reichspräsident, the chancellor's responsibility to the Reichstag, Article 48 emergency powers, and the presidential cabinets from 1930 onward. Identify who the relevant players were, what their constitutional options and incentives looked like, and what the theory would treat as the early warning signs. Where does the theory genuinely illuminate the breakdown, and where does it misdescribe or miss what happened — for example, the effects of proportional representation and party fragmentation, the Nazi electoral surge, the depression, and the conservative elites' decisions around Hindenburg? Finally, say what the episode should do to the theory: should Weimar be counted as evidence for the perils of presidentialism, evidence against it, or mainly evidence that the real object of study is semi-presidential design and party-system dynamics? Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing the case to fit it.
