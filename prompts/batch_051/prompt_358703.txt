I am a philosophy PhD student working in population ethics, and I keep finding that my verdicts in procreative cases outrun the principles I would have named before I started chasing them. I would like help extracting a principle—or a small set of candidates—from the following judgments, most of which I am fairly settled on.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. The contraindicated conception. Maya and Jonah know before conceiving that any child they conceive now would have a genetic condition that causes a short life of dense, unrelieved pain, with no periods of enjoyment and no capacity for higher goods. If they wait a month, a different, healthy child would be born instead. I think it would be gravely wrong to conceive now. The fact that the child would not have existed otherwise does not soften this much at all.

2. The forgone happy child. Rosa and Emil could easily have a child whose life would be, by any ordinary measure, flourishing and clearly worth living. They choose to remain childless. I think this is permissible, and I want to say nothing more than that: no one has been made worse off, and no unbestowed benefit remains that could ground even a weak duty.

3. The fixed-number choice. Deirdre has a transient medical condition. If she conceives now, her child will have a life that is clearly worth living but permanently and moderately difficult: manageable chronic pain, a somewhat shortened lifespan, and significant restrictions on activity. If she waits three months, the condition resolves and a different child would be born free of these burdens. She is resolved to have exactly one child either way. I think it would be wrong—or at least not fully justified—for her to conceive now merely to avoid waiting, even though the child she would have now is not made worse off than that same child would have been in any alternative. But I cannot say who, if anyone, is wronged.

4. The existing child. Abbas and Jun have a two-year-old whose condition makes her life just worth living. A cheap, safe intervention would raise her welfare from just-positive to very high. I think they are required to intervene, and this judgment feels much more secure than my judgment in case 3.

5. The asymmetry, stated directly. Let the zero level be the threshold below which a life is not worth living. Compare two possible acts: bringing into existence a child whose life lies just below the zero level, and failing to bring into existence a child whose life lies just above it. I judge the first to be seriously wrong and the second entirely permissible. I do not take the first reason merely to be stronger in degree; it engages something the second does not—the would-be child's suffering matters in a way the other would-be child's happiness does not.

6. The risky case. A couple may either conceive now or not at all. If they conceive now, there is a 90 percent chance of a child whose life is flourishing and a 10 percent chance of a child whose life is one of severe, lasting suffering; the expected value, on ordinary cardinal welfare, is comfortably positive. I find I cannot sign this case. I lean against conceiving, but I cannot tell whether that is the asymmetry doing legitimate normative work or mere risk-aversion I should discard when deciding how someone else's whole life will go.

I keep reaching for a package of three principles, roughly: decisive reasons not to bring into existence a life below zero, grounded in the would-be person's suffering; no corresponding reasons to bring into existence a life above zero, because possible benefits are not owed to anyone who does not yet exist; and, in a same-number choice in which someone will exist either way, a reason to choose the possible person with the higher welfare. But I cannot see what makes the third principle true that does not also underwrite a weak duty in case 2: if the difference between a possible child at plus-five and a possible child at plus-nine grounds a wronging or a should in a fixed-number choice, why does the difference between no child and a plus-nine child not ground even a weak reason to create? Yet I am unwilling to assert such a reason. And the first principle seems to compare existence with nonexistence in exactly the way the second principle is designed to avoid; I do not know how to state it without letting back in the symmetry I am trying to keep out.

Please do three things. First, state precisely the principle or principles that best capture the pattern of these judgments, separating the clauses that concern actual persons, merely possible persons, and fixed-number choices. Second, say which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to abandon, and why. Third, apply the resulting view to a case I cannot settle: Lena can have one child now or not. The child would have a life that is, on the whole, clearly worth living, but would live through a multi-year episode of severe depression as a young adult, during which they would sincerely regard their life as not worth living. Is conceiving, not conceiving, or neither required on your view? Does the fact that the suffering is embedded in a life I judge positive on the whole change the principle's answer, or does my inability to decide about Lena reveal a flaw in the principle's handling of the asymmetry?
