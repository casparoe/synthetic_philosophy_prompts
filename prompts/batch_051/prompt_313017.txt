My epistemology professor has spent the term ruling on cases of peer disagreement, and we are now trying to extrapolate her view. Her verdicts so far: (1) you and an equally good friend get different totals while splitting a restaurant bill, and she says you should suspend judgment; (2) you and a colleague with equal statistical training analyze the same data and reach different conclusions about whether a regression model is appropriate, with each of you denying the other's alleged error, and she says you should lower your confidence substantially but need not suspend; (3) you and a colleague with equally good spatial memory remember a route differently, and she says you should suspend; (4) you and another philosophy graduate student of equal ability and background disagree about the moral permissibility of abortion after a long discussion, and she says you may retain your original view; (5) you and a friend look at a perceptual illusion and disagree about whether two lines are equal, and she says you may retain your perceptual belief. Next week's case is this: you and an equally competent logician, both fully awake and informed, independently work through a long formal proof one of you has just constructed; you judge it valid, the other says it is invalid, and neither of you can identify any error in the other's reasoning. Predict her verdict on this new case, state the principle or principles that best reconcile her earlier rulings, say which of the earlier cases is the closest analog, and specify a further case that would distinguish the remaining candidate principles.
