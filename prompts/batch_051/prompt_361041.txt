I've been arguing with a freind about calendar reform, and I've realized the actual problem is that neither of us has a clear criterion for what makes a calendar epoch good, so we just swap examples. Give me a working definition of a well-chosen zero point for a calendar. It should explain why the Unix epoch (Jan 1 1970) is a perfectly good choice even though the date is arbitrary and culture-bound, and why a calendar starting at the Big Bang (~13.8 billion years ago) is a bad choice even though it's the least arbitrary event there is. Then make the definition work on these: the traditional AD epoch (supposed birth of Jesus, now thought a few years off, with no year zero between 1 BC and AD 1); the Hijri epoch (622); the Holocene calendar's epoch at 10,000 BCE; the French Revolutionary calendar starting Year 1 at the autumn equinox of 1792; and Japanese-style eras that reset to year 1 with each new emperor. Say which cases the definition handles cleanly, where it spits out a wierd answer or goes silent, and whether the right move is to revise the definition or defend the weird answer.

Structure your answer:

1. Definition (100-150 words): state your criterion or small set of criteria.
2. Easy cases (75-100 words): check it against the Unix epoch and a Big Bang epoch.
3. Hard cases (200-250 words): run the AD, Hijri, Holocene, French Revolutionary, and imperial-era cases through it, and flag the troubling ones.
4. Revision or verdict (100-150 words): either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict, and give the bottom line.
