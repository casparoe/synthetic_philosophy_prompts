I am working out the naming customs for a society in my current novel, in which family names are assigned by the household that adopts a child rather than by birth, and in which an adult's given name is formally replaced at a ceremony once the person's trade has been determined. This has sent me back to the theory of proper names, and I have been collecting objections to the causal-historical account, according to which a name refers in virtue of a chain of uses extending back to an initial act of assignment. I already have four standard objections. First, the underdetermination objection: an initial act of naming does not by itself settle whether the thing was named as a person, as a body, or as a piece of marble, so the chain transmits a referent without transmitting a sort. Second, the drift objection: a community can collectively mishear or mistake a name and entrench the mistake, so that later use converges on an object other than the one originally assigned; the chain then records the community's error rather than constraining it. Third, the empty-name objection: a name can be perfectly meaningful to competent speakers even when there was never a historical individual at the source of the chain, as with names introduced in fiction. Fourth, the cognitive-value objection: once the causal-historical account is paired, as it usually is, with a direct-reference semantics for names, it must explain why a true identity statement coupling two co-referring names can be informative in a way that the corresponding self-identity is not. I do not need any of these repeated or reheated. I want an objection to the causal-historical account that is not on that list and not a variation, special case, or combination of the four, preferably one that grows naturally out of the custom I described, in which both given names and family names can change their bearers or their roles across a life. Then tell me candidly how strong that objection is.
