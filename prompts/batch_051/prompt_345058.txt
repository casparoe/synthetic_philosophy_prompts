I'm a journalist covering the experimental-philosophy literature on intentional action, and I keep coming back to the asymmetry Joshua Knobe reported in his 2003 paper, "Intentional Action and Side Effects in Ordinary Language." In the paper's central vignette, as I understand it, participants read about a corporate chairman who, told that a new policy will harm the environment, says he does not care at all about harming the environment and only wants to increase profit. In the parallel version, the side effect is helping the environment, and the chairman says the same thing about helping. The famous finding is that most participants say the chairman intentionally harmed the environment, while most deny that he intentionally helped it.

I want to treat that asymmetry as a datum about the criteria ordinary people deploy when they decide whether a foreseen but unintended side effect was brought about intentionally. Here are four candidate criteria I have stitched together from the literature:

1. Foresight: the agent knew the side effect would occur.
2. Normative status: the side effect is something the agent ought to avoid bringing about, not merely neutral or positive.
3. Indifference: the agent's stated attitude is that the side effect matters not at all to them.
4. Counterfactual stability: the agent would have proceeded even if the side effect had been somewhat worse.

Now a new case. A hospital administrator approves a cost-cutting protocol under which certain patients are discharged earlier. She is told, and accepts, that the protocol will cause a measurable increase in readmissions among a small group of elderly patients. She regards the readmissions as genuinely regrettable and has allocated funds for follow-up care; she says she would not have approved the protocol if the predicted readmission increase had been twice as large, but she did approve it knowing the increase would be nontrivial. Is the increase in readmissions something she brought about intentionally, by these four criteria?

Go criterion by criterion. Where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, say so and explain why. Then tell me whether this case shows that one of the four criteria is not doing the work it was supposed to do, or whether the criteria themselves need to be revised. Finally, tell me what would change your verdict: which argument, additional piece of information about the administrator's state of mind, or alteration to the case would lead you to answer differently, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
