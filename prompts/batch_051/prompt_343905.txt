I recently spent an evening arguing with a colleague about whether a term in our field had changed its meaning over the last decade or had simply been applied to newly discovered cases, and the dispute pushed me back into the analytic-synthetic distinction. In Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) I found a passage I cannot get past:

"The general terms 'creature with a heart' and 'creature with a kidney,' e.g., are perhaps alike in extension but unlike in meaning."

Please construct a thought experiment to specifications. Give me a pair of ordinary general terms that are alike in extension but unlike in meaning, in the manner of Quine's example. Then embed one of those terms in a realistic episode of empirical discovery in which a competent speaker must revise claims involving that term, and arrange the case so that it becomes genuinely undecidable whether the term's extension has been corrected or its meaning has changed. Constraints: no stipulative redefinition, no authority issuing a linguistic ruling, no possible-worlds machinery, and no presupposition that meanings are entities of any particular kind. After the scenario, state each term's extension, state the difference in meaning, and walk through how Quine, using the passage above and its larger argument, would analyze the case, and how a defender of a principled analytic-synthetic boundary should respond. End by identifying the feature of the case that does the work, and say what happens to the undecidability if that feature is removed.
