I'm a novelist, and I keep chewing on a problem I have with the economists' picture of choice. They tell you: don't ask people what they want, look at what they do. Choice reveals preference, and utility is just whatever numerical ranking makes those choices come out on top. That's supposed to save the concept of utility from soggy introspection. Fine. But it's also a theory of character, and as someone who builds characters it feels wrong in a particular way — it can't see any difference between doing a thing and wanting it, and it seems to drain the word 'want' of all its meaning.

The Remains of the Day feels like the right test case. Stevens chooses duty, restraint, professionalism over Miss Kenton, over and over, in small daily refusals and in the big scene where she tells him she's going to marry Mr. Benn and he answers her like a butler instead of a person. Revealed preference forces a reading where he must have preferred keeping his role; whatever he felt about her was, by definition, ranked below the the dignity of the position. And there is something to that: nobody coerces him, he's consistent, and his whole identity is a chosen performance. The novel seems to insist on the opposite, though — that the tragedy lives in the gap between what he chooses and what he wants, or between what he values and what he desires. Ishiguro gives us a man whose choices are stable and whose interior life nevertheless shows them as not quite his own.

So heres my ask. Give me a reading of the novel through the revealed-preference debate over what utility means. Where does the novel vindicate the idea that choice just is preference — where do Stevens's choices reveal a real ranking? Where does the novel push back, and how does it get access to an inner 'true' preference that behavior alone would miss? Take the Miss Kenton announcement scene seriously: is he acting on a higher preference for dignity and loyalty, or is he failing to convert what he wants into what he chooses, and does that distinction even survive if all we have is behavior? Be honest in both directions — don't turn the novel into a chalkboard example, and don't pretend the novel refutes the economists just by having a sad butler. I'd like you to end by saying what Ishiguro, or fiction generally, can show about wanting and choosing that the preferences-as-choice model has to leave out, if anything.
