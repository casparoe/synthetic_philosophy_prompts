I am not an academic, but for the past few months I have been reading on my own about the Buddhist denial of a permanent self—the idea that what I call the "I" is not a thing standing behind my experience but a convenient name for a set of processes. The part I most want to get right, and the part I find people most often get wrong, is that this is not the claim that I do not exist at all. The tradition positions itself between two errors: a self that persists unchanged, and a self that is simply absent or annihilated. What remains is a person in the way a chariot is a chariot—real enough for ordinary purposes once the parts are assembled and arranged, but not discoverable among the parts as an extra ingredient.

I want a poem that captures this as a lived realization rather than a slogan. Please write a strict sestina (six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoi) whose six line-ending words are: form, feeling, perception, habit, awareness, self. The first five words are meant to stand for the five aggregates—material form or body, feeling-tone, perception, the patterns of habit and mental formation, and consciousness—and the sixth is what the tradition says is only a conventional designation for the other five operating together. The structure should do real work. In a sestina the same six words rotate through every position: no word is ever demoted to a supporting role and none is ever promoted to master term. I want the poem to let that pattern make the claim—that "self" is not the owner of the other five but another recurring word, and that the rotation itself, the changing arrangement of the aggregates, is what a person is. Let the poem use the first person freely; the point is to use the grammar of "I" against itself, not to pretend the word can be avoided. The envoi should gather all six words and land on the middle path: neither a changeless essence nor a flat denial of the person, but continuity without a carrier, something like a flame passed from one candle to the next. It should be accurate enough that someone who has actually studied these ideas would recognize the five aggregates and the rejection of both eternalism and annihilationism, but it should also stand on its own as a good poem for someone who is meeting the idea for the first time.
