In a philosophy of science course I'm taking, the instructor has been sorting examples all semester as a way of working toward a principle for separating science from non-science. Her record so far: she marks astrology, homeopathy, phrenology, and Freudian psychoanalysis as not science; she marks evolutionary biology, plate tectonics, quantum mechanics, and climate science as science; and when a student asked about string theory, she called it "science in principle but not yet in practice," which I took to be a major clue about her criterion.

Next session she has to rule on the modern search for extraterrestrial intelligence. As I understand that case, it builds serious instruments, tests specific hypotheses about radio and optical signals, and publishes null results as constraints on the search space; but its organizing claim—that a communicative civilization exists somewhere and is detectable—could survive an indefinitely long sequence of null results, because only a tiny fraction of the relevant parameter space has ever been searched. I am less interested in the bare prediction than in reconstructing the rule behind her record. The candidate principles I can see are: a Popper-style falsifiability rule, on which a field is scientific if its characteristic claims are framed so that observations could refute them and it actually treats failed tests as refutations; a predictive-success rule, on which a field earns scientific standing only after it has generated surprising novel predictions that were subsequently confirmed; and a Lakatos-style research-program rule, on which what matters is whether a field has a protected core plus an evolving belt of auxiliary hypotheses generating new testable implications, regardless of whether any have yet been confirmed. Which principle best fits her record, what verdict does each principle imply for the new case, and which further test case would most cleanly separate the competing principles?
