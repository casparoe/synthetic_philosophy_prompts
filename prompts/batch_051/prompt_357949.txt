I've been working out a distinction I call signal displacement, and I want an honest assessment of it. Take the handicap principle: a costly signal can be honest because the cost is easier for high-quality signalers to bear, so a peacock's tail is genuine evidence of condition precisely because a weak bird can't afford it. My worry is that many signals we treat this way are costly in the wrong place. I'll say a signal is displaced when the audience reads it as evidence for trait T, but the cost of producing it differentially burdens a different trait U. The signaling isn't a lie exactly — someone really does endure a cost — but the cost doesn't filter for the trait the signal is taken to prove.

Two examples. An unpaid internship in an expensive city is read as evidence of dedication to the field, maybe of talent. But the cost that does the filtering is the months of forgone wages plus rent; that burden falls hardest on people without savings or family support. So the internship screens for financial backing, not dedication — a highly motivated person who needs to earn money can't produce the signal, while a subsidized but indifferent one can. Or take luxury goods read as signals of taste or discernment: the cost is just money, so it filters for wealth, not for aesthetic judgment.

I think this explains why these signals stay persuasive: the waste is real, and real sacrifice feels like evidence about the trait in question, so no one notices that the evidence is about the wrong trait. My claim is that in status-heavy labor and consumer markets, most costly signals are displaced in this sense; the peacock's tail is the exception, not the rule.

I want to know whether this is a genuine and useful distinction or just a clumsy restatement of something already worked out — handicaps versus indices, conspicuous consumption, Spence-style screening, or something else. Reconstruct it in its strongest form, name the nearest existing ideas and say exactly where my version overlaps and where it departs, and then tell me where it breaks. For instance, is the alignment criterion even well-defined when traits like wealth and taste or parental support and diligence are correlated? And what happens when the signal's actual message is "I can spend" and the audience knows that — is that displaced or aligned? Give me the strongest counterexamples to my claim that the peacock tail is the exception, and tell me whether any objection is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
