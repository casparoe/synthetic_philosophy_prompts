Define "complex" as a property of an individual object or structure — not the complexity of a computation — so that it gets the right results on these seven cases: a sand dune, a large quartz crystal, a mechanical watch, an E. coli bacterium, a 10,000-bit string generated by fair coin tosses, a shuffled deck of cards, and a snowflake. I take the watch and the bacterium as clear cases of complexity; the dune, the crystal, and the random string as clear cases of non-complexity; and I honestly do not know what to say about the snowflake or the shuffled deck. Two definitions I am considering should come apart: (a) complexity is how hard an object is to describe exactly, measured by the length of the shortest description from which it can be reconstructed; (b) complexity is how hard an object is to build, measured by the number of steps in the shortest recipe from a small stock of elementary parts, where at each step you may join or copy parts you have already made. A random string is hard to describe but trivially easy to generate, and a crystal is easy by both measures yet people do not ordinarily call it complex, so both candidates need work; in particular, say whether functional organization is part of the definition or merely correlates with it. Propose a definition, or two if you think the word does two jobs, run it through all seven cases, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer and whether you would revise it or defend the verdict, and identify the case you are least confident about and what would move you.
