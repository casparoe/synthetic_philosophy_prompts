Write a 600–800-word parody of a page from Johannes Kepler's annual astrological almanac, set in Prague around 1605 during his service as Imperial Mathematician, formatted as the public prognostication with smaller-print "astronomical clarifications" appended. In the clarification layer his reformist conscience shows through: he quietly deletes the zodiac's arbitrary pictured signs, replaces them with the geometrical machinery he actually believed in — angular aspects, harmonies, sublunary weather effects — concedes that most judicial astrology is worthless while remaining unable to refuse the patron's fee, and half-papers over a recent failed prediction. The humour should come from the real split in Kepler's position: astrology as the foolish daughter who feeds her starving mother astronomy; genuine conviction about aspects alongside contempt for the signs; and financial dependence on the very practice he was trying to reform. Keep the register early modern — no "falsifiable," "Bayesian," or "correlation" — and make the irony legible to a newcomer while remaining fair to an expert. The target is court astrology and Kepler's divided accommodation with it, not Kepler himself.
