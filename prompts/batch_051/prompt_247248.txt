A philosophy blog for intelligent general readers is preparing a piece on the Stoic dichotomy of control and its modern revival. They want a decision tree that sorts readers into the position they actually hold about the dichotomy, on the strength of their answers to substantive claims rather than which label they prefer.

Take the following as the working material. At the start of the Enchiridion, Epictetus distinguishes what is up to us from what is not: roughly, our judgements, desires, aversions and impulses are up to us, while body, property, reputation and office are not. Modern popular Stoicism has turned this into the advice to sort one's concerns into what one can control and what one cannot, and to focus on the former. These formulations are not equivalent. The differences can be organised around three questions:

Q1, the criterion of "up to us". (a) Only the operations of the choosing mind — forming judgements, assenting to impressions, setting desires and aversions — are up to us; everything else is not. (b) Whatever we can causally influence, even partially, is up to us to that degree. (c) What is up to us is not a matter of causal control at all, but of what we can properly be asked to answer for.

Q2, the structure of the distinction. (a) Two categories suffice. (b) A middle category of partial control is needed.

Q3, the point of the distinction. (a) It marks the boundary of the good, so that happiness and virtue depend only on what is entirely up to us. (b) It is a practical technique for reducing distress and improving effectiveness. (c) It fixes the boundaries of responsibility, praise and blame.

Within the branch that answers Q1 with (a), one further question separates two readings. Q4: do externals that are not up to us nonetheless have selective value — health, for example, is naturally preferred even though it is not good in the strict sense — or are they simply not worth pursuing?

Produce a decision tree with five to seven leaves. The following positions must appear as leaves: the orthodox Epictetan position; the modern two-box self-help position; the trichotomy-of-control position (three categories, with goals internalised for the partial-control category); the quietist reading; and the answerability reading (which may split into binary and graded variants, in which case it may occupy two leaves). Begin by laying out the full space of answer combinations (Q1 × Q2 × Q3, with Q4 branching where Q1 is answered with (a)), locate the named positions within that space, and briefly diagnose every combination that no leaf occupies, saying whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If any further occupied combination strikes you as a distinct and defensible position, add it as a leaf rather than leaving it undiagnosed. If two of the required leaves cannot be separated by the questions as stated, say so and revise the questions rather than papering over the difference.

Every question must be answerable by a reader who does not know the labels, and no question may simply ask which position the reader accepts. Each leaf should carry the position's name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Write in British English.
