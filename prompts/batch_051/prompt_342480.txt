I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I keep noticing an exploit I don't have a word for. A lot of communities have a reputational norm against saying false things: if your sentences are false you lose standing, if they're true you keep it. People get very good at routing around this norm without ever breaching it—every sentence is technically true, sometimes checked clause by clause like a lawyer, but the arrangement, emphasis, and silences are what do the deceiving. When someone objects, the defense isnt "I didn't mislead you," it's "I never said anything false," and what bothers me is that the defense mostly works: the outright liar gets burned, while the person who produces the same false belief with scrupulously true sentences comes out looking more honest than average. And people optimize for it. They build a whole style around sentences that are each defensible in isolation but jointly leave the listener with a picture the speaker knows is wrong.

Two instances. A team told not to put false statements in user docs wrote things like "this feature is available to customers whose plans include premium support"—true, but deliberately silent about the plan being discontinued—and treated "find one false sentence" as the whole audit. In online arguments, someone asked "did you mean X" quotes their earlier sentence back, strictly true and with the unhelpful bit bolded, and walks off as if the false impression they'd allowed to form wasnt their doing.

It isnt exactly lying by omission, because the omissions are active and picked for effect, not gaps. But "misleading" is too blunt, because that covers accidental cases and one-off spin. I mean the systematic, reputation-managing, asymmetry-exploiting version. Is there an established name for this exact pattern, the way motte-and-bailey names an adjacent exploit? And if the nearest concepts only half fit, I want the carving more than the label: where the edges are, where it stops being ordinary careful phrasing and starts being this, how much of the work is done by the reputational asymmetry between false sentences and false impressions, and whether the underlying truth-norm is just ambiguous between "don't produce false sentences" and "don't produce false beliefs."
