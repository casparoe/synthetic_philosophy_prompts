OK here's an argument I made against a friend's choices, and I want to know if it actually works or if there's a sneaky step I'm not seeing.

He was offered Choice 1: A = a sure $1 million, or B = 89% chance of $1 million, 10% chance of $5 million, 1% chance of $0. He took A.

Then Choice 2: C = 11% chance of $1 million, 89% chance of $0, or D = 10% chance of $5 million, 90% chance of $0. He took D.

I told him you cant coherently do both. Here's my reasoning:

(1) In Choice 1, both options have an 89% chance of $1m. They differ only in the leftover 11% branch: A gives $1m for sure in that branch, B gives a 10/11 shot at $5m and a 1/11 shot at $0.

(2) In Choice 2, both options have an 89% chance of $0. They differ in the same leftover 11% branch: C gives $1m for sure, D gives a 10/11 shot at $5m and a 1/11 shot at $0.

(3) So the two choices are the same gamble over the 11% branch, just with the common 89% background changed from $1m to $0.

(4) The independence axiom (or sure-thing principle) says that when two options share a common component, your preference should be driven only by the components where they differ; changing the common background shouldnt flip your preference.

(5) Since he preferred the sure-$1m branch over the risky-$5m branch in Choice 1, he should prefer it in Choice 2 too.

(6) So he should take C over D. But he took D. Contradiction: either he made a mistake in one choice, or the independence axiom has to go.

I think the argument is valid and he's just chickening out of rejecting independence. But maybe steps (1)-(2) are sneakier than I think, because I redescribed A as having an 11% branch when A is literally just a sure thing with no branch. Is that redescribtion legitimate? And if he rejects step (4) instead, is his position actually coherent, or is that just renaming the mistake? Tell me exactly which step, if any, fails, and what the defender of his two choices has to give up.
