I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about statistical evidence in law, and I've ended up with a set of verdicts that feel as if they obey some principle I can't state. A bus hits a pedestrian and the only thing tying it to the Blue Bus Company is that its buses make up 80 of the 100 buses in the town. I'd throw the case out, even though the civil standard is only balance of probabilities. Now add one fact: a witness with a brief glimpse identifys the bus as belonging to Blue Bus; I'd find for the plaintiff even though I secretly think the witness would only be right about seven times in ten. At a rodeo, a thousand people get in but only 499 paid; a randomly chosen attendee is sued for the ticket, and I wouldn't make them pay even though it's 50.1 percent likely they didn't. In a prison yard, 99 of 100 prisoners beat a guard to death and one just watched; I wouldn't convict any particular prisoner, even though the criminal standard is higher and 99 percent looks far above it. Yet a clean DNA match from a crime scene, with a random-match probability of one in four million and no other link to the suspect, and I'm inclined to convict.

The rule I keep reaching for is that a verdict has to rest on evidence that's tied to what this particular person did, not merely on a frequency over a class they belong to. The witness feels like the first kind; the bus statistics and the rodeo feel like the second. But the DNA match is also, at bottom, a frequency, so I don't know whether that rule survives my own verdict. Is there one coherent principle behind these judgements, or at least the best compromise? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it keeps and which it forces me to drop, and then apply it to the case I keep flip-flopping on: the bus case again, but with 99 out of 100 buses blue. If I let that one win, where is the line, and can the line be drawn in a principled place rather than just by feel?
