I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about pragmatist theories of truth. I keep running into a contrast that I suspect is deeper than the usual Peirce-the-logician versus James-the-popularizer narrative. In Peirce's 1878 essay How to Make Our Ideas Clear, he argues in substance that truth is the belief that all sufficiently sustained inquiry is fated to converge on, and that the real object is just whatever such a converged belief would represent. But in his 1907 book Pragmatism, James has a passage where he says truth is not a stagnant property an idea has; it happens to an idea, it becomes true through the process of verification, and its truth consists in the practical consequences of believing it. I'd like you to compare these two pictures in a way I can actually draw on for the piece. For each view, what does it imply about a claim that is currently unverifiable but widely accepted, and about a claim that pays its way for some community but would be rejected by future inquiry? Push past the easy reading that James is just about individual usefulness and Peirce is just about community consensus, and tell me which of the two gives a better account of why we are entitled to call some present beliefs true even though inquiry could in principle end up somewhere else.
