So I've ended up on a governance team at an insurance company — not my background, I came from the data side — and our model flags claims for human review, writes a little memo saying why it thinks a claim is suspicious, and then that's it. A human adjuster makes the actual decision. The other week a compliance person kept calling the model a 'moral agent' and saying we should think about 'who's deciding,' and it's been gnawing at me since. I never used to think about this stuff — I'd have shrugged of the whole thing a year ago — but now I have to sit in those meetings. I went down a rabbithole and read an article that sums the position up like this:

'Most philosophers think a moral agent has to be able to act on its judgments, and an AI that only produces recommendations can't do anything in the world. Since it can't act, it can't be a moral agent, and therefore it's just a tool, like a spreadsheet. A tool can't be praised or blamed for anything, so the AI's judgments have no more moral status than a calculator's output. And anyway, the AI doesn't have intentions or desires, so there's nothing there to be an agent in the first place — the whole question is settled as soon as you notice that. The real worry is only about the humans who decide to follow its recommendations.'

Something about this feels too quick. For one thing, the model does shape outcomes in a real way — its memos change which claims get denied, and nobody reads a calculator's output like that. But I don't have the vocabulary to say where the summary goes wrong. Could you go through it and tell me which parts are accurate, which are conflations or overstatements, and whether the final conclusion actually follows? In particular I'd like to know if 'not a moral agent' really means 'has no moral status,' and whether a thing that judges but can't act might still be something other than a tool.
