Yesterday evening a friend and I spent several hours arguing about Bayesian convergence, and by the end he had assembled a chain of reasoning that seems to show Bayesianism quietly undermines itself. I have not been able to find the step that gives way, and I would like a precise diagnosis rather than a summary of the standard results.

His argument, as he laid it out, ran as follows. (1) The standard convergence results in Bayesian statistics say that, under conditions usually described as mild, two agents who assign positive probability to the same set of hypotheses and then observe the same data will have posterior distributions that merge: their probability assignments grow arbitrarily close as the evidence accumulates. (2) In that limit, the posterior is fixed by the data and the model, with nothing left over from the original prior beyond the support it assigned at the outset. (3) So wherever the conditions hold, the prior is eventually washed out entirely; any two admissible priors would have produced the same limiting opinion. (4) Whether a prior has already washed out is only a matter of how much data one happens to have seen so far, and the rationality of a belief should not depend on that. (5) Therefore two rational agents with different priors who have inspected the same data are not, in any deep sense, disagreeing; their disagreement is rationally idle because it would vanish in the limit. (6) But this is hard to accept: informed and competent people persistently disagree about difficult empirical questions, and we routinely evaluate priors as better or worse, for example when we criticize a prior so dogmatic that no finite body of data could ever move it. (7) So the convergence results seem to force a dilemma: either persistent rational disagreement is impossible, or all priors are equally rational, and either horn abandons something central to how Bayesian inference is actually practiced and criticized.

He concluded that once you take the washing-out of priors seriously, Bayesianism can promise that differences will disappear eventually but can no longer say why one prior is better than another now. I would most like to keep the convergence results and the idea that priors are open to rational criticism, and I am prepared to give up whichever connecting step is weakest, but I want to know the price. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
