You are a moral philosopher who works on consequentialism and its critics. I am compiling a list of demandingness objections to consequentialism for a graduate seminar, and I already have the following five, each stated in compressed form:

1. Integrity: consequentialism requires agents to abandon their ground projects and deepest commitments whenever the impersonal calculus says so, thereby undermining the agent's integrity.
2. Negative responsibility: in holding agents responsible for all the consequences they allow or fail to prevent, consequentialism generates obligations with no principled limit.
3. Partiality: consequentialism's impartial maximization leaves no room for the special weight we properly give to our children, friends, partners, and communities.
4. Ought-implies-can: consequentialism demands more than ordinary agents can psychologically sustain, and a moral theory must not require what agents cannot do.
5. Supererogation: consequentialism cannot recognize acts as supererogatory, because whatever produces the best consequences becomes obligatory rather than beyond duty.

These cover the familiar territory, and I do not need variants, illustrations, or combinations of them. I want one more demandingness objection that is not on this list: it should rest on a different underlying diagnosis of what makes consequentialism's demands objectionable. Think carefully before answering—first brainstorm several candidate ideas, test each against the list, and discard anything that is just a restatement, special case, or merger of the listed items. Then present the strongest surviving candidate, explain precisely what makes it distinct from the five listed objections, and give your honest assessment of whether it is independently forceful or merely novel.
