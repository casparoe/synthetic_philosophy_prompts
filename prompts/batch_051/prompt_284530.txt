I'm a high school student taking a bioethics elective, and I'm planning my final paper around the film Gattaca and the idea of liberal eugenics. We read about Julian Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence, which says, roughly, that parents using reproductive technologies should select the child, from among the possible children they could have, who can be expected to have the best life, on the basis of available information, including genetic information and including traits that go beyond avoiding disease. The principle is supposed to be liberal rather than old-fashioned eugenic: the choice stays with individual parents, is aimed at the child's own welfare, and does not license state coercion or discrimination. But in class Gattaca keeps getting described as the obvious dystopian counterexample: a society full of genetic selection and engineering, divided into the genetically engineered valids and the naturally conceived in-valids, where employers secretly test people's DNA and sort them into jobs accordingly. I cannot shake the feeling that the film's dystopia runs on exactly the things Savulescu's principle does not include: compulsory testing, genetic discrimination, and the assumption that a person's genotype is their destiny and their worth. So my question is whether Gattaca is really an objection to procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics, or whether it is mostly an objection to a different, coercive thing that shares some of the same imagery.

For my paper, I would like a careful reading that is fair in both directions. Which specific scenes or character choices in Gattaca genuinely dramatize problems that would remain even if selection were purely a private, parental, welfare-oriented choice, for instance the worry that a selected child's life has been planned for him without his consent, or that choosing for certain traits expresses the view that other possible lives are worth less? And where does the film's dystopia instead depend on assumptions Savulescu could reject, so that the film is not actually landing a blow on his view? Does the film seem aware of the difference, or does it blur the two on purpose? I am also wondering, and would like your judgment on, whether the more serious challenge is cumulative: that even if no individual parental choice is objectionable, millions of such choices in a market could still produce something like Gattaca's caste structure without any state eugenics program, and whether that would count as a problem with the principle itself or only with the society around it. I do not want a polemic either way; I want to know where the philosophical reading illuminates the film, where the film complicates the philosophy, and where an honest critic has to admit the film resists the reading. Please finish with a one-sentence summary of your overall assessment, formatted as \answer{...}.
