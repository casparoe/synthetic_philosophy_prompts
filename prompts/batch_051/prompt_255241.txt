For months, the one recurring argument in the rationalist forum I read that never converges is whether modesty — intellectual, moral, and social — is a virtue or a disguised failure mode. One camp treats modesty as the epistemic core of rationality: confident contrarian takes are usually wrong, peers and experts are genuine evidence, and updating toward the median respectable view is what accuracy requires. The other camp answers that modesty is self-undermining — a norm of deferring to disagreement would have suppressed almost every non-consensus insight — and that in practice it functions as status-signaling and conformity rather than truth-seeking. Neither side is defending arrogance, and both care about accuracy rather than social harmony. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are largely talking past one another because they have not separated intellectual modesty as a policy for belief revision, social modesty as a policy for how one presents and holds a view, and moral modesty as a policy for how much credit or standing one may claim; one camp's arguments are mostly about belief revision, the other's mostly about presentation, and treating these as a single virtue keeps the argument stuck. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is — a hidden empirical question about base rates, a difference about what counts as a virtue, a verbal dispute about the word modesty, or something else — and an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress: a sharper distinction, an empirical estimate, a change of question, or an argument that none should be expected.
