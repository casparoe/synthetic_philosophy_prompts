I help run the philosophy club at my high school, and for the past few weeks we have been stuck on a disagreement about Kurt Gödel's 1949 essay A Remark about the Relationship Between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy. We all agree on the basic content: Gödel argues that the relativistic picture of spacetime, especially once he introduces his rotating-universe solutions to Einstein's field equations, undermines the idea of an objective lapse of time, and he takes this to support a broadly idealist conclusion about the unreality of change. In one passage he reasons that because there is no single series of now-moments that all observers can share, the notion of an objective passage of time cannot be maintained. But the club has split into two camps. One side reads him as making a metaphysical claim: time as it is ordinarily understood does not exist, because there is no objective now, and in solutions like Gödel's there are closed timelike curves. The other side says this is a philosophical overreading of the physics: relativity only shows that simultaneity is frame-relative, which is an epistemological or representational point, not a discovery that change itself is unreal. Some of them also point out that Gödel was a mathematical Platonist, so reading him as a full-blown idealist about time may be too crude.

We have been going in circles for a month, and I suspect the actual standstill is not about the physics but about what a physical theory is allowed to settle. One side treats a globally consistent description of spacetime as enough to dissolve objective becoming, while the other demands that the theory say something about temporal experience before any metaphysical conclusion follows. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is—whether it is verbal, a difference about the status of scientific models, a clash of bedrock intuitions about time, or something else—and what kind of argument, distinction, or shift in question would actually move it.
