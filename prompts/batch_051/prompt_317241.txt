I am a novelist, and I am writing a historical novel set in a twelfth-century abbey. One of my central characters is an abbess who acts as the community's moral arbiter in its confession scenes, and I have been trying to make her rulings embody a recognizably Abelardian ethics — the position, roughly, that sin is located in consent rather than in the desire, the external act, or what happens afterward. I have kept a file of the rulings she has already given in earlier draft chapters so that I do not make her contradict herself when I write a new scene. The rulings are as follows:

1. Brother Madoc confesses that he is tormented by recurring lustful fantasies about a widow; he hates the thoughts and has never formed any plan to act on them. She rules that he is not in a state of sin, only of temptation.
2. Brother Piran confesses that, in a rage, he resolved to kill the cook, sharpened a knife, and was walking toward the kitchen when the bell for prayer interrupted him; he then abandoned the plan and disposed of the knife. She rules that he sinned gravely.
3. Sister Agnes, following the infirmarian's instruction, gave a dying guest water; the guest choked and died, and Sister Agnes is distraught. She rules that Sister Agnes did not sin.
4. Brother Jowan was forced by robbers, on pain of death, to set fire to a barn; he did it weeping and unwilling. She rules that he did not sin.
5. Sister Iseult the cellarer, knowing it to be theft, takes coins from the abbey's alms for her starving family; she takes no pleasure in it and wants nothing for herself, but she deliberately does the deed. She rules that Sister Iseult sinned.

Now I am drafting the scene that worries me. Brother Odo confesses that he spent a sleepless night deciding to poison a guest he hates: he formed the intention, settled on the means, and expected to carry it out, but by morning he was ashamed, gave the plan up without doing or saying anything to the guest, and no one else ever knew. I want the abbess's ruling in this new scene to be the one most consistent with her earlier rulings, read as a track record rather than as a set of abstractions. Please give me: the ruling she ought to give; the precedent that is the closest analog and how far it reaches; the candidate principles that fit the earlier rulings and what each would predict about Brother Odo; exactly which principle she would be adopting if she condemns him versus if she does not; and a further case, beyond Odo's, that would cleanly distinguish those principles if Odo's does not settle matters. Finally, please tell me how confident you are in your answer overall and single out the point you are least sure of.
