For a long-read I am writing on why the law treats blackmail as a distinct offense, I have been trying to work through a reform proposal rather than simply rehearse the familiar paradox. The paradox, as usually stated, is that two things that are generally lawful on their own—asking for money, and revealing true information that one is otherwise free to reveal—can become a serious crime when the request is made under threat of disclosure. One possible response is that the problem lies not with the underlying transaction but with the secrecy and the extortionate structure surrounding it. On that view, one might try to legalize and heavily regulate the transaction instead of banning it outright.

The mechanism I want stress-tested is this. A person who possesses true, non-privileged information that could damage another person's reputation may lawfully demand money in exchange for silence, but only through a registered "priced silence" procedure. The deal must be in money, must be reported in advance to a public registry, and the seller of silence must pay a steep tax—say, 60 percent—on the payment. The registry records the parties' names, the amount, and a coarse category of the information (commercial, personal, political, and so on), but not the underlying facts. Payments above a threshold trigger an audit. Buyers receive a certificate of silence that gives them standing to sue for damages if the seller later discloses anyway.

Walk through how this would actually behave. Start with the strategic situation facing the would-be seller, the target, and third parties who might value the information; then trace who wins and who loses, how the scheme gets gamed, and what information ends up revealed, buried, or sold off the books. Pay particular attention to whether the mere existence of a registry entry functions as a public signal that something damaging exists, whether a fixed tax makes threats more or less credible, whether a target who pays once becomes a more attractive target for re-extortion, and how confederates, intermediaries, or disclosures of "metadata" could be used to circumvent the rules. Conclude by identifying where the scheme would most plausibly fail first, and whether that failure could be repaired without collapsing back into the original prohibition.
