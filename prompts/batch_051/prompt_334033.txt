A study group argument has been going in circles and I would like a diagnosis. It seems to show that linguists are wrong to say grammars contain rules, which I find hard to accept.

(1) A rule of grammar specifies how a given kind of form must be built, so a genuine rule has to be exceptionless; a regularity with exceptions is at most a statistical tendency, not a rule.

(2) Every proposed rule of English morphology has exceptions. The past tense is formed with -ed, but go–went and sing–sang do not take it; the plural is formed with -s, but we get mice, oxen, and sheep; the comparative is formed with -er, but bad–worse is suppletive; person/number agreement is formed by adding endings, but am–is–are is suppletive rather than built up. Even in sound change, apparently exceptionless shifts are found to have conditioned exceptions, which are then made regular by a second statement, whose exceptions are in turn handled by a third.

(3) These are not marginal failures: the irregular forms are disproportionately the most frequent words in the language, so the exceptions land exactly where a rule should be most robust.

(4) The standard reply is that irregular forms are individually listed in the lexicon and block the regular rule, which applies to everything not overridden. But without independent constraints on what may be listed as a blocker, this just redescribes the rule failing here as the rule being blocked here; any arbitrary collection of forms, however messy, can be simulated by a rule plus a large enough list of blockers. So the reply does not yet explain what it is for the rule to govern the forms.

(5) Therefore no rule of English grammar is exceptionless, and by (1) there are no rules of English grammar, only stored forms and analogy.

(6) But this conclusion cannot be right: speakers produce and judge novel forms they have never heard, children regularize irregulars, and some subsystems are fully regular.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would rather keep (6) than abandon the idea of grammatical rules, but I want to know precisely which step to reject and what that rejection costs for how we think about rules elsewhere — in historical phonology, in law, or in games.
