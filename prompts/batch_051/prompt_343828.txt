In discussions of the proposal to align AI systems through debate, its defenders often reach for a courtroom analogy: two expert advocates argue opposite sides of a question before a judge who lacks the expertise to answer it directly, and the assumption is that adversarial argument lets the less informed party identify the truth anyway. I would like a careful audit of how far this analogy actually carries.

Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what maps to what between a trial and an AI debate episode—the advocates, the judge, the jury if any, the evidence, cross-examination, rules of procedure, standards of proof, verdict, and appeal. Then separate the disanalogies that are merely ornamental from those that affect the conclusion the analogy is being used to support, namely that a human judge who is far less capable than either AI debater can still reliably tell which side is right solely from the transcript of their exchange.

In particular, press on the following, and add any further pressure points you think matter. First, a real courtroom tracks truth only because of a surrounding apparatus that the analogy does not mention: rules of evidence, discovery, penalties for perjury, standards for admissible expertise, professional obligations to the tribunal, and the fact that the parties are not the only source of information. Which of these, if any, have genuine counterparts when the two debaters are AI systems and the judge is a human reading or listening to their outputs? Where a counterpart is absent, say whether the debate proposal supplies some other mechanism that plays the same role, or whether the analogy has quietly imported it. Second, in the AI case both debaters are, in effect, optimizing for the same proximal target: producing outputs that move the judge's belief in their preferred direction. A truthful debater is not truthful except insofar as truth-telling happens to persuade. Explain how, if at all, this changes the courtroom mapping, and what assumptions about the truth-teller's persuasiveness the optimistic conclusion must fall back on. Third, a court can compel the production of evidence a party would rather suppress, while debaters can omit, reframe, equivocate, or exploit predictable patterns in the judge's reasoning with claims the judge cannot check and other participants may not be able to rebut cheaply. How damaging is this disanalogy, and under what conditions, if any, does cross-examination in debate compensate for it?

End with a verdict: once the important disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that debate provides a route to scalable oversight survive, and in what weakened or conditional form? If the courtroom analogy is doing more work than the proposal's formal machinery warrants, say so explicitly; if some other analogy—such as a zero-sum persuasion game, an adversarial contest, or a teaching dialogue—fits better, state what it captures and where it too breaks down.
