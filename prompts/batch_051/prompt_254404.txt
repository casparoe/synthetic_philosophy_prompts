I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar (about 14 students; one 80-minute session). Please design a structured classroom activity that makes students feel the problem of the criterion before it is named. The goal is for the group to attempt, under explicit rules, to justify a general method for distinguishing trustworthy from untrustworthy sources of belief, and to discover for themselves that every proposed criterion either draws on one of the very sources it is supposed to certify or must be accepted arbitrarily. The activity should require no prior reading in skepticism. Include roles and rules, materials (cards for candidate sources such as perception, memory, deduction, induction, testimony, and introspection), a timeline, the facilitator's scripted instructions, a debrief, and an honest note on which part of the skeptical force — for example, the felt possibility that one's whole point of view is deceived — a procedural game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto: the demand for a criterion of truth that is not itself in need of certification, the Cartesian worry that the faculty used to test our faculties might itself be defective, and the Humean point that argument appears to run out and something like natural habit or unreasoned trust has to take over. It should also distinguish what the activity actually demonstrates — the difficulty of giving a non-circular validation of our basic sources — from what is genuinely contested among epistemologists, namely which response to that difficulty is correct, and it should not present any one of the standard replies as the consensus. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
