I'm trying to get clear about what makes a community a community in the thick sense — the kind whose dissolution is a genuine loss to its members, rather than just a group of people who share a hobby or a postcode until it becomes inconvenient. In a seminar we've been working with five criteria: (1) regular direct interaction among members; (2) mutual recognition, where people know who belongs and treat insiders and outsiders differently; (3) shared practices or goods that can't be enjoyed alone; (4) a common history or narrative members can tell about themselves; and (5) membership that is at least partly unchosen, so it shapes people's practical identity rather than just being a contract one could opt out of without loss. The tutor has been pressing us on whether the fifth criterion is doing too much work, and I still can't decide.

Consider this case. In the 1960s a whole valley village was compulsorily purchased and razed to make way for a reservoir, and the villagers were rehoused in a dozen different towns. The village no longer exists as a place. For fifty years the surviving villagers, and some of their children, have kept a roll of everyone cleared from the village and their descendants. They send each other news of births, marriages and deaths, and they contribute to a fund for the upkeep of the graveyard, which sits on the hill just above the waterline. Once a year, on the anniversary of the eviction, whoever can comes to the graveyard for an hour and then to a service station on the nearest motorway. Apart from that they don't interact much; they don't cooperate on any project; the grandchildren mostly don't turn up; and there is a running dispute about whether spouses and descendants count as members or only the people who were actually cleared. Go through the five criteria and say whether this group is still a community in the thick sense, or whether the community already dissolved when the village was razed and what is left is only its memory. If the criteria give a messy answer, say whether that is because one of the criteria needs changing rather than because the case is genuinely borderline.
