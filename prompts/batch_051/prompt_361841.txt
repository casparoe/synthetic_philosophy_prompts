For my philosophy of mathematics course I want a decision tree that sorts someone into a response to Benacerraf's dilemma—roughly, the problem that mathematical statements appear to be true and about mind-independent, causally inert objects, while our best account of knowledge seems to require causal contact with what is known. Please build the tree from diagnostic claims rather than from the names of positions, so that each branch is a statement a student could accept or reject without already knowing which position it leads to. At minimum the tree should ask: whether mathematical statements such as the claim that there are infinitely many primes are literally true in the same way ordinary empirical statements are; whether the objects such statements appear to be about are mind-independent and causally inert; whether knowledge requires causal contact with what is known; whether mathematics could retain its content and scientific usefulness if there were no mathematical objects; and whether mathematical truth is better explained by proof, coherence, or structure than by reference to objects. The leaves must include a robust platonism that retains mathematical objects and abandons the causal requirement on knowledge; a structuralism on which mathematics concerns possible or shared structure rather than particular objects; an if-thenist or deductivist position on which theorems assert only what follows from axioms; an error-theoretic fictionalism on which mathematical statements are not literally true but remain useful in science; a logicist or analytic conventionalist position on which mathematical truth reduces to logic or meaning; and an intuitionist or constructivist position on which truth is constrained by constructive provability. Where a view can be reached through more than one combination of answers, make the crossovers explicit rather than forcing a false branching. Mark logically possible but apparently empty regions—for example, accepting mind-independent objects and the causal constraint while still claiming mathematical knowledge—and say briefly whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Aim for about seven leaves, and assume the reader knows the basic Benacerraf problem and a causal theory of knowledge but not the standard responses.
