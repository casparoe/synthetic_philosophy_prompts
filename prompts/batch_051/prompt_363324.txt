I'm a student in an AI safety course and we've been circling the side-effects problem: you want a home robot to do the task you asked (say, put away the groceries) without, like, smashing a vase on the way or reorganizing the whole house for no reason, but you can't write down everything you care about. Someone in seminar sketched a fix and I've been trying to make it concrete, because I can't tell weather it's clever or just laundering the value judgment.

The proposal: give the robot a simulator that can enumerate, or at least sample, the possible states of the house before it acts. For any candidate plan, the robot runs the plan forward and then counts how many of the before-states are still reachable under its remaining action repertoire. Define impact as the fraction of before-states that got closed off. The robot has to pick, among all plans that accomplish the assigned task, the one with the lowest impact. The pitch is that this penalizes side effects without ever saying vases matter: break a vase and you've removed a big chunk of the reachable state space, so a low-impact robot won't do it unless the task requires it.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. I want to know how it would actually behave if a moderately clever optimizer lived under it. What does it incentivize? How is it gamed? For instance, what stops the robot from first hacking the thermostat to have a hundred continuous settings instead of three, manufacturing a bunch of new reachable states, and then "spending" those extra options so it can break the vase and still come out with a lower impact score? And what happens with tasks that are supposed to be at least partly irreversible — locking a door, throwing out spoiled food, nailing something to a wall — where doing the job well means closing off options? Also, the score depends on what counts as "the same state" before and after and on what the simulator treats as distinct at all, which looks to me like a value judgment hiding inside the bookkeeping. Is that a patchable bug, or does every version of "avoid reducing options" secretly need a specification of what matters? I genuinely can't tell if the whole approach dissolves once you push on it.

End your answer with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that says whether you would keep, modify, or abandon this proposal, and the one main reason why.
