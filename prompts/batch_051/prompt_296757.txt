So I've been reading The Prince on my own, not for any class, just because I got curious about how much of politics is skill versus luck, and two chapters keep bugging me because they seem to pull in opposite directions. In chapter 25 Machiavelli compares fortune to a river that floods when you're not prepared, but he also says fortune only controls about half of our actions, maybe even a little less, and leaves the rest for us to direct through foresight and preparation. That sounds pretty hopeful about what a capable person can do. But then in chapter 7, when he talks about Cesare Borgia, he seems to say the opposite. My understanding of that part is that Borgia did basically everything a prudent and skilled ruler could do, and Machiavelli even wants to set him up as a model for new princes, but Borgia still lost everything because of a run of terrible fortune: his father dying, Borgia himself getting sick at exactly the wrong moment, and his enemies moving before he could recover. Machiavelli says, as far as I can tell, that Borgia was brought down not by his own fault but by extraordinary and extreme bad luck.

So I'm stuck on how these two fit together. Did Machiavelli change his mind between those chapters? Is there a way to read the chapter 25 fifty-fifty claim so it doesn't actually promise that virtù can beat fortune in a case like Borgia's? Maybe chapter 25 is about princes in general and chapter 7 is admitting that sometimes fortune just wins no matter what, but then why would he say the half thing so confidently? I'd like you to walk me through the main ways someone could reconcile these two passages, and then give me a verdict on which reconciliation is most honest and which ones feel like special pleading. If they can't really be reconciled, what does that mean for the idea of virtù in Machiavelli: is it only effective when fortune happens to be cooperating? Or is there a reading where Borgia's failure actually shows that no amount of virtù is ever enough, and chapter 25 is Machiavelli trying to keep himself from admitting that?
