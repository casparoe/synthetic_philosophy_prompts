I was a chemist for thirty-five years — mostly reaction mechanisms — and only started reading philosophy of mind after I retired, so forgive me if I'm mangleing the jargon. The split-brain cases have completely absorbed me. Take the standard setup: a word or command is flashed to a patient's left visual field so that only the right hemisphere receives it, and the patient then does something with the left hand — say, stands up and walks, or points to a matching object. When the experimenter asks why they did it, the speaking hemisphere (the left, in most of these patients) smoothly offers a reason, but it's a confabulation, because the speaking hemisphere never saw the stimulus that actually triggered the action. I keep chewing on an argument I've seen floating around, which I'll rephrase like this:

(1) In such cases the speaking hemisphere doesn't report ignorance; it produces a fluent, confident, wrong explanation for an action it can't have known the cause of.
(2) The non-speeking hemisphere's processing is genuinely conscious — it isn't a reflex; it follows instructions, answers yes/no with hand signals, and can point to a matching picture.
(3) If the patient were exactly one conscious subject at that moment, that subject would have at least in-principle first-person access to the conscious state driving the action, rather than having to infer or invent a reason afterwards.
(4) So the patient must be at least two conscious subjects at once, with the speaking subject simply cut off from the other's reasons.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step or steps fail, and what's the strongest reading of the weak step that could still do the job? My instinct is that (3) is the sneeky one: ordinary people confabulate all the time without being two minds, so lacking the real cause can't by itself prove a second subject. But in the ordinary case the unaccessed cause is usually unconscious, whereas here the missing content appears to be conscious in the other hemisphere — and that seems like exactly the sort of difference that should matter. Either talk me out of that, or show me the argument still goes through once (3) is repaired. And if the argument is invalid as a piece of formal logic, I'd like to know that too, not just that a premise is false.
