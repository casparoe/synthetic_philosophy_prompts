I'm a PhD student working on the boundary of logic and game theory, and a "strategy stealing" argument from a workshop last week is still bugging me. It seemed to prove something way too strong, and I can't find the weak link even though I know the conclusion is false.

The claim was: in any finite two-player game of perfect information with no draws, where both players have exactly the same legal moves from every position (think nim-style games), the first player cannot lose. The argument went:

(1) Suppose the second player had a winning strategy S.
(2) The first player makes some arbitrary legal opening move m, then pretends to be the second player for the rest of the game: after each move by the actual second player, the first player consults S and makes the move S would prescribe for the second player from the corresponding postion.
(3) If S prescribes a move that would be illegal from the actual position — say because m changed part of the board — the first player just makes another arbitrary legal move instead.
(4) Since m was their own move, and having an extra one of your own moves on the board can never make you worse of, this extra move can't hurt; S should still deliver the win.
(5) So the first player wins, contradicting the supposition that S wins for the second player. Therefore the second player never has a winning strategy, and the first player wins or draws.

But that conclusion can't be right. Take the tiny game with one pile of three counters: either player may remove one or two counters on their turn, and whoever takes the last counter wins. Same moves for both players, finite, perfect information, no draws. Yet the second player wins: if the first player takes one, the second takes the remaining two; if the first player takes two, the second takes the last one.

So the argument must go wrong somewhere, but each step sounded fine when I heard it. I'd like to know exactly which step to reject, why that step looked harmless, and what a corrected version of the argument would need to assume to actually go through.
