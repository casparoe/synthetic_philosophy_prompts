I've been tinkering with an argument and it still compiles, but the output is absurd: it seems to prove that every debate about what exists is just a verbal dispute. I don't believe that, so I'm trying to find the bug. Here's the argument.

(1) For any question about whether Xs exist — holes, numbers, tables — you can paraphrase each side into its own language or conceptual scheme so that each side comes out true in its own scheme. "Holes exist" comes out true in the hole-friendly scheme; "holes don't exist" comes out true in the hole-free scheme.

(2) If both sides can each be made true in their own scheme, and they don't disagree about any of the underlying facts, then their dispute is just about which scheme to adopt — which is a verbal choice, not a factual disagreement.

(3) In the hole case, both sides accept the same underlying facts about where the matter is and what shape the opening is. They only disagree about whether to count the hole itself as a thing.

(4) So the hole debate is verbal.

(5) Nothing about that procedure depends on the details of holes; you could run the same translation trick on numbers, or on whether tables exist or only particles arranged table-wise.

(6) So every debate about what exists is verbal.

(7) But thats too much: someone who denies that tables exist and says only particles arranged table-wise are real seems to genuinely disagree with me about what the world contains, not just about which words are handy.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd like to keep (7), but I also can't point to the flaw myself. Don't give me a survey of possible fixes — pick the one you think actually fails, say why it looked fine, and tell me what I'm stuck with if I reject it.
