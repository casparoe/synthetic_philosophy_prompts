Here's the thing. Every time I read Hofstadter on Aunt Hillary (the ant colony in the Ant Fugue) I come away thinking a colony could literally think, and every time I talk to a friend about it I come away thinking its all just a cute metaphor. Before I go back and forth again I want the spec: what would a theory of whether a composite system like an ant colony has a mind actually have to get right? I don't want a yes-or-no answer, I want the requirements list. For each requirement, give me the case or thought experiment that forces it onto the list, and tell me wich requirements are non-negotiable and which ones are just the prejudices of one camp. The cases need to cover at least: individual ants versus the whole colony, a colony whose ants you replace one at a time, a colony where the signal-passing gets slowed down or garbled, and a colony that looks goal-directed but where nobody wants to say it has a mind. The distinctions a good theory can't collapse are the part I care about most: stored information versus represented belief, being predictively useful via the intentional stance versus literally having mental states, a mind versus a vivid metaphor, and maybe consciousness versus cognition. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular answers — simple functionalism ('yes, the colony thinks'), hard eliminativism ('no, it's all a metaphor'), and ant-level reductionism ('only individual ants have minds') — and whether any two of the requirements look like they can't be jointly satisfied. Don't give me a bibliography; I want the criteria and the case for each.
