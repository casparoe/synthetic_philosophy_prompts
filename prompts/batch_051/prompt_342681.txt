I am a high school student working on a paper for a philosophy program, and I have been studying Scanlon's contractualist objection to aggregation. As I understand it, Scanlon holds that an action is wrong if it is forbidden by a principle that someone affected could reasonably reject. From this starting point he argues that a decision cannot be justified by aggregating a large number of small benefits to many people so that those benefits outweigh a serious burden imposed on one person; the person asked to bear the burden does not gain a reason to accept it simply because many others each get a small benefit. The argument seems to assume a setting in which a human agent deliberates about a principle and could in principle be asked to justify the decision to each affected person.

I want to ask whether this argument survives a changed setting. Suppose a hospital now replaces case-by-case human deliberation with a triage algorithm designed to allocate scarce resources by maximizing total expected life-years saved or some similar aggregate score, and its decisions are not reviewed individually. Does Scanlon's objection to aggregation still go through when the decisionmaker is a scoring rule rather than a person deliberating about principles? Which premise of the argument is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version of the objection that still says something important about algorithmic triage, or should the objection be set aside in that setting? Please answer in American English.
