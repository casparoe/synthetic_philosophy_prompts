I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me if I'm using terms sloppy. My current book is about a near-future climate movement that starts acting like a church—confessions, relics, expulsions—and I keep writing "religion" in scare quotes because I'm not sure the word has any stable meaning. Then I read William James's The Varieties of Religious Experience, where he tries to define religion by focusing on what he takes to be the essential case: the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual persons in their solitude, insofar as they see themselves as standing in relation to whatever they consider divine. I don't have the passage infront of me, but that's the gist.

I wrote a little argument for one of my characters and now I'm worried I gave him a bad argument. It goes:

(1) If something is a religion, it must fit James's definition: its core must be individuals, in solitude, relating to what they take to be divine.
(2) Confucianism does not have that core. It's mostly about ritual, social relationships, and family obligations, not a private relation to a divine being.
(3) So, on James's definition, Confucianism is not a religion.
(4) But an adequate category of "religion" should not exclude Confucianism; if it does, the category is just a Western idea pretending to be a neutral tool.
(5) Therefore James's definition fails, and the whole category "religion" is not a coherent kind—it's a family resemblance or a political label.

I find steps 1 through 4 convincing, but the conclusion feels too strong, and I suspect there's a slide somewhere between a proposed definition and a claim about what the category should cover. Also, step 2 might be a caricature of Confucianism—I don't know enough. Could you tell me: is the argument valid? If not, wich step fails exactly, and what would a more defensible version of it say?
