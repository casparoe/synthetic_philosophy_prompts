I cover language and cognition for a science-and-ideas magazine, and this term I am doing a stint as an outside assessor for a university course in the philosophy of language. The exam contained the following short-answer question, and the answer below it was submitted by one of the students. Before I record a mark, I would like an independent read: please give the answer a numerical mark out of 20, and then write the specific, point-by-point comments that justify the mark. I am not looking for a general impression of the writing; the comments should track accuracy and precision about the Gricean categories involved.

Exam question: "Grice drew a distinction between what a speaker says and what a speaker conversationally implicates. Suppose the chair of a graduate admissions committee receives the following reference letter, in full, for a philosophy PhD applicant named Jones, from one of Jones's professors: 'Jones has excellent handwriting and is always punctual.' The chair remarks, 'I read Smith as saying, in so many words, that Jones is not a strong philosopher.' Write a short essay explaining: (i) whether the chair is right to treat that content as part of what Smith literally said, or whether it should be treated as something else; (ii) which feature or features of Grice's framework explain how the content arises; (iii) whether the content is a conversational or a conventional implicature, and whether it is generalized or particularized, giving reasons; and (iv) whether the content could be cancelled by adding a further sentence to the letter, and what your answer reveals about the classification in (iii)."

Student's answer: "Smith does not literally say that Jones is not a strong philosopher. The sentence 'Jones has excellent handwriting and is always punctual' has as its literal content only that Jones's handwriting is excellent and that he is punctual; it does not entail anything about Jones's philosophical ability, so the chair is wrong to treat the negative assessment as part of what is said. The assessment is an implicature. The committee assumes Smith is following the Cooperative Principle, notices that a reference letter consisting of a single piece of praise for peripheral qualities is less informative than the exchange requires, and reasons that if Smith could truthfully assert something stronger about Jones's philosophical work, Smith would have. This is a flouting of the Maxim of Quantity. Because the inference carries over to any occasion in which someone praises only peripheral qualities of a candidate—every competent reader of such a letter picks it up immediately, regardless of the details—the implicature is best classified as a generalized conversational implicature. It is conversational rather than conventional because it does not derive from the standing meaning of any individual word in the sentence. Finally, the implicature cannot be cancelled. If Smith added 'Jones is a superb philosopher,' the letter would still communicate the negative assessment, because that assessment arises from what Smith left unsaid. Only by endorsing Jones's philosophical ability at some length could Smith remove it."

If the question itself is badly framed—for example, if it asks for a distinction in a way that does not actually fit Grice's view—tell me that plainly rather than grading around it.
