I am the moderator of a citizens' assembly that convenes tonight for its first session. Its charge is to produce a recommendation, over three weekends of deliberation, on whether the city should permit a supervised drug-consumption site on a specific block. The room will include forty randomly selected residents — retired police officers, social workers, landlords, small-business owners, a high-school student, people in recovery and people who have lost family to addiction — along with six technical experts, two city council members, and several journalists. A handful of the experts know Habermas well enough to hear the commitments behind what I say; most of the residents never will, and would be put off if I named them.

I have ten minutes, at most, to open the assembly. The opening has to do real structural work. It must establish the norms that will govern the next three weekends: that disagreement is the point of the exercise; that participants should speak to be understood and listen in order to understand, rather than to win; that anyone may challenge anything, including my framing, the experts' data, and the officials' authority; and that the only pressure anyone should feel inside the room is the pressure of the better reason. But the speech cannot pretend those conditions are already met. The room will not shed power, status, expertise, race, money, fluency, or confidence at the door, and a speaker who pretends it will has already coerced the conversation rather than opened it. So the speech should honestly name that gap — between the public room we can actually build over these few weeks and the ideal of unforced agreement among equals — and then invite the room to build the best approximation of it that we can, together.

In substance, the speech should practice communicative rationality rather than announce it: it should orient the room away from using speech strategically and toward reaching mutual understanding, and it should leave behind a short, plain vocabulary of ground rules that anyone in the room can invoke later. Write the opening remarks I should deliver. They should be in American English, written for the ear — short sentences, concrete words, no terms of art, nothing that sounds like a lecture.
