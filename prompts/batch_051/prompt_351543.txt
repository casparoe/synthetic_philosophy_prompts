I am a novelist constructing a secondary world whose history is identical to our Earth's up to the year corresponding to our 1950. In that year, one law of human nature changes overnight and then holds for every human being alive and every child born afterward. I call the change Buridanic paralysis, after the old puzzle of the ass placed between two equally distant, equally attractive piles of hay. A person with this condition does not lack preferences and does not fail to deliberate. But whenever, at the moment of acting, two or more available options are exactly tied in the person's all-things-considered ranking, deliberation does not terminate: she freezes, even amid danger or urgency, until some asymmetry appears among the options, the preferences, or the environment. An agent cannot break this tie by a bare act of will while frozen, since any such act would itself be a selection among equals; however, an agent can adopt a standing tie-breaking policy in advance, such as an unconditional rule to take the leftmost option, and the paralysis does not trigger once a prior policy has made the options unequal. The asymmetry that ends a freeze may be trivial, arbitrary, or supplied by another person.

I do not want a plot from you; I want the chain of reasoning I would need to build the next seventy years of this world coherently, with the reasoning visible and with confidence marked at each step. Please work through the following. First, the first decade: what breaks down immediately, what crude coping practices appear, and which features of ordinary life change fastest. Second, the mature adaptations of the next two generations: the institutions, legal doctrines, commercial practices, and social norms that arise to pre-empt, detect, or break exact ties; whether people learn to weaponize the condition by manufacturing ties for others; and who gains power, those who can exploit ties or those who design defaults and choice architectures. Third, the long-run cultural and psychological consequences: how people raised from birth under the condition relate to choice, commitment, regret, and selfhood, and which of our ordinary attitudes toward choosing become impossible or get replaced. Fourth, state which of your predictions are close to forced by the stipulated change and which depend on further assumptions, such as how reliably people can bind themselves to standing tie-breakers, how easily a rival can engineer an exact tie, and how good people are at foreseeing which future choices might tie; say what would have to be different for your less certain predictions to be wrong. Fifth, close by saying what this world shows about the underlying philosophical problem of choice without preference: whether the Buridan's ass puzzle exposes a genuine gap in the concept of rational choice, whether it merely shows that real agents are saved by unnoticed tie-breaking mechanisms and precommitments, or whether the idea of choosing among exactly equal options is an artifact of an over-precise formalization.
