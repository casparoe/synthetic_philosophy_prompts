I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have come to defend a position about meritocracy that I would like to see stress-tested. The position is this: meritocratic allocation cannot, by itself, be a principle of desert. What we call merit — cognitive ability, conscientiousness, creativity, even the capacity for sustained effort — is jointly produced by genetic endowments and environments that no one chooses. Because the magnitude of a person's merit is not ultimately attributable to the person, a desert-based defense of meritocracy is caught between two options: it can adopt an implausible notion of self-creation, according to which people deserve credit for traits and capacities they did not author, or it can quietly convert into an instrumental argument about efficiency, incentives, and information. On my view, the second option is the only defensible one; meritocracy survives as a useful sorting device, not as a theory of moral desert.

I want to know what would count against this position, in the strongest sense of "count against." Which empirical findings, arguments, or cases should move me — sorted from decisive to merely awkward — and why? For example, would a credible account of ultimate responsibility for one's own abilities, or evidence that the relevant psychological traits are less heritable and more responsive to voluntary choice than I have assumed, be a serious defeater? Are there clear cases in which it is simply irresistible to say that someone deserves an outcome in proportion to developed ability, and should those cases be treated as data against the position or as evidence that we use "desert" loosely? What does the rival view predict that mine cannot — for instance, that people will converge in considered judgments about who deserves what, or that desert claims can remain stable across different incentive schemes?

I would also like you to say which objections a defender of my position should honestly concede as counting against it, and which objections it should dismiss as confusing desert with adjacent values such as entitlement, legitimate expectation, or admiration for excellence. Finally, tell me whether the view is insulated — whether it has been qualified so that every apparent counterexample can be absorbed — and whether that insulation, if it exists, is a defect in a claim of this kind or the correct shape for a conceptual boundary. My strongest personal test would be this: show me that effort can serve as a desert base without the capacity and inclination for effort itself being explained by unchosen factors, and I would have to revise substantially. Please answer in American English.
