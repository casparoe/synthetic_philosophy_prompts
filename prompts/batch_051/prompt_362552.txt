I'm a functional programmer who has worked on compilers and used proof assistants in earnest, so I think natively in terms of raw syntax versus alpha-equivalence, capture-avoiding substitution, De Bruijn indices as nameless syntax, quotienting a term algebra by a group of renamings, and representation independence. I have almost no background in philosophy of physics, so treat the following as the only physics I know.

In general relativity, a possible history is often represented as a pair (M, g): a manifold M of spacetime points together with a metric field g that assigns geometric structure to those points (I mean "model" in the physicist's sense, not model theory). The field equations are diffeomorphism-invariant: if (M, g) is a solution, so is (M, h*g) for every smooth invertible map h from M onto itself that respects its differentiable structure, where h*g is the metric obtained by pulling g back along h. Now let H be an open region lying entirely to the future of a Cauchy surface Σ, and let h be the identity outside H but a nontrivial rearrangement of points inside H. Then (M, g) and (M, h*g) agree outside H and on and before Σ, but disagree about which geometric structures sit at which points inside the hole. The field equations are deterministic in the relevant sense: initial data on a Cauchy surface fix the solution uniquely up to diffeomorphism. A manifold substantivalist holds that the points of M themselves exist, so the two pairs describe two distinct possible worlds: the same spacetime points carrying different geometries in the hole. Since the two worlds agree on everything up to Σ and diverge only in the future of Σ, substantivalism appears to make a deterministic theory radically indeterministic. The relationist answers that the two pairs are merely two labels for the same physical situation, so there is no genuine divergence. A third view keeps the substantivalist ontology but insists the two pairs represent the same world anyway, treating diffeomorphisms as gauge rather than physical difference. This is my understanding of what philosophers call the hole argument.
