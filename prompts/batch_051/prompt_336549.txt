i'm teaching an upper-level seminar on work and the good life, and our last stretch has been on Huizinga and Pieper — play and leisure as ends in themselves. the reading emphasized that play is voluntary, bounded, and serious in its own way, and that leisure is a receptive, contemplative attitude rather than a pause that exists to make you better at work. every discussion, though, circles the same rut: someone says play is valuable because it restores us for work, builds social bonds, sharpens skills; then someone else points out that the moment you justify it that way it stops being the thing we were reading about. we ended the semester unresolved on that.

so i'd like to close the final session with a poem they can sit with — read it aloud, maybe fifteen students, last meeting before finals. a villanelle. the refrains should carry the two voices of the semester: one in the register of the instrumental justification (play as useful, leisure as recuperation), the other in the register of the autotelic view (the activity carries its point within itself). the strict, arbitrary machinery of the form should feel like the rules of a game — freely accepted, not efficient, doing nothing beyond the play itself. dont name Huizinga or Pieper or throw around terms like autotelic; a specialist should recognize the view and a stranger should still get a good poem. let the poem live inside the tension rather than resolve it, and in the final quatrain let the two refrains land together and show what they do to each other. if the poem ends up feeling slightly useless, that may be the most accurate thing about it.
