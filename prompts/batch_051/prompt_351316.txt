OK so my friend and I just spent a whole evening arguing about desuetude — the idea that a law nobody ever enforces basically stops being a real law. He thought it was a scandal for the rule of law, I thought it's just how legal systems honestly work. Anyway the argument got me thinking about an actual mechanism, and I want it gamed out properly.

Here's the proposal. A jurisdiction changes its constitution so that any criminal statute that goes 25 straight years without a single enforcement action — arrest, citation, or formal charge under it — automatically lapses on its 25th anniversary. The legislature can save it only by holding a recorded vote to re-enact it sometime in the year before the deadline. If they miss that window, the law is just gone, and they'd have to pass it again from scratch if they still want it.

What I want to know is how this actually behaves in practice, not whether it's a nice idea. What does it do to prosecuters' and police departments' incentives, especially for laws they like to keep around as an occasional threat? How do legislators, interest groups, or ordinary people game it — like doing one token prosecution every 24 years to keep a dead law alive, or quietly letting an unpopular law lapse so nobody has to take the blame for repealing it? Does it make the statute book more honest and improve the rule of law, or does it just create a new flavor of selective enforcement and definitional squabbling over what counts as an enforcement action? And where does it break first, and is that breakage fixable without reintroducing the original problem?
