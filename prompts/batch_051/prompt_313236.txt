My mother is seventy-four and has Alzheimer's disease, and my two siblings and I are facing a decision that has split us. More than a decade ago, after watching her own mother decline and die with dementia, she signed an advance directive stating that if she ever reached a state in which she no longer recognized her children and could no longer read or garden, she refused antibiotics, hospitalization, and other life-sustaining treatment. She also appended a handwritten instruction that we should treat any later request from her for such treatment as a symptom of the disease rather than as a genuine change of mind; she had watched relatives beg for treatment in moments of panic and did not want that to undo her decision. She has now reached exactly the condition the directive describes, and she has developed pneumonia, which the doctors say would be easy to treat with antibiotics but otherwise likely fatal within days.

What none of us anticipated is that over the past several months she has been saying—calmly, and more coherently than she has spoken in a long while—that she knows what the papers say and that she has changed her mind. She enjoys the singing sessions and craft afternoons at her nursing home, she looks forward to meals, and she had said several times, before she became ill, that she would want treatment for something like this. My brother insists that honoring the directive is the only way to respect the person who wrote it, and that the disease should not be allowed to rewrite her most considered wishes. My sister insists that the mother in front of us is still a person, and that forcing her to die for the preferences of a woman she no longer is would be cruelty masquerading as fidelity. I can feel the pull of both arguments, and I have less than a week to reach a view. I am not asking who would prevail in a legal dispute; I am trying to understand whether there is a principled way to decide whose wishes should have authority over what happens now.
