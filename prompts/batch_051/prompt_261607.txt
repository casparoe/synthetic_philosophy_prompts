I've been reading about the Daoist idea of wu-wei, usually translated as non-action or effortless action, and I keep running into the same problem: the moment I notice that I'm forcing something, and I deliberately try to force less, I only become more tense and self-conscious. Out of that frustration I've built a distinction I want checked, because I suspect it is either genuinely useful or completely empty.

I call the two sides first-order trying and second-order trying. First-order trying is effort aimed directly at the task: writing the sentence, making the tackle, keeping my voice steady. Second-order trying is effort aimed at my own effort: monitoring whether my effort is of the right kind, attempting to relax, trying not to try. My principle, which I've been calling the Second-Order Veto, says that wu-wei is perfectly compatible with intense first-order trying, and that what actually destroys it is second-order trying. The moment I try to try, I have taken on an extra task, managing myself, that competes with the original one, and the doubled effort shows up as stiffness or self-consciousness.

Two examples persuaded me. A pianist practicing a hard passage can be sweating from sheer effort and still be, by my account, in wu-wei, so long as her attention is entirely inside the passage; the stiffness begins when she listens to herself in order to check that she is playing as she should. And in conversation, my worst silences happen not when I have nothing to say but when I find myself trying to be interesting.

What I want is an honest assessment. Is the distinction stable, or does second-order trying just collapse into ordinary first-order trying aimed at a different object? Does this already exist under a cleaner name I haven't encountered? And give me the strongest counterexample you can: a case where someone is clearly trying to try, and yet the action stays effortless, or a case where second-order trying is absent and the action is still rigid. I would rather find out now than defend a distinction that breaks under the first good objection.
