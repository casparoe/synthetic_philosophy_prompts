I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I've somehow found myself moderating a structured adversarial collaboration between two moral philosophers who've spent years arguing about moral worth. Camp A says an act has moral worth only when it's done from duty — from the thought that it's the right thing to do. On their view, acting from warm feelings or self-interest, even when it happens to produce the right action, is morally flat; it's in accordance with duty but not from it. Camp B says that's backwards. What matters is that the agent is responding to the features that actually make the act right — the danger, the injustice, the need. The feeling can be love, sympathy, or plain conviction, but the explicit thought "this is my duty" adds nothing, and sometimes gets in the way.

They both use the same cases in class — the shopkeeper who gives correct change only because it's good for business, the person who jumps in the river to save a child out of sheer empathy, Huck Finn helping his friend escape while thinking he's doing wrong. They just read the cases differently, and after two days of arguing they've agreed to write a joint statement.

Draft that document for them. It should have (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about the cases, about what moral worth is not, and about where they actually overlap; (2) a disagreements table, each row stating the precise point of disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own view; and (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete test, experiment, or thought experiment both would agree bears on it, and record what each camp predicts in advance.

I suspect one crux is whether the explicit thought "this is the right thing to do" contributes any moral credit over and above responding to the right-making features. Another might be whether natural sympathy toward someone's need can carry the same moral worth as acting from duty in the same situation. But don't just take my guesses — work out what the real cruxes would be, and if part of this fight is merely verbal, say which part and why, and seperate the part that's verbal from the part that's real.
