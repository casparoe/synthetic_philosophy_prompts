Ok so I just got out of a two hour argument with a friend about what Machiavelli actually means by virtù, and I want a fresh read on it. Here are the interpretations we came up with, so don't just feed me these back: (1) virtù as amoral skill or competence at getting and keeping power, the whatever-works reading where success is its own standard; (2) virtù as flexibility or adapting to the times, which gets weird becasue Machiavelli also says people are stuck in their natures and the cautious man can't become impetuous when circumstances demand it; (3) virtù as masculine forcefulness or virility, tied to the passage where fortune is called a woman who has to be struck down; (4) virtù as republican civic virtue, the public-spiritedness and military discipline of a free citizen body in the Discourses; (5) virtù as what founder-legislators like Moses or Romulus have, the ability to impose form on formless material by spotting the opportunity fortune opens and seizing it. I'm convinced there's at least one more genuinely distinct reading of virtù in Machiavelli thats not on this list—one that isn't a restatement, special case, or blend of the five and that actually picks out something in the text the others ignore. What is it? And would that reading make better sense of the tension in chapter 25, where he seems to recommend adaptability but then admits no one can actually adapt?
