I've long taught the Van Meegeren affair as an anecdote, but this year I want to make it a proper case study in the value of originals. The lens: the thesis that the value an original has over a perceptually indistinguishable forgery is constituted entirely by its causal history — who made it and under what conditions — and not by anything available in the painted surface. The facts I'll stipulate for the classroom: in the 1930s Han van Meegeren painted several works he passed off as newly discovered Vermeers, the most important an Emmaus scene that leading experts praised and a major Dutch museum acquired; during the occupation he sold a work as a Vermeer to Hermann Göring; after the war he was arrested as a collaborator for selling Dutch cultural property to the enemy, and his defense was that the painting was a forgery he had made himself, which he demonstrated in custody. Analyze the episode through this lens: what the theory predicts about the rise and fall of the paintings' value, which features it explains and where it is silent or wrong, what actually happened to the canvases' value once the confession was accepted, and what the case in turn shows about whether authenticity is a historical rather than a perceptual property.
