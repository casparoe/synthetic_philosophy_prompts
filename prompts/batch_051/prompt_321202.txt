In a seminar on G. E. Moore's ethics, I've been collecting my instructor's verdicts on what has intrinsic value, and I'm trying to extract the principle behind them. Over the semester she has said the following. A beautiful but unseen valley, one no conscious being ever contemplates, has some intrinsic value, though far less than the value of someone's actually appreciating it. A listener who understands and enjoys a genuinely beautiful symphony counts as a very great good. A listener in the same state but secretly hearing random noise that merely sounds like music counts for little or nothing, because the feeling is not directed at anything really beautiful. A friendship in which each person accurately appreciates the other's genuine virtues and knows the appreciation is mutual is among the greatest goods. A one-sided attachment, in which A admires B for B's real virtues while B is merely polite and indifferent and A never learns this, is worth far less than the mutual friendship, perhaps nothing positive as a whole. Malicious pleasure, meanwhile, is unambiguously evil, and the pleasure in it redeems nothing.

There is one case we have not discussed. Suppose two people neither of whom knows the other each accurately loves the other's genuine virtues from a distance, through one another's published work, for instance, and neither ever learns that the feeling is reciprocated. Would she treat this as two separate instances of a valuable kind of love, roughly doubling the good, or as a defective whole lacking the mutual recognition that made the friendship case so valuable, and therefore worth far less? I am less interested in the exact verdict than in the principle she seems to be running, the other principles that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, what each of those principles predicts about this case, your best guess about which principle she is actually using and why, and which further case would best tell the competing principles apart.
