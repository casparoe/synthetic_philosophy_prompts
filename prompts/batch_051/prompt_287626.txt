I'm preparing a short paper for an early modern philosophy seminar on decision-theoretic reconstructions of Pascal's wager, and I've realized that I assert five things at different points in the draft that I have never checked against one another. I would like a consistency audit of the package before I circulate it. The claims are as follows.

(a) Expected utility theory is the correct account of practical rationality wherever states and utilities are finite and well-defined.
(b) The wager should not be run as an expected-value argument over infinite utilities; it should be run as a dominance argument, because infinite payoffs make expected values undefined or too easy to manipulate.
(c) The many-gods objection can be set aside: the only live supposition is the God of classical theism, and the other deities are under-described ad hoc posits that can be excluded without a general criterion for which gods count.
(d) The wager licenses only a policy of religious practice — behaving as if God exists and putting oneself in the way of belief — not belief itself; epistemic and practical rationality remain distinct.
(e) The wager is a tie-breaker: it may legitimately decide a genuinely even evidential standoff, but it could never rationally override a strongly adverse evidential balance.

I'm looking for an audit of the package itself, not a general survey of the wager. Please go through these and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, and say what each tension turns on. Then rank the genuine tensions by severity and, for each, give the cheapest revision and what it costs. Finish by telling me which single revision of one of (a)–(e) would do the most to restore coherence. End with a line exactly of the form \answer{ranking: [most severe first]; revise-first: [which of (a)–(e) to change]}.
