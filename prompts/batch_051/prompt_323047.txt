I just started as a product manager at a small start-up that makes a commitment-contract app: people set a goal (going to the gym three times a week, writing 500 words a day, that kind of thing), put money at risk, and if they miss the target the money goes to a person or charity they've chosen — sometimes a 'friend' they dislike, so the sting is real. The engineering team want to add features that go beyond plain financial penalties. One proposed feature lets users select an "I'll definitely do it later" mode, where the app under-weights their past failures when forecasting their future success, so they stay optimistic and are more likely to enter the contract in the first place. Another feature is a "guilt module" that sends messages written as if from a disappointed parent. I keep thinking of Parfit's hitchhiker: the hitchhiker knows that once he's been dropped off he won't pay the driver, but the driver only picks up people who solemnly promise to pay; so in order to be believed, the hitchhiker needs to be irrational in a controlled way — genuinely moved by a promise when he shouldn't be, or self-deceived about whether he'll actually pay. The lesson people draw is that it can be rational to arrange to be irrational. But when I mention this, my team says "great, so anything that gets users to stick to a contract is on the table." That feels wrong, and I can't yet say why.

You are a professional philosopher who works on rationality, self-control, and precommitment. Please turn my half-remembered Parfit point into a procedure a product manager can actually use. I want a checklist or decision tree, in plain language, that my team can run at weekly feature review — we review maybe five proposals a week, the stakes for user's are real (lost money, guilt, possibly self-deception), our legal person has to sign off on anything that manipulates users, and the team has no philosophy background and won't read anything longer than a page. Before you write the final procedure, brainstorm the key distinctions and the failure modes; then think carefully about which distinctions have to survive in a simplified checklist. The procedure should separate features that use rational irrationality in a defensible way from features that merely exploit users' biases. Work through at least three worked examples — the optimistic mode, the guilt module, and a third feature: a "future-self lock" that stops users from editing their contract during a short window of weakness (say, the hour after they mark a goal complete). Make sure at least one worked example is a case where the procedure tells us to kill a feature the team loves. Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot decide, where a human has to make a judgment call.
