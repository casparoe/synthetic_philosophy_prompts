You are a philosopher of mathematical practice, asked to reconstruct commitments that are rarely stated explicitly but show up in how mathematics is done and taught. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate commitments before defending the reconstruction you find most plausible.

Consider a practice visible throughout contemporary pure mathematics. A first course in real analysis defines continuity of a function on the real line by the ε–δ condition and calls a subset of ℝ compact when it is closed and bounded; these definitions look like descriptions of ℝ itself. Later, a topology course offers new definitions: a function between topological spaces is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open, and a space is compact when every open cover has a finite subcover. For subsets of ℝ these are only *shown*, by nontrivial theorems, to be equivalent to the earlier definitions — yet they are standardly treated not as optional alternatives but as definitions that capture what continuity and compactness were really about all along. The same expository and evaluative pattern appears in algebra: students first meet groups through symmetries and permutations, but the subject then defines a group axiomatically as a set with an associative binary operation, identity, and inverses, and treats the resulting theorems as covering rotations, matrices, number systems, and permutations at once. Nor is this marginal: it structures the standard curriculum and the way mathematicians argue about whether a proposed definition is good.

The practice seems to contain a tension. Mathematicians often speak as though definitions are free stipulations — nothing stops a person from defining a group differently — while also using evaluative vocabulary in which a definition can be more or less right, natural, general, or ad hoc, as though the concept itself sets a standard that a proposed definition can fail.

What philosophical commitments about mathematical definition, mathematical concepts, and the value of generalization does this practice presuppose, if any? Reconstruct the implicit theory at work. In your answer: (i) distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely coheres with and from what is historical accident or pedagogic convenience; (ii) say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several accounts of definition would license it; (iii) address the apparent tension directly, explaining whether it reflects a genuine inconsistency the practice must resolve or a distinction that should be drawn differently; and (iv) spell out what would change — in teaching, in the culture of natural versus artificial definitions, and in what counts as a satisfactory definition — if the presupposed view were replaced by a deflationary, instrumentalist account on which definitions are only compression devices or proof-shaping tools, with no fact of the matter about the right level of generality.
