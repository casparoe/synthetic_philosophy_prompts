I am in a seminar on the metaphysics of fiction, and our tutor has spent the term ruling on a long series of cases without ever stating her general theory. I have been keeping a log, because a classmate and I disagree about what rule she is actually applying. Her rulings so far include these. She says that Sherlock Holmes does not exist, meaning there was no flesh-and-blood man of that name, and that the name does not refer to any actual person. She also says the name does refer to something: a fictional character, an abstract object brought into being by Conan Doyle's writing, though not a person. She says the sentence Sherlock Holmes was a consulting detective is true in the way claims about what happens in a story are true, and that it would be a mistake to read it as saying someone of that name really lived and did detective work. She ruled that it is true in the stories that Holmes had a liver, even though the text never bothers to establish it, because a human detective living in London obviously has one. And she ruled that it is false in the stories that Holmes owned a smartphone, because the stories are set in late-Victorian London and a smartphone belongs to neither the text nor the period.

Next week's case pushes the unmentioned-background-fact question further: we are to decide whether it is true in the stories that Holmes had 46 chromosomes in his cells. That is an ordinary fact about human beings, but one that science established only in the mid-twentieth century, long after the stories were written and first read. I want to predict how she will rule, given her record. I can think of at least two rules consistent with everything she has said so far. On one, a fiction imports the whole actual world except where the text overrides it, so the chromosome claim comes out true simply because actual human beings have 46 chromosomes. On another, a fiction inherits only the background its narrative and setting presuppose — the manifest facts of human biology, cities, and history that a competent reader needs to make the story cohere — and stays silent beyond that; on this rule the chromosome claim would be neither true nor false in the stories. There may also be a third, artifact-based rule tied to her view that fictional characters are abstract objects, on which a character has only the features the author conferred, explicitly or by obvious implication.

The verdict itself matters less to me than the diagnosis: which rule or rules fit her documented verdicts, what each one predicts about the chromosome case, which rule best explains her pattern so far, and what further case would cleanly separate the live options.
