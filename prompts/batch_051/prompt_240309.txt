I'm TAing a seminar on sovereignty and international organization this term, and for the unit on customs unions and confederations I want to build a decision tree that sorts students by what they actually think joining does to a state, not by which label they'd pick for themselves. The root should be the question whether sovereignty is best treated as a stock of competences you can transfer or spend (so a state can literally have less of it) or as a status that persists until the political unit dissolves (so a state can only delegate or pool the exercise of it). After that, route people through concrete cases: (a) a member tries to negotiate a bilateral trade agreement with a non-member; (b) during a food crisis a member wants to impose a temporary tariff on grain above the common external tariff; (c) the union's dispute body rules that a member breached the common rules and the member refuses to comply; (d) a member announces it is leaving the union; (e) the union votes to admit a new member over the objection of an existing member; (f) the union starts negotiating a common defense clause with an outside alliance. Phrase every node so a student can answer without knowing which leaf it sends them to.

Leaves that must appear: mere delegation (sovereignty itself is untouched), pooled sovereignty (the power is now exercised jointly and cannot be unilaterally recovered), transferred/functional sovereignty (for that issue-area the union is now the sovereign), confederalist (real membership requires a continuing member veto; without it, sovereignty is lost), and a deflationary leaf (sovereignty-talk is too coarse to do the work; just track specific competences). Keep it to at most eight leaves, and give each leaf a one-line note on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the answer combinations they allow — and then locate the named positions in it; for empty corners, say briefly whether they're empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely seperate. Also, if the exit-rights case and the new-member-admission case turn out to be doing nearly all of the sorting work, say so — I keep feeling like the other four cases are window dressing.
