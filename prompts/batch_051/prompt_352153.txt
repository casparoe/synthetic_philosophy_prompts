William James once proposed, in The Moral Equivalent of War, that a peaceful society should conscript its young people into a civilian service corps and set them to hard and dangerous work in coal and iron mines, fishing fleets, on roads, and in dishwashing, so that the moral virtues of war might be preserved without war itself. That proposal exposes an asymmetry in our ordinary practice: we decorate the soldier who endures hardship, deprivation, and risk in service of the group, and we speak of that service in the language of honor, sacrifice, and virtue; yet we treat the miner, the roofer, or the sanitation worker who endures comparable hardship and often comparable danger in service of the group as mere labor, and usually as low-status labor. Is there a defensible principle that justifies reserving honor, ritual, and civic recognition for martial service rather than dangerous or arduous civilian service? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it specifically against James's proposal: where would a civilian conscript in a coal mine differ, in the respects that ground honor, from an enlisted soldier?
