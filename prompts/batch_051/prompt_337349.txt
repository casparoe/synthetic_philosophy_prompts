I'm putting together a list of objections to frequentism for a reading group I run, where by frequentism I mean the view that the probability of an outcome is just the relative frequency with which it occurs in a suitable sequence of trials — usually understood as the limit of that frequency as the sequence gets longer, possibly an infinite limit. So far I've collected these five:

1. The reference-class problem: a single event belongs to many different classes, and the frequency of the outcome depends on which class you use, so frequentism gives no non-arbitrary answer to which frequency is the probability. 2. The single-case problem: frequentism assigns probabilities to sequences or collectives, not to individual events, so it struggles with "this patient has a 40% chance of surviving" and has nothing to say about genuine one-offs. 3. The hypothetical-sequence problem: the actual world only gives finite frequencies, so the frequentist has to invoke what would happen in unrealized trials, which smuggles in modal and counterfactual machinery that the view was supposed to be an austere alternative to. 4. The circularity problem: the standard bridge from probabilities to observed frequencies is the law of large numbers, which says that the frequency converges to the probability with high probability — a theorem stated in terms of the very notion it's meant to interpret. 5. The mismatch problem: a deterministic machine that alternates H, T, H, T forever has a limiting frequency of 1/2 for heads but no chance of heads, while a genuinely fair coin could actually produce a long-run frequency that's way off — so frequency isn't necessary or sufficient for chance.

I've also noticed that the obvious sixth candidate — that frequentism leaves no room for degrees of belief or evidential probability like "probably the butler duunit" — looks to me like the single-case problem in other clothes, so I'd need to be persuaded it's actually doing different work. Maybe that's just how it is; maybe these five are all the distinct objections there are. But I'd like you to try. Act as a professional philosopher of probability, think carefully, brainstorm several candidate further objections, throw out any that are just rewordings, special cases, or combinations of items on my list, and present the strongest survivor. Say explicitly how it differs from each of the five. Then tell me as honestly as you can whether it's a good objection in its own right, or whether it only looks new. If after genuinely trying you think the list is complete, say so and defend that. No bibliography, no name-drops, just reasoning.
