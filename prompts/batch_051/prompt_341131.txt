Critics of natural selection have long charged that the principle of the survival of the fittest is an empty tautology: if the fittest organisms are simply the ones that survive and reproduce most, then the statement that the fittest survive and reproduce most says nothing about the world. The standard reply, as I understand it, is the propensity interpretation of fitness, according to which fitness is not actual reproductive success but a probabilistic disposition to survive and reproduce in a particular environment. What I cannot see is how this helps. If fitness is a disposition to reproduce, then is it not just the organism's expected reproductive output under some probability distribution? And if so, is not the claim that organisms with higher fitness tend to leave more offspring true by definition? The tautology seems to have been moved rather than removed: instead of saying that the fittest are the ones who in fact reproduce most, we now say that the fittest are the ones disposed to reproduce most. Where does the empirical content of natural selection come from once fitness is defined this way, and how does the propensity interpretation actually answer the original objection rather than merely relabel it?
