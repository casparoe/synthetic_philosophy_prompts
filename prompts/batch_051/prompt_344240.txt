I'm teaching a graduate seminar on what's real in physical theories, and I keep running into a move that I can't decide is a genuine argument or a bit of philosophical sleight of hand. The move goes like this. In electromagnetism you can add the gradient of any smooth scalar to the vector potential A, while making the matching time-derivative change to the scalar potential, and the E and B fields don't change at all. Physicists and more than a few philosophers of physics then say: guage freedom is just like choosing coordinates. If you relabel the coordinates, a tensor's components change but the underlying geometry doesn't; the changing components are just artifacts of the description. By the same reasoning, the potentials are surplus structure, and only the gauge-invariant quantities — the electric and magnetic fields, or more precisely the field-strength tensor — are real. I've heard lecturers and colleagues use exactly this analogy to close the question of what's real in a gauge theory.

I'm not so sure it should close it. Coordinates are a passive relabeling of the very same points, and nobody thinks switching from polar to Cartesian reshuffles the world. A gauge transformation is not obviously the same sort of thing: it acts on an internal space sitting over each point, and the potential can leave a gauge-invariant trace in loop integrals. The usual story about the Aharonov–Bohm effect is that a charged particle can show a measurable phase shift even though the magnetic field is zero along its entire path, with the shift depending on the loop integral of the potential rather than on any local field the particle encounters. If that's even roughly right, then at least some of what the analogy treats as disposable description is doing physical work. I also wonder whether the analogy proves too much: E and B are themselves frame-dependent, since different inertial observers decompose the invariant electromagnetic field differently, but most of the same people don't conclude that E and B are therefore mere artifacts. So why should gauge dependence, rather than frame dependence, be the test for unreality?

I'd like a proper audit of the analogy. Set up the correspondence table — what corresponds to what — then the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion and the ones that don't, and say whether the conclusion, that the potentials are merely descriptive surplus, survives, needs weakening, or was being carried by the analogy all along. If there's a better analogy that does less damage, I'd want to know that too. And please keep track of what's actually settled in philosophy of physics here and what's genuinely contested, rather than presenting one side as the consensus.
