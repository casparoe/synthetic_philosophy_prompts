I am a novelist. In each of my novels I make, often without noticing, a series of decisions that determine whether a character's condition is presented as a disease, a moral failing, a neutral variation, or an intelligible response to circumstance; and these decisions quietly control what the reader is invited to blame, excuse, pity, or medicalize. I want to stop making them casually. I want a reusable procedure for making them while drafting, grounded in the philosophy of medicine's naturalism-versus-normativism debate about the concept of disease.

I understand that debate only in outline. Naturalists hold that disease can be defined in value-free terms, as a failure or impairment of functioning relative to the organism's design, so that whether a condition is a disease is a factual matter independent of our evaluation of it. Normativists hold that the concept is inescapably evaluative, so that calling something a disease always brings in judgments about harm, flourishing, and social norms. I am told there are also hybrid views that require both a dysfunction and a harm, and positions that deny there is a value-free notion of function available at all. I do not need a survey of these positions; I need the dispute converted into a procedure I can use without philosophical training, in under an hour, for a single character. I will use the procedure on every novel I draft, and my editor will expect me to be able to defend each decision in revision.

The procedure should be an ordered checklist or sequence of tests I can run on a character while drafting. It should keep apart the questions that the debate keeps apart — above all, whether something is a failure of some functioning and whether it is bad — and it should not let me quietly substitute my disapproval for evidence of dysfunction. It should say what to do when a condition passes some tests and fails others, and it should mark the exact steps at which the procedure gives out and the novelist's judgment, or the stance of the novel itself, has to do the work. I also need an honest warning about the cases in which the procedure will misfire or will return an answer I will resist, since I will be tempted to override it for the sake of the story.

Apply the procedure to the three characters I am currently drafting.

First, a woman whose gambling has cost her her marriage and savings; she is unimpaired in her work and friendships, is not distressed by the gambling, and does not wish to stop.

Second, a man whose wife died four years ago and whose grief is as acute now as on the first day; he works, eats, and sleeps, but has withdrawn from every other attachment and describes the grief as the only way he still has her.

Third, a teenager whose defiance and cruelty have gotten him expelled, but who lives in an environment in which exactly that hardness is what keeps him safe and keeps others from preying on him.

For each case, show what the procedure instructs, where it falls silent, what the novel's stance would add once the procedure has finished, and — for at least one case — a result the procedure produces that I will not like.
