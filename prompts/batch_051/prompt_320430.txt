Like much of the advice I have absorbed from rationalist-adjacent writing, the lesson of the sunk-cost fallacy sounds simple: costs that are already gone should be let go. But when I apply that advice to concrete cases of escalation of commitment, my verdicts do not sit under one rule, and I want to know whether a clean principle can be stated.

Here are the cases as I see them.

(1) The nearly finished software system. A project has absorbed $8 million and is still not done. Completing it would cost another $1 million, and the finished system would be worth $2 million to the business. My verdict: finish it. The $8 million is gone either way; the marginal $1 million buys $2 million.

(2) The same system with worse numbers. Everything is as in (1), except the final $1 million would buy something worth only $500,000 in the best case, and the team has equally good alternatives. My verdict: kill it without regret, even though that means admitting in public that the $8 million was lost. I do not think the size of the already spent sum should move me at all between (1) and (2); only the future numbers differ.

(3) The PhD. A student has spent five years in a PhD they no longer enjoy. One more year gets the degree, and the degree genuinely opens non-academic paths they want to pursue. My verdict: finish, but only because the final year buys the credential. If we remove the career benefit and hold everything else fixed, I say quit despite the five years. The five years count for nothing on their own.

(4) The classic dollar auction. Two bidders, a one-dollar prize, both leading bidders pay, open ascending bids. Once the auction passes one dollar, each bidder faces a choice between losing a smaller amount for nothing and losing a larger amount while getting the dollar, so each raise looks locally better than folding. My verdict: the error is entering without a binding precommitment to stop, not the final overbid considered by itself. Winning at $1.50 is not a coup; it is the predictable endpoint of a structure that makes even the winner lose.

(5) The undervalued asset variant. Same auction mechanics, but both bidders know the object can be resold for $1.50 because a buyer is already waiting. Now bids can pass one dollar and I feel no fallacy at all, until they pass the point where continuing no longer has positive expected value, at which point the pathology returns. The difference from case (4) seems to lie entirely in the value of the prize, not in anything about the bidders' psychology.

(6) The costly retaliation. A government has publicly and deliberately committed to punishing defectors, and the threat has kept the peace. A defection occurs; carrying out the punishment is now expensive, and the immediate damage is already done, but everyone is watching whether the commitment is kept. My verdict: follow through. This is not throwing good money after bad; the cost buys credibility in later rounds, and the original commitment was rational because the threat deterred.

(7) The founder's public vow. A founder has promised employees and investors, repeatedly, that the company will not abandon a certain product line. The line is failing; continuing costs real money and the product probably will not recover. Abandoning it saves money but damages the founder's credibility for future announcements. My verdict: this can be rational, but it is much less clearly rational than case (6), and I suspect many real instances of it are self-deception wearing case (6)'s clothes. I cannot state, in general, where (6) and (7) come apart.

(8) The counterinsurgency. A campaign has cost two thousand lives. Staying costs still more lives and has only a small chance of leaving things better than withdrawing. Withdrawing will be publicly read as letting the dead die for nothing, and it will damage the state's reputation. My verdict: withdraw anyway. The dead are sunk, and dying more to rescue the meaning of the dead is the fallacy at scale. But I admit this case weakens my confidence: it looks like case (6) in every structural particular except scale, and I am not sure scale is supposed to matter to the principle.

My tentative principle is this: past costs count only through their effects on future payoffs, such as assets, credibility, information, or options. If continuing buys nothing in the future, spent resources count for exactly zero, no matter how large or how public they were. The problem is that this principle can be made to fit almost anything, because credibility can be invoked for any instance of doubling down, and I worry it collapses into counting sunk costs whenever I feel like it. Cases (6) and (8) are the sharp version of that problem: both involve a public commitment and a reputation at stake, and yet I want to follow through in the one and withdraw in the other. Cases (1) and (2) are the clean version: I am sure the spent money is irrelevant and the future payoffs do all the work.

So I want a committed ruling, not a menu. Give me the best available principle, or a tight set of candidates you would jointly endorse, and show precisely how it handles each case above. Tell me which of my verdicts it overturns and why those are the ones to go. Then apply it to cases I have not settled: a person who stays in a long queue mainly because leaving would make the wait wasted; two firms in a price war that have each publicly said they will not cave; a friendship maintained largely out of shared history; and a bidder who, halfway through a dollar auction, tells the room he will not lose. I would rather have a principle that is honestly wrong about some of my verdicts than one that is vague enough to appear to fit them all.
