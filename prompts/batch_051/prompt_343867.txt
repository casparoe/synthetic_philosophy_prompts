I am a retired natural scientist, and since taking up philosophy in retirement the habit from my old trade that has most shaped my reading is the discipline of separating a physical situation from the redundancy of its description: a gauge choice, a coordinate system, a way of labelling identical particles. I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute session for a small circle of retired scientists and engineers; a few of them once took undergraduate mathematics, but none has worked with Galois theory, and I should not presuppose anything beyond quadratic equations. The single idea I want the session to transmit is that the two roots of an equation such as x² − 2 = 0 carry no canonical labels between them — the notation '√2' and '−√2' records a choice of embedding or ordering rather than a difference available before that choice — and that this absence of a canonical choice is not an inconvenience to be brushed aside, because the permitted relabellings, the automorphisms of the situation, the permutations of the roots that preserve every algebraic relation over the rationals, are themselves non-arbitrary structure, rather as a symmetry group can carry physics even when the coordinates in which we write it do not.

Don't explain the idea to me; plan how I should lead the session. I want the order of ideas and what depends on what; the one carrying example and a defence of it, including whether the pair i and −i is the right example or whether it quietly encourages the thought that everything is already settled; the misconceptions the group will bring or form, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off — I suspect at least '√2 is the positive one, so it is canonical' and 'if the labels are arbitrary, nothing about the roots is objective'; what I should openly set aside as too technical for this audience; and one closing question whose answer would show me whether they have separated a redundant description from an absence of content. Please give no reading lists.

Lead with the bottom line of your proposed session in the first sentence — the one sentence you would most want the group to leave with — and then give your reasoning, without preamble and without restating my brief.
