Ok so I'm a PhD student working on the philosophy of machine learning, specifically the question of whether LLMs can make genuine intuitive leaps beyond their training distribution. I got into a debate with my advisor about whether the old Lovelace objection still has any teeth. You know the one—Ada Lovelace says the Analytical Engine has no pretensions to originate anything, that it can only do whatever we know how to order it to perform. That's usually read as an early version of "machines can't be creative, they only follow instructions."

But I've been going back through her Notes to Menabrea's paper and found a passage in Note A that I think reframes the whole thing. She writes:

"The science of operations, as derived from mathematics more especially, is a science of itself, and has its own abstract truth and value; just as logic has its own peculiar truth and value, independently of the subjects to which we may apply its reasonings and processes."

So here's what I'm trying to figure out. Lovelace's original argument seems to rest on a premise that the operations are explicitly known and ordered by the programmer. In a modern system trained on a huge corpus, the operations aren't explicitly programmed; they're learned as a byproduct of minimizing a prediction loss. But maybe the distinction she draws between operations and subjects can be used to repair the argument: even if the operations are learned, they still constitute a distinct causal structure from the data, and the system never encounters "operations" apart from their expression in subjects. So my question: does Lovelace's objection survive the shift from explicit programming to learned models? Which premise is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still important about novelty beyond the training distribution? I'm not looking for a yes/no on machine creativity so much as a careful analysis of what the argument needs in order to bite.
