I'm teaching an upper-level epistemology seminar — around 14 students, 75 minutes — and before I give them any readings on foundationalism, coherentism, or infinitism, I want to run an activity that makes them feel the Münchhausen trilemma instead of just hearing me lay it out. Last time I tried to explain it, everyone nodded along and then said something like "well, eventually you just hit the facts, right?," so the lecture clearly didn't take. I want the three exits — infinite regress, circularity, and a dogmatic stop — to show up as things they do in the activity, not terms I write on the board afterwards.

So: design a runnable 75-minute activity. I'm imagining a structured justification game where one or two students have to defend a claim they actually believe (or one I assign, if their own beliefs are to messy), and a designated skeptic keeps demanding a reason for the reason, with actual rules — maybe: every previously used claim is off-limits once challenged, "that's just obvious" is not an allowed move, and if you get caught repeating yourself or stalling you lose a point or a token or somthing. But I'm open to a different shape if you think it works better. I'd want roles, rules, a timeline, any materials, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto regress, circularity, and dogmatic stopping, and that connects each exit to at least one live position in epistemology (foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism) without making those positions feel like the "right answer." Also be honest about what the activity can't capture. My worry is it might make the trilemma feel like a merely practical fact about conversations running out of time or patience, when the philosophers version is supposed to be about the structure of reasons themselves — so tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that.
