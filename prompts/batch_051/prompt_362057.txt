Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals in the Essays starts from what sounds like a relativist principle: he says, roughly, that the Tupinambá should not be called barbarous just because their ways differ from ours, since all people call barbarism whatever is not their own practice, and we have no other standard of truth and reason than the example and idea of the opinions and customs of the country we grew up in. That's a paraphrase, not a quotation to check.

I want to see if that criterion can actually be applied rather than just used as a slogan.

Consider this borderline case. A country in the near future develops a funeral rite in which cells taken from the deceased while alive are used to grow a small amount of edible tissue, which the mourners share in a highly ritualized meal. The tissue is not from the corpse itself, no one is killed, and participants describe the practice as honoring the dead by taking them back into the community. Critics call it cannibalism and say it is barbarous. Supporters say its no more cannibalism than a mother eating her placenta, and that the disgust outsiders feel is just custom.

Apply Montaigne's stated criterion — barbarism is whatever is not our own practice, because we have no other test of truth and reason than our own customs — to this case. Work through exactly what counts as "our practice" when the practice is new and contested even inside the country, which customs are doing the judging, and whether the case qualifies as barbarous under Montaigne's standard.
