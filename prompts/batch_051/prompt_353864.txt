I've been defending something I call the 'confidence tax' and I want an honest read on it before I get too attached. The idea is that evidence about your own reasoning works completely differently from evidence about the world. When you find out your first-order reasoning might have been off—you were tired, you misread a table, the topic is one where you're overconfident—the rational response is never to go back and re-litigate the first-order reasons. It's to take the confidence you ended up with and apply a flat discount to it, the way a tax is applied to income rather than to the hours you worked. So if I'm at .9 after reading the study, and then I learn I'm bad at reading that kind of study, I don't reopen the study; I just drop to, say, .75 and keep moving, everytime. The size of the discount comes from how unreliable I think the whole process was, not from which specific part of the reasoning the new evidence impugns. I actually think this is the only way to update on higher-order evidence without falling into epistemic akrasia, because first-order evidence is the only coin you can use to change your mind about the world, and evidence about you isn't that kind of coin—it can only shrink what you've already got, never add or reweigh. Somebody must have said this already, right? Tell me whether this is a real position, whether the normative version is defensible, and the strongest case where it gives the wrong answer. Also tell me what would change your mind about it: which argument or kind of case would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Answer in American English.
