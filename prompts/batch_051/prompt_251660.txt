I have been turning over an idea I first hit upon while arguing with a friend about paying people for organ donation, and then again when I read a news story about a day care that started fining parents who picked their children up late. At first I assumed you could just think of money as one more incentive to be added to the pile, but I have come to suspect that putting a price on certain activities changes the activity itself: a kidney given freely is a gift, a kidney sold is a commodity, and a fine may turn a guilty, apologetic lateness into a purchased service. The same would seem to go for votes, blood, and other things we usually treat as matters of duty or generosity. My worry is that this remains just an intuition, and I would like to know whether it can actually be tested rather than endlessly asserted.

Please take my claim, that introducing payment into a practice that was previously governed by duty, generosity, or civic obligation tends to alter the meaning of the act rather than merely adding an incentive, and design the strongest test of it that you can. Tell me concretely what you would measure, what groups or situations you would compare, and what pattern of results would persuade you that the claim is true and what pattern would persuade you it is false. Then be strict about what the test cannot settle: which parts of the claim can be captured by evidence, and which parts are a matter of interpretation, such as whether altered meaning amounts to corruption or loss, or whether a finding from one practice would carry over to another as different as kidney donation and day care pickup. I would like your answer in continuous prose only, as if you were writing me a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
