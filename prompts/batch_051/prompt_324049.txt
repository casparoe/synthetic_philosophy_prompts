I'm a second-year PhD student in political science, and I keep running into a pattern in seminars that I don't have a name for. The setup is always the same. Someone lays out a chain of reasoning—sometimes a formal model, sometimes a careful argument in moral or political philosophy—whose starting assumptions are individually plausible and whose steps are hard to fault. The reasoning then lands on a conclusion that almost everyone in the room finds unacceptable: a familiar institution turns out to be unjust, a widely used rule turns out to be irrational, a person in a contrived example turns out to be blameworthy or blameless against every prior judgment. At that point the discussion splits down a predictable seam. One side treats the unacceptable conclusion as a decisive objection: since the conclusion cannot be right, something in the innocent-looking assumptions or steps must be wrong, even if nobody can yet say what. The other side treats it the opposite way: the argument worked, our prior judgments were inconsistent, and we should revise them, however uncomfortable that is. What unsettles me is that the two sides seem to be applying opposite rules about where the burden of proof falls, and neither side ever puts its rule on the table.

Two recent examples may make the pattern clearer. In a discussion of fair allocation, a friend showed that a principle we had all endorsed in simple cases—something about treating equal claims equally—implied, in a contrived case, that it would be just to leave a badly injured person untreated while using the same resources to cure several people with minor complaints. Half the room said the example revealed that our principle was actually monstrous; the other half said the example revealed that our "obviously just" reaction was simply inconsistent with what we had already accepted. In another discussion, a formal voting model entailed that a rule we defended would, for a particular pattern of preferences, select the candidate everyone ranked last. Again, some treated this as a refutation of the model, and others treated it as a refutation of our informal confidence in the rule.

I would like you to answer as an expert in epistemology and the methodology of philosophy and social science. What I want to know is whether this kind of standoff—the question of when a firm pre-theoretic judgment should be allowed to veto an otherwise compelling chain of reasoning, and when it should instead give way—has an established name or literature. I am not satisfied with a bare label, and a name for just one side's move is not enough. Please start by brainstorming the different things people might be doing when they say "that just can't be right" in response to a well-argued conclusion. Then walk me through the nearest established concepts, identify how each differs from what I have described, and say which fits best. If nothing fits well, propose a term of your own, define it clearly, and give boundary cases that show where the concept ends. Please write your answer in American English.
