I'm teaching my first intro stats class, and last week I used a kidney-stone example to warn students against averaging percentages. Then a student pushed back and I ended up, half by accident, defending an argument that I now can't decide is actually flawed or just feels wrong.

Imagine a trial of a new treatment against a control, run in two clinics: one handles mostly severe cases, the other mostly mild cases. The data: (1) In severe-clinic patients, the new treatment succeeds 30% of the time while the control succeeds 20%. (2) In mild-clinic patients, the new treatment succeeds 90% while the control succeeds 80%. (3) So the treatment does better in each clinic: better among the severe and better among the mild. (4) But the whole study population just is the severe-clinic patients plus the mild-clinic patients; there's no third kind of patient. (5) Therefore the treatment must do better overall, because it's better in every part and the whole is nothing but the parts.

I know this is where Simpson's paradox is supposed to bite, but each step looks fine to me on it's own. Is the argument valid, and if not, exactly which numbered step fails? How would you rewrite that step so the conclusion, or the nearest true conclusion, actually follows? And separately, tell me what would change your mind about this: what extra condition or piece of data would make the argument go through, and where is your own diagnosis most likely to fail?
