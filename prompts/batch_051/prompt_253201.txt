I write about AI and cognitive science for a magazine, and I keep catching myself and my sources in a weird double standard. When a model gets a task right, people quote its chain-of-thought as if it were a window into the model's actual reasoning: it checked the constraints, noticed a shortcut, then solved. When a model gets the same kind of task wrong, the same people say the chain-of-thought is just generated text, maybe confabulated, and tells you nothing about what happened inside. So the same kind of output is treated as a faithful self-report when the answer lands right and as noise when it doesn't. I want you to build the strongest case that this asymmetry is principled. Then stress-test that case: a model can reach the right answer through wrong steps, and one can reason almost perfectly and slip at the end. Meanwhile a student who gets a physics problem wrong but describes her flawed steps is still treated as reporting how she was thinking, not as spinning a story. At bottom this seems to be about whether a language model can report its own reasoning at all. Is the asymmetry defensible, or is it outcome bias? Tell me wether you'd keep it, reverse it, or give it up.
