I sit on the scientific advisory board of a municipal health department. We must decide this month whether to advise the health commissioner to expand street-tree planting as a heat-health intervention, on the premise that tree canopy itself reduces heat-related emergency calls. The evidence is not experimental. The city's data science unit learned a causal Bayes net from administrative data: block-level tree canopy, heat-related call volume, income, age, tree maintenance records, and several other variables. The search procedure selected the sparsest graph satisfying the Markov condition for the observed distribution, and the selected model contains a directed edge from tree canopy to lower heat-related calls. That edge is the reason the proposal is before us. There is no measured confounder on it, but the skeptics on the board point out that a model with an unmeasured confounder — plausibly, neighborhood investment — was available and was passed over because it was less sparse, not because the data ruled it out. A staff statistician adds that the orientation of the edge is not forced by the conditional-independence constraints: the skeleton is well supported, but other members of the same Markov equivalence class leave its direction unresolved.

The board is split. One faction says this is exactly how minimal Bayes nets earn their keep: among the models that preserve the observed independencies, the simplest is the one to believe and act on. The other faction says minimality and faithfulness are the very assumptions under dispute: faithfulness can fail when distinct causal paths cancel one another, and there is no good reason to expect an administrative dataset to be free of latent common causes. Both factions agree we cannot wait for a randomized trial before the heat-season work begins.

Draft the board's decision memo to the health commissioner. It must have an unambiguous bottom line — issue the recommendation now, reject it, or issue it with conditions — and it must do three things. First, say exactly what minimality and faithfulness are doing in this inference, what must hold for the learned edge to be causal, and what the data could not establish even if both assumptions held. Second, distinguish the assumptions that are defensible methodological defaults from the assumptions that are least plausible for city data assembled for administrative purposes. Third, state the strongest consideration on the losing side in terms its proponents would accept. The memo should treat the question as one about when a formal model licenses action, not merely as a dispute about statistics.
