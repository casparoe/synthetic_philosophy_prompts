I've been going back over the moral deference debates after a conversation, and I realize my position keeps changing depending on which case I think about first. So I'd like someone to run a consistency audit on the whole set, because I cant tell which of these are real conflicts and which just look like conflicts from a distance.

(1) I don't think you should adopt a moral view on someone else's say-so. If a friend I consider smart and morally reliable tells me a proposed water-pricing scheme is unjust, I don't just update. I want the underlying reasons, and until I have them my old view stays roughly put. (2) But I'm perfectly happy to update on the non-moral ingredients from the same friend. If he tells me the scheme would fall hardest on households that can't afford the upfront meter cost, that shifts me, because now my own moral reasoning is running on a different input. (3) For ordinary empirical testimony I have no such scruples: I'll accept my doctor's antibiotic recommendation without understanding the pharmacology, and I'll believe a physicist about the properties of a particle I've never heard of. (4) At the same time, I hold that the fact/value gap is overstated—moral inquiry and empirical inquiry look to me like the same kind of corrective sense-making, both evidence-responsive, both fallible, neither answerable to a special realm of non-natural facts. (5) But I can't shake the feeling that my moral views have to be *mine* in a way my factual views don't. I'm fine acting on a pharmacological belief I couldn't reconstruct; I'm not fine acting on a moral belief I couldn't reconstruct. (6) And yet in hard cases I do drift a few percentage points when someone who has genuinely spent years in a moral domain gives a confident verdict I didn't expect, even without sharing their reasoning—like a person who's worked on triage ethics telling me a scarcity policy is more of a rights violation than I'd clocked. Moving 5 or 10 percent feels fine; flipping outright feels less fine. (7) On disagreement: I'm a conciliationist about empirical peer disagreement—if a cognitive peer with the same evidence disagrees about a factual matter, I move toward her. But if a cognitive peer disagrees with me about a moral question we've both thought hard about, I don't feel the same pressure. It seems fine to mostly stay put.

So is this package consistent? Where are the actual stress points, which pairs just look tense but aren't, and what's the cheapest repair, with what costs? I'm not wedded to any particular item; I just want to know if im quietly helping myself to a fact/value distinction in (5) and (7) that (4) rules out, or whether (6) is already a small contradiction with (1).
