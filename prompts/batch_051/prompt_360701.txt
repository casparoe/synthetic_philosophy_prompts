I just got back from a walk where a friend and I spent a stupid amount of time arguing about Moore's paradox, and now I'm thinking of turning my side of the argument into a short essay. He basically thinks the whole thing is one phenomenon and I was trying to split it in two, but I'm not sure my split survives. I'd like comments on the notes below before I sink more time into the full thing—mostly I want to know where a referee would press hardest, and whether my answer to his best objection actually works.

The distinction I want to hang the essay on is between an assertion case and a mental case. In the assertion case, someone tells you it's raining and then adds that they don't believe it's raining. That's odd, but I think it's odd in a kind of uninteresting way: an assertion normally signals that the speaker believes the asserted content, so the second bit undercuts the first. The content itself isn't contradictory—p and not-believing-p can both be true at once—and a third person can describe the same situation without any weirdness (he's saying it's raining, but he doesn't believe it). So the assertion case seems to be a problem about the act of asserting, not about belief itself.

The mental case is when someone takes it to be raining while also taking themselves not to believe it's raining. My friend kept saying this is just the assertion case run internally, because inner speech is still a kind of act. But I don't think that's right. The tension can be there without anyone saying anything to themselves. Imagine using the proposition that it's raining in one piece of planning while, on reflection, finding no belief about the weather in yourself at all. That seems wierd in a way that isn't about speech acts, public or inner. So I want to claim there's a distinct doxastic version of the paradox.

The best objection he kept hitting me with: if belief comes in degrees, the omissive version is only weird because we're treating belief as binary. Someone with credence .9 that it's raining can properly say it's raining for practical purposes while denying flat-out belief if their threshold for flat-out belief is higher. If that works, the paradox is just a artifact of a binary vocabulary. My reply was supposed to be that the paradox doesn't disappear, it just moves up a level—something like rain being .95 likely while one also lacks high credence in rain should also be weird. But now I'm honestly not sure the graded analogue is weird in the same way, rather than just a boring point about thresholds.

So, comments I'd actually use: (1) Is the split between the assertion version and the mental version deeply real, or can everything in the mental case be explained by speech-act norms after all? (2) Does the graded-belief objection dissolve the assertoric paradox but leave a residue in the mental case, or does it dissolve both? (3) Does my "it just moves up a level" reply work, and if not, what's the strongest salvage of it? Also, if the whole essay strikes you as a "this is a non-problem" essay, just say that—I'd rather not spend a week making a boring point.
