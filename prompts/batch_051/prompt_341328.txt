I'm putting together a session for a reading group on self-locating belief and I want to use Groundhog Day as the film case, mostly because everyone in the group has seen it and it's plot is common property. The obvious move is to say Phil Connors' looping February 2nd is basically the Sleeping Beauty problem played for comedy: every morning he wakes up in the same centered world, everyone else has reset, and the question is where he is in the sequence of wake-ups. But that feels off to me and I can't quite articulate why. Phil isnt amnesic — he remembers every prior iteration, so on any given waking he usually knows it's Feb 2 and roughly how many loops he has lived through. Sleeping Beauty, on the other hand, can't tell Monday from Tuesday and has to decide whether her evidence that she is awake now should push her credence in Heads below one half. So I suspect the neat analogy is doing quite a lot of work by ignoring the feature that makes Sleeping Beauty a problem in the first place: the awakenings are indistinguishable. Phil's memory makes his distinguishable, so the self-locating uncertainty might just be gone. I'm also curious whether the film transfers the self-locating uncertainty to us in the audience instead: we see an apparently arbitrary sample of iterations, and we don't know how many there are, whether they're selected the way Sleeping Beauty's awakenings are, or what the analog of the coin toss would even be (whether the loop eventually ends? whether some real Feb 3 exists?).

I'd like a careful reading that doesn't reduce the film to an illustration of the problem. Where does the analogy hold, where does it break, and does the movie actually pose a live self-locating question to Phil, to the viewer, or to neither? If you were going to use Groundhog Day to teach the halfer/thirder dispute anyway, which moments or scenes would you point people to, and which tempting things would you warn them not to say? Please end by telling me what would most change your verdict: which detail of the formal problem, or of the film, would make you flip to the other reading, and where is your reading most likely to fail.
