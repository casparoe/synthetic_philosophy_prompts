I am a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience, and our methods reading group has been circling around a proposal to make heterophenomenology something more than a philosophical stance—namely, a standing publication policy for consciousness research. The proposed rule runs roughly as follows. Any paper that makes a claim of the form "the subject was (or was not) conscious of X" or "processing occurred with (or without) awareness" must include, before its main interpretation, a subject's-world reconstruction: a neutral account of what the world would have to be like for the subject's verbal reports and other intentional behavior to make sense, explicitly marked as a fiction about the subject rather than a transparent transcription of their phenomenal life. Reports can be quoted and explained, but they may never be cited as direct evidence for any conscious content. Any conscious state the paper posits must be earned through third-person instruments—dissociation logic, forced-choice performance, signal-detection parameters, neural markers—and the reconstruction itself is then treated as one more body of data to be explained. Journals would enforce this with a mandatory section, and reviewers would be instructed to ask, for every claimed awareness effect, "Would this conclusion survive if the report were confabulated?" Grant committees would require preliminary evidence to be presented in the same format.

I want to understand how this policy would actually behave if it were genuinely enforced, not whether it is right in principle. What experiments become easier or harder to publish under the rule—blindsight, masking, change blindness, pain, afterimages, lucid dreaming, binocular rivalry? How would labs adapt? Would they collect reports in more constrained ways, relabel subjective scales as behavioral tasks, recruit subjects whose reports are sparse and predictable, or write reconstructions so permissive that any finding can be squared with any report? Would the field drift toward response-bias and signal-detection talk for everything, and would that be a change in methods or just in rhetoric? Where does the system get gamed first, and by whom—authors, reviewers, or journals themselves? And is there a repair that preserves the methodological discipline without quietly turning consciousness science into behaviorism?
