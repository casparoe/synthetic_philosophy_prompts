I'm a PhD student in AI safety and I've been going back and forth with my advisor about the "control" versus "alignment" distinction — not the definitions, but what the field actually looks like if you're forced to pick one. So here's a counterfactual I want someone to work through carefully.

Suppose that starting in 2030 a binding international agreement, enforced about as well as nuclear nonproliferation norms (so mostly but not perfectly), prohibits any training or fine-tuning whose stated purpose is to shape a frontier model's goals, values, or motivations. No RLHF, no constitutional AI, no preference shaping meant to make the model want to be cooperative rather than merely capable of cooperation. Labs can still pretrain and do instruction-tuning for capabilities, and can do whatever they like below some capability threshold. Above that threshold, every model has to be treated as potentially misaligned and can only be deployed with control-style measures: sandboxing, no long-term memory across sessions, monitored tool use, steganography checks, tripwire honeypots, and human approval for consequential actions. The treaty's operating assumption is not "we made this model safe" but "we can't trust it, and we're watching."

Predict how this plays out over the next twenty years. I want the chain of reasoning, not a story. Which kinds of work still get automated under a control-only regime, and which kinds don't? How do labs and customers adapt — do they break tasks into small supervised pieces, accept slower workflows, or quietly route around the rules? Do we end up with more or fewer serious misalignment incidents than under an alignment-heavy regime, and why? Does the regime push labs toward more capable models (because control lets you supervise stronger models) or less capable ones (because sandboxing overhead grows with capability), and does capability progress speed up, slow down, or stay about the same? Where is the whole thing most likely to break down — weak enforcement, insider misuse, agents with too much autonomy under the threshold, or control measures that don't scale to superhuman models? And flag which of your predictions you're confident in and which turn on assumptions you're unsure about, like whether alignment methods were doing much real work or whether steganography detection can actually keep up.
