I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working through a piece about long-run forecasts that miss their dates but still seem to contain a live mechanism. I've been reading Malthus's 1798 *An Essay on the Principle of Population*, and this passage from chapter 1 has stayed with me:

"The most important argument that I shall adduce is certainly not new. The principles on which it depends have been explained in part by Hume, and more at large by Dr Adam Smith. It has been advanced and applied to the present subject, though not with its proper weight, or in the most forcible point of view, by Mr Wallace, and it may probably have been stated by many writers that I have never met with. I should certainly therefore not think of advancing it again, though I mean to place it in a point of view in some degree different from any that I have hitherto seen, if it had ever been fairly and satisfactorily answered."

I want to treat Malthus's claim as a live philosophical question about theory evaluation. Malthus did not claim novelty; he claimed that a known argument had never been "fairly answered." Two centuries later, he is often treated as falsified because the crisis he predicted did not arrive on the schedule implied by his arithmetic. But "falsified" and "never fairly answered" are different accusations.

My question: What is your actual credence that the core Malthusian constraint—roughly, that finite resource flows impose a real check on the long-run expansion of human population and consumption, a check that operates through some combination of scarcity, rising costs, conflict, or deliberate restraint—will produce at least one recognizable crisis of Malthusian form in a major world region within the next two hundred years, absent a substantial planned transformation of the economy? I'm not asking whether Malthus's specific ratios were right. I'm asking whether the underlying claim was "right but early."

Please decompose your credence. In particular, assign rough probabilities to:
- the claim that energy and material substitutability can postpone the constraint indefinitely under current institutions;
- the claim that the demographic transition is a permanent escape rather than a temporary regime;
- the claim that a "recognizable crisis" is a coherent enough category for this question, rather than a rhetorical artifact;
- the claim that environmental and climate feedbacks will function as the modern positive checks;
- the claim that the theory becomes unscientific if its failures are always absorbed by "early" rather than "wrong."

Then combine them and say which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence would move you most.
