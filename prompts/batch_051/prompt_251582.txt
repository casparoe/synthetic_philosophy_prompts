I help run a reading group in legal philosophy that has just spent several weeks on Bentham's critique of the French revolutionaries' declaration of rights. For our end-of-term satirical broadsheet I would like the centerpiece to be a Swiftian modest proposal of about 700 words, written as a memorandum to the Home Office by an invented Benthamite civil servant. The proposal should argue for the creation of a General Registry of Rights: any claim calling itself a right must be certified before it may be invoked in public debate, and certification requires either a statute that creates the right or a demonstration that recognizing it increases aggregate utility. Claims described as natural, imprescriptible, or inalienable are to be returned with a form letter explaining that a right without a law to back it is no right at all, and that the applicant has mistaken a reason for wishing for a right; the letters should carry the spirit of Bentham's dismissal of natural rights as nonsense upon stilts. The tone should be deadpan and bureaucratic, with the reductio emerging gradually: the reader should feel the unease of rights becoming revocable licenses without the author ever winking at the joke.

The humor has to come from getting Bentham right rather than from making the author a generic bean-counter. Work in his actual positions: that rights are the creatures of law and cannot exist before government; that natural-rights language is vague and substitutes intuition for calculation; and that declaring imprescriptible natural rights is dangerous because it teaches people to set their own convictions against enacted law. A specialist who knows the text should recognize the arguments and find the satire fair, while a reader who has never read Bentham should still follow what is being sent up. Please append a note of about 150 words indicating where the piece exaggerates Bentham's position or presses a contested reading of it; in that note, separate what Bentham plainly says from what is interpretive dispute, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
