Someone proposes that a forecasting-and-debate forum adopts what the mods call the common-prior rule. The mods' pitch is just the standard result about common priors: if two ideal Bayesian reasoners start from the same prior and their current credences become common knowledge, they can't keep disagreeing, so any lingering gap has to mean one of them is irrational, lying about their stated credence, or never really accepted the prior. The rule tries to turn that into a moderation device.

Here's the setup. Any time two users are about to argue over a factual or predictive question, they first have to jointly post a prior—a probability distibution over the main hypotheses the thread could be about—and both of them have to sign it. If they can't agree on a prior within 48 hours, the thread doesn't happen and both users eat a small reputation ding. Once a signed prior exists, every substantive comment from either disputant has to include a number: their current credence in the hypotheses. A bot records the two credence histories and publishes updates to the thread, so each disputant's credence, and the fact that the other has seen it, is common knowledge. Either disputant can then invoke the theorem: after both have posted in view of each other's latest credence, if the two credences still differ by more than, say, five points, both users are suspended for a week—regardless of who's closer to the truth. The only out is that either disputant can amend the signed prior and keep arguing, but each amendment costs posting privileges and gives the other person an upvote multiplier in the thread.

I want to know how this would actually play out, not whether it's fair in the abstract. What does it incentivize for someone who genuinely wants their mind changed, for someone who just wants to look right to the audience, and for someone who wants to get a rival suspended? Where is it gamed first, and does the amendment escape hatch make the gaming better or worse? In particular, does the rule mostly make people's stated credences converge while their real beliefs stay put, or can it actually force real updates? And can it distinguish people who really do share a prior but have different information from people gaming it by writing vague priors, or by subdividing the hypotheses so finely that the prior barely constrains anything?
