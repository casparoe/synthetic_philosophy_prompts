I’m a software architect by training; I spend my time writing API schemas, invariants, and failure-handling paths, and I read no philosophy. Lately I keep meeting Bentham’s dismissal of natural rights — in my phrasing, he says the doctrine is nonsense propped up on stilts — and I want to know whether my instinctive reading of it is right. I gather that in his Anarchical Fallacies Bentham attacks the French Declaration of the Rights of Man by saying that terms like natural rights, imprescriptible rights, and inalienable rights are declared without any specification of what they are, what they require, or how conflicts between them are resolved; that they are not backed by any law or institution; and that, precisely because they are so indeterminate, whoever appeals to them can justify whatever they already want, including resistance to every actual legal system. To me this sounds exactly like a downstream system that exposes a required field called rights with no type, no possible values, no validation rule, and no error semantics, and whose consumers each supply their own incompatible implementation and then blame the upstream system when the integration fails.

Please restate Bentham’s critique of natural rights systematically in the vocabulary I actually use — schemas, optional and required fields, null versus undefined, validation, contracts, invariants, API versioning, deprecation, undefined behavior, adversarial inputs, and the difference between a specification and an implementation. Then give me the residue report: which parts of Bentham’s argument transfer cleanly, which transfer only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary. In particular I want to know whether the fact that I keep wanting to ask “who writes the schema?” already imports Bentham’s own legal-positivist answer, and whether anything in his view of law’s coercive enforcement is lost in the translation. Around 900 words.
