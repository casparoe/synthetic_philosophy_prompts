In my psych stats course we've been on the replication crisis and p-hacking for weeks, and there's one asymmetry my TA kind of hand-waved past that I can't get out of my head. Two labs run literally the same experiment: same measures, same population, and, by coincidence, the exact same final dataset. Lab A decides in advance to collect 100 participants, collects them, and reports p = .04. Lab B doesn't pre-register; it peeks at the data every 20 participants and plans to stop the first time p drops below .05. Nothing is significant at 20, 40, 60, or 80; at n = 100 it finally is, so Lab B stops and reports the exact same p = .04. Same data, same numbers, same conclusion. Any quantity that depends only on the observed data — a Bayesian posterior, a likelihood, anything — has to be identical for the two labs. But nearly everyone treats Lab A's result as proper evidence and Lab B's as p-hacked and compromised, even though nothing in the data separates them. The only difference is what each lab would have done in histories of data that never actually happened, which feels like it should be irrelevant to what the actual data is telling us. So: is the practice justified in treating these two results differently? I want you to commit and defend one side. If you think yes, don't just say 'the sampling distribution differs' — explain why facts about unobserved possible outcomes should change how much we should believe the actual outcome. If you think no, tell me what that does to the whole case for pre-registration. I dont want a tour of positions; I want yours, with the strongest objection to it answered.
