I've developed a rule for when I read or listen to counterfactual history, and I'm wondering if it's actually defensible. The rule is: any claim that one small event "changed everything" gets discounted almost to zero, while claims that the broad outcome was overdetermined by structural forces start with a big advantage in my mind. So if someone says the First World War would still have happened even if Princip's bullet missed, I basically nod along; if someone says that missed bullet would have meant a very different twentieth century, I file it under storytelling. I can't actually run either experiment, but my default is that the structural story is the serious one and the hinge-event story is a just-so tale.

Last week this came up with a friend, and he called me out. He said I haven't actually studied the July Crisis in any depth, so my "structural" conclusion is just as under-evidenced as the butterfly-version I'm dismissing. His point was that my rule isn't a finding about history, it's a taste for big causes, and because "broad outcome" is such a vague phrase, my rule is basically unfalsifiable: almost anything that happens can be re-described as overdetermined, while the hinge-event person is at least making a specific claim that could fail. I'm not sure he's wrong, but I also think there is something genuinely suspicious about picking one contingent detail after the fact and narrating backwards from it.

Is my rule rationally defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a method? Is there a version of it that keeps whatever is good about it—the resistance to hindsight storytelling—without pretending I've done historical work I haven't done? And when you answer, please say what would change your mind: what kind of argument or evidence would actually establish that a single event was a genuine hinge rather than a detail, and where you think your answer is most likely to fail.
