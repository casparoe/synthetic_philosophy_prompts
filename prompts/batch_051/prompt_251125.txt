I've been reading about the prisoner's dilemma, and the standard argument that defecting is the only rational move in a one-shot game strikes me as unassailable in its ordinary form: the other player will either cooperate or defect, and in both cases I do better by defecting, so cooperation has to be explained by ignorance, altruism, or the shadow of future interaction. Yet I also cannot get rid of an intuition about a special case. Suppose the players choose, and can inspect in advance, not just actions but the full decision procedure each will run, and suppose further that both are running the identical procedure and know this. Then each can reason: whatever I conclude, the other will conclude, so if I cooperate, the other cooperates, and we both do better than if either of us defects. That makes cooperation look rational in precisely the kind of one-shot case the dominance argument says forbids it. I am not asking which side is right. I am asking what it would cost to keep the dominance argument and reject the intuition, what it would cost to keep the intuition and amend the argument, and whether there is a sense of the word rational in which both claims are true. Please write your answer in American English.
