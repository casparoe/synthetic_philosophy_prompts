I am taking a philosophy of mathematics course this term, and we have been working through what computational complexity might tell us about the nature of proof. The picture I find attractive is that a proof is a certificate: a finite string whose correctness can be checked by a mechanical, polynomial-time procedure, even when finding such a string is computationally intractable. A mathematical community can therefore verify a result that no individual member could realistically have discovered from scratch. Against that background, I have been trying to fix my own criteria for what counts as a proof. My working criteria are these: a proof must be (a) a finite object; (b) mechanically checkable step by step, with no appeal to intuition; (c) surveyable, meaning that a single qualified mathematician can hold the whole chain in view and see why the theorem follows; and (d) explanatory, meaning that it shows why the theorem is true rather than merely certifying that it is true.

The case I keep returning to is the 1976 Appel–Haken proof of the four-color theorem. The proof reduced the theorem to checking an enormous finite catalogue of reducible configurations, and that checking was performed by computer. No single human examined every case, and many mathematicians initially withheld full acceptance precisely because the proof could not be surveyed by hand. In 2005 the theorem was given a fully formal, machine-checked proof in the Coq proof assistant.

I would like a careful adjudication of whether these two proofs meet my criteria. Take each criterion in turn and say whether the Appel–Haken proof satisfies it, and whether the Coq formalization fares better. Where a criterion is not met, tell me whether that is a defect in the proof or a defect in the criterion. In particular, I want to know whether surveyability is doing real work or is only a historical accident of how proofs used to fit inside a single human head, and whether explanation is a genuine requirement or a desirable extra. What should I conclude from the episode: that my criteria need to be revised, or that some computer-certified results are better described as something other than proofs?
