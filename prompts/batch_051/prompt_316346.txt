I'm helping run an undergrad reading group on collective intentionality, and we keep hitting the same problem: people either assume a 'shared intention' is just a private intention with matching content, or they jump to a vague group-mind story, and they end up sorting themselves by which author they vibed with rather than by what they actually think. I want a sorting quiz / decision tree that sorts a reader into a view based on their reactions to concrete cases, with the views revealed only at the end. Use these cases as the fuel: (a) A and B are walking side by side to the station, A believes they are walking together and B is just walking to the same train; (b) two painters each intend to paint the house together and their subplans mesh so each is doing half the job; (c) two duet singers each intend to do their own part but neither thinks the other could hold them to it if they walked off; (d) two people each want the same outcome, say a department rule changed, but neither knows the other wants it; (e) one person abandons a joint project midway and the other feels wronged, not merely disappointed. Build the tree from the distinctions that actually do work here, e.g. whether a we-intention reduces to I-intentions plus some kind of interlock, whether common knowledge is required, whether joint commitment creates obligations and entitlements, and whether the content of the shared intention has to be identical for each participant. The leaves I want are roughly: (1) an individualist functionalist view on which shared intention is interlocking individual intentions plus common knowledge, (2) a view with irreducible we-intentions but no special obligations from joint commitment, (3) a Gilbert-style plural subject view on which joint commitment grounds normative relations, and (4) a deflationary view that adds only common knowledge to ordinary individual intentions. Phrase every node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate, and note unoccupied combinations of answers and whether they're unoccupied because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored.

Format: return a JSON object with fields: title, opening_prompt (the one-sentence instruction a student reads before starting), nodes (array of objects with id, question, yes_goes_to, no_goes_to, and distinction_tested), leaves (array of objects with id, position_name, one_paragraph_summary, and which_answer_combination_places_someone_here), unseparated_pairs (array of objects with leaf_pair and why_the_tree_cannot_separate_them), and unoccupied_combinations (array of objects with combination, why_the_view_is_absent, and what_case_might_make_it_occupied).
