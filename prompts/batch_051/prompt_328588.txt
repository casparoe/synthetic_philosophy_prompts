I've been taking a game theory class and I keep noticing a structural similarity between the backward induction argument in the centipede game and ordinary slippery-slope reasoning, and I can't figure out whether it's a deep point or just pattern-matching on the word "chain." In the centipede game, at the last node the player takes; knowing that, the previous player should take one step earlier; knowing that, the one before should take; and so on, so the very first player should take immediately — even though both players would be better off if the pot kept growing and they passed for a while. Each local step looks sound, but the endpoint of the induction looks wrong. A slippery slope argument seems to run the same way: no single step from k to k+1 seems bad — allowing a tiny bit of technology in a sport, then a slightly larger bit, then a bit more — but after enough small steps you're somewhere everyone agrees is unacceptable. The local transitions are each claimed to be fine on their own, but the terminal state is rejected.

I can't decide whether the slippery slope is just informal backward induction over a social state space, or whether the analogy breaks. In the centipede game the chain depends on common knowledge of rationality and on each player's beliefs about the other player's future moves; the slippery slope is usually a prediction about probabilities or about how norms erode, not about what a single rational agent would do. But then again, when you introduce the standard fix for the centipede paradox — a small chance that the other player is cooperative or irrational, which makes passing rational again — does the same move translate to slippery slopes? Would a small chance that each incremental step gets resisted make the bottom less inevitable, or would it instead make the first step safer to take? Is the resemblance a real structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion? And what exactly would a slippery slope chain need to have in common with a centipede game tree for the game-theoretic results to transfer — where does the analogy stop being load-bearing?
