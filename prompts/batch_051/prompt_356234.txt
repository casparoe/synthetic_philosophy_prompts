I've been on a bit of a combinatorial game theory kick lately, and I can't shake the feeling I'm missing something about the strategy-stealing argument. The way I learned it: in Hex we know the first player has a winning strategy, even though nobody can actually exhibit it, because you assume the second player has a winning strategy, let the first player make a throwaway move, and then have the first player pretend to be the second player and follow that stolen strategy for the rest of the game, making another throwaway move whenever the stolen strategy tells them to play the cell they've already occupied. Since an extra stone never hurts in Hex, the second player can't have a winning strategy, and since Hex can't draw, the first player must have one.

Here's what's eating me. The proof seems to use almost nothing about Hex: it's a finite two-player game with perfect information and no dice, the players have symmetric roles, and draws are impossible. Chess is also a finite two-player game with perfect information and symmetric roles; the only difference I can see is that chess games sometimes end in a draw. Go also seems to satisfy everything, except komi makes a draw virtually impossible, and extra stones usually help anyway. So why isn't the strategy-stealing argument the standard proof that White wins in chess and Black wins in Go? My Go friend said 'komi exists for a reason' and my chess friend muttered something about zugzwang, but neither could say exactly which step of the proof stop being valid. Is the argument secretly a theorem about all such games, and we just don't like the conclusion because it'd make chess and Go trivially solved?
