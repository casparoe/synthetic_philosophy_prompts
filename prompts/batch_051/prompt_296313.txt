I keep coming back to the idea of free-floating rationales — the thought that evolution can produce design without a designer, and that the reason a trait exists can be a perfectly good reason even though no mind in the lineage ever represented it. Gills are for extracting oxygen; hollow bones are for flight; nothing needed to hold that purpose in mind. The move I see most often is deflationary: it treats this as a façon de parler, or at best an as-if shorthand we adopt because it compresses selectionist explanations, while insisting that genuine reasons exist only where a mind weighs considerations.

I want you to argue for the literal version. State the thesis precisely — something like this: selection processes can constitute a form of reasoning, and free-floating rationales are the basic case of a reason; the conscious, represented reasons of an agent are a special case of the same phenomenon, with the selection happening inside a single nervous system rather than across a population or lineage. Give the underlying argument for locating the concept of a reason at the level of what gets selected rather than at the level of what gets represented, and argue against both the deflationary reading and the as-if reading. Do not retreat to the hedged claim that the metaphor is harmless; defend the stronger claim that it is not a metaphor.

Approach this as a philosopher of biology and mind who is comfortable with selection theory and the literature on reasons. Before writing, work out what you take to be the two strongest objections to the literal version and decide which one actually threatens it; do not strawman the opposition.

Structure the response as follows:

- Thesis and stakes (roughly 250–350 words). State the literal thesis precisely, distinguish it from the deflationary and as-if readings, and say what accepting it would change about how we think of deliberation, normativity, or mind.
- The core argument (roughly 600–800 words). Give the strongest positive case for the claim that free-floating rationales are the basic kind of reason and agentive reasons are a special case.
- Objections and replies (roughly 500–700 words). Present the two strongest objections and answer each. Let the best objection make real trouble before you answer it.
- What would change my mind (roughly 200–300 words). State which consideration or argument, if it held up, would push you back toward the deflationary reading, and identify the premise in the core argument you are least confident in.
