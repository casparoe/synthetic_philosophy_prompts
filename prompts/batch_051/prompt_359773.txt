At a reading group on the history of economic ideas, two members clashed over a claim from the week's reading — that Malthus's doctrine of population was not refuted by the industrial era but was simply early, the two centuries of rising output per person that followed being a temporary escape before the underlying pressures reassert themselves. I listened to the argument and could not decide who was right. The exchange went roughly as follows.

The first member argued that this is an unfalsifiable rescue. If a theory says population tends to outrun subsistence unless checked by famine, disease, or deliberate restraint, and then output per head rises steadily for two hundred years, it cannot simply answer 'not yet.' A doctrine that can absorb any observation by pushing back the deadline is compatible with both catastrophe and abundance, and therefore explains neither.

The second member replied that this is too crude an understanding of how long-run claims are tested. No one judges a claim about a centuries-scale process by looking at a few decades and declaring the matter settled; that would be like deeming a coin biased after a single toss. The Malthusian mechanism, he noted, visibly operated for most of recorded history, and the industrial escape depended on special conditions — abundant fossil fuels and steeply rising agricultural yields — that the theory did not rule out but simply failed to foresee. Correcting for an unforeseen condition is a revision of an auxiliary assumption, not an abandonment of the core mechanism.

The first member answered that the original theory made the stronger claim that the checks would bind, and the escape is exactly the outcome it excluded. To retreat to 'the mechanism holds as a tendency unless technology intervenes' is to hold a position consistent with every possible outcome. The second member countered that science often revises in just this way: when the orbit of Uranus departed from Newtonian predictions, astronomers did not discard gravitational theory; they posited a further planet whose position could then be checked. The test, he argued, is whether a rescue is precise and risky enough to be falsified in its turn, and a genuinely long-run prediction legitimately inherits a long testing horizon.

They asked me to referee the exchange, and I found I could not. Please adjudicate it point by point: identify which moves are sound, which are mistaken, and what both participants are overlooking. In particular, address the difference between a mechanism's being real and a theory's being testable, and state which of the two, if either, is more right overall.
