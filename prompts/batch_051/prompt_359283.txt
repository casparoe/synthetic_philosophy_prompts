I'm a novelist, and I've been stuck on a book about a man who raised a piglet by hand after its mother died. The pig is now full-grown, the man is broke, and the whole question of whether he may slaughter it for meat has become the quiet center of the novel. While trying to give the character a plausible interior life I read a little Nozick on animals and found I'd more or less adopted the position myself. As I understand it: animals aren't mere things—there are genuine side-constraints against causing them pain and death—but they also aren't full persons, so the constraints are weaker than they are with people. Torturing a dog for a a marginal thrill is simply out, but killing an animal can be permissible when something substantial is at stake for humans, and the constraints can reasonably give way under genuine conflict or scarcity. That middle-ground quality is exactly what I want my protagonist to feel, so I'm inclined to let the book run on it.

But before I hand the view to a character, I want it stress-tested. What would actually count against it? I don't mean just the familiar objection that a full animal-rights view would say the constraints are too weak or that a tough-minded utilitarian would say only experiences matter—I mean a more fine-grained map of defeaters. Which possible cases, facts about animal minds, marginal-case arguments, wild-animal or environmental considerations, or consequences for practices like factory farming would be most damaging? I'd like them ranked from "a committed Nozickian would concede this is awkward" to "this should make me give up the view entirely." And I want an honest answer to whether the "weaker but real" qualifier makes the position unable to lose. If the constraints can be weakened whenever they're inconvenient, has the view said anything at all—and does that softness matter for a novel, if the view is meant to capture a character's genuine moral vertigo?
