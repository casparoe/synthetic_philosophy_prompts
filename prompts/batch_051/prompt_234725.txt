I have been thinking about a parallel between Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater and a certain architecture in distributed computing, and I would like to know whether the parallel is deep or merely superficial.

On the philosophy-of-mind side: the Cartesian theater is the picture of consciousness as a single place and moment at which everything comes together and is exhibited to an inner audience. Dennett's Multiple Drafts model denies this. There is no central venue and no canonical stream of consciousness; instead, many parallel, partial narrative fragments are continuously revised, and what gets reported as the conscious state at a given time depends on which draft happens to win influence over later processing and overt behaviour.

On the computing side: a centralised database keeps one master copy, and all updates are serialised through it, so there is always a single authoritative order of events. Replicated, masterless systems give this up. Each node keeps its own copy, updates may arrive in different orders at different nodes, and consistency is either achieved later through conflict resolution or remains merely eventual. A client may be unable to distinguish reading a stale value from reading a value that was overwritten before the query completed; there is no single global timeline from which every question can be settled.

The mapping that strikes me is this: the Cartesian theater corresponds to the centralised system with a serialisation point, and the Multiple Drafts model corresponds to the replicated system without a master, in which each draft is like a node's local version of events and no version is authoritative until some later process effectively reads it. I want to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. If it is genuine, do any results from distributed systems transfer, for instance the idea that a system cannot simultaneously guarantee consistency, availability, and partition tolerance? And what is one thing the analogy predicts that the Multiple Drafts model would not actually accept?

In the five points, please address: the exact mapping; where it holds; where it breaks down; whether any distributed-systems result carries a real lesson for the Multiple Drafts model; and one prediction the analogy makes that turns out false.
