I am a PhD student in philosophy of probability, and I am trying to pin down what Bertrand's paradox actually shows about the principle of indifference — roughly, that among mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities about which one lacks differentiating information, one should assign equal probability.

The puzzle is as follows. An equilateral triangle is inscribed in a circle, and one asks for the probability that a chord of the circle, chosen "at random," is longer than a side of the triangle. Three natural ways of choosing the chord yield three different answers. If the chord is determined by two endpoints picked uniformly on the circumference, the probability is 1/3. If it is determined by picking a radius and a point uniformly along that radius, and then drawing the chord perpendicular to the radius at that point, the probability is 1/2. If it is determined by picking the chord's midpoint uniformly inside the disc, the probability is 1/4.

The two positions I want the collaboration to represent are these. The invariance-resolutionist position holds that the principle of indifference is sound but under-specified: it must be constrained by the symmetries of a problem, and in the circle case invariance under translations, rotations, and scale changes singles out the radial method, so that 1/2 is the uniquely correct answer and the other two methods are incorrect because they use parameterizations that fail the relevant invariance. The underdeterminationist position holds that the original question is simply ill-posed: each procedure defines a different chance setup, the three answers are all correct relative to their respective setups, and no a priori principle can say which setup the bare phrase "a random chord" picks out. On this view, the paradox shows that the principle of indifference yields probabilities only relative to an already-specified background measure or chance mechanism, and an appeal to symmetries merely moves the need for that specification back one step.

The document should include: (a) claims both sides can sign about the mathematics of the three Bertrand procedures, about what the principle of indifference minimally requires, and about what the paradox does and does not establish; (b) each residual disagreement stated in neutral terms, followed by each side's formulation of its own view in wording that side would accept; (c) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side; and (d) for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or extension of the Bertrand setup that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

I suspect one crux is whether the three classic procedures should be described as three answers to three different questions or as competing answers to one question. Another likely crux is whether invariance under translations, rotations, and scale changes is strong enough to single out a unique prior in the circle case, or whether the choice of which symmetries matter is itself an underdetermined step. For that second crux, a useful test case may be the cube-factory variant: a cube is known only to have side length somewhere between 0 and 1, chosen at random; applying the principle of indifference to side length gives Pr(side ≤ 1/2) = 1/2, whereas applying it to volume gives Pr(side ≤ 1/2) = Pr(volume ≤ 1/8) = 1/8.
