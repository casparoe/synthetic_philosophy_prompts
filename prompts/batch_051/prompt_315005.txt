You're a philosopher who works on argumentation theory and incentive design—the kind of person people bring weird institutional proposals to for stress-testing. So no surface-level "wouldn't it be nice if people debated more civilly" takes; I want the gears.

I've got a proposal to run by you: the Steelman League, a competitive argumentation sport with promotion and relegation. Here's the ruleset.

1. Every match has two teams and a question drawn at match time from a pool (ethics, policy, epstemology, whatever). Teams get assigned pro/con by coin flip five minutes before the match, and they can't decline.

2. Each team has to open with exactly four minutes of steelmanning the other side: presenting the strongest version of the opposing case, in good faith, as if they believed it. Three judges score the steelman on charity, completeness, and whether it treats the other side as a rational position rather than a strawman.

3. Then the actual debate happens. But here's the weird scoring: teams earn points for every significant objection they acknowledge and then genuinely address, more points if the objection came from the judges rather than from their opponents, and negative points whenever judges flag them for reframing the other side as a weaker position than the one they themselves steelmanned.

4. At the end, each team has to state one concrete observation that would make them lower their confidence or change sides, and the judges score the specificity and riskiness of that statement, not whether it's actually occurred. Vague "we'd change our minds if we were wrong" statements score zero.

5. Teams accumulate Elo. Top division gets prize money, sponsors, live audiences, the usual highlight-reel culture.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral question about whether debating is good. Walk me through how it actually behaves. Who rises to the top: the most honest reasoners, the best performers of honesty, or some third thing? How do teams game the steelman requirement, the objection rule, and the change-your-mind rule? What leaks out of optimal league strategy into ordinary public argument? Where does the whole thing rot first—judging, the question pool, sponsorship? And can any of those failure modes be patched without introducing a worse one? If you think the entire enterprise is doomed, start by giving the steelman of the League itself before you kill it. Take your time, brainstorm at least four ways each rule could be exploited, and only then give me your overall read.
