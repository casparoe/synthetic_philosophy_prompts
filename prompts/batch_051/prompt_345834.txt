I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of decision theory, with 16 students and one 75-minute session. Design a classroom game that makes participants feel the force of evidential cooperation in large worlds before the term is named. Here is the target idea in plain terms: an agent can have reason to perform an act that has no causal effect on the choices of other agents when that act nevertheless carries evidence about how a large number of relevantly similar agents will choose, especially if the agent can regard its own decision procedure as selected from the same population as the others. In a large enough world, these evidential correlations need not be engineered by an unusual predictor; they can arise from ordinary processes such as selection, copying, or shared causes of decision procedures.

The game should begin with an anonymous, one-shot interaction in which the natural move is defection in a prisoner's dilemma–style payoff structure: no communication, no reputation, no enforcement, and no future interaction. Then, through successive rule changes or new information about how counterpart choices are generated, it should become genuinely rational or at least strongly tempting to cooperate, even though no act causally influences the other paired agent. The reversal should come from the structure of the activity, not from the facilitator explaining it. There should be no lecture up front, and students should not hear the name of the idea until the debrief.

Provide exact rules, materials, a point-based payoff scheme that does not require real money, a rough minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto three things: causal reasoning, evidential reasoning about one's counterparts, and the specifically large-world move from a designed test to selection among broadly similar decision procedures. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time the game is run, such as students importing reputation, repeated-game, or empathetic-altruism considerations, and how to redirect each failure without collapsing the exercise. End with an honest note on one feature of evidential cooperation in large worlds that an in-class social game cannot fully reproduce because it is a point about enormous populations and indexical selection, and explain how the instructor should supply that feature in the debrief.
