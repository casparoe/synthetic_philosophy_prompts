I've got an asymmetry I keep running into, and I can't tell whether anything justifies it or whether it's just a story about who's easier to picture. I'm trying to get clear on complicity—sharing in a wrong without making any causal difference to it.

Compare two people connected to a company that's knowingly dumping poison into a river. Marta works in the shipping office, filling in the manifests for the trucks that haul the waste. The manifests are accurate and, if she quit tomorrow, someone else would fill them in and the same trucks would roll; she doesn't cause one extra barrel to go in the river. Still, 'complicit in dumping poison' feels right for her. Jonas buys the company's product every week for his shop, knows about the dumping, and keeps buying. His orders are just as causally inert as Marta's keystrokes—if he stopped, the company would sell the same ammount. But 'complicit in dumping poison' feels much too strong for him; I'd say something weaker, like he's looking away from something ugly.

So is there a principle that delivers both verdicts? The obvious candidate is something like: you're complicit when you play a role inside the cooperative scheme doing the wrong, but not when you merely transact with it from outside. Is 'inside/outside' doing real moral work there, or is it just tracking visibility, power, or how easy it is to imagine a person going through the motions? Give me the strongest version of that principle you can, test it on the cases between Marta and Jonas (the janitor who sweeps the office and knows what the manifests are for, the shareholder living off the dividends, a bank that lends the company money to build a second pipe), and tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.
