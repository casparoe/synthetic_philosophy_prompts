At his obedience experiments at Yale in the early 1960s, Stanley Milgram found that ordinary volunteers would, with surprising frequency, follow an experimenter's instructions to administer what they believed were increasingly severe electric shocks to a learner, even after the learner stopped answering or protested. Treat the experimental situation as a real episode and analyze it through the lens of Harry Frankfurt's critique of the principle of alternative possibilities in his 1969 paper Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility. In particular: who or what is the relevant agent in the Milgram setup, and what would the alternative-possibilities condition require of the volunteer? Do the volunteers genuinely lack the ability to refuse, or do they possess the ability but fail to exercise it? What would a Frankfurt-style intervention have to add to the Milgram scenario to turn it into a case in which the volunteer could not have done otherwise and yet is still responsible? Then assess the fit in both directions. Which features of the Milgram experiment does the Frankfurt framework illuminate, and which does it distort or miss, especially given the role of authority, incremental pressure, and the absence of an actual counterfactual intervener? Finally, state what the episode shows about Frankfurt's claim that alternative possibilities are irrelevant to ascriptions of moral responsibility: does it support the claim, complicate it, or reveal a limitation in the way Frankfurt cases are typically constructed?
