In the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, early in the first section, Kant has this bit about moral worth. He describes a person whose temperment makes them find an inner satisfaction in spreading happiness around: they do beneficent things out of warm sympathy, taking delight in other people's contentedness as their own doing. Kant says this action is amiable and in accordance with duty, but it has no true moral worth. He contrasts it with someone who lacks that sympathy—maybe their own grief has clouded it—but who still does the same beneficent action from duty. That action, he says, has moral worth. I don't want a summary; I want the conceptual machinery pulled apart.

Here's what's bugging me. Suppose someone deliberately cultivates her own sympathy—trains herself to feel distress at others' distress—precisely because she thinks that will help her notice and do her duty. When she later helps out of that cultivated sympathy, does that count as acting from inclination (and so as having no true moral worth), or can the training change the status of the motive? Second, suppose an agent acts from both motives at once: she recognizes the duty and would do it anyway, but she also feels the sympathetic pull. On Kant's view, does the extra inclination-based motive change the action's moral worth or leave it unchanged? Is there a reading where what matters is what the agent herself would give as her reason if asked why she did it, rather than the causal background of the motive?
