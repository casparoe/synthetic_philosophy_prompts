Consider two ways in which a new image might come to look unmistakably like Cézanne's. In the first, a human painter studies Cézanne's canvases for years and then produces work whose debt to him is obvious; we tend to call this influence, tradition, or an artist finding a voice, and we credit the result with originality. In the second, a generative image model is trained on a corpus that includes Cézanne's paintings and is then prompted to produce an image in his style; many people call the result derivative, mechanical, or even a kind of theft. The causal dependence on the earlier work is, on its face, similar in the two cases: features of Cézanne's painting are absorbed and reappear in the new image. What could justify the difference in verdicts? Give the most defensible candidate justification you can, and test it against adjacent cases — a human who slavishly imitates without adding anything, a model that demonstrably transforms its sources, an artist working from memory rather than from the originals, a training set consisting of a single painter's entire oeuvre — before concluding with a clear verdict on whether the asymmetry is worth keeping, needs revision, or should be abandoned. If you think the setup itself rests on a confusion, such as assuming the two cases are comparable in a way they are not, say that outright rather than furnishing a justification.
