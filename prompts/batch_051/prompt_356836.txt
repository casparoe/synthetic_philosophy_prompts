I work in the policy office of a national environmental protection authority, and I keep running into a problem with our own internal Decision Framework. Section 4 says, roughly, that we may only refuse or restrict an activity when we can show with numbers that the expected harm of allowing it outweighs the expected benefit, and it adds that a risk is just a probability times a magnitude, so heavily discounting an outcome that is vanishingly unlikely is arithmetic, not a failure of nerve. Section 9 says, roughly, that where an activity could cause serious or irreversible harm and the probability cannot be estimated confidently, we must err on the side of prevention, and uncertainty about the probability cannot be used as a reason to delay cost-effective measures to prevent the harm.

A real case is sitting on my desk: a company wants a permit for a new industrial process. There estimate of the chance of an accidental release is maybe one in a million, and they admit the number is not based on much; if a release happened it would contaminate a city's drinking water for a generation. Section 4 seems to tell me to run the numbers and probably approve; Section 9 seems to say the uncertainty is exactly why I should not. Before I advise the minister I need to know whether the two sections can honestly be read together, and what each reading would cost.

Give me exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should state one candidate reconciliation of sections 4 and 9 in a sentence or two, drawing on a different sense of a term, a different scope, or a distinction the document never draws explicitly, and say explicitly whether that reading is an honest way to interpret our framework or special pleading to get the answer the writer wants.
