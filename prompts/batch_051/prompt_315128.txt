Here's something I keep going back and forth on. The moral-hazard argument against researching solar geoengineering says: if the stuff starts to look like a cheap fix, governments will treat it as a substitute for cutting emissions rather than a complement, so even developing the technique is dangerous. That argument was built when stratospheric aerosol injection looked like a one-shot thermostat you could turn up and down more or less at will. But now suppose we take seriously the termination-shock picture: the injections would have to be sustained for decades, and if they stopped abruptly the planet would warm to roughly where it would have been without the scheme within a decade or two. Does the moral-hazard argument survive that change? I can see it weakening (no sane government treats a risky, can't-stop intervention as a substitute for mitigation) but also strengthening (the cost of a change of government or a funding crisis is now enormous, so nobody should be volunteering for that). Work through which premise of the original argument the termination-shock picture bears on, and say whether the argument should be kept as is, repaired, or junked. Answer in British English.
