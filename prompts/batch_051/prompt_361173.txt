I keep coming back to Skolem's paradox and I can't figure out why smart people are still arguing about it nearly a hundred years later. Here's my understanding of the formal part: the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem says that any countable set of first-order sentences with an infinite model has a countable model. Apply that to ZFC: if ZFC is consistent it has a countable model. But that model satisfies "there is an uncountable set," because ZFC proves Cantor's theorem — in particular the model contains a version of the real numbers that it takes to be uncountable. From outside the model, though, the whole domain is countable, so there is a bijection between the model's "reals" and the natural numbers; it's just not an element of the model. So far so good, that part is just mathematics.

The fight, as I see it, is over what this shows. One camp takes it to mean that mathematical notions like "uncountable" are relative to a model and that there's no absolute set-theoretic universe — set theory is always about some stipulated structure. Another camp says no, it's only a limitation of first-order logic's ability to pin down the intended powerset; in actual mathematical practice "the natural numbers" and "the reals" do have their ordinary meanings, and the theorem is just a fact about formal languages. Then there are people who think the whole thing dissolves once you allow second-order logic, and people who think second-order "full semantics" is just a way of importing the very absoluteness it pretends to explain.

This has been going round in circles since the 1920s. My question isn't who's right. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't about any theorem, since everyone accepts the same formal facts, but about what can count as the intended model and about whether model theory gets the final say about what mathematical language means. Is that right? Is there instead a hidden formal question that's actually still open? And what would count as a move: a theorem, a precise distinction, or just a change of question? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, and an honest answer about whether the impasse itself tells us the issue is ill-posed.
