Consider the compute-governance case for pausing or sharply slowing frontier AI development. As I understand it, the argument runs something like this: the main driver of increases in frontier model capabilities has been increases in training compute; training compute is a legible, concentrated chokepoint—it requires scarce chips, large datacenters, and coordinated large institutions; therefore regulators can target that chokepoint to slow the frontier; and absent a pause, competitive pressure will push labs to scale capabilities faster than alignment and safety work can keep up.

A specific recent development seems to put pressure on this: a growing share of capability gains appears to come not from bigger pretraining runs but from inference-time or test-time compute—models that search over many candidate responses, reflect, or run long chains of reasoning at deployment, extracting dramatically better performance from a model of fixed size by spending more compute per query. Some systems now get better results by thinking longer, not by training larger.

My question is whether the compute-governance pause argument survives this shift, needs repair, or is mostly dead. Which exact premise or premises does inference-time scaling bear on? Can the chokepoint argument be restored in some other form—for example, by governing serving infrastructure, inference chips, or reasoning budgets—or does something structural change once dangerous capabilities can be latent in already-deployed weights and switched on with more inference compute? And if the argument survives only in repaired form, how does the repaired version differ in what it licenses: is it still a case for pausing frontier training, or does it become a case for deployment controls, capacity limits, or something else?

Lay this out as an analysis rather than a policy recommendation: say what the original argument needs to be true, which of those needed things this development undercuts, and whether each undercut premise can be restored with the same or different instruments.
