I'm a PhD candidate in research ethics, and a year as the student member of a data and safety monitoring board has forced me to revisit what I actually think about clinical equipoise. I used to assume I held the familiar view: randomizing patients in a clinical trial is permissible just when the relevant expert community is genuinely uncertain about which arm is better. But when I report my gut verdicts on a series of cases, they no longer seem to add up to any single principle, and I want help figuring out what principle, if any, they do support.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can give them. (1) A randomized head-to-head trial of two standard antihypertensive drugs is plainly acceptable when the expert community is roughly evenly divided about which is better. (2) A surgeon who enrolls her own patients in a trial of a new operation, while personally convinced by her own case series that the new operation is superior, does nothing wrong so long as the broader surgical community remains genuinely divided and the consent process does not inflate the evidence; I want to say her personal conviction is beside the point. (3) Yet when an oncologist in an open-label trial becomes convinced midway through, from her own trial patients, that the experimental chemotherapy is superior—even though no stopping boundary has been crossed and the wider community remains uncertain—I think she would be wrong to keep randomizing the next patient who walks through her door; the coin flip has stopped being honest for her. (4) I also think a data and safety monitoring board may permissibly recommend continuing a trial after an interim analysis shows a trend favoring one arm that has not crossed the pre-specified stopping boundary, even though I would no longer describe the investigators as fully uncertain. (5) And I think it would be wrong to randomize a patient who matches a subgroup (say, nonsmoking women over sixty) for which a credible but unplanned subgroup analysis from an earlier trial markedly favors one arm, even if the trial as a whole still satisfies community equipoise.

The sixth case is what I keep going back and forth on. A multicenter trial compares two standard antibiotics for sepsis; the national expert community is split, but one site's own local audit strongly favors one of them. I cannot decide whether that site should stop randomizing and treat according to its audit, or whether doing so would let merely local, possibly confounded data override the only justification the trial has.

My tentative hypothesis is that something in the neighborhood of clinical equipoise is right but needs repair—perhaps equipoise is not one thing, or perhaps it operates at several levels (the community, the enrolling clinician, the patient subgroup, the interim data) with different jobs. Please state the most defensible principle, or small disjunction of principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible; say which of my verdicts it cannot capture and exactly how it would revise them; and tell me what it says about the local-audit case. I would like the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, covering in turn: the proposed principle stated precisely; how it handles my first five verdicts and which clauses do the work; which verdict or verdicts it forces me to revise and how; its verdict on the sixth, local-audit case; and the strongest objection to the whole proposal together with a one-sentence reply.
