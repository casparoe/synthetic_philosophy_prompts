Simpson's paradox is usually introduced as a curiosity about how an association can reverse when a table is aggregated as opposed to partitioned. But the same structure routinely appears in decisions where something must actually be done. Suppose a new treatment shows a higher overall complication rate than the standard treatment, while showing a lower complication rate than the standard treatment within each of several severity strata. The reason is that clinicians assign the new treatment disproportionately to high-risk patients. The severity classification is made at intake, mostly from pre-admission records, but its boundaries are known to be affected in part by how patients respond early in treatment.

Five standing proposals for how to proceed in such a case are given below. Please sort them into tiers by how much you would trust each as a guide to what the treatment really does, and say in one sentence why each lands where it does:

1. Trust the overall estimate, because it uses all the data and describes the actual population in which the treatment would be deployed.
2. Trust the stratum-specific estimates, because the reversal shows that the overall estimate is an artifact of an uneven distribution of severity.
3. Decide whether to aggregate or stratify case by case, using background knowledge of the causal structure, and refuse to condition on variables that the treatment itself helps determine.
4. Refuse to choose at all, because a reversal of this kind shows that observational data underdetermine the effect, so no decision should be made until randomized or quasi-experimental evidence is available.
5. Report the aggregate and every stratum, and leave the choice of level to the decision-maker, since each level of analysis answers a different question and no single level is categorically superior.

I am interested less in the final order than in the reasons you use to distinguish the proposals, including what each one implicitly assumes about confounding, mediation, and decision relevance.
