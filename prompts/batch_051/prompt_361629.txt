I'm a social historian of laboring people in early modern England. My default interpretive practice, which I have never fully pressure-tested, is this: when the men and women I study are missing from a parish's administrative records, I treat that silence as a positive finding about the recorders — a sign of what they declined to write down or actively suppressed — rather than as evidence that the people were not there or were not active. When those same people do appear but only in punitive records (petty-theft prosecutions, poor-relief disputes, vagrancy complaints), I read the pattern as a map of the authorities' powers and anxieties, not as a portrait of the people's lives; their surfacing chiefly through coercion tells me they come into view only at the point of conflict with an institution, and I take their unrecorded stretches to be more continuous and more self-directed than any document could license. I apply this asymmetry in only one direction: for parish officers and landlords, I treat an absence of records as genuine, if defeasible, evidence of absence.

The question: is this a defensible epistemic policy, or is it a shortcut that makes my historical beliefs unanswerable to the evidence by letting silence, abundance, and every intermediate pattern all count in my favor? If it is partly defensible and partly not, where precisely does it stop being sound, and what restated version would preserve the insight without reducing my reading of the archive to unfalsifiable confirmation of what I already believe?
