I'm definately not a policy person (backend engineer, so this is well outside my lane), but I got pulled into a content moderation dispute and have to write up a recommendation. The clause we're fighting about is from our Community Standards, Dangerous Organizations section: "You may not post, share, link to, or otherwise make available any video, image, audio, or written statement that was produced by a designated violent extremist organization, or that directly promotes recruitment or fundraising for such an organization." The second half is the obvious one; the first half is the catch-all we use to take down anyone mirroring their content.

The case: a university research institute runs a public archive account that posts 20-to-40-second clips of recruitment propaganda from a designated group, each embedded in a longer post with timestamps, analysis of what the video is trying to do, and warnings about how the group targets teenagers. The clip is the original video with only a watermark that says "archived for criticism." Our classifier flags the account constantly because the audio-video fingerprints match, but the account is years old, gets almost no engagement, and is basically a reference archive for journalists and other researchers. On a plain reading of the text, they're "making available" video "produced by" the group, full stop. On a purpose reading, they're doing the opposite of what the clause is for: the group doesn't gain recruits from a labeled archive, and taking it down would destroy evidence and context researchers rely on. And I genuinely don't know what the drafters would have said if you'd asked them about this: they'd probably have imagined some edgelord reposting raw propaganda and claiming it was just critique, not an institutional archive with overlays.

I need to decide whether the account violates the clause. I care more about the reasoning than the operational verdict: lay out what the text, the purpose, the likely intent of the drafters, and a reasonable user's expectation each say, where they come apart, and which approach should win in a case like this. Does it matter that the account's reach is tiny while the same video is already circulating elsewhere?
