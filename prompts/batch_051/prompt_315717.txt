I spent four years in a vegan student co-op, and the members' agreement I signed has a clause I never had to think hard about until now: 'Members commit to a plant-based diet whenever a plant-based alternative is reasonably available and nutritionally adequate, whether eating in the co-op or elsewhere.' I'm still a non-resident member, paying reduced dues and joining meetings by video, so this isn't just nostalgia.

I've moved for a research job to a small, dry highland town in a country I'd rather not name. Fresh vegetables show up in the market only a few months a year; the affordable year-round food is grains, lentils, dairy, and goat or chicken; and the local way of welcoming someone is to cook them meat. There is also a strong norm that you dont turn down food a neighbour has cooked, because refusing is read as a judgement on their table. Some of my old co-op friends say the clause plainly covers me: lentils and rice are sitting in the shop, and being boring or expensive does not make the alternative unreasonable. Others say the drafters never imagined a place where the plant-based option means months of dry lentils cooked without much oil, where a clinic nurse is already worried about my iron and B12, and where refusing goat stew from a neighbour comes across as contempt rather than conscience.

Part of why this is heated is that my co-op used to insist veganism is easy anywhere once you take it seriously, and I'm starting to think that was a rich-world thing to say. I'm less interested in the practical answer than in what each approach to reading the clause — the plain words, the purpose, what the original members would have said if someone had asked, and what a reasonable person in this town would take it to mean — would give. Which approach should win here, and does 'reasonably available and nutritionally adequate' do the real work or just move the argument inside those words?
