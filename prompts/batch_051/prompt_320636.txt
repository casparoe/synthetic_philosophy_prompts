You are a professional philosopher specialising in aesthetics and the epistemology of testimony. Please read the following disagreement carefully. Before reaching a verdict, briefly brainstorm the strongest considerations on each side; then deliver a point-by-point adjudication. Use British English throughout.

At a film society meeting, a member, Angela, told the group that a newly restored 1970s film is one of the greatest films ever made. She has not seen it; she has only read two long reviews and listened to a trusted critic's podcast about the restoration. Two other members, Miriam and Jonas, argued about it afterwards, and each thought the other was missing the point. As a bystander, I could not decide between them.

Miriam said Angela's claim was improper: an aesthetic judgement must be grounded in one's own experience of the work; otherwise it is only a report of other people's reactions, and Angela should not assert it in the unhedged form. Jonas replied that this is exactly the kind of snobbery film people are prone to, since we routinely form beliefs about restaurants, plumbers, scenic routes and holiday destinations on the word of trusted informants, and there is no principled reason films should be a special case. Miriam answered that the difference lies in the content: what would justify Angela's judgement is the particular texture of the images, the pacing, the way the final scene lands, and no critical sentence can transmit that texture to her; testimony can at most give her the proposition that the critic had such an experience. Jonas said this proves too much: I am justified in believing what journalists report and what textbooks say about cell structure even though the underlying experiences were not transmitted to me, so what fails to be transmitted is the experience, not a justified aesthetic belief. Miriam then said that Angela should at least mark the source, since 'the critics say it is one of the greatest' feels honest in a way the unhedged sentence does not, and that difference marks the line between borrowed judgement and her own. Jonas replied that we do not mark sources in ordinary testimonial knowledge: I say 'my train is delayed', not 'the station board says my train is delayed'; if anything differs, it is conversational etiquette, not epistemology. Miriam's last move was that the unhedged form is epistemically defective, because a judgement of taste must be reached autonomously through one's own response to count as a judgement of taste at all. Jonas answered that autonomy is an ideal rather than a condition on justified belief: we cannot experience everything first-hand, and division of epistemic labour is a virtue, so Miriam is making an unprincipled exception for art.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each substantive move, rate it from clearly mistaken through defensible but incomplete to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly; then say who is more right overall; finally, identify the strongest point each side is missing, especially any way of distinguishing what aesthetic testimony can transmit from what it cannot. Treat the disagreement as self-contained and reason without external sources.
