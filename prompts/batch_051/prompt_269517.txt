I recently had to choose between a much more lucrative post and one that would do more direct good to others, and I found that the philosophical considerations I could muster left the tension between prudence and impartial beneficence unresolved in principle. That brought me to Sidgwick’s closing discussion in The Methods of Ethics, where he contrasts two standpoints: from the point of view of the Universe, one’s own good is of no more importance than the equal good of any other person, but from the point of view of one’s own existence, one’s own good presents itself as what one has most reason to pursue. He concludes that this opposition is ultimate, at least for the methods of ethics he has examined.

Suppose I could fund a small research group — say, three philosophers for two years — to work on the questions that cluster around this dualism of practical reason. Which open questions or lines of work should they prioritise, and why? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey: for each priority, explain how it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is for philosophers rather than, say, psychologists or economists, and what would count as genuine progress. In particular, weigh the relative promise of trying to show that the dualism is merely apparent, that one of the two claims is not rationally authoritative after all, or that we should accept the dualism and work out how practical reasoning can operate under it.
