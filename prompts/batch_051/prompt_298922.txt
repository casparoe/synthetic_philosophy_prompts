I've now had the same fight twice in a month, and I want to know if I'm the one being irrational. I habitually assign intermediate credences to claims that are already determined by logic but that no one has managed to work out yet. Right now I'd give maybe 0.85 that P ≠ NP, around 0.7 that Goldbach's conjecture is true, and 0.6 that no computationally cheap collision for SHA-256 gets found in the next decade. My friend, who is trained in formal epistemology, says this is a category error: probability is for uncertainty about which world you're in, not for what the axioms of a fixed world already settle, so the only defensible states are 0, 1, or "I don't know." Giving 0.85 is, in his words, confusing my own ignorance with the kind of uncertainty probability can quantify. I push back that these numbers show up in real decisions: I would take a bet on Goldbach at favorable odds, and I don't spend weekends trying to write a short SAT solver because I strongly but not certainly believe P ≠ NP. Refusing to assign numbers would, it seems to me, leave me either paralyzed or forced to invent some other currency for practical reasoning.

So the question is: is my habit defensible, or is my friend right that I'm dressing up ignorance as probability? I'm not asking for a survey of the formal logical induction literature; if you bring it in, use it to judge the everyday policy rather than just gesturing at it. What I want is an evaluation of the policy itself. Under what conditions does an intermediate credence in a logically determined claim earn the right to be called a probability — for example, that it updates appropriately when I actually see a proof or disproof, that it avoids being exploited by an adversary who is allowed to reason at least as well as I can, that it approximately respects logical consequence, and so on? Which of those conditions, if any, are individually load-bearing? And where does the line run: if "this 400-digit number is composite" can get a credence, can "this Turing machine halts" get one, and do Gödel-type self-reference cases break the policy even though ordinary mathematical conjectures don't seem to cause trouble? At the very end, give me a one-sentence summary and then a final line with a short label like \answer{defensible}, \answer{not defensible}, or \answer{defensible with caveats}.
