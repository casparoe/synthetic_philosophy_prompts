I have been working in the foundations of quantum mechanics, and I keep finding myself dissatisfied with the standard way of sorting interpretations of the quantum state. The usual dichotomy, as I understand it, is as follows. On a psi-ontic view, the wavefunction represents some feature of physical reality: different wavefunctions correspond to different physical situations, so the wavefunction is, in some sense, out there. On a psi-epistemic view, the wavefunction represents an agent's knowledge or information: two distinct wavefunctions can be consistent with one and the same underlying physical situation, and they differ only in what the agent knows. I find this dichotomy too coarse, and I have been reaching for a third reading, which I have been calling the psi-nomic reading.

The thought is that the wavefunction is neither a representation of reality nor a representation of ignorance. It is nomological structure. It functions the way laws of nature are often said to function: a law does not itself describe a particular object, and it does not summarize someone's beliefs; instead, it constrains what can happen and supports counterfactuals. On my proposal, the wavefunction is that kind of thing. For a given preparation it specifies the set of possible measurement outcomes and their amplitudes. It is objective in the way a law is objective—not relative to any particular agent—but it is not a physical entity one could prepare or detect in isolation. And it is not information, because information is information about something, and there is no further 'something' that the wavefunction reports on.

Consider the double-slit experiment. A psi-ontic reading says the wavefunction is the actual state of the electron. A psi-epistemic reading says it is a compendium of what we know about which slit the electron passed through. The psi-nomic reading says both mistake a rule for a thing: the wavefunction is the rule by which the two path-contributions combine, which is why amplitudes interfere where probabilities would merely add. It is neither the electron nor our dossier on the electron; it is the constraint that fixes what the electron can do.

But I worry that the position is a distinction without a difference, and I would like it checked rather than indulged. Four difficulties seem pressing. First, if laws are themselves part of reality, as a realist about laws would hold, then 'nomological' may just be a species of ontic, and psi-nomic collapses into psi-ontic. Second, if laws are instead summaries or inferential instruments, then psi-nomic collapses into psi-epistemic. So the middle position may be hostage to one's prior philosophy of law, or it may be empty. Third, psi-epistemic views are usually taken to require that two distinct wavefunctions can be compatible with the same underlying ontic state, and there are now results purporting to show that no such account works, at least not without abandoning some independence assumption about independently prepared systems. Does the psi-nomic view inherit that commitment, and with it those difficulties? Fourth, a rule that assigns amplitudes does not by itself deliver determinate measurement outcomes, so the view looks parasitic on an independent solution to the measurement problem—a collapse, additional variables, or something else. Is it fair to call the view an interpretation of quantum mechanics at all, rather than a promissory note?

I would welcome an honest assessment. Does psi-nomic earn its place as a third position, or does it collapse under pressure into one of the two familiar camps? If there is a strongest version that survives, what exactly does it commit one to, and where does it finally break?
