Suppose a big online encyclopedia introduces a rule against bare generics in article text. Any sentence that predicates a property of a kind or social group — ducks lay eggs, mosquitoes carry malaria, pit bulls are dangerous, Germans are punctual — has to carry an explicit quantifier or frequency marker: all, most, many, some, typically, occasionally, or a number. A bot flags sentences that look like bare generics, and the article is witheld until a human reviewer approves a revised sentence. The rule covers factual claims but not quotations. Walk me through what happens over the next few years. Which claims are easy to repair and which become hard to state honestly, given that generics are useful precisely because they tolerate exceptions? How do editors and interest groups game the quantifiers — using "some" to smuggle in a stereotype, or "typically" to hide a thin evidential basis? And does the rule cut misleading generalisations, or just push them into framing, implication, and which articles get written? Where does it fail first, and can it be fixed without gutting the policy?
