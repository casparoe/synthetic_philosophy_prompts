I cover science and ideas for a living, and lately I keep running into economists and decision-theory types who repeat, as if it were just obvious, that rational preferences must be transitive. I can't get the self-torturer puzzle out of my head. The setup (roughly as Quinn gave it): a person has a dial with settings from 0 to 1000; at any setting he can nudge it up one notch for $10,000, and each single notch adds only an imperceptable bit of pain, so taking any particular step looks rational. But he would never jump from 0 straight to 1000—even for the full $10 million—because the cumulative pain is unendurable. That looks like a case where every adjacent step is preferred but the endpoint isn't, which violates transitivity, and yet the person isn't obviously confused or badly informed. So here's the question: how would you actually test the claim that transitivity is a requirement of rational preference, rather than just argue over it in a seminar? Design the experiment concretely enough to hand to a behavioral lab, say what outcome would count as evidence for the claim and what against, and be explicit about what a clean result in either direction would still leave unsettled (for instance, whether intransitive choices reveal irrationality or just a finer-grained way of valuing). Then give me the bottom line as a table with these columns: operationalization, result favoring the claim, result against the claim, and what that result would still not settle.
