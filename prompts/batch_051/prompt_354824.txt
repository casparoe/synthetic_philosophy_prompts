I hold a position in ethics that I’ve come to think of as Schopenhauerian negative consequentialism, and I want you to show me what it risks. The position is: reductions in suffering have strict moral priority over the production of happiness; a sufficiently severe suffering cannot be morally offset by any amount of happiness for the being who suffers it; and, other things equal, a world containing less suffering is better than one containing more, even if the first world contains less positive happiness. The view's origin for me was not a formal argument but a passage in Arthur Schopenhauer’s essay On the Sufferings of the World, in the collection Studies in Pessimism. I don't have the text in front of me, so I'll paraphrase. Schopenhauer argues there that pain, want, and dissatisfaction are the positive and directly felt elements of experience, while what we call happiness or satisfaction is negative: it is the felt absence of pain or the brief cessation of a want, not a positive state in its own right. He treats this descriptive asymmetry as the ground of a normative lesson: the best a life can offer is relief from suffering, and optimistic systems that treat happiness as the positive aim of life misunderstand what experience is actually like. My question is: what would count against the view I have built on that passage? I want a map of its exposure. Do not just list standard objections; distinguish the evidence, arguments, intuitions, possible cases, and formal results that would be decisive from those that would merely make the view awkward or force me to qualify it. Target the descriptive-phenomenological claim as well as the move from that claim to the normative priority of suffering reduction. Say which defeaters a committed holder of the view should themselves concede as counting against it, and which they would reasonably dismiss. Then give your honest assessment: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability, or does it still risk something? If the Schopenhauerian descriptive claim turned out to be false—say, if happiness were shown to be a positive state after all—would the normative position lose its foundation, or could it survive in another form?
