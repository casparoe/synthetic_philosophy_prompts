I've been trying to puzzle through whether the standard disagreement in Taurek's one-versus-five rescue case survives if you mess with the probabilities. In the stock version you can save either one stranger or five, all of them drowning and all else equal. The Taurek-ish position is that the bare number of extra lives doesnt decide it: you may toss a coin between the two groups so each person gets an equal shot at being the one you rescue. The numbers-count position says no, save the five, since the greater number is at least tie-breaking and usually just decisive.

Now change the case. You have one drone that can drop a flotation device exactly once. If you send it to swimmer A, A survives with probability 1. If you send it to the zone where B1 through B5 are scattered, each of them survives with an independent probability of 0.2, so the expected number saved is exactly 1. Everyone is a stranger, everyone knows the probabilities, and there are no other relevant differences.

Work through what both sides say now. For the Taurek-style equal-chance view: what procedure gives each of the six swimmers the same ex ante chance of surviving, and is it still a coin flip? Show the arithmetic. For the numbers-count view: does a save-the-greater-number principle still tell you to aim at the five, or does it fall silent once the expected number saved is tied? Does it matter whether the principle is stated in terms of expected lives, best possible outcome, or worst possible outcome? And does this variation dissolve the original dispute, or just move it into a fight about ex ante chances versus actual outcomes?
