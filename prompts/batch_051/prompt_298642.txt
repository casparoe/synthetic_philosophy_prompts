For the last few weeks I've been trying to work out what supervaluationism about vagueness actually buys over epistemicism, and I keep constructing a little argument that looks like a reductio of the supervaluationist position, though I can't tell whether it sticks. The view's advertised advantage, as I understand it, is that it lets you deny that there is any particular fact of the matter about where the cutoff in a sorites series falls while still keeping classical logic; the epistemicist gets the sharp cutoff back only by saying there is a fact of the matter we cannot know, which has always seemed to me the worse bullet. The supervaluationist story is that a sentence is true simpliciter iff it is true on every admissible sharpening (precisification) of the vague term, false iff it is false on every such sharpening, and neither otherwise.

Consider a finite sorites series for 'bald': zero hairs is definitely bald, two hundred thousand hairs is definitely not bald, and each step adds one hair. The argument goes as follows.

(1) On every admissible sharpening of 'bald', there is some case n in the series such that n is bald and n+1 is not bald; each sharpening draws a last-bald cutoff somewhere.

(2) Therefore the existential sentence 'there is a last bald case in the series' is true on every admissible sharpening.

(3) Hence, by the supervaluationist truth condition, that existential sentence is true simpliciter.

(4) But for any particular case n, the sentence 'n is the last bald case' is not true on every admissible sharpening, since different sharpenings can draw the cutoff in different places.

(5) So there is no particular case n of which it is true simpliciter that it is the last bald case.

(6) We are left with an existential sentence that is true simpliciter while none of its instances is true simpliciter.

To me this already sounds like a quantifier-scope mistake, but I also don't want to reject a theory just because its metainferences look unfamiliar. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? If it is valid, is it the reductio it appears to be, or is the right response to accept (6) as a known price of the view and deny that a true existential must have a true witness? And if that is the response, what should I make of the claim that supervaluationism preserves our ordinary understanding of 'there is'?
