Since retiring from an experimental career in physiology, I have been reading philosophy, and the timing of experience strikes me as exactly the sort of problem I once assumed careful measurement could settle. The color phi phenomenon—two brief, spatially separated flashes, one red and one green, reported as a single moving light that changes color midway—has disabused me of that, and the Orwellian/Stalinesque contrast in Dennett's treatment of it has shown me how much theoretical load the data can be made to carry. Before I read further, I want a clear statement of the explanatory bar: what any adequate theory of the timing and content of conscious experience must deliver. Please write the desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each. Cover at least: the phenomena the theory must get right, beginning with color phi and extending to whatever adjacent temporal illusions or findings you regard as part of the same explanatory family; the distinctions it must respect, such as those among stimulus time, represented time, and the time at which a content becomes available to consciousness, or whatever better-motivated distinctions you would substitute; and the neuroscientific or psychophysical constraints it must satisfy. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of a particular camp. In particular, say whether some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied—for example, whether the demand that a theory specify whether the revision is Orwellian or Stalinesque conflicts with the demand that the theory be empirically decidable. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a scorecard of existing theories.
