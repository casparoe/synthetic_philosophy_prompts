I have spent most of my career in the physical sciences and have only recently begun reading legal and political philosophy. One asymmetry in how we treat private adjudication puzzles me. If a contract term provided that all disputes would be heard by an adjudicator on one party's payroll, courts would refuse to enforce it: we would call the tribunal partial and the supposed consent worthless. But courts routinely enforce mandatory pre-dispute arbitration clauses in consumer and employment form contracts, where the drafting company chooses the arbitration provider, typically pays the arbitrator's fees, and is a repeat player while the individual will likely appear only once. The arbitrator is not an employee, but the financial arrangement looks as though it rewards decisions that favor the drafting company.

Can this difference be defended on principle, or is the legal treatment of arbitration simply an entrenched exception to our ordinary standards of impartial adjudication? Present the strongest justification for the asymmetry you can construct, test it against a concrete nearby case, and say whether the current practice should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
