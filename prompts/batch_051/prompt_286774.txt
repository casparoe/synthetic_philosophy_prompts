My roommate and I have been going in circles on hedonism, and I'm starting to think the argument we keep using is too powerful in a way neither of us can pin down. Here it is. (1) If hedonism is true, pleasure has to be a single kind of thing: there's got to be some one property all pleasures share, and that property is what makes them good. (2) But pleasures are wildly heterogenous. The pleasure of a cold drink when you're thirsty, of finally getting a proof to work, of schadenfreude, of a song, of sex, of winning an ugly argument — what single felt quality does any of that actually share? (3) So either pleasure isn't a felt quality at all but something else (say, taking an attitude of liking toward a state of affairs), or 'pleasure' is just a loose label for whatever I happen to like. (4) If pleasure isn't a felt quality, hedonism gives up the thing that made it intuitive — that a pleasant experience feels good and that's obviously what's valuable — and turns into something weirdly abstract. (5) If pleasure just means whatever I like, hedonism quietly collapses into desire-satisfactionism, or is empty. (6) So hedonism is either false, empty, or a disguised version of a rival theory. But then I noticed the same argument seems to prove too much: run it on pain, or belief, or hunger, and you get the same skeptical result about mental categories we all accept. So I suspect one of the steps is doing too much work. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (2), because it just seems like introspection to me, but I can't see how to keep hedonism substantive if I do.
