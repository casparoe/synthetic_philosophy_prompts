I have spent the past year dealing with two automated decisions that affected me directly — a welfare-benefits determination and a professional-licensing decision — and in both cases the deciding system was a machine-learning model whose decision function no human, including the appeal officer, could fully state. That experience has made the rule of law personally urgent for me, and it has also made me uncertain about what the ideal actually requires.

Let me say what I mean by the rule of law. I am drawing loosely on the debate associated with Fuller, Raz, and Waldron, but the list below is my own synthesis and is not meant as an attribution to any one of them. I take the rule of law to be satisfied only if the following conditions hold:

1. Generality and prospectivity: people are governed by general, public, prospective norms, not by ad hoc commands or retrospective penalties aimed at identifiable individuals.
2. Publicity: the norms, and the standards by which they will be applied, are public enough that an ordinary person can learn them and use them in planning.
3. Clarity and consistency: the norms are sufficiently determinate, and not mutually contradictory.
4. Stability: the norms do not change so frequently or unpredictably that planning becomes impossible.
5. Feasibility: the norms do not require the impossible.
6. Congruence between announced rule and official action: officials actually apply the announced norms, and there is some mechanism for detecting and correcting any gap between the announced rule and what officials do.
7. Contestability and reason-giving: a person subject to a decision can challenge it before an independent body, and that body can evaluate the decision by reference to the announced norms rather than merely to the decision's internal consistency.

Now consider a borderline case. Suppose a jurisdiction replaces the eligibility rules for a major income-support program with a continuously retrained machine-learning model. The enabling statute is general, public, and prospective. The agency publishes the model's training objective, its performance metrics, and the fairness constraints imposed on it. But the learned decision function itself is not published, and the agency states that it cannot express that function as a set of humanly intelligible rules. Applicants receive individualized explanations of the form: "Your application was denied because your reported income was above the threshold; had your income been $200 lower, you would have qualified." These counterfactual explanations are generated for each applicant, but the complete set of conditions under which the model awards or denies benefits is unknown to applicants, to agency staff, and to the appeal board. The model is frozen between annual releases, so decisions are stable within each year; at each retraining, however, the decision boundaries shift in ways the agency cannot fully summarize. There is a right of appeal. The appeal board is independent of the program office and can reverse a decision, but it too has no access to the decision function; it can check only whether the applicant's data were entered correctly, whether the explanation is internally consistent, and whether the statutory constraints were encoded in the training pipeline.

Does this system satisfy the rule of law as I have characterized it? Work through the seven criteria one by one. Where the system fails a criterion, say whether that shows the system is defective or whether the criterion is doing too much work. Where a criterion gives no determinate answer, say so explicitly. Finally, tell me whether the case exposes a flaw in my list — for instance, whether publicity should be understood to require not just that the standards be public, but that they be available in a form an affected person could actually use to anticipate and contest a decision, and whether that would change the verdict. I am not asking whether the system is lawful under any particular constitutional doctrine or whether it is good policy; I am asking whether it meets the ideal as specified.
