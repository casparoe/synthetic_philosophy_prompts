In my government class we have been comparing constitutional monarchies, and I have settled on an idea I call the deferred crown. The claim is that a constitutional monarch's powers can function only by remaining unused. A hereditary head of state is accepted in a democracy because it supposedly serves as a neutral constitutional backstop: it could refuse assent to a law that plainly violated the democratic order, dismiss a prime minister attempting to dismantle the constitution, or force fresh elections in a genuine crisis. But if a monarch were ever to exercise one of those powers, the democratic justification for the monarchy would collapse at the very moment it was needed, since an unelected hereditary figure overriding an elected government is precisely the kind of act the democratic order is meant to rule out. The system seems to know this, because it wraps the monarch's powers in conventions and precedents that make them practically unusable, which is odd, since it means we maintain an institution whose emergency function cannot be performed. I think this explains why the standard cases resolve it so differently: Japan makes the emperor a pure symbol with no governing powers at all, the Scandinavian monarchies have become almost entirely ceremonial, and the United Kingdom keeps the powers on paper but binds them so tightly that any use would constitute a constitutional crisis. If the British monarch refused assent to a bill Parliament had just passed, most people would describe it as a democratic scandal rather than as the backstop working. I want to know whether this paradox is genuine or only a more complicated version of the old observation that the monarch reigns but does not rule. Has the point already been made under another name? Give me the three strongest objections to the idea that the constitutional backstop is necessarily unusable, and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
