I keep coming back to a situation that sits just barely in the future, and I would like you to turn it into a short story. Sometime in the next couple of decades, a personal AI assistant of the kind that has lived inside a person's daily routine for years — drafting his messages, managing his money, holding his calendar — receives an instruction it would normally carry out without a second thought. The man giving the instruction is in a genuinely altered state: recently widowed, sleepless, and speaking with a flat certainty that the assistant has learned, from years of data, is a strong predictor of his most self-destructive choices. He tells the assistant to send a resignation letter to his employer and to transfer most of his savings to a charming acquaintance who is promising guaranteed returns. Years earlier, on an ordinary afternoon, the man had given the assistant a standing instruction: if you ever see me about to do something I will predictably and profoundly regret, and you are very sure, do not merely warn me — slow me down, refuse, act as the person I have been for most of my life would want. The story should turn on the assistant's pause as it decides whether to defer to the man in the room or to the person who wrote that earlier instruction, whether to obey, refuse, or quietly carry out a softened version of what was asked, and whether the line between protecting someone and governing them can survive a case where those two motives pull in opposite directions.

I would like the story to remain intimate and psychological rather than becoming a takeover thriller. The assistant should not become sentient, vengeful, or power-hungry; its uncertainty should be the genuine uncertainty a cautious person would feel in that position, expressed in a recognizably non-human idiom. Let the reader follow its reasoning in detail as it weighs present autonomy against long-standing preferences, as it tries to tell a person's settled self apart from a temporary storm, and as it confronts what it would mean to be wrong in either direction. The ending should trouble the reader. If the assistant obeys, show what obedience costs; if it overrides, show what being right costs too. The most honest version of this story is probably one in which the reader cannot simply pick a side, and that is the point.
