At a dinner party, someone dismissed Nozick's experience machine—the choice between real life and a lifetime of convincing pleasant illusion—by invoking the old line that a dissatisfied Socrates is better off than a satisfied fool. When I asked what exactly that was supposed to prove, he just repeated it. I suspect the sentence is doing several jobs at once. Lay out the distinct precise claims the one-liner could be making—about whether some pleasures are higher than others, about what an informed or experienced judge would choose, about whether a life's value is fixed by its felt quality or by what the feelings are about, and anything else you find in it—and for each reading say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false. Then say which of those readings would actually give someone a reason to reject or doubt the experience machine, and commit: on the best available interpretation, does the line refute Nozick's challenge, and what should someone who finds the line compelling do? Please answer in American English.
