I've been stuck for the last couple of weeks trying to actually implement a decision rule that takes moral uncertainty seriously (long story: a system I'm building has to make choices while we're openly uncertain between a consequentialist and a deontological view of what it should do), and the part that breaks my brain is intertheoretic comparisons. The literature on this has been going back and forth for years and, as far as I can tell, nobody gives ground. One camp says the rational rule under moral uncertainty is maximize expected choiceworthiness, and since that needs a shared scale across theories, you have to normalize: pick a zero point like the option of doing nothing or the worst available option, or rescale so each theory's spread over the available options has the same variance, something like that. The other camp says this is fake precision: a unit of utilitarian value and a unit of deontological wrongness aren't measured in any common currency, there is no fact of the matter about how they trade off against each other, and any normalization is an arbitrary modeling choice — so the expected-choiceworthiness approach either never gets off the ground or just reports whatever arbitrary scaling you picked. Every proposed normalization gets hit with a counterexample where a different choice of reference options or a different zero point flips the ranking, then someone proposes a fix, then it gets hit again. The debate seems stuck less on moral substance and more on what it would even mean for a comparision between theories to be correct. My working hypothesis: it's not really a moral disagreement — it's a dispute over whether choiceworthiness is the sort of thing that has to be a real, theory-independent quantity before you can do expected-value-style reasoning with it. But I don't trust that hypothesis. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a survey of positions. Is it verbal, is it a disagreement about the semantics of choiceworthiness, is it a substantive disagreement about rationality under uncertainty, or is the question itself ill-posed? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal theorem, a distinction, a different way of framing the question — or is this a debate that should just be left behind?
