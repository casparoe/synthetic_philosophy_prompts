Last week in my social choice seminar we looked at a three-person hiring committee that votes on three statements: (1) the candidate's research is strong, (2) her teaching is strong, and (3) she should be hired. The committee's announced rule is that she should be hired if and only if both (1) and (2) are true, and each member applies that rule consistently. Ana votes yes on research, no on teaching, and no on hiring. Ben votes no on research, yes on teaching, and no on hiring. Cleo votes yes on research, yes on teaching, and yes on hiring. So a majority says her research is strong, a majority says her teaching is strong, and a majority says don't hire her—even though each individual voter is perfectly consistent. The professor called this a discursive dilemma.

After class, two people in my group got stuck on what it actually shows. Maya said the committee is collectively irrational: it says yes to both premises and no to the conclusion that follows from them, which is a flat contradiction you'd never except from an individual reasoner. Devon said that's way too fast. The majority for each premise isn't the committee's "view"—it's just a tally. The committee as a group only holds whatever its decision procedure outputs, and since the procedure was issue-by-issue majority vote, the only real group act was the hiring vote; mixing those tallies into one imagined corporate agent is what creates the appearance of contradiction. Maya shot back that if the committee writes those two premise "yes" votes in its minutes and then rejects the candidate, it is committing itself to an inconsistent set of claims, and an organization that does that can't explain itself to the candidate or to anyone else. Devon replied that this just means the procedure is bad, not that the group is irrational—a board can vote to cut service A, vote to cut service B, and vote against cutting both, and we'd call that a voting paradox or bad agenda-setting, not group irrationality. Maya said that's a distinction without a difference: an aggregation rule that produces conclusions no consistent individual could hold is exactly what a discursive dilemma is suppose to expose, and if the group's outputs aren't held to consistency, there's no sense in calling it accountable judgment at all. Devon said Maya is sneaking in the assumption that a group is a rational agent in the first place, which is the very thing in dispute.
