Ever since I joined our town council, I haven't been able to stop chewing on this. A guy with a snowplow cleared the parking lot at the senior center for free all winter, and we gave him a plaque calling him selfless. A different guy quoted us a price to plow the same lot, and half the council grumbled that he was greedy and should be ashamed to charge the town, becuase seniors use that lot. The second guy's wife had just lost her job and he said outright that he needed the money. I've caught myself doing something similar at work: I think warmly of the colleague who stays late unpaid to finish other people's work, but I privately think the colleague who insists on a raise when her workload doubles is being mercenary. Both of them are just valuing their time—one gives it away and the other won't. Is there really a principle that makes the first admirable and the second greedy, or is that just an anti-self-interest reflex? A friend who's deep into Ayn Rand says our whole picture of altruism is backwards: that treating self-sacrifice as noble and self-interest as something you have to apologize for is the suprising part, not the guy who wants to get paid. I want to know if the ordinary asymmetry can actually be defended against that. Give me the strongest principled justification for praising unpaid giving while looking sideways at charging for your time, and test whether it survives the case where the so-called greedy person genuinely needs the money—and if it doesn't, say whether we should keep the asymmetry anyway, reverse it, or drop it.
