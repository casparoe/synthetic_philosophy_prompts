I am assembling a survey of the main objections that have been raised against Bernard Williams's internal reasons theory. For the purposes of this request, take that theory to be the claim that an agent has a normative reason to φ only if there is a sound deliberative route from the agent's subjective motivational set — the agent's desires, projects, attachments, and similar states, together with what sound practical reasoning (including the correction of false beliefs and the exercise of imagination) can generate from them — to the conclusion of φ-ing. I have so far collected the following objections.

1. The moral-reasons objection. An agent whose motivational set contains no materials from which sound deliberation could generate moral motivation — imagine someone thoroughly callous or self-absorbed — still seems to have reasons not to harm, deceive, or exploit others. Williams's theory appears forced to deny this.

2. The prudential-reasons objection. An agent who is severely depressed, anhedonic, or profoundly self-neglecting may have no sound deliberative route from his or her current motivations to acts of self-care; and someone who is now entirely indifferent to his or her own future suffering still seems to have a reason to prevent that suffering. Internalism appears to deny reasons of self-interest in such cases.

3. The rational-norms objection. Some requirements of practical reason — coherence among one's commitments, and arguably fundamental principles of prudence or morality — do not seem to derive their normative force from whatever an agent happens to want. They present themselves as binding on every rational agent, which sits badly with the idea that reasons are relative to an agent's contingent motivational set.

4. The third-person ascription objection. Our ordinary reason-ascriptions remain apt even when the agent cannot be moved by the consideration — 'he has every reason to see a doctor', 'she has a reason to leave him'. Williams's theory must treat these ascriptions as false or systematically misleading, and the error theory required may be less plausible than rejecting the internalist constraint.

5. The conversion objection, associated with John McDowell. A person can be brought, through ethical upbringing or a transformation of outlook, to find a consideration genuinely reason-giving even though no sound deliberative route from the person's earlier motivational set leads there. If this kind of transformation can be rational rather than merely causal, then reasons can outrun an agent's prior motivational set.

As far as I can tell, these are the standard objections in the literature, and I am not looking for any of them again, nor for a restatement, special case, or combination of them. I would like you to supply one further objection to Williams's internal reasons theory that is different in kind or structure from each of those above. Set the objection out clearly, say how Williams or a defender of the theory would most plausibly reply, and give an honest assessment of whether the new objection succeeds.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among those who work on this topic, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Write in British English. On the very last line, give a short label for the objection you have proposed, in the form \answer{label}.
