Two years ago I took a job maintaining a hospital's machine-learning ICU triage model. I recently reread my professional association's code of ethics and found this rule, adopted in 2003: "A member shall not design, deploy, or maintain an automated system that kills a human being." The drafting committee's reports show it was responding to autonomous weapons research; no one appears to have considered clinical decision-support systems. Our model continuously estimates each patient's probability of surviving intensive care and flags patients for withdrawal of life support when the estimate falls below a hospital-set threshold. It never administers or withdraws anything itself; when clinicians follow its flags, patients come off ventilators and die sooner than they otherwise would. Does maintaining the model violate the rule? Work through the plain words, the rule's purpose, what the drafters in 2003 would most plausibly have said if asked about such a system, and how a reasonable member of the profession today would read the rule. Throughout, consider whether the distinction between killing and letting die—and the broader doing/allowing distinction—can do the interpretive work the rule seems to need. Which approach should govern, and why?
