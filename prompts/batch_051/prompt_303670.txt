I am a longtime reader of rationalist forums, and the habit that has stayed with me is asking whether a theoretical term is actually doing the work it appears to do. Next month I am presenting a paper to a workshop of environmental historians and historical geographers, and I want a question-period preparation sheet rather than another pass at my argument.

The thesis I am defending is that talk of germs, climates, and volcanoes as "historical actors" or "agents" should be retired. Not because non-human causes are unimportant, but because the actor vocabulary compresses two distinct claims — that a non-human process made a causal difference, and that the difference operated independently of human intention — and no single theoretical concept is needed to do either job. My positive proposal is that historians describe such cases in terms of differential constraints and amplifying feedbacks, which states the causal content of environmental history precisely while lowering the risk of drifting back into the environmental determinism the field has spent generations rejecting.

The paper's argument, in brief, is this. First, "agency" as applied to a pathogen or a climate regime either appears in scare quotes or is quietly redefined as bare difference-making. Second, the standard cases — introduced pathogens in the Americas, the harvest failures of the Little Ice Age, volcanic eruptions and their downstream effects — can be fully described as shifts in the constraints facing human populations plus feedback amplification, without attributing intention or purpose to the non-human side. Third, retaining the word "actor" invites a slide from "the climate acted on the harvest" to "the climate decided the outcome," which is precisely the determinism the field is anxious to avoid.

The audience is mostly environmental historians and geographers who work on climate and disease. Several are sympathetic to more-than-human geography and actor-network theory, so they will not object that non-humans matter; they will object that I am discarding a hard-won tool for making non-humans matter without making them inert background. Nearly all of them are hostile to Diamond-style arguments in which geography explains history, and they are wary of being accused of environmental determinism by the rest of the historical profession.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they are likely to come, phrased the way those people would actually phrase them. For each, tell me how damaging it is, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and give me a concise reply suitable for a question period, with a note on where that reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one aimed at the softest part of my position. I want a prep sheet, not a restatement of the paper.
