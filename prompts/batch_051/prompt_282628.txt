I follow an explicit rule in my reasoning and I want to know whether it is defensible. Whenever I am uncertain about anything, I refuse to leave my uncertainty unquantified: I assign a number between 0 and 1 to the claim, require the claim and its negation to sum to 1, and combine my judgments about compound claims exactly as the probability calculus prescribes. When new evidence arrives, I update by Bayes's rule and by nothing else; if my intuition wants a smaller or larger shift, I overrule it. I adopted this rule after learning the argument—I think of it as Cox's theorem—that any consistent system for grading plausibility, once you demand that plausibilities can be ordered and that the ordering be the same however you carve up the evidence, must either be ordinary probability theory or some rescaling of it. The rule feels like rigor. But in practice it keeps fitting badly. Last week a friend asked how likely it was that a political movement would succeed, and I found myself assigning a number to a proposition whose boundaries I could not have stated. During a renovation I caught myself moving my confidence in a contractor's finish date by exactly the Bayesian amount when a permit came through, and the process felt mechanical, as if arithmetic had replaced judgment. I have also forced a set of rival explanations to sum to one even though I knew the list might not be exhaustive. So I am no longer sure my discipline is earned. Is a representation theorem about consistent plausibility judgments really a license to force every real-world opinion into the probabilistic mold, or am I over-reading it? Where does the theorem's authority run out—vague propositions, evidence I can name but not articulate, partitions that may not be exhaustive—and what kind of policy should govern reasoning in those cases? And could my rule be a bias dressed up as precision: a way of smuggling arbitrary numbers into my beliefs under the cover of coherence?
