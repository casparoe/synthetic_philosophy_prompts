I've been going round in circles about metacognition lately — whether thinking about your own thinking actually helps or just gets in the way. Ive got a feeling the answer is 'it depends', but not in the boring way people usually mean. Can you build me a matched pair of thought experiments where the same person is doing the same everyday task under the same pressure, and in one version thinking about their thinking makes them do it better, and in the other version it makes them do it worse. Rules: ordinary life only — no brain damage, no clinical anxiety, no zen monks, no tasks a normal person couldn't do. Don't let the difference be skill level, stakes, tiredness, or that one version is more practiced. The only thing that can change is something about the self-monitoring itself (what it tracks, when it happens, how fine-grained it is, whatever you think is the real lever). Also try to avoid the standard move where a fluent skill gets ruined by paying attention to the mechanics — I've seen that one alot. This is for a blog post, so the cases have to be fair — no making one version of the self-monitoring obviously useless. After the two scenarios, walk me through exactly why metacognition helps in one and hurts in the other, then tell me which single feature of the self-monitoring is doing all the work. Remove that feature and show me the two cases collapse back together. Finish by saying what someone who insists that thinking about thinking is always helpful (or always harmful, your pick) would say to try to sink the example, and say whether their objection actually works. And write the whole thing in British English, I cant stand American spellings.
