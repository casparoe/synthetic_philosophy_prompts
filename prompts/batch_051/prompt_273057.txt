I am currently taking a course on decision theory, and I have been trying to turn Pascal's mugging from an intuition pump into a deductive argument, in order to see exactly where it goes wrong. The conclusion seems unacceptable, but I cannot tell which move is doing the work. I would like an assessment of whether the argument below is valid and, if it is not, an exact identification of which numbered step fails and what the least costly repair would be. If the argument is valid, say so plainly. And if I have formalized the puzzle in a way that distorts it, tell me that directly rather than answering a version I have invented.

Consider a one-off encounter with no reputational effects, no repeat play, and no consequences beyond those stated. Let C be a finite cost, such as the money in your pocket, and let H be the harm the mugger threatens to inflict if you do not pay C; think of H as something arbitrarily large, such as the suffering of an astronomically large number of simulated lives. Let p be the probability you assign to the state in which the threat is genuine.

(1) A rational agent is required to take an act whose expected utility is at least as great as that of every alternative.

(2) The expected utility of act A relative to act B is the sum over each possible state s of p(s) multiplied by the difference in utility between A and B in that state.

(3) Take any threat of the following form: pay C, or H will occur. The state in which the threatener can and will bring about H is a possible one, and a rational agent should assign it some probability p > 0, however small.

(4) In the state where the threat is genuine, refusing rather than paying has utility C − H: you keep C but suffer H. In the state where it is empty, refusing rather than paying has utility C: you keep C and no harm occurs. Paying has utility −C in both kinds of state.

(5) Therefore the expected utility of refusing relative to paying is p(C − H) + (1 − p)C, which simplifies to C − pH.

(6) Since no upper bound has been placed on H, one can always describe a threatened harm H large enough that H > C/p, for any finite C and any p > 0. For any such H, C − pH < 0.

(7) It follows that for any finite cost C and any positive probability p, there is a possible mugger's threat relative to which refusing has lower expected utility than paying.

(8) By (1), an expected-utility maximizer is then rationally required to pay in response to such a threat.

(9) Because C was arbitrary and H can be made larger without limit, the point generalizes: an expected-utility maximizer can be rationally required to hand over any finite amount in order to avert a sufficiently enormous threatened harm, no matter how tiny p is.

Which step, if any, fails? If the argument is valid but rests on a false or optional premise, I want to know which premise and what it costs to reject it. If the generalization in (9) goes beyond what (1)–(8) actually establish, say exactly where the slide occurs. And if this way of setting up the puzzle already presupposes something false about how probability and utility must work, I would rather be told that than have a step condemned only for the sake of giving an answer.
