In a class on evidence and inference, an instructor offers the following analogy. Two diagnostic tests for the same condition, when they are "independent" in the relevant sense, combine cleanly: each contributes its own likelihood ratio, so a 3:1 test combined with a second 3:1 test gives a joint likelihood ratio of 9:1, and a third gives 27:1. Eyewitness corroboration, the instructor says, works the same way. Two witnesses who tell the same story without having colluded are two independent tests; their agreement compounds the evidence multiplicatively, and so, in the absence of evidence of collusion, we are entitled to presume witnesses independent and let corroborating testimony sharply raise our confidence in the account. Audit this analogy. Lay out the correspondence between the diagnostic-test case and the witness case; state precisely what probabilistic condition "independent" must satisfy for likelihood ratios to multiply as claimed; identify the features of the diagnostic-test setting that the analogy quietly imports into the witness setting, distinguishing those that matter for the practical conclusion from those that do not; and say whether the instructor's conclusion survives the disanalogies. If the analogy is better treated as a formal idealization than as a description of actual witnesses, explain how far that idealization can still be used, and give a better analogy if one is available.
