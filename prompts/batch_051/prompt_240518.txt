Bit of a weird one. I write maintainance manuals for food processing equipment at a mid-size company. Last month I finished a 340-page overhaul of our packaging line manual. I checked every procedure more than once, and honestly, if you pointed to any single instruction in the book and asked me whether it's correct, I'd say yes. But in the front of the manual I also wrote the usual disclaimer that says errors and omissions may remain and users should report them. A coworker says I'm being incoherent: if I believe every individual claim in the manual is right, then I'm stuck believing the whole manual is right, so I can't also believe the manual as a whole probably still contains a mistake somewhere. I feel the force of that, but I don't want to give up either belief.

What makes it worse is that the manual case feels a lot like a lottery to me. In a million-ticket lottery, I'd say of each ticket "that one won't win," and I'd also say "some ticket will win," and nobody treats that as a contradiction. So why is my preface any different? Or is my coworker right that the disclaimer is just a fancy way of admitting I was sloppy?

I'd like you to think through this as a philosopher who works on rational belief and logic. Start by brainstorming the different ways someone could try to hold both beliefs without being irrational, then give your honest verdict on my situation. Am I allowed to keep both the claim-by-claim certainty and the whole-book doubt? If it depends, tell me what it depends on. If the field genuinely splits, say how live the other options are rather than pretending there's a settled answer. And if I've set this up in a confused way, or the lottery and the manual aren't really analogous, just say that flat out rather than going along with it — I'd rather know now.
