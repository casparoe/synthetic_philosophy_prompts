Two friends in my linguistics-adjacent reading group, Ana and Ben, spent an evening arguing about whether there is such a thing as bad grammar. Neither is a linguist by training: Ana has been reading descriptive linguistics and sociolinguistics, and Ben works in editing and tends to defend prescriptive standards. I tried to referee and found myself agreeing first with one and then with the other, so I want a careful adjudication. Here is the exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can.

(1) Ana: Any serious linguist will say there's no such thing as bad grammar. Constructions like "between you and I" are produced routinely by native speakers, so they are grammatical in those speakers' grammars. Calling them ungrammatical is just empirically false.

(2) Ben: But no one uses "bad grammar" as a descriptive claim about what a speaker's internalized grammar generates. It's a normative judgment: the construction violates the standard that governs formal writing and public communication. Redefining "grammatical" to mean "actually produced" doesn't refute the normative use; it changes the subject.

(3) Ana: It exposes the subject. The standard you're enforcing is just the variety of a socially powerful group, dressed up as correctness. Rejecting "between you and I" while accepting "between him and me" is standard-language ideology, parallel to calling a regional accent "bad pronunciation." No variety is linguistically more correct than another.

(4) Ben: Even if the standard was selected for arbitrary social reasons, it doesn't follow that no variety can be better in any respect. Take negative concord. "I didn't do nothing" is standard in some varieties, but for hearers outside those varieties the polarity depends on context, whereas "I didn't do anything" removes that ambiguity. That's a functional reason to prefer one form, not mere snobbery.

(5) Ana: Functional preference isn't correctness. And ambiguity is usually resolved by context, prosody, and repair. More importantly, even if standard negation is sometimes clearer, that makes "I didn't do nothing" a worse instrument for a particular job, not bad grammar. A hammer isn't a bad screwdriver.

(6) Ben: But that's all "bad grammar" needs to mean: a worse choice relative to a communicative function governing a context. You've moved from "there's no such thing as bad grammar" to "it's rude to say so," which is a much weaker claim.

(7) Ana: I haven't moved. The grammar itself isn't at fault; the choice of register is. "Bad register choice" and "bad grammar" are different accusations, and only the first is defensible. So my original claim stands.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say what is right and what is overstated or confused in it, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say where the two are talking past each other, what distinction would most have helped resolve the argument, and who, if either, is more right overall.
