Act as a philosopher of perception working in the cognitive penetration debate. Think slowly: before defending a position, brainstorm the candidate principles that could ground the asymmetry, and stress-test each against hard cases.

Ordinary epistemic and legal practice treats some perception as a neutral check on belief and other perception as contaminated by the perceiver's expectations, desires, and stakes. A police officer who has been told a suspect is armed and then reports seeing a gun in the suspect's hand will have that identification treated with suspicion, on the ground that her expectation shaped what she saw. But the same practice treats an observer with no obvious stake as simply reporting how the world is: her perception of a stoplight or a face may serve as independent evidence against which hypotheses, memories, and testimony are checked. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that at least some perception is cognitively penetrable — that beliefs, desires, and expectations can alter perceptual experience itself, not merely its later interpretation or report. Find the strongest principle that could justify degrading the officer's perception while retaining ordinary perception as a neutral input. Is there a difference in kind between the two cases, or only one of degree — and if only one of degree, what should practice do: discount much more ordinary perception, stop singling out the officer's, or redraw the line elsewhere, and at what cost? Throughout, separate what is empirically settled about top-down influences on perception from what is genuinely contested (including whether cognitive penetration occurs at all, as opposed to effects on attention, interpretation, or judgment), and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
