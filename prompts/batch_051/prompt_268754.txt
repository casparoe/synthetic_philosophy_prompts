I run an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of science: around 20 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity, run as a small competition or game rather than any kind of lecture, that makes students feel the force of the underdetermination of theory by evidence — the fact that a finite body of observations is compatible with many mutually incompatible generalizations, and that choosing among them requires assumptions the observations themselves do not provide.

The activity should be runnable in a normal classroom with at most paper, a whiteboard or projector, and play money or tokens; no software and no advance reading. Within the first half of the session, participants should themselves produce at least two incompatible rules or hypotheses that fit exactly the same displayed data and yet make different predictions about a not-yet-observed case. I want a staged design in which the data are secretly generated by the less natural, more contrived of the candidate rules, so that the intuitively obvious rule — the one most students will have settled on — is revealed to be false in the next round. The contrived generator should agree exactly with the natural rule on every displayed observation and diverge only on the held-out case, so that no amount of looking back at the old data could have settled it. The shock of that reveal, not a facilitator's explanation, should do the teaching.

Please give me:

1. Rules and setup. How teams are formed for 20 students, exactly what a "hypothesis" or "rule" must look like to count (for example, statable in one sentence, deterministic, computable by hand for small inputs), how predictions are submitted, and how many rounds there are.

2. Materials. The precise small data sets to display in each round, what actually generated them, how the data are presented, and print-ready instructions if anything needs to be handed out. Do not use real money; give a scoring or payoff scheme that creates genuine small stakes and rewards successful prediction, with an explicit note that scoring should not penalize the contrived rule merely for being ugly or complicated.

3. A minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, with no lecture up front and no mention of underdetermination or philosophers' names until the debrief.

4. A debrief script structure that maps what happened in each round onto the idea: that the old data did not discriminate between the rules; that the rules diverged only in their predictions for a new case; that any finite set of data points can be fit by indefinitely many distinct rules; that rejecting the contrived rule requires a non-empirical commitment to something like simplicity or natural projectibility; and that auxiliary assumptions can always absorb an apparent failure if one is willing to complicate the hypothesis. Keep the terms accessible: only after students have articulated the experience themselves should labels like underdetermination — and, if you use them, Duhem's point about auxiliary hypotheses or Goodman's point about projectible predicates — be introduced.

5. Predictable failures the first time I run it, and how to handle each. Think about these at a minimum: students refusing to propose contrived or "cheating" rules because they feel those rules are not credible; a team insisting the data must contain measurement error; someone proposing a rule too vague or too complicated to compute by hand; time overruns; and the class tipping into a general skepticism about science instead of a precise lesson about what inductive practice assumes.

6. An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular, in real science no hidden generating function is ever revealed to settle which hypothesis was true, and real data are noisy in ways this design may deliberately suppress. Say which part of the philosophical point must therefore be supplied by the facilitator in the debrief rather than by the game itself.
