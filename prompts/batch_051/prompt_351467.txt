Since retiring from a chair in geochemistry, I have been spending my time on the philosophy of science and, increasingly, on philosophical questions about rational choice; it has made me aware of how rarely the principles invoked in real funding decisions are made explicit. I am now a trustee of a charitable trust that supports long-term environmental observation programmes, and we face a difficult decision at Thursday's board meeting. For fifteen years the trust has funded a project intended to establish a permanent network of deep borehole sensors capable of verifying the long-term containment of carbon dioxide sequestered in a basalt formation. The project is currently five years behind schedule and has absorbed roughly £46 million, almost all of the original programme allocation. Our independent review panel now reports that the sensors cannot reach the detection threshold needed to meet the project's stated aim, and it judges that the probability of ever meeting that aim is very low. The panel adds, however, that with a further three years of work the network could be repurposed to address a more modest question about seasonal groundwater movement.

The board is split. Some trustees argue that to stop now would be to admit that so large a past outlay was wasted; they also point out that the partner institutions have built staff and research plans around the project, and that our donors expect the trust to act with resolve rather than abandon long investments. Others insist that the past expenditure is gone and cannot be recovered, and that the only defensible question is whether any further funding is justified by what it would buy from this point onwards. I have been asked to prepare the written recommendation that will be circulated before the meeting. It should set out a definite course of action, with a time frame and, if further funding is recommended at all, a ceiling on additional expenditure. It must also take seriously the strongest argument for the option you do not finally recommend, rather than dismissing it as a mere fallacy. Please write the document in British English.
