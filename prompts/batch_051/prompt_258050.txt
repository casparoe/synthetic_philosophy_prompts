I keep going back and forth about vagueness, and I want to turn it into a short essay for other developers, but I'm not sure my central analogy is actually fair. So I'm hoping someone will kick the tires on the idea itself — not point me to a reading list.

Here's the sketch. In ML you train a classifier to label "heap" vs "not heap" from grain counts, and after training the model has an actual, fully determinate decision function. The weights give a crisp yes/no cutoff for every possible input, even if we never probe most of them and couldn't say by hand where the boundary ended up. That strikes me as epistemicism: there's a fact of the matter about whether 11,247 grains is a heap, we just can't know it.

Supervaluationism, as I understand it, says instead that the concept is a family of admissible sharpenings. A sentence is true if it comes out true on all of them, false if false on all of them, and otherwise it's neither true nor false. So "10,000 grains is a heap" gets no truth value because some sharpenings put the cutoff above 10,000 and some below.

My essay would push the analogy that epistemicism treats one trained model as the hidden truth, while supervaluationism treats the concept as an ensemble of acceptable models and only counts a classification when the whole ensemble agrees. But I've got a nagging worry that this setup is rigged: it assumes from the start that there's a space of candidate models sitting around, and the only question is whether one or many of them matter. An epistemicist would say no, there's just the one real boundary, full stop. And a supervaluationist might say that a penumbral case is not like two ensembles disagreeing—it's more like a gap in the language itself.

So: (1) is the classifier-vs-ensemble analogy fair to at least one of these views, and which one? (2) Where does it start to leak? (3) If you were editing this essay for software engineers, would you keep the analogy, or would something like fuzzy logic or type systems get the idea across better? Mostly I want to know if I'm smuggling in an assumption that begs the question against one side.
