I'm putting together a list of objections to Chesterton's fence — the Burke-ish principle that you shouldnt tear down an institution or practice until you know why it was put there, because the fact that it survived is meant to be some evidence that it does something worth keeping. So far I have these:

- Survival isn't function. A practice can stick around through drift, path-dependence, or just being costly to remove, without solving anyone's problem.
- The original reason might be a bad one. A fence can survive because it protected a monopoly or kept some group out, not because it served a defensible purpose.
- Even a real original reason can go stale. The principle tells you to find out why before acting, but says nothing about what to do once you've found out and the reason no longer applies.
- The asymmetry is doing all the work. It demands caution about change but not about keeping things as they are, even when the fence is actively harmful.
- You can almost always invent a plausible function after the fact, so the principle can never lose; it just rationalizes whatever already exists.

I keep feeling there's a well-known objection I'm forgetting that isnt on this list. What is it? Don't just reword one of these or give me a historical illustration of one — I want an objection with a different structure. Then say whether it's actually a problem for the principle, or just another way of pressing the burden-of-proof point.
