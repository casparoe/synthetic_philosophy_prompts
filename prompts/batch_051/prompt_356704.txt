For an upper-level unit on environmental value theory, I want to treat Hayao Miyazaki's 1997 animated film Princess Mononoke as a philosophical case study rather than as an ecology fable with a clean moral. The course has already distinguished three ways of valuing nature: intrinsic value, or worth possessed by the natural world independently of human interests; instrumental value, or worth only as a means to human ends; and relational value, or worth that arises within relationships of care, respect, or interdependence between humans and the more-than-human world. Read the film through that taxonomy, with the aim of finding both where the film clarifies it and where the film breaks it.

Where does the film present the forest as intrinsically valuable, and do those presentations survive the film's sympathy for Iron Town, whose people depend on clearing the forest and whose leader, Lady Eboshi, is given real moral standing rather than being drawn as a simple villain? Where does the film treat the forest instrumentally, and does it condemn every instrumental use or only extractive domination? Is Ashitaka's effort to see both forest and town without hatred a genuine relational alternative, or is it instrumentalism with a more reverent manner? Does San's uncompromising defense of the forest function as the voice of intrinsic value, and what does the film ultimately do with that voice? The ending, in which the Forest Spirit dies and the landscape returns but not as it was, is the key test: which of the three categories, if any, does it vindicate?

Argue for a single considered reading, point to the scenes and character choices that support it, and then honestly flag the moments that pull against it, including any place where the film resolves the question too cheaply or simply stops asking it. Conclude by saying what the film contributes to this debate that a standard philosophy article on intrinsic versus relational value does not.
