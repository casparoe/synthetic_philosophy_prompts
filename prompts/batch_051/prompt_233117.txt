A science editor recently pushed back on a long profile I was writing of a researcher who had built an early prototype of a technique now credited to a major laboratory. His objection was that "the discovery was in the air": he cited Newton and Leibniz, and Darwin and Wallace, as evidence that when a result is reached independently at nearly the same time, no single discoverer was necessary. The phrase had a precise job in that conversation. It was meant to dissolve, rather than answer, the question of whether my subject deserved more recognition than standard accounts grant him. I am sure something is too quick in that move, but I cannot state exactly what it is.

Can you lay out the distinct claims someone might be making with "the discovery was in the air"? I can imagine several: a prediction that someone else would have reached the result within a short window; a claim that the discovery was overdetermined by the state of the field; a claim that the identity of the discoverer is arbitrary; a claim about what priority attribution is even for. For each reading, I would like a verdict: is it plausible, is it supported by cases of independent multiple discovery, and does it actually license the editor's conclusion that individual credit should be deflated? Is there a defensible version of the slogan that survives scrutiny but still does the work he wanted it to do, or does the move only look forceful because the phrase blurs those readings together?
