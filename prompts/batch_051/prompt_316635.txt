I am a PhD student in epistemology, and over the past year I have settled into a set of commitments about Cartesian skepticism and its aftermath that I now want checked for internal consistency rather than defended against outside objections. The commitments are these. First, I think the evil demon argument is sound, at least relative to epistemic standards I myself accept. For any ordinary empirical proposition—that I have hands, that I am sitting at a desk—there is a scenario in which the proposition is false while my total evidence is exactly as it is, and I cannot rule that scenario out. So, in the strict sense, I do not know such propositions. Second, I take the problem of the criterion to be genuine and unsolved: any attempt to justify a criterion of knowledge either presupposes the very criterion at issue, invites a regress, or terminates in an arbitrary stipulation, and I do not see that starting from particular knowledge claims rather than from a criterion provides a non-question-begging escape. Third, I accept epistemic closure under known entailment—if someone knows that p and knows that p entails q, then she is in a position to know that q—and I regard the Cartesian argument as making legitimate use of this principle. Fourth, I hold, in a broadly Humean and naturalistic spirit, that the fact that skeptical doubts do not and cannot dislodge our ordinary beliefs is a fact about our psychology to be explained rather than a normative failure to be repaired; custom, instinct, and the needs of common life keep those beliefs in place, and I do not regard this as intellectually embarrassing. Fifth, I nonetheless insist that a purely causal-psychological story about belief fixation is incomplete: the fact that we cannot help believing something in ordinary life does not by itself amount to its being warranted, and I want to keep a principled line between ordinary beliefs that are warranted and beliefs that are merely psychologically irresistible—for example, between my belief that there is a table in front of me and someone's evidence-resistant belief in a conspiracy theory.

I am not sure where the difficulty lies, assuming there is one. I can see that several pairs of these commitments appear to be in friction, but I cannot tell which frictions are genuine and which dissolve once the commitments are stated carefully. I would like you to treat the set as the object of study: identify the real conflicts, order them by severity, say which apparent conflicts are harmless, and tell me what would have to be revised in each case. I would rather learn that one specific commitment must go, and what abandoning it would cost me, than be assured that everything is fine.
