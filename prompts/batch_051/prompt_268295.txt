I keep seeing the idea that deepfakes do not merely make audio and video less reliable, but that they attack an epistemic backstop: the role that photographs, audio recordings, and video have allegedly played as an independent check on human testimony. I do not yet see what is philosophically distinctive about this threat. Photographs have always been stageable and retouchable, and recordings have always been editable, so competent consumers have long treated them with a degree of caution. Why, then, should deepfakes require anything beyond assigning a lower prior to audio-visual evidence, much as one rationally lowers one's trust in testimony after catching a series of liars? Is it a mistake to treat the epistemic backstop as simply one more fallible channel of evidence, or is that the correct way to understand it?
