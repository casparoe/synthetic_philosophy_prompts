Next month I'm chairing a session with a political theorist who has been pushing a Ulysses-contract account of constitutionalism. His line, in outline, is that constitutional devices — supermajority rules, separated powers, entrenched bills of rights — are best understood as a polity tying itself to the mast in advance, because the polity knows it will have predictably dangerous episodes: panics after an attack, deficits timed to elections, the scapegoating of unpopular groups. He's not a cartoon version of this; he's thought about the obvious objection that the people actually bound are not the people who did the binding. His standard move is to say that the amendment clause is the rope, not the mast — later majorities can unbind themselves whenever they can assemble a large enough coalition over a long enough period, and the delay and supermajority burden are the cooling-off mechanism, not a denial of popular sovereignty.

Could you draft eight or so interview questions that make this amendment-clause reply progressively harder for him to invoke — each with a follow-up aimed at the answer he's most likely give? I want at least one question on each of these: whether a whole society can be said to have the kind of single persisting will or self over time that self-binding requires; the difference between binding yourself and one generation (or faction) binding another; whether very rigid amendment rules or entrenched economic arrangements start to look less like Ulysses and more like the crew keeping him tied even after the Sirens are gone; and the bootstrap worry that the amendment procedure is itself one of the ropes, so later generations can only loosen it on the terms set by people now dead. I don't want generic objections to constitutionalism — the questions should force him to do real work inside his own picture.
