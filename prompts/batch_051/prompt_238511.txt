I run an MA programme in translation studies, and I've been asked to give a five-minute welcome speech at our autumn colloquium. The audience is going to be split, almost evenly, between two groups who are barely talking to each other: working literary translators, a lot of whom are quietly furious that the university has started using machine translation for adminstration and even some teaching materials; and a delegation from the university's 'AI in the humanities' initiative, who keep saying the technology will free translators up for 'creative work' — which, honestly, just makes the translators angrier. I don't want the speech to sentimentalise the human touch, and I also don't want to announce that the machine has won. My own view is something like Hofstadter's worry: a statistical system can produce a flawlessly fluent sentence without any event of understanding happening anywhere in the system, and the real mistake is treating 'translation' as a single, univocal activity rather than several different ones that happen to share a name.

Could you write the opening remarks around that idea? It has to actually welcome people (it is the opening of a colloquium, not a polemic), but it also ought to make each half of the room see why the other half isn't simply stupid or sentimental. Please write it in American English spelling. And try not to let it become a listicle of 'things machines still can't do' — that feels defensive and will age badly. If you need to name the view, paraphrasing is fine; no need for citations.
