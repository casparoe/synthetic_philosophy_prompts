Here is an argument I have been unable to settle, and I suspect it proves too much. (1) If knowledge is valuable for its own sake, then any piece of knowledge has at least some intrinsic value. (2) If two kinds of knowledge both have intrinsic value, a preference for funding one over the other must be grounded either in a difference in the degree of their intrinsic value or in some further, non-intrinsic consideration. (3) Compare cosmological knowledge with a carefully compiled census of the blades of grass in one yard; neither need have much practical application, so it is hard to locate a further non-intrinsic consideration that favors cosmology. (4) The preference must therefore be grounded in a difference in degree of intrinsic value. (5) But there seems to be no principled way to assign cosmological knowledge higher intrinsic value than grass-count knowledge: candidates such as generality, depth, or systematicity either stop short of covering everything we want to fund or collapse into disguised practical benefit. (6) So the preference for funding cosmology cannot be grounded in the intrinsic value of knowledge after all. (7) Therefore, if we refuse to fund grass censuses, we must either admit that intrinsic value cannot justify funding cosmology, leaving only applications as its defense, or accept that our funding preferences are arbitrary. Both alternatives strike me as false. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would rather keep the intrinsic-value defense of cosmology than accept equal funding for grass censuses, but I want to know what keeping it commits me to.
