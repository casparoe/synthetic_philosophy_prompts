Using Problema I of Fear and Trembling, explain what Johannes de Silentio means by a teleological suspension of the ethical, and how he distinguishes the knight of faith from the tragic hero. Then say whether, on de Silentio's view, this suspension can actually be justified in ethical terms.

And here's the student's answer, copied verbatim:

De Silentio says the ethical is the universal, and our duty is to the universal. The tragic hero, like Agamemnon sacrificing Iphigenia, stays inside the ethical: he does something terrible, but he does it for a higher ethical end, namely the good of the people, so his act can be spoken and understood by others. Abraham is different because he has no higher ethical end for killing Isaac; the command to do it has no universal meaning. His situation is a teleological suspension of the ethical: the whole sphere of duty gets set aside for a higher telos, which is his absolute duty to God. This duty is not universal because it's a private relation between him and God, which is why Abraham can't speak and Sarah and Isaac couldn't be made to understand him. Unlike the tragic hero, Abraham is a knight of faith, and the knight of faith makes the double movement: first infinite resignation, giving up Isaac, and then the movement of faith by virtue of the absurd, where he expects to get Isaac back. On the last part of the question: de Silentio does think the suspension can be justified in ethical terms, just not in everyday ethical terms. God is the ground of the ethical, so the absolute duty to God doesn't really leave ethics behind; it elevates it. Abraham's act is therefor more ethical than Agamemnon's, not less, because he obeys the highest ethical authority. The real point is that he trusts God to restore Isaac, so in the long run the act isn't against the ethical at all.
