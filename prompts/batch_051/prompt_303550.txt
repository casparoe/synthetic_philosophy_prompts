I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course on nineteenth-century German philosophy, and I am grading a short-answer question on Schopenhauer's aesthetics. The exam question read as follows: "With reference to Schopenhauer's hierarchy of the arts, explain the special status he assigns to music. In your answer, specify what the various arts represent, why music stands apart from them, and what music's distinctive relation to the will is supposed to be."

The student wrote the following response:

Schopenhauer treats the arts as successive presentations of the Platonic Ideas, which he identifies with the various grades of the will's objectification. Architecture, landscape painting, historical painting, and poetry form a rising hierarchy, from the lowest grades of the will—gravity, rigidity, and cohesion—up to the complexities of human motivation and character. In all the representational arts, aesthetic experience is disinterested: the subject ceases to be an individual pursuing private ends and becomes a pure, will-less subject of knowledge, contemplating Ideas. Music, however, is the great exception. Music does not depict the Ideas, because it is not a representation at all; it is a direct copy of the will itself. That is why, for Schopenhauer, music affects us with an immediacy and power that no representational art can match. One might put the point by saying that music presents the highest of the Ideas—the Idea of the will. Because music copies the will's restless strivings, it achieves its effect by arousing and amplifying the very emotions it copies: it lets the listener feel their own longing and satisfaction more vividly than they otherwise would. Thus music alone addresses not the phenomenon but the will itself, and its value lies in the intensity of feeling it excites.
