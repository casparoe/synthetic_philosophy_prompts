I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on joint action, and I've been feeding my advisor little vignettes about when people act together versus merely in parallel. He's got a long memory and he's surprisingly consistent, but I've never managed to pin down the exact rule. I'm about to present a new case in our reading group and I want to predict what he'll say for consistency with his prior calls.

Here are his calls from my notes.

Joint action, according to him:
1. Two colleagues at a conference decide over coffee to carry a heavy equipment crate to the loading dock. They count down, lift, and steer it around corners, adjusting to each other the whole way.
2. A family decides in advance to host a block party. They split the errands, and on the day one grills while another sets up chairs, with a quick check-in before they start.
3. Two street musicians catch each other's eye, one says "same key, on three," and they play a tune together, each listening and adjusting as they go.

Not joint action, according to him:
4. Two shoppers both lunge to catch a falling display and each grabs a side, keeping it from tipping. They never look at each other or intend to do it together; each was just trying to catch it by himself.
5. Two people walking the same route happen to fall into step. Each notices and privately enjoys the accidental unison, but neither intends that they walk together, and either would be fine if the other peeled off.
6. Two friends agree to meet at a café, then one orders a sandwich and the other a coffee and they sit quietly reading. He says the meeting was planned, but the eating and reading aren't something they do together.

The new case is one I've never brought to him. Two grad students are in a shared office around 10pm when they both realize a jointly-authored grant report is due at midnight and neither can finish it alone. Without saying a word, or even making eye contact, one opens the shared document and starts fixing the intro. The other notices and starts rewriting the discussion. They tacitly split what's left by simply not touching the sections the other has claimed; each intends to do his section, knows the other is doing the other section, and wants the report finished. They never agree to coordinate, and the only thing that makes their activity public is the shared document itself.

My question: if he's consistent with his track record, what should I predict he'll say about the new case, and which of his earlier cases is the closest analog? If his prior calls genuinely underdetermine it, tell me which principle he'd be adopting with each possible verdict. And give me one further vignette I could run past him that would cleanly seperate the candidate principles. Answer in American English, please.
