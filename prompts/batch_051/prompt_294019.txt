Me and my friend got into a long argument last night about whether death is actually bad for you, and he kept falling back on Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus. He read me the part where Epicurus says:

"Accustom thyself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply sentience, and death is the privation of all sentience; therefore a right understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life an illimitable time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality."

His whole point was that harm and benefit only show up in sentience, so once there's no sentience, there's no subject left to be harmed or benefited. Okay. But then we started fighting about cryonics. Suppose someone is declared legally dead, brain activity undetectable, and they get vitrified, and a hundred years later they're revived with their memories and experiences intact. Forget wether the technology ever actually gets there — just grant that the revival happens. What should Epicurus say about the interval between vitrification and revival? He says death is the privation of all sentience, and a vitrified body has no sentience during that stretch, so on his own wording the person would seem to be in exactly the state he says is nothing to us. But the reason that state is nothing to us, for him, is that there's no sentient subject at all, not that no sentient subject will ever exist again. So I want you to predict/extend Epicurus's view to a working-cryonics world. Would he bite the bullet and say the suspended years were nothing to the person, and the only sensible care about them is instrumental? Would he instead say a still-revivable person wasn't really dead, and that 'death' in his argument always meant irreversible loss of sentience? Or would he have to redraw the premise, maybe saying harm and benefit are tied to sentient episodes at particular times, full stop? I'm asking for an argued prediction of what the view from the letter commits him to, not a survey of cryonics.
