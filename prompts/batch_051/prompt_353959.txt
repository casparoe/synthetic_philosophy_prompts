You are to act as a senior philosopher of mind with a reputation for brutal but constructive Q&A preparation, and who knows both the private language argument and the sociology of consciousness studies well. I am giving a paper next month to an interdisciplinary consciousness research society, and I need a question-period prep sheet.

My thesis is that the project of a pure first-person science of consciousness—one that starts from subjects' private ostensive identification of their own phenomenal states, of the kind sometimes proposed for advanced meditation practitioners—cannot get off the ground. The private language argument, I will claim, shows that no one can establish a practice of picking out a state as the same one again by introspection alone; what looks like a private act of identification only works because it is embedded in a public, normative practice of describing, correcting, and calibrating reports. I will argue that this is not a dismissal of introspection reports. On the contrary, such reports can be scientifically useful, but only once they are treated as moves in a shared language game rather than as readouts from a private inner ostension. The apparently pure first-person method, I will conclude, is an implicitly social method once you look at what makes its identifications stable.

The audience is not a Wittgenstein crowd. Roughly half are analytic philosophers of mind, mostly representationalists or illusionists about phenomenal consciousness, who tend to regard the private language argument as refuted or irrelevant to cognitive science. A small but vocal group of enactivists and ecological psychologists will like my conclusion but will suspect I have made the whole issue linguistic rather than about skilled embodied practice. There will be at least one meditation researcher who believes long-term practitioners report subtle states with a reliability ordinary subjects lack. And there are several phenomenologists in the Husserl and Merleau-Ponty tradition who accept the idea of a disciplined first-person method and will resist my deflation of its autonomy. Please do not waste preparation time on exegetical objections from Wittgenstein specialists; there will be none in the room.

Before writing the final sheet, brainstorm the objections each faction is likely to raise in the terms they would actually use, then select and merge those that overlap. Then produce the prep sheet in order of expected damage within rough likelihood bands: each objection phrased the way that audience member would put it in a Q&A, with a two-minute reply, an explicit note on where the reply is weakest, what I should concede up front, and what I should hold firm on. End with the single question I should most hope nobody asks, and explain why it would be the most damaging.
