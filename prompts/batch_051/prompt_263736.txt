I've been arguing with two friends about a governance mechanism called futarchy, and I have a feeling the disagreement is less about economics than about what the mechanism presupposes. The design runs roughly like this. A community first selects a social welfare metric: real median household income over a ten-year window, average self-reported life satisfaction, a combined index, something like that. For each policy proposal, a prediction market is opened in which a contract pays out according to the realized value of that metric if the proposal is implemented. Whichever proposal commands the highest market price gets enacted. Supporters say this is almost metaphysically neutral: the citizens choose the end by choosing the metric, and the market merely supplies the best available means-end inference. Markets aggregate dispersed information; voting aggregates preferences; so using markets for means and voting for ends just applies each institution to the job it is good at.

But I suspect the mechanism is not nearly so neutral. It seems to presuppose that the public interest can be represented by a single scalar that is exogenous to day-to-day politics, that disagreements about means can be separated from disagreements about ends, and that a conditional market price encodes an impartial best judgment about the future rather than a wager by people with unequal wealth, unequal information, and speculative horizons that may not match the policy's time horizon. So my question is: what view of political legitimacy, value, and knowledge does a functioning futarchy presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory carefully, but also distinguish what the design would strictly require from what it merely fits well with—for example, does it require some form of welfarism, or could a proceduralist accept it? What would have to change about the design if the presupposed view were replaced by a deliberative account of legitimacy, or by the view that preference aggregation for ends and belief aggregation for means are not cleanly separable?
