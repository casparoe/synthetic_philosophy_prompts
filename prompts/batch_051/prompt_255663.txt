I am a high school student, and I have been reading about linguistics outside of class. I find myself attracted to a picture of language on which knowing a language is, centrally, knowing a system of rules: it explains why we can produce and understand sentences we have never heard before, and why a child acquiring English is not simply memorizing a list of utterances. But I keep tripping over forms like go and went. The past tense of go shares no sound with the present tense, belongs to no pattern I can identify, and seems to be nothing more than a brute historical accident that English speakers have to memorize as a pair. My intuition is not merely that went is an irregular form that a rule fails to cover, but that it is ruleless in a stronger sense: there is nothing about the grammar of English, as opposed to its history, that makes went the past tense of go.

The standard reply, as I understand it, is that the regular past-tense rule applies by default, and that an irregular form such as went is stored in memory and blocks that default. My worry is that this preserves the word rule only by building a list of violations into the system, which may make the claim that grammar is rule-governed unfalsifiable.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes. Give me the strongest version of each side: the strongest case that a suppletive form like went is genuinely outside the rule system, and the strongest case that a rule-based grammar can absorb such forms without becoming vacuous. Then explain whether there is a reading of rule on which both the systematizing theory and my intuition about went can survive, or whether one really must be given up.
