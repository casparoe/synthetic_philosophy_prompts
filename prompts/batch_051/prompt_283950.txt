I trained in physics and now work on foundational questions in cosmology, and for years one argument has kept me pinned between two things I cannot easily give up. On one side is the Boltzmann brain argument, which strikes me as nearly a theorem rather than a speculation. Given what I already accept about the universe — that it has been and will remain in an inflating or de Sitter-like phase, so that it persists indefinitely with random thermal fluctuations — statistical reasoning seems to force the conclusion that disembodied brains briefly assembling with my exact apparent mental state will vastly outnumber ordinary embodied observers with my history. If self-locating probabilities work the way I normally use them, I should expect to be such a fluctuation: a brain that has just now come together with a full load of false or merely apparent memories, in a world whose apparent order is a local accident that will dissolve on a short timescale. The argument is compelling to me precisely because it adds nothing exotic; it simply applies statistical mechanics I already trust to cosmology I already accept.

On the other side is a conviction I cannot shake: I am not a fluctuation. I take myself to have a body, a continuous history, memories that are broadly though imperfectly accurate, and — more importantly — I rely absolutely on the expectation that the regularities I have observed will continue, that the world around me is real and stable, and that my reasoning is in contact with that world. I do not experience this as a theoretical preference that might be outbid by an argument; it is the background against which I evaluate arguments at all. Yet the argument seems to anticipate this objection, because a Boltzmann brain with my apparent memories would, by construction, find the fluctuation hypothesis just as absurd as I do. So my intuition looks as if it is predicted either way and therefore cannot count as evidence against the argument.

I want a negotiated verdict, not a tally of which side wins. Give me the strongest version of the case that the intuition should be debunked and dropped as self-undermining; the strongest version of the case that the theory must bend — for example, because its typicality assumptions, its choice of measure over observers, or its account of rational self-location are the genuine weak point, and what it costs to reject them; and the strongest version of a reconciliation on which both survive, perhaps because my current observations screen off the fluctuation hypothesis, or because the argument only shows that the measure is ill-defined without licensing the first-person expectation. Then tell me which move you would actually make, how confident you are, what specific consideration would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
