I am a novelist working on a cycle of stories about how historical reputation hardens into worship, and I want to return to the oldest theoretical hypothesis about this process: euhemerism, the idea that gods are simply dead people whose deeds were exaggerated over generations until they crossed the threshold from memory into myth. The primary source that keeps pulling me back is Snorri Sturluson's Ynglinga Saga, the opening portion of Heimskringla. There Snorri treats Odin not as a supernatural being but as a mortal chieftain from Asaland, east of the Tanaquisl, a shrewd war leader who leaves his kingdom and migrates north; before his death he has himself marked with a spear point and burned, and he tells his followers he is going to Godheim, where he will await them. After his death, Snorri says, people began to call upon him in battle and offered him sacrifices, so that the man becomes the god. Write a short story that makes that transition psychologically legible without settling whether it is a fraud, a misunderstanding, or a genuine response to something that happened. Anchor the story in a small community of chroniclers, skalds, or court attendants who knew the leader as a faulted human being and then watch the cult form over the next generation. I would like the story to dramatize one concrete mechanism by which realistic memories get discarded and selected memories get magnified: perhaps the exigencies of a funeral, a succession dispute, a military crisis, or the pressure of composing memorial verse. The story should not simply illustrate the abstract thesis; it should make the reader feel the force of the question whether the true person and the god are continuous or whether deification requires a kind of enshrinement that kills the history it claims to preserve.
