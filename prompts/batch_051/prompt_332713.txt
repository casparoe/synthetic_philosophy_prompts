I am giving a talk next month at a university philosophy of biology colloquium, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a polished defense. My thesis, in compressed form, is this. Popper's early verdict that Darwinism functions as a metaphysical or heuristic research program rather than an empirically testable theory was too strong, but his later retraction was right for the wrong reasons. He effectively conceded that individual claims about adaptations or selective histories can be tested, while leaving unclear how the central Darwinian principle is itself testable. I will argue that the central principle — that heritable variation and differential reproduction can produce adaptation — earns empirical content not by issuing point-predictions about which traits will evolve or which lineages will persist, but by ruling out certain combinations of trait, environment, and reproductive regime that are logically possible but incompatible with the constraints of the selectionist research program. Once fitness is required to be specified ecologically rather than read off from observed reproductive outcomes, the claim that selection favors the fitter variant stops being a tautology and begins to constrain which populations can be modeled as products of selection. On my account, the testability of Darwinism is a property of the constraints the program imposes on models, not of its ability to make risky predictions about future evolution.

The audience is known to me, and it is not uniformly sympathetic. About half are philosophers of biology with model-based and likelihoodist sympathies who distrust falsificationist language. Two are working evolutionary biologists who regard the testability of natural selection as settled by field and laboratory studies and will see the topic as a philosophers' shadowboxing match. One is a committed Popperian who treats falsifiability as the demarcation criterion and will defend Popper's earlier verdict against my reading of the retraction. One works in evolutionary developmental biology and will argue that my focus on natural selection as the core principle smuggles in adaptationism and neglects constraint, drift, and plasticity.

Draft the prep sheet. Organize it as the objections I am likely to get, in roughly the order they will come. For each objection: identify who will raise it and the exact shape it will take in that person's vocabulary; assess how damaging it is; say what I should concede up front and what I should hold; and give a reply that can be delivered in about two minutes. Include the objection I should be hoping nobody raises, and flag the point in my own position that is softest and that I should prepare to defend rather than hope the audience misses. Write the prep sheet in American English.
