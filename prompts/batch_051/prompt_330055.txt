The usual argument for letting voters decide policy by ballot initiative is that it lets the popular will speak cleanly on a single, well-defined question, where a legislature might be captured or just refuse to choose. But actual ballots, say in California, are rarely that clean: one proposition will lock in new spending and another will cap the tax that was meant to pay for it, and people vote yes/no on each row independently, with no vote on the package the two jointly create. The median supporter of one needn't be the median supporter of the other, so the resulting bundle may be one no coherent majority or median ever endorsed. Does the classic case for direct democracy survive this? Or was it silently assuming the questions are seperable and that a single popular will can be read off each row? If it doesn't survive, is that a repairable point about ballot design, or does the argument lose the precise thing it was leaning on? I'm asking about the fate of the argument, not about how to write better ballot measures.
