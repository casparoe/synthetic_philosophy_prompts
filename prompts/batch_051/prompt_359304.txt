I'm a philosophy of physics grad student, and a friend from the decision theory group gave me his one-paragraph version of Lewis's Principal Principle after I admitted I'd never read the original. His version went like this: the Principal Principle says a rational agent's credence in a chance outcome has to equal the objective chance whenever she knows it — chance is an expert about what will happen, and you defer to experts by using their numbers as your own. So if the chance of a particular atom decaying in the next hour is .3 and you know that, you're rationally required to be exactly .3 that it decays; if you insist on being .6 anyway, you're letting your gut get a bigger vote than physics. He also said the principle is basically Lewis's definition of objective chance: chance just is whatever credence an ideally rational agent would settle on about the outcome, given full information about the chance setup. From that he drew the conclusion that the principle rules out deterministic chance, since if the real chances were only 0 and 1, a rational agent with full information about the setup would be certain of every future fact, which is absurd, so determinism and the Principal Principle can't go together. I have a feeling he's gliding over the caveats — I vaguely remember something about "inadmissible evidence," and something about chances being relative to a time and a history — but I can't tell which parts of his version are harmless shorthand and which are actually off. Can you go through his summary sentence by sentence, say what a defender of the Principal Principle would accept and where they'd push back, and tell me whether his deterministic conclusion survives once the caveats are filled in?
