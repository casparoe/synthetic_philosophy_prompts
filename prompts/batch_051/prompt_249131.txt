Consider two much-discussed problems in epistemology: the problem of easy knowledge and the bootstrapping problem. Both begin from the thought that a subject can form beliefs by trusting a source—sense perception, say, or a fuel gauge—without first having independent evidence that the source is reliable. In the easy knowledge problem, a subject moves in a single deductive step from such a basic deliverance of the source (she sees that the wall is red) to the denial of a defeating alternative she has not independently ruled out (the wall is not white and illuminated by red light). In the bootstrapping problem, a subject compiles a track record of a source's outputs together with the states of the world those outputs report, and then infers inductively that the source is reliable, even though the world-states on one side of each correlation are accepted only on that very source's authority.

Give the clearest version of each problem you can, and then compare them. In particular: Do they involve the same kind of inference, or different kinds? Do they put pressure on the same epistemological commitments, or on different ones—for example, closure under known entailment, the permissibility of basic knowledge, reliabilism, or internalism about justification? Is one problem best understood as a special case of the other, and if so, does it matter for how the underlying circularity should be diagnosed? End by arguing for a classification: are these ultimately one problem or two, and what hangs on that classification for an account of when a source may legitimately vouch for its own reliability?
