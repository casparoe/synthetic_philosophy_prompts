I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I run a technical onboarding program for engineers who work on autonomous systems. The program includes a short written exercise on the responsibility gap in autonomous systems. Below is the question we assigned and one candidate's answer. I want an impartial second opinion on how to grade this answer, so I will not say what I think of it myself.

Question (assigned to candidates):
An autonomous credit-screening system makes final approval and rejection decisions without human review. After launch, the team discovers that the system rejects applicants from one postal code at several times the rate of otherwise similar applicants. No engineer intended this disparity, and the system passed the team's pre-launch fairness checks. Explain what the responsibility gap is, whether this case exhibits one, and evaluate whether each of the following measures closes it: (i) fining the company that deployed the system; (ii) requiring a human employee to approve every final decision.

Candidate answer:
The responsibility gap is the situation in which a harmful outcome occurs but there is no human being who can appropriately be held morally responsible for it. It arises for autonomous systems because the standard conditions for responsibility — intention, foresight, and control — are distributed across many people and partly handed over to a learned algorithm. The credit-screening case appears to exhibit exactly this gap. The data scientists chose the objective function, the engineers built the pipeline, the product manager chose which features were included, and the compliance team ran the pre-launch fairness checks. None of them intended the neighborhood disparity; each followed standard practice and acted in good faith. So there is a harmful outcome and no obvious responsible human.

There are two standard responses. The first is to impose fines or damages on the company that deployed the system. This closes the gap because the company, as a legal person, becomes the responsible party: it can be punished, it can be required to compensate the applicants, and the penalty gives the institution a reason to fix the model. Even if no individual engineer is blamed, the organization itself is held responsible, and that is what matters in practice.

The second response is to require a human employee to approve every final credit decision. Once that requirement is in place, every rejected applicant corresponds to a named employee who signed off on the rejection. That employee is responsible for the outcome, because they always had the power to override the model and were on notice that the model could be biased. So requiring human approval guarantees that the gap is closed: there will always be an identifiable human who can be blamed.

Taken together, the two responses close the gap on both of its fronts. The fine addresses the collective, legal dimension; the human-approval requirement addresses the individual, moral dimension. Once both are implemented, what remains is not a conceptual problem but an engineering one: making the model interpretable enough that the human approver can review it quickly and competently.
