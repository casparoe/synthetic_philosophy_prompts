I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and for the past two semesters I've been a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course on the rationality of religious belief. In my discussion sections I keep running into an argumentative move whose exact structure I cannot find named anywhere. A student will insist that atheism is not a claim at all—it is merely the absence of a belief—and will say, on that ground, that no evidence or argument can fairly be demanded of them. Then, often in the same exchange, the same student will answer the question whether God exists with an unqualified no, describe religious belief as unreasonable rather than merely unsupported, and treat the theist's premises as obviously false. When someone points out the apparent tension, they retreat to the original formulation: they assert nothing and therefore bear no burden. The striking feature is that the person does not experience this as a shift between two positions. They insist that a single stance—absence of belief—does both jobs at once: it wards off any demand for justification when the demand appears, and it functions as a negative verdict the rest of the time. My question is therefore narrow. Is there an established name in epistemology or the philosophy of religion for this specific maneuver, and how exactly does it differ from the neighboring categories I keep reaching for—the burden of proof, the argument from ignorance, agnosticism as a distinct propositional attitude, and ordinary equivocation between two theses? If there is no established label, coin one and provide its definition, boundary cases, and a test that sorts this maneuver from ordinary agnosticism and from a legitimate refusal to take on a burden one never had. A bare label would be of little use; the carving is what I am after.
