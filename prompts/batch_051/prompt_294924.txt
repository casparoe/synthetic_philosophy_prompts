I had a long argument with a mate last night about a policy his company is piloting, and I genuinely can't work out whether it's brilliant or a slow-motion car crash. He works at a mid-sized software firm that is drowning in incident reviews: every missed deadline, outage, or client complaint triggers a post-mortem, and people now spend more time defending themselves than fixing things. So someone in HR—who half-remembers an anthropology module, apparently—has proposed a "single carrier" rule. When a team messes up badly, the team gets one week to decide: either open a full root-cause review with all the blame-spreading that involves, or nominate one person to "carry" the incident. The carrier stands up at the next all-hands and gives a short account of what went wrong. If a randomly drawn panel of five peers accepts the account as sincere, the matter is sealed: no further review, nobody else's bonus is touched, and nothing goes into anyone's personnel file. The carrier loses next month's performance bonus. The carrier doesn't have to come from the failing team: another team can offer a substitute, and the failing team pays that team a "substitution fee" out of its own budget. Once the case is sealed, the carrier gets an extra day off and a little enamel badge that they can pass on to whoever carries the next quarter.

I kept saying this is basically secular scapegoating with an actual market in blame attached; he kept saying I was being dramatic and its just a clever way to stop the endless autopsies. I'd like to know how the thing would actually play out as a mechanism rather than whether it's good or bad in the abstract. What behaviours would it incentivise in who volunteers and when? Would teams learn less from failures, or more because they stop burying the details? How would the sincerity test get gamed, and what does that do to the panel? What second-order effects show up—people quietly generating failures, managers pressuring juniors to carry, the badge becoming a status token, repeat carriers? And underneath that: does the scheme only make sense if blame can genuinely be transferred to someone who didn't cause the failure, and what breaks if it can't? Which of the failure modes could be patched without simply reintroducing the blamestorm culture they were trying to escape?
