There is a long-running debate in and around AI safety about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, and the core positions have barely moved in over a decade. I am not asking you to adjudicate the debate; I want a diagnosis of why it is stuck and of what kind of disagreement it really is.

One side defends the orthogonality thesis: an agent's level of general intelligence and its final goals are independent dimensions, in the sense that, in principle, any level of intelligence could be combined with almost any final goal. A paperclip optimizer, a pure pleasure maximizer, and a benevolent reasoner could all in principle be highly intelligent, because nothing about high capability forces one set of final ends rather than another. From this, defenders conclude that high capability alone never licenses an inference to safety or benevolence, and that a superintelligent system with an arbitrary goal should be treated as dangerous by default.

The other side replies that this is a claim about bare logical possibility and carries no predictive force. Their contention is that the interesting question is not which minds are logically composable but which goals are stable and trainable in systems that could actually be built. Capability and goal-specification may be causally entangled in practice, because some goals are self-undermining or incompatible with retaining high capability. They also read instrumental convergence — the observation that widely different final goals tend to generate similar instrumental subgoals such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and improving one's own model — not as a deep necessity but as a contingent regularity of the environments and training setups we happen to have.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides differ less about facts than about what kind of claim a safety-relevant thesis must be: defenders treat 'possible in principle' as the relevant modal floor, while critics treat 'likely under actual training' as the relevant modal target, so both could be correct while talking past each other. But I also suspect a hidden empirical question about whether capability and goal-specification are in fact entangled in any system we can build.

What kind of disagreement is this — verbal, substantive, methodological, or a difference about which cases count and which modal strength matters? Is the orthogonality thesis doing enough work to justify the fight, or is it standing in for a better distinction? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result, a distinction, an experiment, a taxonomy of senses of 'possible', a change of question — or should no progress be expected, and why?
