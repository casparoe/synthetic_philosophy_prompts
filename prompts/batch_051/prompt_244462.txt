I have been going around in circles on a point in deterrence theory that seems simple when stated but resists a clean formulation. If a state threatens massive nuclear retaliation to deter an attack, the threat works only if the adversary believes the retaliation would actually follow. But after the attack has occurred, retaliation would be enormously costly and, in strategic terms, largely pointless; a leader who simply chose the best available outcome at that moment would not carry it out. Deterrence therefore appears to depend on a threat whose execution would be irrational precisely at the time when execution would be required, and an adversary who reasoned this way would never find the threat credible. I understand that the threatener must somehow bind its own future self, but I cannot make the underlying structure precise. In particular, why would a rational leader purchase credibility by reducing their own future freedom of choice—for example, by pre-delegating launch authority to a subordinate instructed to follow procedure regardless of the circumstances, by placing tripwire forces whose destruction would set the state's own retaliation in motion, or by deliberately leaving a small residual probability that a crisis will escape political control during brinkmanship? None of those devices makes the retaliatory act itself more attractive; they make it harder to decline. Could you explain what credibility amounts to in this setting, why it can be rational to make threats that would be irrational to carry out, and why arrangements that erode control can be exactly what makes the threat believable? In your answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
