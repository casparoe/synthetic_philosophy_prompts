Okay so I just spent an hour arguing with a friend about advance directives, and I came away half-convinced of a view that also strikes me as cruel in one specific case, and I can't tell which half to keep.

The view (the one I'm half-convinced by) is roughly this. When a competent person writes a directive saying that if they get advanced dementia they refuse life-saving treatment, that is a genuine act of self-government. The earlier self is the one who's values should matter, because she's the only one who can step back and decide what kind of life is worth living for her. The later self has experiential preferences — she likes the nurse, she wants her pudding, she hums along to songs — but those aren't the kind of settled, critical values that make a life hers. If we set the directive aside because the later self seems content, we're treating the later self as a whole new person, and we're also making it impossible for anyone to genuinely refuse a demented future in advance. The friend even added a wrinkle: the directive in question included a clause saying, in effect, that the author knew she might be content later, that this later contentment should not count, and that any consent or refusal her later self gives should be disregarded. The friend calls this a Ulysses pact. I think it's either that, or it's the theory begging the question.

My problem is I keep picturing the actual person. She's in the nursing home with moderate dementia. She's not in pain. She feeds herself messily, she lights up when her daughter visits, she asks to go back to her room when the nurses come to give her the antibiotics, and she says she doesn't want to die. The directive says no antibiotics for pneumonia, and she's got pneumonia. And my gut says: honoring that document when the woman in front of me is protesting is barbaric. The theory says that's cheap sentimentalism and that I'm failing to respect a whole life. The intuition says the theory has quietly decided the woman in the bed doesn't count because her self is inconvenient to the earlier one.

I don't want someone to just tell me which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to go, what it would take for the theory to go, and whether there's a reading on which they're compatible. In particular: is a clause instructing everyone to ignore the later self's dissent even coherent, or does it assume exactly what it's supposed to prove? Does it matter whether the later self is the same person, a different person, or something in between? And what's the difference between honoring a person's own past decision and letting a dead person rule a live one?
