You are a professional philosopher of mathematics, with expertise in the epistemology of proof. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, and I have been trying to pin down what it means to understand a proof, because my work increasingly puts me next to formal proof assistants and language models that produce mathematics. I am going to give you my honest verdicts on a series of cases, plus a first attempt at a principle, and I would like you to take your time and reason carefully about whether any single principle can capture what I actually think, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

Let me start with the cases. First, a student can reproduce the standard proof that the square root of two is irrational line by line, with every step correctly labeled, but when you ask why the proof begins by assuming the fraction is in lowest terms, they can only repeat the next line, and they cannot adapt the argument to show that the square root of three is irrational. I would not say they understand the proof; they are executing a procedure. Second, another student cannot reproduce the proof from memory, but can give the rough structure and explain that the contradiction is that numerator and denominator both turn out even, and can say exactly where the analogous argument does and does not work for the square root of four. I would say they understand it. Third, a proof assistant verifies every line of an enormous formal proof, checking it against a small trusted kernel; I cannot bring myself to say the machine understands the proof, even though it holds all the formal detail. Fourth, a language model asked for a proof of the infinitude of the primes returns the standard Euclidean proof correctly, but its accompanying explanation of why the key construction works is partly wrong in a way that sounds plausible; I am genuinely torn about whether the system counts as understanding the proof. Fifth, a colleague explains a proof entirely through a geometric picture and cannot translate it into symbols; I would say they understand it. Sixth, another colleague can give a fully formal proof of a combinatorial identity by double counting, but reports having no intuition or picture for why it is true; I would still say they understand it, though not deeply. Seventh and last, a case about myself: I can explain the idea behind a standard randomized primality test and even implement it, but I cannot follow the error analysis that turns the heuristic into a theorem, and I do not claim to understand the proof — though I notice that this looks uncomfortably like the second case.

My first draft of a principle is that understanding a proof is having a compressed representation of the proof's underlying "why" that lets you regenerate the argument and adapt it when the assumptions change. But I can immediately see that this would attribute understanding to the proof assistant's small kernel, to a zip file that stores the proof, or to a lossy summarizer — and I do not want to say that those understand anything. So please treat that as a rough seed to repair or replace, not as a constraint. The deliverable I want is this: state the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, as precisely as you can; say which case or cases motivate each clause; apply your principle to a few cases I have not listed, such as a theorem that no single person can hold in their head at once but a group can reconstruct together, an exhaustive computer search that certifies a result by checking thousands of cases with no explanatory summary, and a student who has memorized the correct intuition but can never fill in the details; and then tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the fault is more plausibly in the principle or in the verdict.
