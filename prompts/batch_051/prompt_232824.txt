I work as a software engineer, and I've got a rule I've been following for years that I want to know is actually defensible, or just a bias I've dressed up. When someone argues for a design, or claims to know the root cause of a bug, I check whether that person has actually built or modified the kind of system in question. If they haven't shipped code in that area — say they've only read about it, or they work in docs or on the support side — I discount what they say by a lot, maybe 80 to 90 percent, no matter how confident or articulate their explaination is. My collegue says I'm committing the genetic fallacy, because an argument stands on its own and a non-builder could still be right about a particular claim. But in practice, people who haven't built the thing make confident claims that fall apart the moment they meet real code, and I can't usually reconstruct their reasoning myself without spending a week on it. I lean on this rule mostly in design reviews and when triaging bug reports, not in casual chat. The underlying question, I guess, is whether knowing how to build is a different kind of thing from knowing that a claim about a system is true, and whether the first is good evidence for the second. Is my discount defensible? If so, can it be given a principled rate and clear limits? Or should I try to evaluate the argument on its own terms even when I can't fully check it?
