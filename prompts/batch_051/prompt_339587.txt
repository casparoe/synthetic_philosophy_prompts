I'm a first-year undergraduate and I've been re-reading Descartes on the cogito for a tutorial, and I keep getting stuck on what feels like a u-turn between the Meditations and the Replies. In the Second Meditation he seems to say that every time he brings before his mind the thought that he is thinking, the proposition that he exists is guaranteed true: even a demon with unlimited power could not make that thought false while it is present. But in the reply to the second set of objections he appears to deny that his existence is worked out by a syllogism, on the grounds that a syllogistic derivation would require the extra premiss that whatever thinks exists. He says, instead, that the mind just sees its own existence by a kind of simple intuition. (I'm paraphrasing both passages; I don't have the text to hand.)

So is there a genuine contradiction here? Is the cogito, for Descartes, an inference or an intuition? In the Meditations he uses language like 'therefore' and builds it into the first certain item of knowledge; in the Replies he says it isn't a syllogism and seems to treat it as a single mental act, not a piece of reasoning. Could these be squared by distinguishing the order of discovery from the order of justification, or by saying the explicit sentence 'I think therefore I am' is just the expression of something known non-inferentially? Or did he actually shift his position after the objectors pressed him? I'd like a careful reading of what each passage is doing and a verdict on whether the reconciliation is honest or just special pleading.
