I sit on the Northland Health Priorities Board, a public body that decides which interventions a regional health system will fund and that also sets allocation policy during shortages. We are about to decide whether to fund belmuzin, a new drug for Althoff syndrome, an inherited condition that causes severe, irreversible motor disability by early adulthood and substantially shortens life expectancy. Belmuzin does not improve function; it adds on average four years of life lived at the same low functional level. Our standard QALY model, using the Board's usual utility weight for life with Althoff syndrome, values the benefit at about $160,000 per QALY gained. Patient advocates have commissioned an equal-life-year analysis, which divides the treatment cost of about $192,000 per patient by the four added years and arrives at $48,000 per life-year gained. They also point to a sentence in our own pandemic ICU allocation protocol that forbids deprioritizing patients on the basis of pre-existing disability. The drug's adult patients are highly visible to the Board, and their advocates argue that applying the QALY threshold in this case would be inconsistent with commitments we have already made.

I am trying to work out what our own track record actually commits us to. Over the last several years we have: approved a colorectal-cancer screening program at about $32,000 per QALY, citing our standard threshold of $70,000 per QALY; rejected a late-line pancreatic cancer drug that added 2.1 months at about $300,000 per QALY despite rule-of-rescue testimony; approved enzyme-replacement therapy for a fatal pediatric metabolic disorder at about $780,000 per QALY, citing severity, absence of alternatives, and death in early childhood; approved a road-safety program expected to prevent six traffic deaths per year at $7.8 million annually, reasoning that $1.3 million per statistical life falls below our $3 million value-of-a-statistical-life benchmark; adopted the pandemic ICU triage protocol that ranks by expected survival to discharge and forbids categorical exclusion on grounds of age or pre-existing disability alone; and rejected a drug for severe adult spasticity that improved comfort and mobility at $420,000 per QALY, on the stated ground that above-threshold quality-of-life improvements do not receive the pediatric-severity exception.

What should we decide about belmuzin if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our precedents is the closest analog, what distinct principles fit most of our record and what does each predict in this case, and if the record genuinely underdetermines the decision, what principle would we be adopting under each choice? Finally, what further case, not this one, would best distinguish the competing principles from one another?
