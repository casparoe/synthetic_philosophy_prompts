Take the role of a professional philosopher of science who works on causation full time, and think through this carefully. Start by brainstorming the exact counterfactual worlds that matter for each theory, then briefly reconstruct in your own words how a counterfactual (but-for) account and a simple interventionist account each handle the classic case where Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle, Suzy's rock arrives first, and Billy's would have shattered it anyway. Then work the arithmetic for the variant below before you commit to a verdict.

Heres the variant. Alice always throws a rock at a bottle; her rock reaches and shatters it with probability p. Beth is a backup: Beth throws if and only if Alice does not throw; she loses her nerve with probability 1-r and so doesn't throw, and if she does throw her rock reaches the bottle with probability q. If Alice doesn't throw and Beth doesn't throw, the bottle stays intact. In the actual run, Alice threw, her rock reached, and the bottle shattered; Beth did not throw. Treat the actual shattering as a token event. For a but-for account with threshold t — Alice's throw counts as a cause only if, had Alice not thrown, the shattering would have had probability below t — for what values of r and q (fix t = 0.05) does Alice's throw count as a cause? For a simple interventionist account, which holds Beth fixed at her actual value (not throwing) and asks whether setting Alice's throw to 0 changes the outcome, for what values of p, r, and q is Alice's throw a cause? Where exactly do the two verdicts diverge, and is the divergence a real defect in one account or just two different questions being asked?

Now add Charlie as a backup for Beth on the counterfactual branch: if Alice doesn't throw and Beth also doesn't throw, Charlie throws with probability s, and his rock reaches with probability z. Charlie gets no chance if Beth throws but misses. Keep the actual run the same as before (Alice throws and succeeds; Beth and Charlie do nothing). Recompute both verdicts. Does the gap between the two theories grow or shrink as you stack up not-quite-reliable backups? Which theory, in your view, is tracking what we mean when we say Alice's throw caused the shattering, and does the stacked case make that clearer or murkier?

Work through all of it step by step. At the very end, give a one-sentence bottom line enclosed as \answer{...}, choosing one of: but-for, interventionist, neither, or both-ask-different-things.
