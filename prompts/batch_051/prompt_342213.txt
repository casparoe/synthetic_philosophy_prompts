I've noticed a recurring move in the rationalist forums I read: someone has an episode of the void — a sudden, felt conviction that nothing matters — and then treats that episode as evidence about the world, as though the affect were a clear readout of cosmic meaninglessness. I'm becoming convinced that this is a skipped step: the feeling could simply be a mood state, perhaps the affective signature of fatigue or social disconnection, which the person then mistakes for a metaphysical perception.

I want you to design the strongest test you can that would separate these two readings. On one side is the veridical-perception view: the felt void carries real information about the absence of meaning or value in the world. On the other is the misattribution view: the void is an affective state that gets projected outward into a claim about reality. Make the operationalizations concrete — what would you manipulate or compare, what would you measure, and what pattern of results would count as support for each side? You may consider things like induced sleep restriction, social exclusion, awe manipulations, or mood interventions, but defend whatever design you choose rather than gesturing at it. Then mark the residue: what would even a clean, decisive-looking result fail to settle, for example whether any affect could ever count as a veridical perception of meaning or its absence, or whether "meaning" is the kind of thing operationalization can reach at all. I'm less interested in the armchair argument than in seeing exactly which parts of this dispute can be pulled into contact with evidence and which cannot.

Please write the answer in American English.
