Had a long argument with my friend last night about what actually makes something a disease. His view was simple: a disease is just a part of the body failing to do what it's biologically supposed to do, so heart failure is a disease and that's all there is to it. I kept pushing back with cases, but when he finally asked me what my own view was, I realized I couldn't state one. So I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and now I want to know if there's actually a principle behind them.

Here they are. Strep throat: disease, no question. Mild essential hypertension with no symptoms but a raised risk of stroke later: I have no problem calling that a disease, or at least a medical condition worth treating. A gene variant that triples your lifetime risk of colon cancer but does nothing else: I'd call that a risk factor, not a disease, though I can't say why it's different from the hypertension. A 30-year-old who can't conceive after a year of trying: disease. A 75-year-old who can't conceive: not disease, that's just age. Chronic widespread pain with normal scans and bloodwork: I still call it a disease, or at least a real medical condition, even with no lab finding. Grief two months after losing a spouse: not disease. The same person a year later who can't get out of bed or go to work: that crosses into disease. Nearsightedness: impairment, not disease. Being four-foot-eleven with no other problems: not disease.

Is there one principle that captures all of these, or close? Which of my verdicts should I give up if not? And what does the principle say about the case we argued longest over, obesity, which I keep flip-flopping between calling it a disease and calling it a risk factor? We also kept coming back to whether "disease" is a factual category or a value judgment—so I'd specifically like to know wich of my verdicts force value judgments into the picture and which could be handled by facts about biological malfunction alone.
