I keep running into a weird feature of oaths, in stories and in real life, and I want to know whether it has a name. An oath seems to need an enforcer: you swear by something that can punish you if you break it — a god, the state, a dead relative. But in Greek myth the gods themselves swear by the river Styx, and that's the one oath even they can't break, even though the Styx isn't stronger than Zeus and can't exactly come after him. So the thing that's supposed to guarantee the oath only has its power because the oath gives it power. I've seen the human version too: someone swears on a Bible or their mother's grave, but if they don't actually believe in it, breaking the oath costs them nothing, while if they do believe, it changes what they'll do. Which makes it look like the binding force comes from the person swearing, not from what they swear by, even though the whole ceremony seems designed to outsource it. Is there an established concept for this — an oath whose enforcer is created or authorized by the oath itself — and what are its edges? I don't just want a label; I want to know how it differs from an ordinary contract with an enforcement mechanism, from a promise to yourself, and from a performative speech act, if it differs at all.
