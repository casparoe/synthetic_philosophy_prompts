After I retired from a career in the natural sciences, I began working my way through moral philosophy, and the debate I keep returning to is the one between Aristotelian virtue ethicists and their situationist critics, associated above all with John Doris and Gilbert Harman. As far as I can tell, that debate has not moved in years, even though both sides appear to accept the same experiments and even concentrate on the same passage in Aristotle.

Both sides treat Milgram's obedience studies, Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan experiment, and the low cross-situational consistency of behavior reported in the personality literature as accepted data. Both sides also fix on this passage from Book II, chapter 4 of the Nicomachean Ethics:

"acts done in conformity with the virtues are not done justly or temperately if they themselves are of a certain sort, but only if the agent also is in a certain state of mind when he does them: first he must act with knowledge; secondly he must deliberately choose the act, and choose it for its own sake; and thirdly the act must spring from a fixed and permanent disposition of character."

The situationists read the passage as a psychological claim, and they take the evidence to refute it: conduct varies far more with the situation than with any stable trait, so fixed and permanent dispositions of character are rare or nonexistent, and virtue ethics rests on a false moral psychology. The Aristotelians answer that this attacks a strawman. Aristotle, they insist, never said most people possess such dispositions; virtue is an ideal whose possession may be rare, and the experiments use subjects who never claimed to be virtuous. Still others hold that local, narrow traits, rather than global ones, would suffice for virtue ethics anyway.

What strikes me, as an experimentalist, is that this no longer looks like a dispute that more data would settle. My working hypothesis is that it is really two fields talking past each other under a single word, one doing descriptive moral psychology and the other doing normative theory aimed at an ideal. But I am not sure, because a genuine factual disagreement may also be buried inside it: about how strong the trait literature actually is, or about what degree of psychological realism a normative theory owes to the facts.

I am not asking for a verdict on who wins. I am asking for a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement: is it substantive or verbal; do the two sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, whether a distinction, an experiment of a different design, a formal result, or a change of question, or should we expect the standstill to continue, and why?
