In my constitutional theory course we have been juxtaposing constitutional precommitment — supermajority amendment rules, unamendable clauses, independent courts and central banks — with personal Ulysses contracts such as advance directives and self-exclusion from casinos. In Federalist No. 49, James Madison, writing as Publius, offers one rationale for making constitutional change difficult: "as every appeal to the people would carry an implication of some defect in the government, frequent appeals would, in a great measure, deprive the government of that veneration which time bestows on every thing, and without which perhaps the wisest and freest governments would not possess the requisite stability." I want to understand what this whole constellation of practices presupposes, not merely whether it works. What theory of the agent — of reason, will, and the relation between an earlier and a later self — is presupposed by treating an earlier decision as one that later selves should not easily revise? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories could license it. I am especially interested in whether Madison's rationale commits the constitutional case to a different view of the agent than the personal case requires. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different reconstruction, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
