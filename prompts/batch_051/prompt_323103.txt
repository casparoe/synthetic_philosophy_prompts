I write novels, and I am drafting one whose protagonist is a political theorist. Early in the book she dismantles a colleague's appeal to the veil of ignorance, and I gave her the following argument because it sounded sharp on the page. Now that I am revising, I suspect one of its moves is cleverer than it is sound, and I want to know which before I let a character present it as though it were settled.

(1) The veil of ignorance is a device for choosing principles of justice: you must choose the rules of a society without knowing which person in that society you will turn out to be.

(2) For a device of this kind to actually settle a question of justice, there must be a uniquely rational way to choose when the only thing you do not know is which life you will end up living.

(3) There are at least two candidate ways on offer, and the veil itself does nothing to referee between them. Harsanyi's chooser treats the unknown position as a fair lottery and maximizes expected utility; run through the veil, this yields average utilitarianism. Rawls's chooser refuses to gamble over life prospects when the odds are unknown and applies maximin instead; run through the very same veil, this yields the difference principle, on which the worst-off are made as well off as possible.

(4) The information available inside the veil is the same in both cases. What the chooser knows, and does not know, has not changed.

(5) So whatever is doing the real argumentative work in each derivation must be imported from outside the veil: a prior and substantive stance about risk, and about what one may do with missing probabilities.

(6) But if that is so, the veil itself contributes nothing except a dramatization of a decision rule the chooser already brought with her. Any distributive principle could be manufactured from the same device by equipping the chooser beforehand with a suitable rule; the original position begins to look like a stage prop.

(7) That cannot be right, because it would make the original position elaborate theater rather than an argument. I would most like to keep the parity claim in (3) and the thought in (4) that the veil presents the same information to everyone; but I want to know what I am paying for that.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please give the diagnosis as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
