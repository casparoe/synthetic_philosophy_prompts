I'm a software engineer, and I want the same thing I spend my days producing at work—a specification—but for an idea instead of a system: a list of requirements a theory of self-sufficiency must satisfy before I'll trust it to tell me when the ideal is well-invoked and when it is doing harm. I mean self-sufficiency in both registers people use it, without assuming they are the same thing: a person who wants to need less from others (grow her own food, not rely on a partner, avoid debt or financial dependence), and a nation or community that pursues autarky in some domain (food, energy, industrial inputs, trade policy). The theory should be adequate to practical judgment rather than mere description: it should tell me when to praise, recommend, or pursue self-sufficiency and when to call it pride, isolation, or economic error.

Give me the requirements list. For each requirement, I want the case or scenario that forces it, why a theory that drops it goes wrong, and whether it is negotiable or load-bearing. I also want the distinctions the theory cannot collapse, each with the case that would collapse them—for example: being able to provide for yourself versus actually doing so; being free of any particular other's power versus needing no one at all; self-sufficiency as resilience versus autarky as a refusal of trade or interdependence; self-sufficiency in one domain versus across a whole life or economy; and the individual case versus the collective, including the question of whether a self-sufficient nation is anything over and above many non-self-sufficient people coordinating. The theory also has to satisfy some basic reality constraints rather than floating free of them—it should not require denying elementary things we know about comparative advantage and the gains from specialization, or about human development and the fact that people are formed by dependence early in life—but it also cannot simply declare the ideal an economic mistake, because it plainly answers to something people value.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most work, measured by how large a share of the positions people actually hold it excludes, and whether any two requirements are in tension, so that the specification itself becomes a finding rather than a checklist. Keep any scoring of existing views brief—a sentence or two per contender—and spend the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating the question.
