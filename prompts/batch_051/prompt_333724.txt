I have no legal training and have been thinking about this on my own, partly because I run a small subscription newsletter that rates local tradespeople and occasionally publishes forward-looking assessments of their business prospects. I have assembled the following commitments about when a rating, review, or forecast should be treated as a factual claim for defamation purposes and when it should be protected as opinion. I want an audit of my position rather than a lesson in the case law.

Here is the set I currently hold.

1. At the clear ends, the distinction between fact and opinion seems sound. A statement that can be checked against a record, such as the claim that a restaurant failed its last health inspection, is importantly different from a statement of pure personal taste, such as the claim that the food was bad.

2. Ratings and professional forecasts sit in a middle zone. A three-star review or an analyst's estimate is not verifiable in the way an inspection count is, because it involves assigning weights and applying standards that reasonable people may not share; but it is not mere preference either, because there are better and worse ways to apply those standards.

3. When a rating or forecast is presented as the output of an objective methodology, as measuring something real such as creditworthiness or reliability rather than the writer's own enjoyment, the publisher has made a factual claim and should be answerable as if it were one.

4. A forecast about the future should be outside defamation claims altogether, because at the time it is made there is no existing state of affairs for it to be true or false about; being wrong later is not the same as lying now.

5. A rating or forecast written in bad faith, where the writer knows the number is not supported by their own stated method and means to injure the subject rather than inform readers, should be actionable even if it is wrapped in language calling it opinion or looking ahead.

6. Prominent cautionary language of the kind that tells readers not to rely on the piece should substantially reduce liability, and in some cases eliminate it; otherwise careful people will withdraw honest assessments for fear of litigation.

7. As a concrete case, I would want liability for a rating agency that downgrades a company while privately knowing the downgrade is unsupported by its own model, even where the agency adds a disclaimer for readers.

Do not assume I rank these commitments or know which one I would surrender; I do not. Which of these are in genuine tension with one another, and which only appear to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating all of them as equally serious, and for each genuinely conflicting pair or group, state the least costly revision and what it would cost. Please also identify any apparent conflict that dissolves once the relevant distinction is drawn.
