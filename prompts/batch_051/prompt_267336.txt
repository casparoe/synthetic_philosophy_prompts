I want a working definition of democratic backsliding. In ordinary argument the phrase seems to do too much: it is treated as an objective description when it is applied to governments the speaker dislikes, and as a partisan smear when it is applied to governments the speaker supports. I want a definition that does real classificatory work, separating backsliding from ordinary but contested institutional reform on one side, and from an outright break such as a coup or self-coup on the other. The deliverable is the definition itself; the cases below are the tests.

Please propose a definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it gives a wrong answer or no answer. Where it gives a surprising verdict, either revise the definition or defend the verdict.

1. A party wins a free election, then uses successive parliamentary majorities to lower the retirement age for judges, replace senior judges with loyal appointees, transfer public broadcasters to allied owners, rewrite electoral districts and thresholds to favor itself, and convert the civil service into a party-controlled appointment system. Elections continue, the opposition still holds seats and speaks freely, and no single law is obviously unconstitutional in isolation. I am confident this is backsliding; a definition that cannot count it is wrong.

2. During a public-health emergency, a government imposes curfews, bans large gatherings, and shifts some budget authority to the executive under statutes with sunset clauses, while courts remain free to strike individual measures down. The measures lapse when the emergency ends. Opposition parties call the whole episode creeping authoritarianism. I am confident this is not backsliding, but the definition should explain why.

3. After losing an election, the incumbent refuses to leave, annuls the result, and stays in office with the army's backing; parliament is dissolved and journalists are arrested. I do not think "backsliding" is the right word here. I want the definition to exclude this as a different phenomenon, or to say explicitly why it belongs on the same scale.

4. A government with a legislative majority passes, by ordinary statute, a package of judicial changes: judges above a new, lower retirement age must retire immediately, the executive can appoint their replacements quickly, and a new disciplinary chamber can sanction judges for their rulings. International courts later find parts of the package unlawful. Elections remain competitive, and the next election eventually returns the opposition to power. I am torn: it looks like backsliding while it is happening, but if the next election reverses it, perhaps it was only a hard-fought contest over judicial power. Does backsliding require durability, or can it be temporary and reversible?

5. In a mature democracy, one side expands early voting, mail voting, and automatic registration, while the other side introduces stricter voter identification and aggressive purges of the rolls. All the changes are upheld by courts, and there is no evidence that any election outcome was altered. Each side describes the other's changes as an attack on democracy itself. I have no stable view here. I need the definition to tell me whether one, both, or neither counts as backsliding, or whether individual measures are the wrong unit and the question only makes sense about overall trajectories.

6. An elected president, frustrated by a highest court that repeatedly blocks their agenda, proposes expanding the court by several seats to secure favorable rulings. The proposal fails in the legislature and is never implemented. Later, observers disagree about whether the mere proposal damaged democratic norms. I lean toward "not backsliding" because nothing happened, but I want to know whether a definition should at least flag failed attempts. Does backsliding include attempts, or only accomplished changes?

7. A free and fair referendum, with open campaigning and accurate information, approves a constitutional change that ends term limits for the incumbent and allows the president to legislate by decree for a decade. A majority votes for it. I am genuinely uncertain here. If the definition says this is backsliding, it seems to override a democratic decision with an external standard of "correct" democracy. If the definition says it is not backsliding, then backsliding seems to track only how change happens, not whether democratic institutions shrink. I want the definition to force the issue.

Finally, separate what is settled about this topic from what is genuinely contested among people who study it, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Tell me which of your seven verdicts you regard as least secure.
