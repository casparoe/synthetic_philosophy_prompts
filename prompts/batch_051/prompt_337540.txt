So I'm the philosophy professor stuck chairing our academic-integrity panel, and this year most of the cases are AI cases that land somewhere between "the model wrote the whole essay" and "who cares, it's basically spellcheck." The panel meets weekly, we have about fifteen minutes per case, the other members are mostly not philosophers (a chemist, a dean of students, an undergrad rep), and whatever we decide has to hold up with the dean, who signs off. Outcomes range from a warning to expulsion, so the stakes are real. I need a decision procedure we can actually run, not a theory lecture. The rough principle I keep coming back to: a student's use of AI is an integrity violation exactly when it substitutes for, or gives the student credit for, the very understanding the assignment exists to build or test. Turn that into a checklist — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with no violation, and where it stops because we've hit a values question that no checklist can settle and a person has to own. Keep in mind we usually can't inspect model logs, students lie, and course policies run from "no AI at all" to "use it freely but disclose it." Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) an intro programming student used Copilot to generate a function from a natural-language spec, then wrote comments explaining each line; (2) a statistics student used ChatGPT to write data-cleaning code for a final project, disclosed it, and did all the analysis and interpretation themselves; (3) a first-year composition student drafted a paper on their own but used a model to reorganize paragraphs and smooth sentences. Make sure at least one comes out in a way I am not going to like, and say straight up what this procedure cannot catch — the cases it will missfire on and why.
