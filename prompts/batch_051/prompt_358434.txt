You are a historian and philosopher of mathematics, expert in the long debate over which curves count as legitimate objects of geometry, from the Greek restriction to compass-and-straightedge construction through Descartes's distinction between the geometrical and the mechanical to the later acceptance of transcendental curves such as the cycloid. You are asked below for a careful, historically informed analysis. Think slowly, and before you commit to an answer begin by rehearsing the competing readings of the rule and what each would require, then refine the one you think best.

The Society for Constructive Geometry, in the founding charter of its journal (1740), laid down Article IV:

"No curve shall be accounted geometrical unless it be drawn by one uninterrupted motion of a point, which motion followeth one fixed rule so as to determine every part of the line. Curves that require the drawing of many several lines, or that stand upon no exact rule, are mechanical, and are not to be received."

A present-day member now puts the following object before the Society for a ruling. Take a straight-line segment. Replace its middle third by the other two sides of the equilateral triangle erected on that third, yielding a four-segment broken line. Apply the same replacement again to each of the four segments, and then again to each resulting segment, without end. The sequence of broken lines approaches, under the appropriate parametrizations, a limiting curve that is continuous at every point and yet has no tangent anywhere: at no point does a tracing point have a well-defined direction or velocity. The curve is generated from one short, fixed instruction, but it is not, at any finite stage or at the limit, produced by a single uninterrupted motion in the ordinary sense of the words.

Does Article IV, correctly applied, admit this curve as geometrical or exclude it as mechanical? Give the answer under each of the following approaches, keeping them distinct:

(i) the words of the article, read literally and with attention to what "motion," "uninterrupted," and "fixed rule" could have meant to its authors;
(ii) the purpose of the article, reconstructed as charitably as you can;
(iii) what the founders would most likely have said if they could have been shown the curve along with a proof that it has no tangent anywhere; and
(iv) what a competent reader of the Society's statutes today should take them to mean.

Then address: which of the four approaches should control, and why? Depending on the answer, say whether Article IV can be repaired with a principled test that admits the curves its authors clearly meant to admit while either admitting or excluding the new one for reasons they would recognize; and whether the case shows that any criterion stated in terms of motion or construction will remain hostage to cases the drafters could not yet imagine. Be precise about where the residual vagueness lives.
