Our department's Hume reading group has spent two terms stuck on the same fight, and two of us have somehow become the unofficial spokespeople for the opposing sides. The question is what Hume actually thought about causation—not just what he said we can know, but what he took the world to be like. On one reading, Hume is a straight regularity theorist: a cause is nothing over and above constant conjunction, contiguity in space and time, and temporal priority, and what we call necessary connection is just a habit the mind projects onto sequences after seeing them repeated; there is no hidden tie or power in nature beyond the patterns. On the other, roughly the New Hume reading, Hume only claims that we never observe and cannot demonstrate real necessary connections, so our idea of necessity has its origin in custom or a felt determination of the mind; that is an epistemic limit, not an ontological denial, and it leaves room for objective causal powers that exist whether or not we can understand them. On this second reading Hume may even believe in such powers, while denying we can say anything informative about them.

We've decided to stop trading weekly speeches and write a proper adversarial collaboration statement instead—the document both of us should be able to sign. It should have at least four parts. First, a numbered list of claims about Hume's actual argument (his account of the idea of necessary connection, the two definitions of cause, the principle that all ideas are copied from impressions, his treatment of inference from the observed to the unobserved) that both sides can accept, worded so neither of us flinches. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, with each side's position stated in terms that side would actually endorse, not a straw version. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions on which the whole dispute really turns. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete piece of textual evidence, argumentative move, or test case that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Also say plainly if part of the fight looks merely verbal to you, and if so which part. Include at least one crux about the missing shade of blue and whether it weakens the copy-principle argument for denying that we could have an idea of objective necessity, and at least one about how to read the second definition of cause.
