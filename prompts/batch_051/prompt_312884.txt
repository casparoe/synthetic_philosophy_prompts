I'm hosting a student-run philosophy podcast, and next weeks guest is a philosopher who defends a strong line on corporate moral agency: roughly, that a business corporation can be a genuine moral agent and a proper target of blame in its own right, not merely a legal person or something we sanction for instrumental reasons. He's drawing on the Peter French line that a firm's internal decision structure turns individual managers' decisions into genuinely corporate decisions, and on the List-and-Pettit-style point that a group's attitudes can come apart from the attitudes of its members when majorities lock onto a set of connected propositions. So he'll have ready replies about a company having intentions that no single member holds.

Write me eight to ten questions for him, in the order I should ask them. Don't let him just restate the view; I want the questions to press the places where this position usually gets pushed. Cover at least: the no-phenomenology worry (a company can't feel guilt or remorse, so how is blaming it anything but a fancy fine?); the scapegoating or many-hands problem (blaming the company seems to give managers somewhere to hide); the judgment-aggregation point (why should a majoritarian decision procedure create a new moral subject rather than just a new decision process?); the reactive-attitudes worry (can I really resent a company like I resent a person, and if we can't, have we stopped blaming in the full sense?); and the punishment problem (if a corporation can't suffer, what would desert even be for it, and would you defend charter revocation as a kind of corporate death penalty?). For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, and end with at least one question about what evidence or real-world changes would make him seriously weaken or abandon the view. Write the response in British English, as a numbered list with follow-ups clearly marked, and keep the whole thing under 800 words.
