You are a philosopher of physics who works on effective field theories, the renormalization group, and their implications for reduction and emergence. Think carefully, and do not settle for generic objections; engage the specific technical commitments of each audience member.

Next month I present to a joint physics and philosophy of physics workshop a paper defending what I call the autonomy of scales. My thesis is that, for many systems and quantities, the physics at a given scale is autonomous from shorter-distance physics: the effective field theory at that scale is not merely an instrumentally useful approximation to the fundamental description, and ultraviolet detail is screened off from the predictions and explanations we want at the longer-distance scale. My argument has three load-bearing parts. First, decoupling: in quantum field theory, heavy degrees of freedom leave behind only local higher-dimensional corrections suppressed by powers of the heavy mass, so their influence on low-energy quantities is parametrically erasable. Second, Wilsonian renormalization-group flow: most microscopic couplings are irrelevant in the technical sense and die out toward long distances, so only a small handful of parameters survive to fix the low-energy physics. Third, universality: microscopically very different theories flow to the same long-distance fixed point, giving a physically precise sense in which the same low-energy behavior is multiply realizable.

The audience is not friendly. It includes (a) two high-energy theorists who use effective field theories daily but regard them as placeholders for ultraviolet completions and consider the shortest-distance description the one that is literally true; (b) a philosopher of physics who has defended derivational reduction between theories and will argue that effective field theory is either reducible to the fundamental theory or at least no threat to reduction; (c) a metaphysician working on grounding who will insist that explanatory autonomy and ontological dependence are different questions; and (d) a condensed matter physicist sympathetic to my conclusion but worried about technical overreach, especially apparent non-decoupling cases, such as a very heavy fermion whose Yukawa coupling grows with its mass, the hierarchy problem as evidence of low-energy sensitivity to ultraviolet physics, and the fact that universality in the strong sense is a property of critical phenomena rather than of generic effective theories.

Give me a prep sheet rather than a defense of the thesis. Begin by brainstorming, for each audience member, what they are committed to and how they would attack each of my three pillars and my overall inference from predictive sufficiency to autonomy. Then organize the objections into the order they will most likely arise in the question period, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them, and score each objection by how likely and how damaging it is. For each, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold or deflect, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me how to handle it if it comes.
