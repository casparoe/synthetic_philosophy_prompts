Next term I am presenting a research talk to a school of social policy whose faculty are mostly empirical sociologists and demographers, along with a few political scientists who study public opinion. The thesis I will defend is that generational categories — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so on — should be treated as nominal conveniences rather than real social kinds capable of bearing causal explanation. By a real social kind I mean a category whose members share a property or mechanism stable enough to support reliable generalizations, as opposed to a label imposed on an arbitrary slice of a continuous distribution. My argument has three steps. First, many apparent differences between generations are confounded mixtures of age effects, period effects, and cohort effects, and the standard age-period-cohort identification problem means the data cannot separate those three components without extra assumptions. Second, where a genuine cohort effect can be isolated, the underlying mechanism — a labor-market restructuring, a change in higher-education financing, a military draft — seldom lines up with the official generational boundaries, so the mechanism rather than the label carries the explanatory weight. Third, although generational labels may become real through self-identification, in the way that a category can loop back and alter the people it classifies, this makes the labels real as social identities, not as the explanatory kinds their users invoke when they say, for instance, that Gen Z does not trust institutions.

The audience is not a single bloc. Some are influenced by Mannheim's sociology of generations, which treats a generation as formed by a shared historical location during a formative life stage; they will think I am attacking a caricature. The demographers will accept the age-period-cohort problem but will notice that it cuts against my argument too. The survey researchers will defend the labels as indispensable shorthand for stable differences in attitudes and behavior. Prepare a question-period preparation document for me. Give the objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they will arise and listed by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased the way this audience would put them. For each, tell me what to concede, what to hold, where the reply is soft, and how to answer in two minutes. End with the question I should be most afraid someone will ask.
