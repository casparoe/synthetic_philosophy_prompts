I've been developing a distinction about consent, and I'd like an honest assessment of it rather than encouragement. I call the two poles first-order consent and second-order consent. First-order consent has a determinate object: I understand a particular set of terms well enough to know what I'm taking on, and I accept those terms. Second-order consent has a procedure or practice as its object: I accept that I will be bound by whatever terms the procedure yields, even though I do not know them at the time of consent. My central claim is that clicking "I agree" on a standard contract of adhesion is almost pure second-order consent. The user is not actually agreeing to the terms — they have not read them and could not restate them — but they are agreeing to be bound by terms they will discover only when a dispute arises. On this view, the problem with clickwrap is not that the consent is fake, uninformed, or coerced in the ordinary cases. It is that a second-order act is being given the moral and legal force of a first-order one. Calling it "agreement" lets a platform claim that you accepted this arbitration clause or that data-sharing provision, when all you knowingly accepted was the practice of being bound by clauses of that kind. To sharpen the idea: it is the difference between signing a contract you have read and signing a blank sheet while the other party fills in the terms later, where the blank sheet — not the later terms — is what you knowingly signed. What I want checked is whether this distinction is sound, whether it collapses under pressure, and whether it is just a dressed-up version of something already worked out under another name. Give me the three strongest objections to treating clickwrap as second-order consent rather than defective first-order consent, and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than merely awkward. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
