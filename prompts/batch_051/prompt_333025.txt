I'm a senior in high school and I run a philosophy and mathematics club. About fifteen students come, mostly juniors and seniors, and we have an hour each week. I want to run a session on the doomsday argument, but I don't want to open with a lecture or even say the name. I want the members to feel the pull of the reasoning themselves — the strange fact that your own position in the order of human lives can look like evidence about how many human lives there will ever be — and only then have the machinery named.

You are an expert in Bayesian epistemology, anthropic reasoning, and the doomsday argument in particular. Please think carefully and work step by step. Before settling on a design, brainstorm at least three candidate activity structures and say briefly why you reject all but one.

The conceptual core I want the exercise to reach is this. Take two hypotheses about the total number of humans who will ever live: Doom Soon, on which humanity ends after about 200 billion births, and Doom Late, on which humanity reaches about 2 trillion births. Now take one datum: my own birth rank, roughly 100 billion. If I treat myself as a random draw from the set of all humans, that datum is ten times more likely under Doom Soon than under Doom Late, so it pushes my credence toward Doom Soon even if I begin at 50/50. The exercise should let participants occupy the role of a random human drawing a birth rank from one of two urns and betting on which urn they are in, so that they discover for themselves that a low number counts as evidence for the small urn and feel how uncomfortable that conclusion is.

What I need is a runnable 60-minute activity for about 15 high-school students, with no lecture up front and with the phrase "doomsday argument" withheld until the debrief. Include all of the following:

- Materials and setup: two opaque boxes or urns, numbered slips (scaled down, for example 200 and 2,000 rather than 200 billion and 2 trillion), score sheets, a fair coin, and anything else the game needs.
- Rules for the betting rounds, including a point-based payoff scheme that does not use real money, and a pre-draw and post-draw round so the group can watch the aggregate bets move.
- A timeline for the hour.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal pieces: prior, likelihood, posterior, the self-sampling assumption, and the shift toward Doom Soon.
- A list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I am especially worried about students saying "the coin was fair, so I should stay 50/50," about someone drawing a number above 200 and thereby revealing the urn, and about small-sample weirdness — with concrete ways to handle each.
- An honest note on what the urn game cannot reproduce about the actual doomsday argument. My suspicion is that the game makes the random sampling too literal and well-defined: there really is a physical urn and a fair lottery, whereas in the real argument the reference class is debatable and no actual lottery made me this particular human rather than another. Tell me clearly which of those gaps I will have to fill myself in the debrief.

The test of success is that before the debrief names anything, at least a few students have moved their bets toward the small urn solely because of a low number, and they can say why that move unsettles them.
