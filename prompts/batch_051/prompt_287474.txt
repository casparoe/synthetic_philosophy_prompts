I'm putting together a unit on definitions of life for my philosophy of biology seminar, and I've been reading the intro bio textbook one of my colleagues uses, to see how biologists themselves frame the issue. The textbook gives this summary:

Living things share a checklist of properties — order, reproduction, growth and development, energy processing, regulation, response to the environment, and evolutionary adaptation — and something has to possess all seven to count as alive. This, the book says, is why viruses are not alive: they don't have a metabolism of their own and can't reproduce without hijacking a host cell. It's also why fire and crystals fail the test, even though each mimics an item or two on the list.

I can't shake the feeling that the book is quietly doing philosophy here and maybe overreaching — the "all seven" sounds more like a grading rubric than a definition, and I though the virus case was exactly where the checklist approach gets murky rather than where it gets settled. But I'm a philosopher, not a biologist. Is this a fair summary of how biologists think about life? Which parts are accurate, which are subtly wrong, and does the inference from "matches the checklist" to "is alive" survive once the errors are corrected?
