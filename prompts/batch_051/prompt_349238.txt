A colleague who works on scientific method has been defending a strong claim about thought experiments. As he puts it when pressed, a good scientific thought experiment—the falling bodies, an accelerating elevator, Maxwell's demon—changes the cognitive state of the person who works through it in a way that reading the conclusion together with a formal derivation does not. The thought experiment, he says, generates something worth calling knowledge or understanding that would otherwise be absent. I find the claim hard to pin down because both routes seem to end with the same proposition accepted; if that is right, then saying one route produced knowledge seems to require saying what knowledge adds to acceptance, and that is exactly where the claim starts to slip.

I would like to know how one could test his claim behaviorally, or at least how far a behavioral test could take us. Design the strongest concrete study you can, specifying the participants, the two or three conditions to compare, and the outcome measures that would distinguish a genuine epistemic gain from an improvement in memory, salience, or motivation. Would you, for instance, use near- and far-transfer problems, think-aloud protocols, or a condition in which the thought experiment's imagery is deliberately misleading? Say what pattern of results would count in his favor and what pattern would count against. Then be exact about what even a clean positive result would leave unsettled: whether the subject has acquired justification or only a skill, whether the imagery is doing the causal work while the reasoning merely rides along, and whether the thought experiment supplies evidence or only makes explicit commitments the subject already had.
