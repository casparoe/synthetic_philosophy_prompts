For the past year I have been the methodologist on a project where two models account for the same data: one is mathematically elegant, the other is a patchwork of fitted terms that predicts slightly better. I recently had to recommend which one the group should pursue, and it pushed me into an argument that now bothers me because it seems to prove both that elegance is evidence and that it is not.

(1) If elegance is ever evidence for a theory's truth, it is evidence in virtue of a robust correlation between elegant theories and true theories.
(2) The only way anyone could know of such a correlation is by induction over the historical record of successful theories.
(3) That record is systematically biased: a theory enters the record only if it was elegant enough to attract committed defenders and empirically adequate enough to survive, while ugly theories that might have been true were abandoned or never formulated, so their fates were excluded from the sample.
(4) An induction from a sample biased on exactly the property being tested cannot support a correlation between that property and truth.
(5) So we have no grounds for treating elegance as evidence.
(6) But if we refuse to treat elegance as evidence, then whenever data underdetermine theory choice, any choice among empirically equivalent rivals is arbitrary—including the choices that produced general relativity, the gauge-theoretic standard model, and other canonical successes.
(7) Those choices are not arbitrary; they are paradigm cases of good theory choice.
(8) Therefore elegance is evidence.

The argument appears to establish the negation of (5) from (5) alone, which I take to be a sign that something has gone wrong. I would most like to keep (7), and I suspect the trouble lies around (3) or (4), but I cannot make the objection precise. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
