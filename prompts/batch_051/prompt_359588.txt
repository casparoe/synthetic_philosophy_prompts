I'm teaching a seminar on the meaning of life, and we've been working through Schlick's essay On the Meaning of Life. I find the position more slippery than I remembered. He argues that the question "What is the meaning of life?" is miscast: it assumes life needs a purpose outside itself, and so it drives us into an instrumental, spectatorial stance toward our own existence. Against that, he suggests that meaning shows up in activities done for their own sake—play being his paradigm—and that we only lose the meaning when we step back and ask about it. There's a passage near the end where he connects this to youth: not a phase of life, but a way of being in the present, absorbed in activity before the means/end distinction has taken hold. I keep wanting to assign that passage as the key to the whole essay.

But here's my difficulty. I'm genuinely unsure what would count against Schlick's view, and I want a map of its exposure rather than a verdict. Suppose someone says his account is too aesthetic and cannot handle lives of sacrifice, duty, grief, or long-term projects that are meaningful precisely because they are hard and chosen for the sake of something beyond the activity. Is that a different view or a counterexample? What about the complaint that absorption in play can be frivolous or even cruel, and that the mere feeling of being caught up isn't enough for meaning? I'd like to know, for each candidate defeater, whether a defender of Schlick could absorb it, whether it merely expresses a rival temperament, or whether it exposes a real hole. Does Schlick's view predict anything that could fail, or is it insulated in a way that is itself a problem? I'm less interested in who wins than in knowing what would have to be true for the position to be in genuine trouble.
