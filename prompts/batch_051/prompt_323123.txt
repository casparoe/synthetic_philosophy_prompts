I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I have been following a long-running dispute between two camps about how effort, money, and institutional attention should be directed toward doing good. The first camp — call it the marginal-intervention view — holds that, under uncertainty, the best guide is to compare concrete, well-specified interventions by their expected marginal cost-effectiveness, because structural reforms are usually too uncertain, too difficult to evaluate, and too easily captured to dominate such comparisons. The second camp — the systems-change view — holds that this focus on tractable, measurable interventions systematically misses the constraints, feedback loops, and distributions of power that determine whether any intervention works at all, so that a serious portfolio should lean heavily toward changing rules, institutions, or norms even when the evidence for specific structural reforms is thinner.

I want to draft the joint statement that a sustained adversarial collaboration between two sophisticated representatives of these camps would produce — not a watered-down compromise, but a document each could sign because it states their own view accurately and makes the residual disagreement exact. Please write that document.

Structure it as follows.

1. A numbered section titled "Claims both sides accept." These should be substantive rather than platitudinous. I expect them to cover: what counts as a reason for choosing one course of action under uncertainty; what each camp concedes about the evaluation methods it is often thought to prefer; and what any adequate account of doing good would have to explain — for example, why some well-evaluated interventions seem to produce persistent benefits while many promising structural reforms remain unevaluated. Treat each camp as internally diverse: use the strongest form a knowledgeable proponent would actually defend, not a caricature.

2. A disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, include: (a) a neutral statement of the point at issue; (b) the formulation of the marginal-intervention side that its own representative would accept as accurate; (c) the formulation of the systems-change side that its own representative would accept as accurate; and (d) your judgment of whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, with the equivocation stated if it is partly verbal.

3. A section titled "Cruxes." Identify two or three sub-questions whose resolution would materially move at least one side. I suspect the cruxes will be near the following, but you should refine them rather than simply reproduce them: whether apparently successful marginal interventions shift underlying system parameters rather than merely producing outputs within fixed parameters; whether the superior evaluability of marginal interventions is a selection effect that makes them look more cost-effective than less measurable alternatives; and whether palliative successes within a poorly functioning system tend to delay structural change or tend to create capacity and momentum for it. For each crux, specify a concrete empirical case, natural experiment, or research program that both sides would agree bears on it — concrete enough that the two predictions could be distinguished in principle — and record in advance what each side expects to find there, including the pattern of results each side agrees would count against its own view.

4. A brief closing statement that both representatives could sign, indicating what change of evidence or argument would lead each of them to reconsider, and stating where the collaboration left the disagreement.

Do not adjudicate the dispute. The deliverable should read as a jointly authored document, with the shared ground nontrivial and the residual disagreement precise.
