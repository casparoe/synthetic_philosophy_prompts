Exam question:

Explain the difference between the law of excluded middle and the principle of bivalence. Does vagueness give us reason to abandon either principle, and if so, which? Discuss at least one non-classical treatment of vagueness.

Student's answer:

The law of excluded middle (LEM) is a law of logic: for any declarative sentence P, "P or not-P" is a logical truth. The principle of bivalence is a semantic principle: every meaningful declarative sentence is either true or false. They are often run together, but LEM is about which formulas are valid, whereas bivalence is about which truth-values sentences can have.

Vagueness puts pressure on bivalence. Take a borderline case of a vague predicate, such as a man who is neither clearly bald nor clearly not bald. It seems wrong to say that "Al is bald" is true, and equally wrong to say that it is false. If we accept these judgements, the sentence has no truth-value, so bivalence fails. And once bivalence fails for this sentence, LEM must fail with it: "Al is bald or Al is not bald" is a disjunction both of whose disjuncts are gappy, and a disjunction with gappy disjuncts cannot be true. So vagueness, if it generates gaps, forces us to abandon LEM as well as bivalence.

A well-known alternative is supervaluationism. On that view, "Al is bald" is true iff it is true on every admissible sharpening of "bald", false iff it is false on every admissible sharpening, and otherwise it is false as well, since we have not shown that he is bald. Supervaluationism is attractive because it lets us keep classical logic, including LEM, while still giving up bivalence.
