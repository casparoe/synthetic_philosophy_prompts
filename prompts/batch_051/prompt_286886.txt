I'm a backend engineer, no formal stats or philosophy background, mostly doing release engineering, and I keep chewing on a parallel I can't decide about. I went down the ergodicity-economics rabbit hole, the time-average-versus-ensemble-average thing, through the coin-toss example where each round you either multiply your wealth by 1.5 or by 0.6 with equal odds. The ensemble average across a bunch of parallel copies grows about 5% per round, but a single player who keeps betting the whole pot has their wealth trend downward over time because what matters for one path is the geometric mean, which is below 1. That already does something weird to my intuitions about what "good for the group" implies for "good for one person."

It reminds me of something I actually see at work. We decide whether a release is healthy by looking at fleet-wide aggregates over a short window: median latency across all pods, error budget computed across all requests, average memory across the fleet right now. But a single long-running pod — or a user whose session sticks to one pod — can be on a completely different trajectory: a tiny leak compounds, a cache decays, an unlucky retry storm spirals. The any-instant fleet average can look fine while any given instance, followed long enough, is basically doomed. So here's the thing I want help checking: is the pod-fleet-vs-single-pod distinction (or aggregate-over-many-users-at-one-time vs. one-user-over-many-days) the same structure as ensemble-vs-time-average in ergodicity economics, or am I just pattern-matching on "average versus individual"? Where does the analogy actually do work, and where does it break — for instance, pods get restarted and load balancers shuffle traffic, so maybe no single instance lives long enough for the time-average regime to matter? And does anything transfer usefully, like a Kelly-type logic for how much variance you can tolerate in a rollout if what you actually care about is the long-run experience of one instance or one user rather than the fleet average?
