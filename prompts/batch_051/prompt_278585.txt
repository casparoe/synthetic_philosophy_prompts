Is there a name for this, or am I just noticing something everyone politely ignores? In climate model projections I keep seeing the following move: researchers run a bunch of global climate models under the same emissions scenario, then report the average across all the models as the central estimate and the spread between them as the uncertainty, like the models are independant measurements of some single true future. But the models are not independant. Some share the same component code, many were built by groups that copy each other's parameterizations for clouds and sea ice, and nearly all of them have been tuned against the same historical record. So when a result shows up in thirty models at once, it can be one assumption showing up under thirty names rather than thirty pieces of evidence. And yet a prediction is also described as more "robust" exactly when more models agree on it, which feels like double-counting all that shared structure. Is there an established term for this configuration — treating whatever models happen to exist as if they were independent samples, and counting agreement across them as confidence in the first place? I know there's a debate about "model democracy" versus weighting models by skill, but that seems to be a debate about how to combine models once you already have your ensemble; the thing I'm trying to name is earlier and weirder, namely that the spread and the agreement of these particular models are being read as uncertainty and support at all. If there is a name, I don't just want the label. I want to know exactly what it covers, how it differs from ordinary structural uncertainty, and how it relates to the distinction between uncertainty about model structure and uncertainty about parameter values.
