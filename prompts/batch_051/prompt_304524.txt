Separately, I help run my school's philosophy club, and I have been trying to understand a view I will call tranquilism. As I understand it, tranquilism holds that the only states of intrinsic value are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. Craving and suffering are intrinsically bad—where by craving I mean any felt wanting, restlessness, or dissatisfaction with things as they are—while their absence is intrinsically good. Everything else, including pleasure, achievement, knowledge, and close relationships, matters only instrumentally, insofar as it reduces craving or suffering. A consequence of the view is that the proper aim of life is to extinguish craving rather than to satisfy it.

Please restate tranquilism in the vocabulary of the welfare economics I just described, working through it piece by piece. What does the view look like when expressed as a utility function or as a set of claims about preferences and choice? Which parts translate cleanly? For each breakdown, tell me whether it is a limitation of the economic vocabulary, a sign that tranquilism itself is underspecified or implausible, or both.
