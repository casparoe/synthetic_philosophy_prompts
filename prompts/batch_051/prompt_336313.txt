While I was reading about algorithmic fairness the other day, I got stuck on a parallel I can't decide is deep or just pattern-matching. The impossibility results there say, roughly, that in a risk-score system you can't have all three of: calibration within each group, equal false-positive rates across groups, and equal false-negative rates across groups — at least not when the groups have different base rates and the predictor isn't effectively perfect. At first glance this looks like a crisp formal version of a point political philosophers have made forever: that you cannot simultaneously have a purely meritocratic/procedurally fair system and guarantee that benefits and burdens fall on different social groups in equal proportions, unless the groups are already similarly situated in all the respects the procedure tracks. If you insist on equal outcomes you have to relax procedural fairness, and if you insist on procedural fairness you have to tolerate unequal outcomes.

Here's the mapping I've been toying with. Calibration plays the role of treating like cases alike: conditional on a given score, the score means the same thing regardless of group. Equalized false-positive and false-negative rates play the role of equal treatment in outcome: each group should bear the same rate of unjustified denials and unjustified burdens. Different base rates correspond to different underlying distributions of qualification, preparation, or circumstance. On the political side, the lesson would be that the conflict between equal opportunity and equal outcome isn't just a matter of scarce resources or bad faith — its baked into any scoring procedure once the groups differ in baseline rates. A friend who does ML shrugs and says the theorem is just Simpson's paradox with new notation, not a political result. A philosophy friend says it's just the old opportunity/outcome trade-off dressed up in math. I can't tell whether they're making the same error in opposite directions.
