I'm a software engineer working on autonomy stuff (perception, mostly, with some sensor fusion), and I keep getting dragged into arguments about how you even blame a self-driving car when something goes wrong. I've got no philosophy background, so I might mangle this, but a coworker pointed me at this idea called the "responsibility gap": when an autonomous or learning system causes harm, all the usual places we'd stick blame—the programmer, the operator, the company—seem to have a decent excuse (nobody wrote the exact behavior, nobody had real-time control, the system sort of "decided" on its own), and the machine itself can't be blamed, so responsibility falls through the cracks.

The case I can't stop thinking about is the Uber self-driving car that killed Elaine Herzberg in Tempe in March 2018. Quick recap of the facts as I remember them: the car was in autonomous mode at night, a backup driver was in the seat but was apparently watching something on her phone, and the car hit Herzberg while she was walking a bicycle across the road and killed her. The NTSB stuff later said the car's automatic emergency braking was disabled while it was under computer control, and the whole safety design just assumed the human would bail it out. Uber settled with the family and suspended testing in Arizona, and eventually the backup driver got charged with negligent homicide but prosecutors didn't charge the company. What I want is a careful mapping of that crash through the responsibility-gap lens, not just vibes. Who are the players, what did each of them actually new and control, what options did each actually have at the time, and what does the theory say each should have been responsible for—and which of those shoulds were missing? Where does the theory genuinely explain why the case feels so weird (a person's dead, but no individual seems straightforwardly blameworthy the way a human driver would be), and where does it have to stretch or paper over the facts? In particular, is this really a special gap opened up by autonomy, or is it mostly ordinary corporate diffusion of responsibility plus a backup driver who was set up to fail—something older legal and moral ideas already had handles for? And what does the crash do to the theory in return: does it confirm the gap as a real feature of automated systems, or make it look like the gap was manufactured by the design and then used as an excuse? My own hunch is that a real responsibility gap matters most for systems that genuinely rewrite their own policies, not for a mostly engineered stack with a human safety net—but I don't know if that's a useful distinction or just me looking for a loophole.
