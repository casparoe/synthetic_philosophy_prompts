Two mathematicians at a seminar I attended got into an argument about labeling roots, and neither would budge. I am reporting their exchange and asking you to adjudicate it point by point.

Mira began: (1) When we solve x² − 2 = 0, we write the roots as √2 and −√2, and "the square root of 2" is unproblematic because exactly one root is positive and the real numbers are ordered. But when we introduce a square root of −1, we write i where i² = −1, and here no order or analogous property singles out one of the two roots. So standard notation treats the two cases differently, and that asymmetry looks arbitrary.

Eli replied: (2) It is harmless because nothing hinges on it. Complex conjugation swaps i and −i and preserves every field-theoretic fact, so any field-theoretic statement true of i is true of −i. Choosing which root to call i is a convention, like choosing which direction to call "up."

Mira: (3) A convention still has to pick something out. If i and −i are genuinely indistinguishable within the structure, then the name "i" presupposes a determinate referent that the structure itself does not determine. What makes one of the two roots the referent?

Eli: (4) A label need not be determined by the structure; we can pick either root and declare it i, and everything that follows remains rigorous. Distinctness is not in question: build the complex numbers as ordered pairs of reals with a multiplication rule, and the two roots of x² + 1 = 0 are (0,1) and (0,−1), plainly different objects.

Mira: (5) That construction settles it only relative to a presentation. The ordered-pair model singles out (0,1) because of a chosen coordinatization; a different presentation, or the conjugation symmetry itself, swaps the two roots. If which object is "i" changes with the presentation, then "the complex number i" still rests on the very kind of choice the standard view says does not matter.

Eli: (6) That is standard mathematical practice: fix a choice, prove that the results are invariant under the choice, and move on. The practice does not presuppose that the roots are identical; it presupposes only that no structural question depends on the choice. So it is a harmless convention after all.
