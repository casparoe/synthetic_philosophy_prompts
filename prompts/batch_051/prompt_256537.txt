I have been turning over Susan Wolf's argument about moral saints and whether the rise of effective altruism quietly dissolves it or merely relocates its target. Wolf's case, roughly, is that a life organized single-mindedly around moral goodness would be an impoverished rather than an ideal human life. She distinguishes two ways of being a saint. The loving saint's happiness is entirely taken up in producing good for others, so there is no space left for non-moral excellences or for the sharp edges that give a personality texture. The rational saint acts from duty rather than inclination and is always reining in the non-moral interests that make a life feel vivid and one's own. Wolf concludes that moral sainthood is not an ideal we should want for ourselves or for everyone.

Effective altruism looks like a new development that changes a background assumption of that argument, because it disaggregates moral agency. The effective altruist need not be personally saintly in either sense. She can earn to give from a morally indifferent career, cap her private consumption at a level chosen in advance, and direct the rest toward interventions selected for expected cost-effectiveness rather than for direct expressions of personal virtue. Morality becomes portfolio management: parts of the self are treated as engines for generating impartial value, while other parts are deliberately fenced off as recreation. Tithing, moral division of labor, offsetting-like practices, and quantified impact estimates let someone produce far more impartial good than many saints ever would, without displaying a unified, all-consuming moral character.

Does Wolf's argument survive this change? One possibility is that her real target was never producing lots of impartial good, but treating morality as the single dominant standpoint from which an entire life is evaluated. On that reading the EA optimizer is simply the rational saint in a new costume, with an added layer of self-alienation. Another possibility is that effective altruism dissolves the target: if moral goodness just is the amount of impartial value one's life causes, then a somewhat boring person who causes enormous value is simply the best person, and Wolf was only attacking a packaging convention about unified virtuous character. A third possibility is that EA preserves the non-moral goods Wolf wanted to protect, but only by turning them into scheduled, explicitly non-moral side chambers, and that this is a new kind of damage to the self—neither saintly self-effacement nor healthy wholeheartedness.

Analyze which premise of Wolf's argument the EA development bears on, and say whether the argument dies, survives, or can be repaired in a form that still has bite. If it survives, state precisely what the residual defect is and what it should be called if not saintliness. If it dies, say what a new anti-saint argument would have to target now that cost-effective optimizers have separated moral output from moral character.
