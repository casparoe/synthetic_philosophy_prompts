I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and the proposal I keep coming back to is futarchy: roughly, the idea that a polity should democratically choose a welfare metric and then let conditional prediction markets select the policies expected to maximise that metric. A critic recently gave me an argument that seems to prove too much, and I cannot find the flaw myself. Here is my reconstruction of the critic's argument.

(1) A policy choice is rational only if it is directed at some outcome one values; absent such an outcome, there is nothing for the choice to be about.
(2) Conditional prediction markets are, in general, the most reliable mechanism we have for turning dispersed information into accurate estimates of how policies affect a specified outcome.
(3) Therefore, whenever a policy question arises, the rational procedure is to specify the valued outcome, run conditional markets on the candidate policies, and adopt whichever policy the market estimates will do best on that outcome. This is the core case for futarchy.
(4) The selection of the welfare metric itself, the choice of which outcome is to count as valued, is also a choice among alternatives, and it too is guided by beliefs about which metric would better promote what we ultimately care about.
(5) By parity of reasoning from (1) and (2), the selection of the metric should itself be settled by a conditional prediction market over candidate metrics.
(6) But a prediction market cannot operate without an advance settlement rule specifying a measurable outcome on which its contracts settle. For the question of which metric is the right one, any such settlement rule would already presuppose a metric, and the question of which settlement rule to use simply repeats the same problem one level up.
(7) So the case for futarchy, if sound, demands an impossible self-referential market and thereby undermines itself. If we instead exempt the choice of metric from market adjudication, then the principle in (3) has an unexplained exception at precisely the most consequential choice of all.

The critic concludes that futarchy cannot get off the ground unless it arbitrarily privileges a metric that was chosen by non-market means, and that the argument for futarchy therefore proves too much: it pushes its proponents either into an infinite regress or into conceding that the real decision procedure is the non-market one that picks the metric. I would very much like to keep steps (2) and (3), since I remain sympathetic to futarchy, but I cannot tell which premise I should reject and whether I can do so without paying a cost I would find unacceptable. Which premise or step should I give up, why did it look so plausible, and what exactly does rejecting it cost the broader case for futarchy?
