I am writing a paper on reasons for attitudes, and the so-called wrong-kind-of-reasons problem has tied me in knots. As I understand it, the problem starts from cases like this: a demon threatens to punish you unless you admire its cruelty, and the threat seems to give you some kind of reason to admire the cruelty even though it is plainly irrelevant to whether the cruelty is admirable. I have been trying to decide whether the "reason" supplied by such a threat is a genuine reason for the attitude itself, or only a reason to try to bring the attitude about. I have assembled the following argument, which I believe shows that the threat gives a genuine reason for the attitude itself and that wrong-kind reasons are therefore real. But I cannot see whether the argument survives scrutiny, and I cannot locate any precise weak point. Please assess the argument step by step, say whether it is valid, and, if it is not, identify which numbered step fails, explain exactly why it fails, and state how that step would need to be corrected in order for the rest of the argument to be re-cast.

Here is the argument:

(1) Suppose a demon threatens to punish you severely unless you admire its cruelty; suppose further, for the sake of argument, that the only way to avoid the punishment is to admire its cruelty.

(2) You have a reason to avoid the punishment.

(3) If you have a reason to avoid some outcome X, and some action is your only available way of avoiding X, then you have a reason to perform that action.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), you have a reason to admire the demon's cruelty.

(5) A right-kind reason for admiring something is a consideration that bears on whether admiration is fitting towards it — that is, a consideration bearing on its admirable features.

(6) The demon's threat bears on the cost to you of failing to admire, not on any admirable feature of the demon's cruelty.

(7) So the reason identified in (4) is not a right-kind reason.

(8) Therefore, it is a wrong-kind reason: a genuine reason to admire the demon's cruelty that is nevertheless irrelevant to whether admiring that cruelty is fitting.

(9) Hence there really are wrong-kind reasons for attitudes, and the distinction between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons is a distinction between two kinds of reason, not between a reason and a non-reason.

I keep suspecting that (2) is the weak point, because a threat issued by a demon may seem too illegitimate a source to ground any reason at all. But I cannot make that charge stick: surely a rational person would take the threatened punishment into account. If the argument is valid, I will accept the conclusion, even though it feels too quick.
