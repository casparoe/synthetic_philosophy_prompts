Here is an argument, set out step by step, for the conclusion that a healthy adult who can pay the fees without serious sacrifice is rationally required to sign up for cryonic preservation:

(1) A rational agent chooses among available actions by their expected utility: if one action has higher expected utility than every alternative, the agent should choose it.

(2) Cryonics sign-up is an action with two states: eventual revival and non-revival. Let p be the probability of revival, V the utility value of the additional years of life that revival would bring, and C the utility cost of paying for preservation. Relative to not signing up, the payoff of signing up is V − C in the revival state and −C in the non-revival state.

(3) The expected utility of signing up, relative to not signing up, is therefore pV − C; the expected utility of not signing up is 0.

(4) It follows that signing up has higher expected utility than not signing up exactly when p > C/V.

(5) C/V is extraordinarily small: the cost is finite, while the value of decades of additional life is many orders of magnitude greater, so the probability needed for sign-up to be advantageous is minuscule.

(6) The probability p is at least that large. We have no decisive evidence that revival is impossible; no known law of nature rules out the technologies that could enable it; and it would be dogmatic to assign this outcome a probability below a threshold that small merely because it is unprecedented.

(7) Therefore p > C/V; signing up has higher expected utility than not signing up; and by (1), a rational agent should sign up.
