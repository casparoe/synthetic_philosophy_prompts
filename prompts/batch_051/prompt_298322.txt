Next thursday I'm presenting to my philosophy of language reading group and I want a Q&A prep sheet for the obvious attacks, not a restatement of my argument. My view is deflationary: slurs have no special semantic content — not a presupposition, not a conventional meaning-level signal, not an expressive property. Using a slur says the same thing its neutral counterpart says; everything else is social machinery: who's allowed to say it, what choosing the forbidden word signals about the speaker's allegiances, how historical domination built the taboo, and what the act does to the addressee. Nothing nasty lives in the word itself. My quick argument is that the projective behavior of slurs — the fact that the contempt still lands under negation, conditionals, and questions — isn't evidence of semantic content, because sentential operators scope over truth conditions while the act of choosing the taboo word stays visible in the utterance. And reclamation, I'll say, is just the social meaning flipping while the content stays put.

Here's who's in the room. Two philosophers of language are convinced slurs carry a conventional derogatory component at the level of meaning: one thinks it's a presupposition that projects, the other thinks it's a non-truth-conditional signal attached to the word. There's an expressivist who thinks using a slur just is the expression of contempt, not a conversational inference, and that taboo-talk can't explain why the contempt comes through even when the speaker flat-out says they don't endorse it. There's a sociolinguist who works on reclamation; she agrees the harm happens in the act, but she'll say my "just taboo plus speaker alignment" story can't explain why in-group uses work differently, or why some slurs harden while others fade. Plus a few agnostics who'll go with whoever sounds less ad hoc.

Give me the objections in the order they'll actually come — most likely first, and flag the most damaging one — phrased roughly as those people will phrase them. For each objection: what I should straight-up concede, what I should hold, and a one- or two-sentence reply that fits a short Q&A. Then add the objection I'm praying nobody asks, and tell me the weakest plank in my position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Keep the whole thing under 600 words.
