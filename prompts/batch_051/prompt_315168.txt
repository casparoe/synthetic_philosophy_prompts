I work on a mental-health app whose feature set now looks to me like it contains a philosophical contradiction.

The product has two complementary flows. First, a guided journal prompts users who log a low mood to drill into it: name the emotion, replay the concrete situation, list the thoughts that accompanied it, and check those thoughts against evidence. The in-app copy describes this as examining your thoughts so they lose their grip on you. Second, a separate feature watches the user's language while journaling or leaving voice notes. When it detects repeated, self-focused negative patterns — the same phrase circling, self-blaming labels, why-am-I-like-this loops — it interrupts and pushes a grounding exercise, warning that the user is ruminating and should step away from self-examination. In short, the same product treats self-examination as the cure in one flow and as the disease in another.

I don't think this is merely a product contradiction, and I don't want to resolve it by A/B testing engagement metrics. It seems to commit us to a view about where the boundary falls between useful introspection and harmful rumination, and about what puts a given act of self-examination on one side rather than the other. The implicit line in the current design is roughly: checking thoughts against evidence is healthy; asking open-ended "why" questions is unhealthy; replaying a concrete episode is healthy; dwelling on self-evaluative labels is unhealthy. But I suspect that line was inherited rather than argued for.

Here is what I want from you. First, reconstruct the theory of introspection and rumination that this two-part practice presupposes, as charitably as you can. Second, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined — that is, where a genuinely rival theory would happily ship the same flows. Third, tell me whether the distinction the app draws is coherent: is there a principled difference between reflecting on evidence and asking why, or between concrete replay and abstract self-evaluation, that could explain why one tends to help and the other tends to harm? Where is that distinction likely to break down when we look closely? Fourth, tell me what we would concretely have to change in the product if we replaced the implicit view with either of two alternatives: (a) the view that self-examination is generally beneficial and what we label rumination differs only in intensity, not in kind; or (b) the view that rumination is harmful because it is involuntary and reduces cognitive control, so the relevant line is not about the content of thought but about whether the user can stop when they choose. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need a conceptual structure I can take into a product review.
