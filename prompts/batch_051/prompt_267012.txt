I am taking a seminar in moral psychology and the philosophy of value, and I keep finding the same structure in otherwise unrelated cases: a value that its holders experience as final, self-justifying, or sacred, but that also appears from the outside to be doing steady instrumental work. The three cases I have in mind are truth-seeking in science and scholarship, respect for tradition, and the moralization of purity. I have been developing a theory I call 'terminal capture', and I would like a candid assessment of it rather than encouragement.

The theory is as follows. Many values begin as instrumental norms: they are learned, selected, or adopted because they reliably serve some further end, such as predictive reliability, social coordination, or disease avoidance. When such a value is transmitted across generations or maintained by institutions, the link to the original end can be forgotten or deliberately obscured. At that point the value is no longer experienced as a means; it is experienced as a terminus, something the person must answer to apart from consequences. My proposal is that this state just is what felt intrinsicality consists in: an instrumental value that has lost its visible instrument. 'Terminal capture' names the process by which the phenomenology of finality overtakes what was originally a working value.

The prediction I want to test is that the values that strike people as most obviously intrinsic should be exactly those whose instrumental origins are hardest to recover, because the work they do is diffuse, delayed, or invisible to the individual agent. This would explain why a commitment to truth can feel as if it would survive the loss of every downstream benefit, why traditional rules can feel binding even after the problems they solved have disappeared, and why purity norms can feel as if they track something wrong in the act itself before any harm is specified. It would also explain a familiar reaction: people often treat a successful consequential justification of these values as an offence to them.

I can see two difficulties from within my own view. First, it may be conflating a story about where a value came from with a claim about what standing it has now; even if truth-seeking began as an instrument, that does not by itself show it is not now genuinely final. Second, I have no account of why a forgotten instrumental origin would produce the distinctive feeling of intrinsicality rather than merely producing habit or confident preference. So: is 'terminal capture' coherent, or am I combining a causal-etiological claim, a psychological claim, and a normative claim under one label? Has something close to this already been worked out under another name I should know? What is the strongest objection to the theory, and does that objection kill it or only restrict its scope? Finally, is there a version of the theory that survives the genetic-fallacy worry while still saying something revisionary about truth, tradition, or purity, rather than merely reporting that people happen to feel this way?
