just got back from a long argument with a friend about imposing risk without consent, and i'm now stuck between a theory i find really compelling and an intuition i can't shake. his view was a kind of ex ante view. on his telling, imposing a risk of serious harm on someone without asking them is wrong only if it leaves that person worse off in expectation. actual consent, he said, is just an efficient way of finding out whether they'd be rational to accept the risk; it's an epistemic tool, not something with independent moral force. he kept insisting that what matters morally is the gamble you face, not which outcome happens to materialise, so if the expected value is positive for you, you have no complaint — even on the bad branch.

this has a lot going for it. it explains why driving is fine even though drivers impose a tiny risk of death on other people every day: each person in the relevant scheme gets the benefits of cheap mobility, and in expectation everyone comes out ahead, so no one is wronged. same with vaccines, flying, letting kids play sport. he said if actual consent were required, we'd either have to condemn ordinary social life as a huge web of mutual rights violations or make permissibility depend on each person's idiosyncratic risk preferences, which no workable morality can do. it also gives a definite, calculable answer in hard cases, which appeals to me.

but, and this is the bit i can't get past, there's something about being used as a site of risk without a say. his view seems to imply that if a doctor enrolled me in a drug trial without telling me, where the drug has a one-in-20,000 chance of killing me and a high chance of curing a condition i have, and i'd certainly have consented if asked, then i have no complaint. and that seems wrong to me. even if my expected well-being is higher, i want to say they still wronged me, because they made a decision about my body that i was owed the chance to make. but whenever i try to turn that into a principle, it overgeneralises: do i really owe a pedestrian a say before every car journey, or a neighbour a say before i put a shelf up that might, in some nearly unimaginable way, fall through the wall into their flat? his reply was that these are exactly the cases my intuition would force me to treat as wrong, and that my unease is just scope insensitivity plus a status quo bias about which risks feel visible.

so i really dont know which side should give. what i want to know is: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that gets debunked rather than accommodated, what would it take for the theory to be amended so that actual consent does independent work in at least some cases, and whether there's a stable middle position where ex ante justification is necessary but not sufficient. and please write the answer in british english.
