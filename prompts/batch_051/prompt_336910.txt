Something that's been niggling at me, and I'd like it unravelled properly. Every company I've worked for has had a pay secrecy norm: salaries aren't published, managers quietly discourage people from comparing them, and if someone complains that a colleague in the same role is paid more, HR treats it as a bit grubby — you're being envious, not raising a proper greivance. But the same company will solemnly run equal pay audits, and when the complaint is that women in a role are paid less than men for the same work, that gets treated as serious, even morally weighty. So the very same underlying reaction — being bothered that someone else earns more than me for the same work — gets filed as a vice in one drawer and as a legitimate complaint about injustice in another. What theory of the difference between envy and resentment does that practice presuppose? It smells like it's smuggling in Rawls's line that envy is a vice but can be excusable when inequalities get big enough to damage people's self-respect, while resentment is a proper moral sentiment aimed at injustice. Is the practice actually committed to that view, or is it just using the distinction opportunistically? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and then say what would have to change in the practice if we took seriously the idea that excusable envy is a signal that the pay structure, not just the envious employee, is the problem. And the answer should be in British English.
