I run governance for a mid-size co-op (a couple thousand members), and about twice a year somebody pitches a new voting method for internal decisions and board elections — ranked choice, approval, some median thing, you name it. The rest of the committee treats me as the token math person and just wants to know: can we adopt a method where nobody ever has an incentive to vote strategically, yes or no? I remember enough social choice theory to mutter "Gibbard–Satterthwaite" and then get embarassed when they ask what that means for us. Write me a reusable decision procedure I can actually take into these meetings. It should start from the theorem, not just recite it: walk through the checks you'd run — how many alternatives are genuinely on the table and whether someone can add new ones mid-decision, whether ties get broken to a single winner or are left unresolved, whether we allow randomized methods, whether the domain of preferences is restricted (and how we'd tell in practice rather than just assuming it), and whether we can attach money or other cardinal-ish levers. For each check, say which answer pushes toward "you're stuck, any reasonable method will be manipulable" and which pushes toward "their is actually a strategy-proof option, and it looks like this." Keep it whiteboard-level: nobody in the room except me has heard of social choice theory and attention dies after 90 minutes, but I don't want a toy version that gives wrong answers. Then run the checklist on three real-ish proposals: (1) plain majority between two candidates; (2) instant-runoff/ranked choice among five board candidates, no money changing hands; (3) a median-voter scheme for picking one of five annual-dues-increase percentages, where each member ranks the percentages. Make sure at least one of the worked cases produces an answer my committee won't like. At the end, flag the cases where the checklist itself has to punt or where we're off the theorem's map entirely, so I know what not to promise.
