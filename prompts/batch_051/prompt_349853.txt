I have noticed what looks like a structural parallel between two debates that do not usually meet, and I want to know whether it is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. On one side is the dispute between propensity and frequentist interpretations of probability. On the other is the dispute between dispositionalist and regularity accounts of dispositions such as fragility. The mapping seems natural: propensity says the probability of heads for this particular coin is a real, single-case tendency of the coin-and-toss setup, just as dispositionalism says this particular glass has a real tendency to break even if it never actually breaks; frequentism says probability is a regularity in a reference class of trials, just as a regularity account reduces fragility to a pattern across cases. If the parallel were deep, the pressure points should align. The reference class problem—which class of trials we should average over—should be the same problem as specifying a disposition's stimulus and background conditions. And a masked disposition (the glass packed in foam that would break if struck but is never struck) should be the same problem as a chance setup with a stable single-case propensity that never manifests. I would like you to assess the parallel rather than simply apply it: what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one debate to the other, and what is one concrete thing the parallel predicts that looks false? In your answer, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these topics, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Please write in American English and format the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
