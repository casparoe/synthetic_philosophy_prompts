I'm in an epistemology seminar that has spent the last three weeks on contextualism and pragmatic encroachment, and I've ended up with a question the readings leave open. The contested claim is that practical stakes are not merely incidental to knowledge: two people can be in the same evidential position regarding the same true proposition, and one can genuinely know it while the other does not, because far more hangs on whether the second person is right. The seminar is split between those who read this as a claim about knowledge itself and those who read it as a claim only about our knowledge-talk — about when we are willing to assert "I know" or to rely on a belief in acting.

I'd like to know whether that claim can be empirically tested, and how much a test could possibly settle. Design the strongest test you can: the manipulation, the measured response, the conditions to hold fixed, and the results that would pull in each direction. A good test should try to get past the obvious objection that one has merely measured the pragmatics of assertion rather than knowledge. Then say what a positive result would and would not show — could it ever confirm encroachment rather than, say, a debunking account of our knowledge-talk? — and whether a null result would refute the encroachment claim or only the operationalisation. Please write your answer in British English.
