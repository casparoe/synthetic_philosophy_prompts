I'm putting together a unit on statutes of limitations and the question of whether the passage of time does any moral work on its own, or whether it's really just a proxy for three different things: evidence decaying, the offender becoming a different person, and the state forfeiting its standing by dragging its feet. Build me a thought experiment to this spec, because I want a case that pulls those three rationales apart.

I want a realistic white-collar crime case where, after the limitation period has run, the evidence-decay rationale says "there's no reason to bar this" and the changed-person rationale also says "there's no reason to bar this," but the state-inaction rationale says "bar it." Usually those first two are exactly the reasons we have limitation periods at all, so the case has to do real work to flip them. Constraints: ordinary workplace or municipal financial office, no murder or war crimes, no newly discovered evidence, no exotic memory tricks or fake neuroscience. The state must have had a complete, admissible case on day one and still have it now; the evidence didn't get better, it just didn't degrade. The offender can't have repented, can't have made restitution, and shouldn't think of the crime as something "the old me" did. Keep the scenario to a paragraph or two.

Then do the check. For each rationale, state the verdict it gives in your case and walk through why. Tell me which exact feature of the case is doing the work — I want the specific combination, not a wave at "good records." Say what happens if you delete that one feature but leave the rest intact: does the case collapse back into all three rationales agreeing, and which way? Then handle the awkward part: the state-inaction rationale comes out looking like the bad guy here, since it has to say the offender walks; give the best defense of that verdict a defender of the rationale could give, in a way that makes it look principled rather than perverse. End by telling me whether there's any residual reason to bar prosecution in your case that isn't captured by those three rationales — i.e., whether time is doing moral work on it's own.
