Okay, my friend has this argument that seems to show the principle of indifference is just broken, and I can't figure out which step to push back on. We both agree it can't be a fully general rule for continuous quantities, but his argument seems to prove way too much: it would also undercut the innocent finite uses, like assigning 1/6 to each face of a die when you have no reason to favour any face. I'd like to keep at least that much. Here's the argument as he gave it:

(1) Principle of indifference (PI): if a quantity is known only to lie in some interval and you have no reason to favour any part of the interval over any other, assign a uniform distribution over that interval.

(2) Suppose a machine produces a cube about which you know only that its side length L is between 0 and 1 metre. By PI, treat L as uniform on [0, 1]. Then P(L > 0.5) = 0.5.

(3) The same cube has a face area A = L², which also lies between 0 and 1 m². You also know nothing that favours one part of that interval over another, so by PI, treat A as uniform on [0, 1]. Then P(A > 0.25) = 0.75.

(4) But A > 0.25 is exactly the same event as L > 0.5. So PI has assigned 0.5 and 0.75 to the same event, from the same evidence.

(5) Therefore PI is inconsistent: it just dresses up an arbitrary choice of description (side length vs face area vs volume) as a probability, so it isn't a sound principle anywhere, not even for finite cases.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? My friend says the only way out is full subjective Bayesianism, where "random cube" without a specified distribution is just an incomplete question. Is he right that there's no non-arbitrary way to pick the right variable, or can PI be fixed cheaply, e.g. by restricting it to finite partitions or to variables with some kind of naturalness property -- and would that still let me say the die has probability 1/6 per face?
