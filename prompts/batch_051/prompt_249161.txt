At a reading from my latest novel, two audience members fell into an argument over a scene in which the protagonist, Iris, finally confesses to a friend that she had sabotaged the friend's career years before. The chapter gives Iris's reasons for confessing — accumulated guilt, exhaustion, love — but it also describes, in unsparing neurobiological detail, the neural cascade in the moments before she speaks, and it states that this cascade was causally sufficient for her confession. The argument resumed in the lobby afterward, and it exposed a tension in the scene I had not fully thought through.

(1) Daniel said the scene is incoherent. The novel offers two causal stories about one act: Iris's reasons led her to confess, and a sufficient neural cascade led her to confess. If the cascade was sufficient, the reasons did no causal work, and the psychological story becomes decoration rather than an account of what moved her.

(2) Elena replied that the two stories are not competitors. The cascade is the guilt and the exhaustion under another description; there are not two causes vying for one effect, but one event described at two levels.

(3) Daniel answered that this identity move makes matters worse. If the motive just is the cascade, then the motive does whatever causal work it does only in virtue of being a neural event, and the reason as a reason — the thing Iris would recognize as what she acted on — drops out. The novel would then be explaining the confession away rather than explaining it.

(4) Elena answered that causation is not the only way an event can be explained. Even if the cascade is sufficient, the reason-story explains the confession in another register: it shows what the confession meant for Iris and why it made sense from inside her life. The novel is not claiming that motives are extra forces inserted between neurons; it is claiming that motives make the act intelligible.

(5) Daniel said this, too, is a concession. If reasons explain only by making the act intelligible, they do not bring the confession about. But the scene's effect depends on the reader's sense that Iris's guilt finally caught up with her and that she spoke because she could no longer bear the concealment. Once the novel admits the cascade was sufficient, the sense-making is a story told afterward about an outcome it did not produce; the reader is being asked to feel the pull of motives that, by the novel's own terms, do no work.

I could not tell who was right. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each of the five moves, say whether it is correct or mistaken and why; then say what, if anything, both of them are missing; and finally say who is closer to being right overall.
