Me and my brother recently started a small painting business, and after a job where we underbid and then rushed the work we got into an argument about whether it makes sense to blame the business itself rather than one of us. He says the business is just the two of us with a name, so faulting the business is just faulting people in different words. I'm not convinced, partly because I can imagine the company outlasting both of us and keeping the same habits, and partly because my gut clearly blames some organizations more than any particular person inside them. But I dont trust my gut on this, so I'd like to know what principle, if any, it's tracking.

Here is my pattern of verdicts. (1) A carmaker installs software that cheats emissions tests; the engineers say they were following orders and the executives say they didn't know. I blame the company itself, and I think a fine against the corporate entity is appropriate rather than just an indirect way of punishing whichever people were involved. (2) A bank acquires a smaller bank that laundered money years before the merger, and the merged company is pursued for it. I'm fine blaming the current company even though nobody currently there did the laundering. (3) In our two-person painting business, if my brother skims from supplier payments without telling me, I blame him, not really the business; saying the business is blameworthy feels like a category mistake, and I think the same about a solo doctor who overbills. (4) In a joint venture where the active partner runs the fraud and a silent investor merely profits from it, I blame the active partner, I suspend judgement on the silent one, but I still say the venture did something wrong. (5) A construction firm's bonus system rewards finishing early so heavily that dangerous corner-cutting falls out of the incentives without anyone ever deciding to cut corners. There I want to blame the company's structure or culture, maybe the company itself. (6) A neighborhood with a mugging problem for forty years, where residents and even crime watches keep cycling out, doesn't strike me as blameworthy; blaming the neighborhood feels like loose talk. (7) A worker-owned cooperative votes to ship a product they know is unsafe, and nearly everyone voted for it. I blame the co-op as a group, and this feels more genuinely group blame than the carmaker case. (8) A public company with dispersed shareholders is fined because one rogue employee violated policy in a way ordinary monitoring wouldn't catch. There I accept corporate liability, but calling the company blameworthy feels wrong.

I want a principle that gets all or most of these, stated precisely enough to rule on cases I haven't listed: a fraternity hazing death, a hospital that cuts nursing staff by formula, a nation-state's military, a trading firm where an algorithm nobody supervises manipulates the market. The pair I can't make peace between is (5) and (6): both are stable patterns that outlive their members, but I blame the company and not the neighborhood. Which verdicts does the principle force me to give up? And do I end up haveing to say that corporate blame is never literal, or that it becomes literal only when there's something like a real group decision?
