I sit on the compensation review board of a medium-sized university, and we've spent two meetings stuck on a formal grievance from a group of long-time administrative and facilities staff. Their core claim: hires in the "growth" units (online programs, fundraising) are coming in at or above what people with fifteen years here make, and this year's equity review once again steered the money to those units while the rest got a thinner cost-of-living adjustment. The petition is full of language about humiliation, about not being able to look new hires in the eye, about watching other people walk around with the salary that should have been theirs. The board has split into two camps. One says the petition is mostly envy and resentment, and an institution shouldn't set pay because some people feel bad that someone else has more; that's a vice, and if we cave to it we teach people to perform grievance. The other camp says that's backwards: the emotional tone is itself information, and when people near the bottom feel this kind of resentment it's a symptom that the inequality has stopped being justifiable, so the real mistake would be to police there emotions instead of the pay structure. That camp keeps invoking the Rawlsian idea of excusable envy — the thought I associate with Rawls that some envy is excusable because the underlying inequality wounds self-respect and it's unreasonable to expect people to bear it without resentment, so the envy is a signal rather than a moral failing.

I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting, and I'd like you to write it as a political philosopher who understands how institutional decisions actually get made. Draft the short memo I can put my name on. It should recommend a concrete course of action for this specific grievance — for example, whether and how to treat the envious/resentful language as evidence rather than as discrediting, whether to commision an external pay-equity audit, how to rule on the grievance, and on what timetable — and give the reasoning behind that recommendation. Spend real time on the strongest version of the losing side, the worry that acting on envy-based complaints converts the board into an instrument of vice and creates bad incentives; don't hand-wave it. Before you settle on the recommendation, start by brainstorming the relevant considerations, and make sure to separate plain envy (wanting the good someone else has) from the kind of resentment that tracks a claim of being wronged — I suspect half our deadlock is just those two being conflated.
