Draft the joint statement that a committed defender of the whole-brain criterion of death and a committed defender of the circulatory-respiratory criterion of death could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Work from the following working characterisation of the two positions. The whole-brain defender holds that death consists in the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, because the brain is what integrates the organism into a single whole; on this view a warm, ventilated body with a dead brain is a corpse whose remaining processes are kept going mechanically. The circulatory-respiratory defender holds that death consists in the irreversible cessation of circulation and respiration, because the bloodstream and gas exchange are what integrate the organism; on this view a body whose heart still beats and whose tissues are still perfused is a living, if severely impaired, organism, and calling it dead is partly a stipulation adopted to make organ retrieval permissible.

Produce the document this collaboration would yield, not a commentary on it. It should contain:

(1) A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover what both sides take themselves to be disagreeing about, what both accept about irreversibility, what both accept about the moral and practical significance of the distinction, and what both deny — for example, that the dispute reduces to a disagreement about what is permissible after death. Make each claim non-vacuous and worded so that both could sign it.

(2) A disagreements table with columns: issue; whole-brain formulation, phrased so its own side would accept it; circulatory-respiratory formulation, phrased so its own side would accept it; and a note on the residual difference. The issues should include at least the following: what death consists in, as opposed to what merely indicates it; whether a ventilated brain-dead body is one organism or an aggregation of living parts; and whether circulation maintained on a ventilator counts as the organism's own activity.

(3) A crux section: identify two or three sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case, test, or argument that both sides agree bears on it — for instance, a patient on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation with preserved brain function, or a brain-dead body that sustains a pregnancy — and state in advance what each side predicts the case will show.

(4) A closing note on which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal rather than substantive, and which residual disagreement cannot be resolved by any empirical facts both sides accept.

Be precise about the distinctions between criterion, test, and operational protocol. Avoid bibliographic references, citations, and reading lists. Write in British English.
