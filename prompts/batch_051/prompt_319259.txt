I am a PhD student in philosophy of law, working on coercion and the moral limits of the criminal law, and I have been circling the familiar problem of blackmail. The puzzle, as I understand it, is roughly this: a person may, in many cases, permissibly reveal a damaging truth about someone, and a person may permissibly ask another for money; yet when the two acts are fused into a single conditional proposal — pay me or I make the information public — the law and much ordinary moral thought treat the result as blackmail and condemn it. Why the conjunction should be more wrong than its parts is what I find puzzling, and I suspect that the answer matters beyond doctrine, for how we individuate actions and distinguish coercive threats from legitimate offers. By the central cases, I mean those in which the threatened disclosure is true and not independently criminal, and the demand is for money in exchange for silence. Please set aside the details of any particular penal code and reason at the level of principle rather than doctrine.

To focus my thinking, I have collected five broad rationales for criminalizing blackmail, stated in my own words rather than as named theories. (1) The privacy/autonomy rationale: disclosure of intimate or reputation-damaging truths is often itself a wrong, or at least an infringement of the subject's legitimate interest in controlling their private information, so the apparent paradox rests on the false assumption that the threatened act was morally innocuous. (2) The third-party harm rationale: blackmail predictably injures people other than the victim and the blackmailer, because victims may embezzle, defraud, or otherwise wrong others to raise the money, and suppressing the information may leave employers, creditors, or a political community exposed to hidden misconduct. (3) The unproductive-transaction rationale: blackmail creates no value and performs no legitimate economic function; it is a pure transfer achieved by a threat, it wastes resources, and it encourages strategic investment in discovering damaging secrets rather than productive activity. (4) The coercive-threat rationale: the decisive feature is the conditional structure — the blackmailer announces that he will perform a harmful or unwanted act unless paid — and this is what makes the proposal coercive, quite apart from whether the threatened act would otherwise have been lawful. (5) The wrongful-relationship rationale: the core objection is not to the threatened disclosure itself but to the relation the blackmailer creates, one that converts another person's vulnerability into a private source of revenue and tends to leave the victim in a lasting condition of dependence on the blackmailer's continued silence.

Please rank these five rationales from most to least explanatory of why blackmail, in its central cases, is properly criminalized. By explanatory, I mean that a strong rationale should account for the wrongness of the act, for why the prohibition attaches to the conditional transaction rather than to either component alone, and for why blackmail differs from ordinary confidentiality agreements or hard bargaining. Give a complete ordering, not loose tiers, and do not simply survey each rationale's strengths and weaknesses. I want you to commit: say which single rationale does the most fundamental explanatory work and which you would demote as weakest, and justify both choices in terms of what you take the core of the wrong of blackmail to be. You should also flag the point in your ordering where you are least confident, even while defending the ranking as a whole.
