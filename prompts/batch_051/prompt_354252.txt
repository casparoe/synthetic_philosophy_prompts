I've recently had to watch someone I respect pay a real price for refusing to soften a finding, and it has made me wonder about the difference between being incorruptible and being merely rigid. Here is the asymmetry that puzzles me. When a public official refuses gifts, favors, or a quiet adjustment to a report, we call it integrity and hold it up as heroic; Seneca, in On the Firmness of the Wise Man, seems to capture the ideal when he writes of Cato: "He alone withstood the vices of a worn-out State, sinking into ruin through its own bulk; he upheld the falling commonwealth as far as it could be upheld by one man's hand, until at last his support was withdrawn, and he shared the crash which he had so long averted, and perished together with that from which it was impious to separate him—for Cato did not outlive freedom, nor did freedom outlive Cato." Yet when a colleague at a research firm refuses a small, harmless adjustment to a client deliverable that would have made everyone's life easier, or when a friend refuses to say anything less than the exact truth to a grieving relative, we are more likely to call them unbending, self-righteous, or difficult. We admire the trait when it is aimed at large public wrongs, and we diagnose it as a character flaw when it is aimed at small private accommodations. Is the difference just a difference in the weight of what is at stake, or are these actually different traits? What is the strongest principle you can give me that generates both verdicts, and does it imply that we should either admire the difficult friend more or admire Cato less?
