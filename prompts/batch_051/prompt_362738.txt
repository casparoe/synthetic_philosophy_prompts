I've been trying to get clear on the Mencius/Xunzi dispute over whether human nature is good, and I keep getting stuck on one specific Mencius passage. In Mencius 6A7 (the Gaozi book), he argues with an analogy about barley. As I remember it: barley seeds, if you sow them in the same soil at the same season, will grow, but in good years the grain is full and in bad years the grain is poor. The difference is not in the seed but in the soil, the moisture, and how the plants are tended. Then he says the sage and I are alike in kind, so we should look at environments rather than claim some people are naturally bad.

A friend of mine who prefers Xunzi said this passage actually backfires. He said Mencius's own example shows the seed simply responds to conditions, and it can just as easily turn out badly. He says that pushes toward Xunzi's view in the chapter Human Nature Is Bad: people are born with desires that, left unmanaged, lead to strife, and goodness has to be deliberately crafted through ritual, education, and a teacher. Xunzi's own images are things like warped wood being forced straight by a press, or a dull blade being sharpened by a whetstone. The object does not straighten or sharpen itself, and the wood's natural grain is no help.

My reply was that Mencius is not denying that environment matters. He is claiming there is a natural trajectory toward maturity or flourishing that gets stunted in bad conditions, the way barley naturally moves toward producing grain even if a drought ruins it. My friend said that is cheating, because a bare biological trajectory has no moral content. The fact that a seed will grow if conditions allow does not show human nature is good in the sense Mencius needs.

So here is what I want compared and adjudicated. Is Mencius's argument in the barley passage really a claim about moral goodness, or only about a natural potential that even Xunzi could accept? Whose reading of the analogy is closer? And if Mencius has a way to absorb my friend's objection without giving up his claim that human nature is good, what exactly is that way? Please compare the two positions at the level of the analogy's logic, not just as two general worldviews. Also, tell me what consideration or argument would most likely change your mind about the verdict, or where your reasoning about the passage is shakiest.
