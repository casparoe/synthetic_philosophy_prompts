I cover AI and the ideas around it for a living, and over the last few years I've absorbed a lot of the longtermist way of thinking without ever sitting down to check weather my own views are coherent. I'm about to write a piece about s-risks — the worry that future technology could create astronomical amounts of suffering, like a misaligned AI running billions of digital minds in pain, or a simulation full of conscious beings who suffer. A source at an AI lab keeps pressing me on why I prioritize this stuff, and I've realized I can't cleanly explain my own position. Before I put any of it in print, I want someone to poke holes in the set of views I've ended up with.

Here are the commitments I seem to have acquired:

(1) If we ever build digital minds that can suffer, their suffering matters morally in the same basic way as human or animal suffering — it's not just a metaphor.

(2) I'm not a utilitarian. I think causing a harm is worse than allowing the same harm, and I don't think you can aggregate welfare across different individuals into one big total that automatically decides what to do.

(3) Preventing s-risks should be one of the highest moral priorities of this century, possibly above a lot of present-day causes.

(4) It would be wrong to run a simulation containing conscious beings who suffer, even if it also contained many more happy beings and nobody would ever find out.

(5) I'm suspicious of arguments that depend on precise welfare comparisons across radically different kinds of minds — I don't think there's a well-defined exchange rate between a human and a digital mind, or between a present being and a far-future one.

Does this set of views hang together? Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look that way. Rank the real tensions by severity, and say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost.
