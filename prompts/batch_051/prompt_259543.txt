I retired a few years ago from a career in the natural sciences—experimental chemistry—and have been spending my retirement reading philosophy. A puzzle from the philosophy of language has been nagging at me, and I would like your help thinking it through. I am not interested in a literature review or citations; I want your own careful reasoning.

You are a professional philosopher with expertise in the philosophy of language and in the ethics of social categories. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that might justify the asymmetry I am about to describe, test each candidate against hard cases, and close with an honest final verdict.

Hofstadter's satirical Person Paper asks the reader to imagine a society whose language marks race in much the way English marks sex. In that imagined society, the unmarked word for a human being in general is simply the word for a member of the favored racial group; the other group must be named with an extra, marked form even when the reference is generic. Professional titles, abstract nouns, and generic statements default to the favored-group form, while the marked form is reserved for the other group. The characters in this society find the convention natural, neutral, and polite, and they find objections baffling. The reader is meant to find the imagined language obviously racist, and then to notice that our own language does something formally similar with sex. If you happen not to know the essay, do not worry; that summary gives you the part that matters.

Here is the asymmetry that bothers me. Many people who would regard the imagined race-marking convention as morally repugnant treat the parallel sex-marking convention as innocent, merely traditional, or at worst a small blemish. In a great deal of ordinary English, the unmarked form for a human being is also the male form, and the female form carries the mark: man and woman, mankind and womankind, chairman and chairwoman, the generic he and the special she. Defenders of these forms rarely offer arguments they would accept if the marked category were race rather than sex. But perhaps a real difference exists. Sex and race are different kinds of categories; the male-default forms have a long continuous history in English; maybe some pragmatic or linguistic principle makes default-male usage less harmful, more natural, or otherwise more defensible than default-white usage would be.

My question is whether any such principle actually works. I would like the strongest principle you can construct for treating default-male language as less objectionable than the imagined default-white language, and then an honest verdict on it after stress-testing. In particular, test your principle against a third case I have not seen discussed: imagine default-adult language, in which the unmarked word for a person is the word for an adult and children are named only with a special marked form, so that a room containing adults and a child is addressed by the adult term and the child must be explicitly marked as not-default. Does your principle classify that case correctly alongside the other two? If the asymmetry cannot survive these tests, say so plainly, and tell me whether it ought to be kept as a harmless convention, reversed in force, or given up as an unjustified inconsistency.
