I teach epistemology, and next month I have to lead a single ninety-minute graduate seminar session introducing the bootstrapping problem and easy-knowledge reasoning to second-year PhD students who have taken the department's core epistemology course but have not read the specialist literature on these topics. They work fluently with the internalism/externalism distinction, process reliabilism, evidentialism, epistemic closure, and the idea of defeaters. The group includes two committed evidentialists, one committed reliabilist, and several students who have not yet chosen a side; each camp will read every example through its own commitments. I want a plan for how to teach the material, not a summary of it.

The session has one overriding pedagogical aim. Bootstrapping and easy knowledge are usually presented together, but they put pressure on different positions. Bootstrapping is, at bottom, a problem for reliabilist or externalist views: a reliable cognitive process seems able to generate, from its own outputs, the empirical conclusion that the process itself is reliable, and thereby to convert bare reliability into reflective, evidence-backed reliability without independent support. Easy-knowledge reasoning is, at bottom, a problem for modest internalist or evidentialist views that let one know ordinary things about the world without first possessing evidence for the reliability of one's sources, since ordinary perceptual knowledge then seems to make the much stronger conclusion that perception is trustworthy too easy to reach. A student who runs the two together ends up thinking that one blunt lesson — track-record arguments cannot confer justification — handles both, when in fact a repair that addresses one problem often leaves the other untouched.

Please give me the explanatory arc for the session: the concepts that must be on the table before either problem can be stated, the order in which they must be introduced, and why each step depends on the previous one. Then give me the one carrying example on which to build the whole session: a single ordinary case whose force students can feel, which can be varied slightly to produce first the bootstrapping problem and then the easy-knowledge problem. Say what the example must contain and what it must not contain, and why the default examples students will import from their prior coursework — the unreliable clairvoyant, the stopped clock, the Cartesian demon — are the wrong vehicles here. I also want the misconceptions to expect, in the order in which they will arise, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than argued down: for instance, that bootstrapping is just circular reasoning and therefore already refuted, that easy knowledge is only a demand for certainty, and that reliabilism can escape bootstrapping by adding a no-defeaters clause. Where should I deliberately let a misconception run because it is a productive ladder, and where must I stop it immediately because the later distinction cannot otherwise take hold? Finally, give me one short question or two-minute exercise for the last ten minutes that would show whether a student has understood the distinction between the two problems, rather than merely memorized that process reliabilism has a bootstrapping problem. Be specific about the dependency structure; I can write the explanatory prose myself, but I need the map.
