I've backed myself into a weird conclusion about rescue cases and I cant find the step that's off. Here's the argument as I wrote it down:
(1) If a rule lets someone be sacrificed to produce a bigger total of benefits for other people, that person can reject the rule from his own point of view, even when the total is huge.
(2) So a fair rule has to be judged by the strongest complaint a single affected person can make against it; you dont get to add complaints up across people.
(3) Take a choice between saving one stranger and saving five strangers, where the harm on each side is the same (death). Each of the five can complain only about being left to die, and none of their complaints is stronger than the one person's.
(4) So the strongest complaint against saving the one is no stronger than the strongest complaint against saving the five. By (2), neither rule is more rejectable than the other.
(5) Hence numbers don't tip the scales, and the fair thing would be to give everyone an equal chance — like flipping a coin.
But it feels obviously wrong not to just save the five. I halfway think the slide from (3) to (4) is underhanded — moving from each person individually to the two sides as wholes — but I cant make the slide precise. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and on what reading? And if it is valid, is the coin flip really what follows?
