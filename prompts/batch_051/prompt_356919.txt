My flatmate and I just spent the whole evening argueing about the simulation argument, and I'm left with the nagging feeling I've only got a handful of genuinely distinct moves in favour of the hypothesis and that I'm missing one. By the end I'd scribbled these on the back of an envelope:

(1) The census argument, basically Bostrom's statistical one. A posthuman civilisation could run stackloads of high-fidelity ancestor simulations, so simulated minds would vastly outnumber organic ones. If such civilisations exist and actually use the technology, then by ordinary anthropic reasoning you should think you're almost certainly among the simulated; so either almost no civilisation reaches that stage, or almost none of them runs sims, or we're probably simulated.

(2) The substrate-independence point. If consciousness doesn't care whether it runs on carbon neurons or silicon, then there's no in-principle barrier to a mind living in a simulation. And since physics already looks computational, and our own brains already build a working model of the world, being simulated starts to look like a modest extension rather than a category jump.

(3) The trend argument. We already run smaller simulations of worlds, agents and histories, and the fidelity and scale keep increasing. So the conditional — that an advanced civilisation would run ancestor sims — isn't the exotic part; it's the default reading of our own trajectory.

(4) The explanatory argument. The hypothesis earns its keep by explaining otherwise puzzling facts: the Fermi silence if the sim is Earth-only or thin, and the fine-tuned look of the physical constants if the sim was built for something. That makes it a live competitor to the multiverse and to sheer chance.

(5) The no-decisive-refutation move. The obvious objections — computational limits, why would they bother, the absence of glitches, the fact that a base universe would itself need explaining — can all be answered or at least turned back on the objector, so the hypothesis stays on the table when a lot of rivals drop out.

I know some of these are proper arguments and some are more like clearing obstacles, but as things that push toward the simulation hypothesis that's my list, and I think it's basically the standard set. My mate, who's annoyingly good at this, says there's at least one more distinct family that doesn't on my list, and I cannot see it.

What's the argument I'm missing? Keep it genuinely different from the five above — not a restatement, not a special case, and not the pragmatic move of acting as if it's true, since that's decision theory rather than a reason to believe it. Then be straight with me: is it actually any good, or does it collapse into one of the five under pressure?
