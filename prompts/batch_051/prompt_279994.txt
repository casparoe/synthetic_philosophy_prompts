A reading-group argument has been with me for weeks, and I cannot find the exit. It starts from statutes of limitations, but it ends by forcing a choice I do not want between a world in which old wrongs expire like parking tickets and a world in which nothing is ever too old to pursue. The argument runs as follows.

(1) If a statute of limitations is justified, it is justified either by the bare passage of time, or by what the passage of time characteristically brings with it — decayed evidence, faded memory, changed circumstances, an offender who has rebuilt a life.

(2) It cannot be justified by what time characteristically brings, because in its basic operation a limitation turns only on the elapsed time: it does not look to whether evidence has in fact decayed, memory has in fact faded, circumstances have in fact changed, or the offender has in fact reformed. It bars the strong case and the weak case alike once the date passes, and it bars neither before the date arrives.

(3) So if a statute of limitations is justified, it must be by the bare passage of time itself — the idea that a wrong, simply by receding further into the past, loses something that made it answerable.

(4) But nothing in the bare passage of time distinguishes one wrong from another. The date recedes for a fraud and for an enslavement in exactly the same way, so if mere age can drain the answerability out of one wrong it drains it indifferently from every wrong.

(5) Therefore, whoever accepts the justification reached at (3) must grant that wrongs committed in the more distant past — a dispossession, an enslavement, a massacre, an exploitation — have, simply by being older, a weaker claim to acknowledgment, repair, or accountability than they had when they were fresh.

(6) But that is too much to grant. A victim or descendant who learns today of a harm done long ago is not owed a smaller response because the harm lies far back; what made the deed wrong belongs to the deed, not to its date.

(7) So the state has no better reason to relinquish a forty-year-old offense than a four-day-old one, and the only honest conclusion is that statutes of limitations, in their actual categorical form, rest on a mistake.

I do not want to accept (7), yet I can see the case against each of (1), (4), and (6), and each alternative costs something I would like to keep. Rejecting (1) means denying that those two options exhaust the field, but I cannot articulate what the third option would be. Rejecting (4) means making time's moral power selective, without saying what marks the difference between a claim that fades and a claim that does not. Rejecting (6) means conceding that wrongdoing can, after enough years, simply cease to wrong. Which step would you reject, and what exactly is the price? I want the diagnosis, not a paraphrase: which step fails, why it looked as though it had to be true, and what the view looks like once it is given up.
