I gave my students the following passage from Nick Bostrom’s “Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?” and asked them the question below. I’m trying to grade it without leaning too hard on my own view, so I’d like you to be the grader.

Passage:

“One thing that later generations might do with their super-powerful computers is run detailed simulations of their forebears or of people like their forebears. Because their computers would be so powerful, they could run a great many such simulations. Suppose that these simulated people are conscious (as they would be if the simulations were sufficiently fine-grained and if a certain quite widely accepted position in the philosophy of mind is correct). Then it could be the case that the vast majority of minds like ours do not belong to the original race but rather to people simulated by the advanced descendants of an original race.”

Question: Suppose someone argues that because humanity’s far future could contain an astronomical number of sentient beings, even very small probabilities of influencing that far future can dominate our moral calculations—call this “far-future fanaticism.” Does Bostrom’s simulation argument, as represented in this passage, dampen far-future fanaticism, strengthen it, or leave it unchanged? Explain.

Student answer:

Bostrom’s simulation argument shows that at least one of three things is true: we almost certainly go extinct before reaching a posthuman stage, posthuman civilizations almost never run ancestor-simulations, or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. This does dampen far-future fanaticism. If we are not simulated, then the first two disjuncts mean we should assign a very low probability to the future that fanaticism cares about, so the expected value of trying to influence the far future collapses. If we are simulated, then our world is just a simulation running inside someone else’s history, and our decisions have no effect on the base-level future of mind; the simulation could be shut off at any time, so far-future interventions lose their expected value there too. Either way, far-future fanaticism is dampened. The only way to preserve it is to reject substrate-independence, since the whole argument depends on simulated beings being conscious.

Grade this answer out of 10 and give the specific comments that justify the grade. I want point-by-point feedback, not just a general impression.
