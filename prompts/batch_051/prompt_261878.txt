I could use a clear-eyed take on a fight my co-founder and I keep having, because neither of us has the background to settle it. We've built a tool that screens applications for our small staffing company. It doesn't auto-reject anyone; it gives each applicant a score from 0 to 100 for how likely they are to stay and do well in the job, and then managers spend more time vetting people with high scores and less time on low scores. The tool doesn't use sex or race or anything like that; we only check the scores afterwords to see how different groups are being treated. My co-founder says the tool is fair only if it makes the same mistakes for every group: the same share of people who would have done fine in the job getting a low score, and the same share of people who would have washed out getting a high score, whether we slice by men and women or any other group we care about. I get the appeal, but I want something that sounds even more basic to me: if the tool says an applicant is an 80, that number should mean the same thing across groups. An 80 should correspond to the same actual chance of working out no matter who the applicant is. Otherwise the score is just lying to us depending on who it's describing. Our data person says we can't have both at the same time, given our past applicant data, because men and women succeeded at different overall rates in that data. She showed us that if you force the mistake rates to match, the scores stop meaning the same thing across groups, and if you force the scores to mean the same thing, the mistake rates split apart. So now we're stuck. Does the fact that these two obviously-fair-looking things can't both be had mean fairness is just impossible and we should pick whichever feels less bad? Or is one of them the right one for a tool like ours, given how we actually use it?
