A colleague's argument last week forced me to notice a rule I have been running for years, and I want to know whether the rule is defensible. The rule: when someone claims that a present-day democracy is "in its late Republic" or that some current development is "how the Roman Republic fell," I discount the argument almost to zero. Last week the claim was that an executive's growing reliance on emergency powers is the modern version of the senatus consultum ultimum, the Senate's decree that the state was in danger and that magistrates should see to it that no harm came to the Republic, and that this is how the slow slide into constitutional death begins. I did not check his history; I mostly regarded the analogy as evidence of his politics rather than of the facts.

My reasons for the standing rule are these. First, I can rarely verify the historical claims, and the people making the analogy are rarely in a better position than I am. Second, the past is large enough to supply a decline-story for almost any conclusion, so the analogy often does no independent evidential work. Third, when I have checked such analogies, they seemed to rest on institutional details that do not transfer to any modern democracy: a senate that was not a representative legislature, armies paid and personally loyal to their commanders, a popular assembly without the structure of a modern electorate, and a tribunate that could veto the ordinary machinery of government. If the mechanism does not transfer, the analogy should not carry the conclusion with it. Fourth, the people making these analogies rarely state base rates: most regimes sharing a few features of the late Republic did not have its trajectory, and many modern democracies have survived comparable stresses without collapsing.

A friend tells me this is not a rule but a bias about a genre of argument. A good analogy should move my credence at least a little, he says, and a strong structural analogy should move it a lot; that a tool is often misused is not evidence about the next use. He adds that the defensible response to not being able to check the history is a modest discount for unverified specifics, not a near-zeroing of the conclusion, and that ordinary non-historians can learn to grade analogies rather than refusing them wholesale.

My question is whether my standing discount is defensible as a policy for someone in my position. More precisely: is there a principled rate at which I should discount arguments from historical analogy, and what would a restated rule look like that keeps the benefits of my skepticism without simply throwing away a class of evidence? Am I entitled to the policy, given that I usually cannot check the historical specifics myself? As you answer, please separate what is settled among people who work on the late Republic or on reasoning by historical analogy from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading of the Roman crisis, or of how to treat historical analogies, as the consensus view.
