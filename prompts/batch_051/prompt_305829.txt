I clerk for a three-judge panel of an administrative appeals tribunal. We sit weekly, decide on the papers, and our dispositions bind the agency below. Our judges vote issue-by-issue on multi-element tests—coverage, breach, remedy—and I have begun to suspect that the panel can hold a majority on every element and yet dispose of a case in a way no majority of judges actually endorses, with a written rationale no single judge accepts. I am told the judgment-aggregation literature calls this a discursive dilemma. I want something I can actually use: write a reusable aggregation procedure for a three-member panel, and indicate where it generalizes, that the panel chair can sign off on and that a judge with no background in social choice theory can follow in an afternoon. For each step, specify what we vote on, in what order, what gets recorded, and the conditions under which the panel should stop voting and deliberate instead. Tell me which steps are forced by the need to avoid collectively inconsistent rulings and which are merely defensible court conventions, and mark the points where no procedure can eliminate judgment. Then run the procedure on two appeals: (1) a benefits case requiring the claimant to succeed on each of three elements, where each element is carried by a different 2–1 majority; and (2) a case in which the panel first decides whether a statute applies and then whether applying it is constitutional, where each question is answered yes by a different two-judge majority and no judge answers yes to both. Say what disposition your procedure produces in each case, and whether that is a disposition we should be comfortable issuing. Lead with a short statement of the recommended procedure, and keep the whole answer under 900 words.
