I'm working on a piece about why mathematicians pick the definitions they pick, and I've been toying with four criteria for calling a definition the right one: it should keep the central examples, kick out the pathologies, make the theorems come out clean, and generalize without feeling forced. Take the open-set definition of a topological space: arbitrary unions, finite intersections, and the empty set plus the whole space both count. I get the pitch — it abstracts what "nearby" means so you can do continuity without a metric. Fine.

But then I started poking at the Zariski topology on the affine plane, the one algebraic geometers use. The closed sets are zero sets of polynomials, so the open sets are these enormous complements; any two nonempty opens overlap, the space isn't Hausdorff, and you can't use the opens to seperate points or measure closeness in any intuitive sense. Yet the same three axioms do real work there — they're a big part of what lets you glue algebra and geometry together into schemes. So here's my question. Is the Zariski topology evidence that the open-set definition is the right one, because sticking with the axioms led somewhere enormously productive? Or is it evidence that the definition has drifted away from the idea of nearness it was supposed to capture? And does this case show that my four criteria are too naive — that "the right definition" just ends up meaning "the one that turned out to be generative"?
