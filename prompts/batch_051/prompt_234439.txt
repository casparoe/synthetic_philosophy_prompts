I've been arguing for months with my cousin, who is a huge Sherlock Holmes person, about whether 'Sherlock Holmes' refers to anything and whether 'Holmes smokes a pipe' is true in a way that 'Holmes is married' just isn't. She says Holmes is more real to her than most historical figures, which I think is a lovely thing to say and probably false. The arguing eventually produced a homemade view I'd like someone to actually test. I call it the vacant office account. The idea is that fictional names are like job titles for offices that happen to have no occupant: 'Sherlock Holmes' doesn't point at a person, but it does successfully pick out a role, and the stories are like a personnel file that fills in that role's traits over time. Truth in fiction, on this view, is a sentence's being entailed by the role as specified in the file plus the normal background a reader would assume. So 'Holmes is a detective' is true-in-the-stories, 'Holmes is married' is false or at least not true, and 'Holmes exists' is true only inside the pretense that the office is occupied. Contradictions in the file — like Watson's old war wound that moves around — I treat as conflicting memos where the more detailed or more central entry wins, and if there's no winner I say that particular claim is just indeterminate in the fiction.

I know this sounds like I've reinvented something half-remembered, and there suppose to be two different problems it half-solves, one about empty names and one about truth in fiction, but I genuinely haven't read the literature. Please take it charitably, rebuild it in its strongest form, and then be candid about were it breaks. You're a philosopher of language who knows the logic and truth-in-fiction debates; think carefully, brainstorm first if it helps, then give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion. In the five, cover: whether the view is coherent and distinct, the closest existing view(s), the biggest thing it gets right, the first place it really fails, and whether I should keep the phrase 'vacant office' or drop it.
