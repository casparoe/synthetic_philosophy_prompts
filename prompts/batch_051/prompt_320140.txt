I've been reconstructing an argument that came out of a discussion about whether an individual who buys factory-farmed meat does anything wrong, and the reconstructed version now seems to me to prove far too much. I'll set it out with numbered steps. (1) An act is morally wrong only if it makes some difference to an outcome that matters. (2) In a sufficiently large collective practice, no single contribution makes any perceptible difference to the relevant outcome: one fewer hamburger purchase will not lead to fewer animals being raised, one more spoiled ballot will not change an election, and one fewer flight or mile driven will not change anyone's climate-related harms in any way anyone could notice. (3) Therefore no single contribution to such a practice makes a difference that matters, and by (1) no individual contribution is wrong. (4) If no individual act within a practice is wrong, then the practice as a whole cannot be wrong, since a collective practice just is the sum of individual actions, and a sum of actions none of which is wrong cannot itself be a wrong. (5) Therefore no large collective practice is ever wrong: not the slow ruin of a common pasture through thousands of individually trivial uses, not a market that degrades a river through many individually imperceptible discharges, not an election lost through the abstention of many individually non-decisive voters. (6) But that conclusion is absurd; at least some such practices are wrong, and the people who take part in them seem to stand in some critical relation to that wrong even when no one of them alone made a perceptible difference.

Where does this argument go wrong? I want to know which step you reject, why that step seemed secure, and what giving it up costs, for instance for appeals to expected harm, threshold effects, group responsibility, or the demand that wrongness bottom out in individual acts. I would most like to keep the observation in (2) that individual actions are dwarfed by the system, and also the conviction in (6) that some collective practices are wrong; beyond that I am prepared to pay whichever price is smallest, but I want to know exactly what it is. Please also tell me what would change your mind about your diagnosis: which consideration or argument would lead you to reject a different step instead, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I'd be grateful if you would write your answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter, and in American English.
