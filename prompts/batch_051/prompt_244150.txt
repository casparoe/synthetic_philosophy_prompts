I'm in a metaethics seminar and we've spent two weeks on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I still couldn't state the distinction cleanly if you asked me to. So here's what I want: a working definition of a right-kind reason for an attitude. By "attitude" I mean the usual stuff—admiration, fear, hope, resentment, that kind of thing.

The demon case is the standard setup: a demon tells you he'll torture you unless you admire him. That's a reason to admire him, but it's the wrong kind—it doesn't make admiring him fitting, it just makes it prudent. And there are clearly right-kind reasons too: your friend is kind, brave, and funny, so there's a reason to admire her that's just about her. The hard part is saying what the difference is without accidentially sorting something onto the wrong side of the line.

So: propose a definition, then run it through each of the following and tell me what it says. I'm confident about the first two and genuinely unsure about the rest:

1. The demon's threat (wrong kind, I'd say).
2. Your friend is genuinely admirable (right kind).
3. Someone offers you $1,000 to admire them.
4. The person is genuinely admirable, and you'd also inherit money if you admired them.
5. You promised a dying friend that you'd admire their spouse.
6. A pill would make you admire someone, and admiring them would make your life go better.
7. Someone is powerful enough to make your life difficult if you don't admire them, but they never threatened you and don't even know you exist.

For each, say whether your definition counts it as right-kind, wrong-kind, or neither, and whether that matches your pre-theoretic verdict. Where your definition gives a weird answer, either revise it or defend the weird answer.

And please commit to one definition and argue for it rather than laying out three options. Also say whether the definition generalizes to fear and hope, or whether it's really just a definition of reasons to admire.
