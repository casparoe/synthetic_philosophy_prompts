I keep getting tangeld up trying to say what it is to have a reason to admire something, as opposed to just having an incentive to feel that way. I'd like a working definition of "a reason to admire X" (or, if you'd rather, generalize to reasons for attitudes like fear and respect) that gets these cases right:

1. A friend stayed up all night helping a stranger for no benefit to himself. That's clearly a reason to admire him.
2. A demon credibly threatens to torture you unless you admire him for being cruel. There's some sense in which you now have a reason to admire him, but it feels like the wrong kind of reason – it doesn't make him admirable.
3. You can take a pill that will make you admire your awful boss. You have a reason to take the pill, but does the pill give you a reason to admire the boss?
4. You learn a snake in front of you is venomous. That's a reason to fear it, and it seems to make the fear correct.
5. Someone offers you $100 to be afraid of a perfectly harmless spider. Is that a reason to fear it, and is it the same kind as in case 4?
6. Your colleagues all respect a judge you know to be corrupt, and you'll be ostracized if you don't go along. Is that a reason to respect him?

My hunch is that the phrase "a reason to admire/fear/respect" does two jobs at once: sometimes it points at a feature of the thing that makes the attitude fitting (the kindness, the venom), and other times it points at a benefit you'd get from having the attitude (avoiding torture, the $100, fitting in). If that's right, give me both definitions, run each case through each, and say where the boundary between the two gets blurry. If it's not right, propose a single definition that handles all six and tell me where my attempted distinction collapses. Either way, tell me how confident you are in the verdicts, and single out the one case or definitional move you're least sure about.
