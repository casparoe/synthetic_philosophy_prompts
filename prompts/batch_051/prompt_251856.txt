I am trying to reverse-engineer the principle a colleague uses to classify finite binary strings as "random" or "not random." She says she applies one fixed principle but will not state it. She has recorded verdicts on four cases. In each case the string was set down before she saw it, and she was truthfully told the origin.

1. 01010101010101010101, produced by the rule "write 01 ten times." Her verdict: not random.
2. 10110001101000101110, produced by a certified hardware random-number generator as a single unedited draw. Her verdict: random.
3. 01101010000010011110, the first 20 bits after the binary point in the expansion of the square root of two, produced by the digit-extraction rule; the string happens to pass the usual frequency and runs tests at this scale. Her verdict: not random.
4. 00000000000000000000, produced by the rule "write 0 twenty times." Her verdict: not random.

A fifth case is now on the table. The string is again 00000000000000000000, but this time it was produced by a certified hardware random-number generator as a single unedited draw; the documentation certifies that the process was genuinely chancy, that the output was recorded before anyone looked at it, and that it was not selected or postprocessed. Treat the chanciness of the process as settled; do not save the analysis by doubting the device.

Two candidate criteria are live in the reading group. On the short-description view, a finite string is random just in case it has no description appreciably shorter than itself, and its origin is irrelevant. On the origin view, a finite string is random just in case it was produced by a genuinely chancy process, and its surface pattern is irrelevant. I want to know which of these, or which further rule, best fits her four recorded verdicts; how each viable rule would classify the fifth string; which recorded case is the closest analog; whether her record forces one verdict on the fifth string or merely makes one verdict more plausible; and what one additional case would most cleanly settle the matter.

Please separate what is technically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the foundations of probability, and do not present a contested interpretation of randomness as the consensus. At the very end, put a single machine-readable line of the form

\answer{predicted verdict; criterion}

giving your predicted verdict for the fifth string and the criterion you think best explains her record.
