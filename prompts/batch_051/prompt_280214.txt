I'm in a class on sincerity and the ethics of self-presentation, and I've been going back and forth on whether you can be faulted for not telling someone how you actually feel even when you never outright lie. Here's my arguement that you can:

(1) If the person would stop texting you, or would at least change how they treat you, if they knew how you actually felt, then by staying quiet you're letting them go on under a false assumption about where they stand.
(2) Letting someone go on under a false assumption about your feelings, when you could easily correct it, is a kind of deception—not as direct as lying, but the same sort of wrong at root.
(3) Lying to someone is wrong (at least absent special justification) because it manipulates their picture of the world.
(4) So staying quiet in that situation is wrong for the same reason lying is.

I keep wanting to say the conclusion must be right, but something about step 1 or 2 feels like it's doing too much work and I can't tell which. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and what's the cleanest way to state the distinction it's papering over—and would fixing that step still get me the conclusion, or not?
