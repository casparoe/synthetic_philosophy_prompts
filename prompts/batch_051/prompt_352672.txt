I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language, with about sixteen students and one seventy-five-minute session to spend on speech acts. Please design a structured in-class activity that gets the students to feel, before they are handed any theoretical vocabulary, that saying the right words in the right situation can be a way of doing something rather than merely describing something, and that when such utterances go wrong they go wrong in at least two sharply different ways: sometimes the act never comes into existence, and sometimes the act comes into existence but is hollow or defective. The activity should include concrete rules, roles, materials, a timeline, and, if it is a game, a scoring or stakes structure that does not require real money, so that students actually attempt, accept or reject, and diagnose utterances such as promises, bets, apologies, invitations, warnings, or declarations of institutional status, and they should not hear the terms for these distinctions until afterward. After the activity, give me a debrief that maps particular moments in what happened onto the distinction between what an utterance says, what the speaker does in saying it, and what the speaker brings about by saying it, and onto the difference between attempts that fail to bring the act into existence and attempts that succeed but are insincere, unauthorized, or otherwise defective. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the activity, and how I should handle each likely failure. Be frank about which features of the theory the activity cannot reproduce and which points I will have to supply myself. Throughout the design and debrief, separate what is settled about performative utterances from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of language, and do not present a contested view, such as the thesis that every speech act requires its addressee's uptake, as if it were the established consensus. Write the entire response in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, as one would write an email or a letter; fold any timeline, list of rules, or set of materials into the prose rather than setting it off.
