I keep running into the same problem in discussions about rationalism and empiricism: the phrase "innate knowledge" is made to do several different jobs, and the debate often stalls once people notice they disagree about what would even count. On one side, the thesis is taken to mean explicit, articulable propositions present in the mind at birth—so strong that almost nobody defends it. On the other, it is taken to mean anything a system can do without explicit instruction—so weak that almost nobody denies it. In between, I think, there is an interesting claim about knowledge whose justification does not come from experience even when experience is needed to bring it to expression. I want a working definition of innate knowledge that avoids both extremes: an empiricist should be able to accept it as a faithful description of the target while still denying that anything satisfies it, and a rationalist should be able to accept it without being committed to the view that infants are born believing propositions.

Here are the cases the definition should run through, and for each I want a verdict: (a) a newborn’s ability to distinguish more from fewer in a small array, two dots versus three, before any training; (b) an infant’s expectation, in the first few months, that an object continues to exist when it passes behind a screen; (c) a newly hatched chick’s tendency to orient toward a face-like configuration rather than a scrambled one, without having seen faces; (d) a child’s belief that no surface can be uniformly red and uniformly green all over at the same time, which the child was never explicitly taught; (e) a competent adult’s knowledge that modus ponens is valid—if P implies Q and P is the case, then Q must be the case; (f) a competent adult’s knowledge that 7 + 5 = 12, acquired through years of schooling; (g) the bias of a reinforcement-learning agent toward simpler hypotheses, which its designers built into the architecture rather than its training data. Where the definition gives a verdict that surprises you, say whether you are revising the definition or defending the verdict.

I have no settled theory, but my hunches are that (a) through (c) are the most plausible candidates, that (g) should probably not count because no answer to the rationalism–empiricism debate ought to be settled by what engineers happened to put into a machine, and that (d) and (e) are where a good definition earns its keep; I would be glad to be shown wrong on any of those. In particular, I want the definition to separate three things: the content a mind starts with, the mechanisms that shape learning, and propositions whose justification is partly independent of experience. If your definition counts something as knowledge that the knower could not articulate, say so and defend it rather than quietly assuming knowledge must be explicit.
