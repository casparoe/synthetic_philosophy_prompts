I've been in a working group this year that was supposed to be about the future of work, and we've spent half our meetings in a version of the work-hours fight that's been running for a century without anyone changing their mind. One camp thinks we basically got the work ethic right: for them, Max Weber's story about the Protestant work ethic is a deep cultural inheritance, and they treat the French 35-hour week as a failed experiment—nice for some people's quality of life, but not a serious economic model, because prosperity comes from discipline, delayed gratification, and long horizons. The other camp points to karoshi in Japan, burnout data, and decades of wage stagnation, and says the real failure is treating more work as an end in itself; they want a politics of time rather than more income. What's strange is that both sides seem to know the same facts. They'll both site GDP per hour and hospital admissions for overwork, both agree karoshi is awful, both admit the 35-hour week didn't create the jobs it was sold as creating—and then they draw opposite conclusions about whether the problem is too little discipline or too much work.

My working theory is that this isn't an empirical disagreement that more data will settle. It feels more like a disagreement about what work and leisure are even for—a disagreement about the shape of a good life—with the economic language (incentives, productivity, growth) doing a lot of work it maybe can't carry. But I can also imagine it's really a confused knot of three separate debates: a historical one about what Weber's thesis actually claims, a policy one about what a 35-hour law does, and a moral one about whether a culture that valorizes overwork is defective. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a verdict on who's right. Use what the two sides actually say—don't just survey the positions—and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, an empirical test, a comparison across the French and Japanese cases, or an argument that the question was just badly posed.
