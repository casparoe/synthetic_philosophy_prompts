I'm a PhD student in philosophy of law, and our reading group got stuck on an argument that seems to show textualism and purposivism are the same theory once you look closely. None of us buys the conclusion, but we cant agree on which premise to drop. The argument goes:

(1) In hard statutory cases, whether a term applies to a new fact pattern depends on which similarities between the new case and settled cases count as relevant. (2) The only non-arbitrary way to fix which similarities are relevant is to say what the provision is for—point or purpose. (3) But an 'easy' or plain-meaning case is just one where we judge that the new facts are relevantly similar to settled applications of the term; nothing else could make it plain. (4) So plain cases rest on purpose exactly as hard cases do; they are just hard cases where the purpose passes unnoticed because nobody contests it. (5) Therefore the textualist's plain-meaning baseline is itself a covert purposive judgment, and textualism collapses into purposivism—or is just purposivism with fewer avowals.

Which premise do we give up, why did it look fine, and what does each side have to pay? Reply with exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion: the step you'd reject, why it tempts, what rejecting it costs a committed textualist, what rejecting (or accepting) it costs a committed purposivist, and what's left of the textualism/purposivism distinction afterwards.
