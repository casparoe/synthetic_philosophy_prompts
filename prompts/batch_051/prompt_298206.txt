I am a novelist by trade, and every year I also run a small literary festival out of a converted warehouse. This year a modest surplus came to us through a translation prize, and I used the occasion to invite a writer I have long admired, a novelist who uses a wheelchair. She accepted. The building, however, is not set up for her: the stage sits up four steps, the only restroom near the reading room is down a narrow corridor, and the entrance has a raised threshold that none of our ramps will sit flush against. Making the weekend genuinely usable for her would mean building a temporary ramp, moving the readings to the flat floor, and renting an accessible restroom trailer. That would consume almost the entire surplus. The same money could instead go to cooling the hall, adding chairs, and providing free water and coffee for the several hundred people we expect over the three days.

Several committee members have told me it would be irrational to spend everything on one visitor while hundreds go without ordinary comforts, and they add that nobody compelled her to accept an invitation to a space like this. I keep feeling that the two alternatives are not on the same scale at all—that this one visitor's claim to be present is different in kind from the summed comfort of a crowd—but when I try to say why, I sound sentimental or merely bad at arithmetic. One member has pressed the point further: a single attendee who sits through three days in an unventilated hall, on a folding chair, waiting for one toilet, will have accumulated a fairly serious burden by Sunday evening, so perhaps we are not comparing one person against many after all.

I would like your advice on what the committee ought to do, and on whether there is a principled reason the numbers should not settle it—or whether I should concede that they do. Please also tell me how confident you are in your answer and which point you are least sure of, because I would rather not enter that meeting more certain than the argument can bear.
