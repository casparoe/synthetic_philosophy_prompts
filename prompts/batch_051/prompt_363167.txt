I'm a PhD student working on the ethics of space exploration, and there's an assymetry in my own judgments, and in a lot of the people I talk to, that I can't decide whether to keep or give up.

On Earth, microbial life is treated as basically disposable. People take antibiotics that wipe out enormous numbers of bacteria without a second thought, bleach kitchen counters, sterilise equipment; mining and construction destroy microbe habitats all the time, and almost nobody treats that as morally serious except in aggregate ecological terms, and usually only when the damage climbs up the food chain to something larger. But move the microbes to Mars and everyone's intuitions flip. People who are perfectly relaxed about killing Earth bacteria think it would be a serious wrong to contaminate Mars with Earth life, or to kill off any native Martian microbes. Space agencies spend serious money keeping spacecraft clean precisely so we don't introduce Earth life.

It gets stranger when you control for scale. A small, unremarkable population of bacteria in a cave on Earth would attract almost no moral concern if it were destroyed for a quarry. Put the same cave on Mars, and many of the same people would call its destruction a tragedy or worse. And if we found out tomorrow that Mars is completely sterile, I suspect much of the strongest opposition to terraforming would soften pretty quickly, which makes me think the judgment isn't really tracking the moral status of microbes but something else: rarity, a second origin of life, the value of an untouched world, or the epistemic value of studying it.

I can see a few candidate principles that might justify the asymmetry. Independent origins of life are scientifically precious in a way ordinary Earth life isn't. Contamination or terraforming is irreversible in a way most Earth-side destruction isn't. We might destroy something we don't yet understand or even recognise as life. Or maybe we should care about whole lineages and biospheres rather than individual organisms. I'm not convinced by any of them yet, and part of me thinks the asymmetry is mostly novelty and the romance of space.

So what I'm after is this: is there a defensible principle that actually generates both judgments, Earth microbes disposable and Martian microbes specially protected? Or is it an inconsistency that should be corrected, either by caring much more about Earth microbes or by relaxing our attitude to Mars? Give me the strongest version of the justification you can build, test it against the cave case and the case where Mars turns out sterile, and tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
