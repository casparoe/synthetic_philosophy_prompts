People keep using the spectrum argument as if it's decisive against the transitivity of better-than: you build a chain of outcomes where each adjacent step looks like a clear improvement — trade one more millisecond of mild headache for a large gain in pleasure or whatever — but by the end the last outcome is obviously worse than the first, so 'better than' has to be intransitive. But that argument was designed for an idealized picture where each pairwise comparison is a clean verdict and the only thing doing real work is the evaluative relation itself.

Now suppose we take seriously something that seems true of how comparative judgment actually behaves: pairwise comparisons between close options are noisy and context-sensitive. People reverse their own judgments at rates that can exceed the tiny differences the spectrum steps trade on, judgments drift with framing and order effects, and small errors accumulate over long chains. Does the spectrum argument survive that? Which premise is actually under pressure — the reliability of the adjacent comparisons, the assumption that 'better than' is a single relation rather than 'at least as good as' plus a separate parity or tie relation, or something else? Is there a repaired version that still says something interesting, or does the whole thing collapse into sorites-style vagueness with a transitive relation quietly absorbing the apparent cycles?
