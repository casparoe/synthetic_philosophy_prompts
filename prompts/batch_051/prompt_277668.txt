As part of a paper on whether parenthood and guardianship could extend to artificial agents, I have been trying to separate two accounts of what makes one artificial agent the guardian of another, and I want a case built to order that shows them coming apart. Let me call them the stewardship account and the architectural account.

On the stewardship account, an agent A is the guardian of an artificial agent Y just in case A stands to Y in an ongoing, responsive, and answerable relation of supervision: A monitors Y's performance, corrects Y's errors, selects or adjusts the inputs on which Y continues to learn, and could properly be held answerable for the dispositions Y ends up with. On this account guardianship is a continuing role; the guardian is the one who shapes the trajectory rather than the one who laid down its initial conditions.

On the architectural account, by contrast, A is Y's guardian just in case A specified the architecture and the objective function, the standing constraints within which all of Y's later learning must proceed. On this account guardianship is fixed at origin: whoever set the ends and the structural bounds is the guardian, while later trainers, however influential, are at most teachers or drill instructors.

I want a single scenario involving three artificial agents, A, B, and Y, engineered so that the two accounts give opposite verdicts. The stewardship account should classify A, and not B, as Y's guardian; the architectural account should classify B, and not A, as Y's guardian. The scenario must generate these verdicts rather than stipulate them: the features each account treats as decisive must actually be present in what A and B do.

The constraints are these. A and B must themselves be artificial agents, not humans; this is AIs raising AIs, not humans owning AIs. No appeal to consciousness, emotion, affection, or any other mental attribution we are not warranted in making may do any work. The verdicts may not be decided by legal ownership or corporate policy; everything should rest on a philosophically intelligible division of labor within a realistic training-and-deployment arrangement. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph, and keep it within something like current or near-current machine learning practice.

After presenting the scenario, run the check. State exactly which features of A's role satisfy the stewardship account and why they fail to satisfy the architectural account. Then state which features of B's role satisfy the architectural account and why they fail to satisfy the stewardship account. Give an honest diagnosis of which single feature of the scenario is doing the work, and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed. Finally, say how a defender of each account would attack the case; each will try to redescribe the other account's decisive feature as peripheral to upbringing. I want to know whether the case survives those redescriptions.

The case is for a paper on whether whatever obligations go with guardianship among artificial agents, if there are such obligations, would attach to the agent that originates another agent or to the agent that does the ongoing work of raising it. A case that simply announces the divergent verdicts would be cheating; I need the scenario itself to make both verdicts natural.
