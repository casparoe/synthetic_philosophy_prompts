I was listening to a podcast where the host summarized Allais's paradox and the independence axiom like this:

"Allais found that if you offer people a million dollars for sure, versus a lottery that gives an 89% chance of a million, a 10% chance of five million, and a 1% chance of nothing, they take the sure thing. But if you shrink all those probabilities down by a factor of ten or so while keeping the payoffs, people suddenly prefer the lottery with the shot at five million. The two versions are really the same choice, except the first one has a common 89% chance of a million tacked on. Since the independence axiom says that adding the same option to both sides of a choice shouldn't flip your preference, this looks like a straight violation. What it shows is that people evaluate gambles differently depending on how they are framed, which makes expected utility a bad model of actual choice and also a bad account of what it is to be rational. The fix is easy: you just weight probabilities nonlinearly, like in prospect theory, and the choices come out consistent."
