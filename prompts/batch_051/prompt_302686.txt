Malthus's principle of population, as it is usually defended today, holds that population tends to grow faster than the means of subsistence and must therefore be held in check by misery, vice, or deliberate restraint. Two later developments are standardly said to have broken the argument. The first is agricultural: the Haber–Bosch process and the Green Revolution allowed food output to rise faster than population for the better part of a century, so the binding constraint was never a fixed stock of land. The second is demographic: as societies industrialise and become wealthier, fertility falls voluntarily and often below replacement, apparently through changed incentives and social norms rather than through famine and disease. Does the Malthusian argument survive these two developments? Identify which premise each one bears on, and say whether the principle can be repaired into a weaker but still meaningful claim, or whether the repairs available amount to redefining checks and postponing the reckoning so that the theory can no longer be falsified. In particular: if the appeal is now to a finite planet and eventual limits to growth, specify what evidence could count against the repaired version, and whether an indefinite series of right-but-early rescues would leave the theory with any explanatory content.
