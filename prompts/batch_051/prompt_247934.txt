A friend who does physics-for-philosophers showed me an argument and now I can't shake it. He says special relativity by itself forces the block-universe view of time — that the future is already as real as the present — and the argument seems way too clean. I'm trying to figure out which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

Setup: I'm on Earth, sitting still, right now. Far away in Andromeda the leadership is deciding whether to launch a fleet toward us. By Earth clocks that decision hasn't happened yet; it's days in our future. Now here's the argument:

(1) In special relativity, which distant event counts as happening 'now' depends on your state of motion. If I'm walking toward Andromeda at this moment, my 'now' on Andromeda runs days ahead of Earth-frame now; if I'm walking away, mine runs days behind. Two people passing each other on the sidewalk can disagree by days about what's happening on Andromeda right now, and neither of them is wrong.

(2) If an event is simultaneous with you-now in some legitimate inertial frame, then that event is real now — not merely predicted or potential, but as actual as your own present experience.

(3) So take the pedestrian walking past me toward Andromeda. In his frame, the fleet launch — an event Earth's frame puts in the future — is simultaneous with him-now. By (2), that future launch is real now.

(4) All inertial frames are equally valid. Walking toward Andromeda isn't an epistemic defect or an illusion; it's just a different but equally correct slicing of spacetime into nows.

(5) So if the launch is real in any legitimate frame's present, then it's real, full stop. Reality can't be the kind of thing that flips on and off depending on whether you happen to be walking toward or away from something.

(6) Therefore the future launch is already as real and fixed as the chair I'm sitting on, even though by my clock it hasn't happened yet.

(7) Apply this reasoning everywhere and to every event: past, present, and future are all equally real, spacetime is a block, and the open future is a perspectival illusion.

I gather this is the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. It seems to prove too much — one minute of relativity-interpretation buys you a massive metaphysical conclusion. I especially don't trust (2): the slide from 'simultaneous in a frame' to 'real now' feels like it's doing all the work. But my friend says rejecting it forces you to say either that simultaneity is unreal or that reality itself is frame-relative, and he thinks both are worse than just accepting the block. I'd rather keep the physics untouched and still say the future is open, but I don't know wich bullet I'm actually biting. Where does this go wrong, if it does? And if I have to give something up, which step should it be and what does rejecting it cost?
