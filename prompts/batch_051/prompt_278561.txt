I have been working my way through the General Scholium to Isaac Newton’s Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica with a small reading group, in Andrew Motte’s 1729 translation, and I have found myself stuck on a paragraph near the discussion of God’s dominion. Like many people, I was trained to think of Newton mainly as the author of the laws of motion and as a cautious inductivist about natural philosophy, so it is still a little disorienting to watch him move, within a few pages, from the mechanics of planets and comets to an extended argument about the grammar of the word “God.” The passage that most puzzles me is this:

“For God is a relative word, and has a respect to servants; and Deity is the dominion of God, not over his own body, as those imagine who fancy God to be the soul of the world, but over servants. The supreme God is a Being eternal, infinite, absolutely perfect; but a being, however perfect, without dominion, cannot be said to be Lord God; for we say, my God, your God, the God of Israel, the God of Gods, and Lord of Lords; but we do not say, my Eternal, your Eternal, the Eternal of Israel, the Eternal of Gods; we do not say, my Infinite, or my Perfect: These are titles which have no respect to servants.”

As a professional philosopher and historian of early modern philosophy, could you help me think this through carefully? I take Newton to be arguing, among other things, that “God” is fundamentally a relative term because it implies servants, and that a being could be eternal, infinite, and absolutely perfect without thereby being “Lord God.” I want to understand exactly what Newton is trying to establish here: is he making a semantic point about how the word is used, a metaphysical point about what property makes a being divine, or a polemical point against those who imagine God as the soul of the world? And does his argument from what we do and do not say—we say “my God” but not “my Eternal”—actually support the conclusion he wants? Please begin by laying out the most plausible alternative readings rather than jumping to one, then give me your best interpretation and an honest assessment of whether the argument works. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which textual or philosophical consideration would most likely make you abandon your interpretation, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
