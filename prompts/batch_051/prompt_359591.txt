Question: State what each of the three main interpretations of probability—frequentism, subjectivism, and the propensity view—takes a probability statement to be about. For each, give one standard objection and explain whether it is best understood as a rival to the other interpretations or as answering a different question. Be precise about the commitments of each view.

Student answer:

Frequentism holds that a probability is the limit of the relative frequency of an outcome in an indefinitely long sequence of trials. To say that the probability of heads on this coin is one-half is to say that, in an endless sequence of tosses, the proportion of heads among the first n tosses converges to one-half as n increases. One standard objection is the reference class problem: a single event belongs to many reference classes, and these classes may yield different limiting frequencies, so the probability assigned to the event depends on an arbitrary selection of a reference class. A related limitation is that the view says nothing about single cases or about a fair coin that is never tossed.

Subjectivism takes probability to be a degree of belief. The statement that the probability of heads is one-half reports how strongly a particular agent believes that the next toss will land heads, rather than reporting a fact about the coin. De Finetti and others showed that the only rational constraint on degrees of belief is coherence—immunity to a Dutch book—and that coherence forces the degrees of belief to obey the probability axioms. It follows that any coherent assignment of probabilities is rationally permissible, and two agents whose assignments disagree are not in any doxastic error. This is usually counted a virtue of the view, since it requires no objective or logical prior probabilities.

The propensity interpretation says that probability is a physical disposition or tendency of the chance set-up to produce a given outcome on a single trial—for example, the coin's bias. Unlike frequentism, it therefore applies directly to single cases. The standard objection is that propensities are empirically idle: because we observe only finite relative frequencies, and a propensity is not itself a frequency, the propensity can never be measured or even indirectly tested; all the empirical content is supplied by the finite frequencies alone.

On the question of rivalry: subjectivism is an account of rational belief, so it answers a different question from the objective interpretations and is not really in competition with them. Frequentism and the propensity view are both objective, but they compete over whether single-case probabilities are legitimate and over whether probability is essentially tied to an actual or hypothetical sequence.
