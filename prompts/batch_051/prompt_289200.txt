I've been working through Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations for a seminar, and I wrote out the argument that convinced me mathematics is quasi-empirical, but now I can't tell whether it's actually valid or whether I've smuggled something in. It goes like this:

(1) A proof doesn't just show that a theorem is true; it shows that the theorem is true for the objects that fall under the definitions and hidden lemmas the proof actually uses.
(2) When mathematicians hit a counterexample, the counterexample doesn't automatically refute the theorem — they can instead revise the definition (bar the monster), restrict the theorem's range, or expose a hidden lemma the proof was tacitly assuming.
(3) So what gets revised under pressure is not only the theorem but the conceptual machinery around it: the definitions, the range of the theorem, the buried lemmas.
(4) But this is exactly how empirical theories behave: what's 'empirical' in Lakatos's quasi-empirical mathematics is that counterexamples play the role of experiments, forcing the conceptual machinery to be rebuilt.
(5) And a discipline whose concepts get revised in the face of counterexamples is quasi-empirical.
(6) Therefore mathematics, at least as it's actually practiced, is quasi-empirical.

I half-suspect the weak spot is either (2) or (4). (2) sounds like it makes every theorem unfalsifiable, and (4) might be stretching what 'empirical' means until its just a metaphor. But maybe I'm wrong and the argument is fine. Is the argument valid as written? If not, exactly which step fails, under which reading, and what's the strongest nearby version of the argument that survives? I'd like a committed answer, not a list of options — if a Platonist or a formalist can dig in at step (4), say which reading of 'mathematics' they'd need and whether the practice of mathematics is still quasi-empirical on that reading.
