A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether introspection is a trustworthy source of knowledge. He defended what he called the final-authority view: a person's sincere report of a current state of their own mind — what they are feeling, wanting, believing, or thinking — should be accepted as correct unless there is overwhelming external evidence against it. I found myself agreeing about some cases, resisting others, and noticing that my reactions were less consistent than I would have liked. The argument left me wanting to get clear about what I actually think.

So I wrote down my raw verdicts about particular cases, followed by the principle my friend and I kept circling around. (1) If I report that I currently have a throbbing pain in my left shoulder, I treat that report as about as reliable as my report of the color of the wall in front of me. (2) If I say that the mental image I am now forming of my childhood home is vivid and that I can make out the red front door, I trust the report. (3) If I say I chose a job candidate because she was the best qualified, but my own notes later show that I rated nearly every early interviewee more harshly regardless of credentials, I conclude that the introspected reason was invented after the fact. (4) If I insist that I am not feeling any resentment toward my sister for missing my graduation — that what I feel is only disappointment — yet I keep making small digs about the missed ceremony, I do not trust that report. (5) If I am asked, moments after solving a puzzle, in what order three key ideas came to me, I may answer confidently, but I think my confidence is largely worthless. (6) If I report that I am currently deliberating about whether to have dinner before or after the gym, I trust the report completely. One further case is still unsettled for me: (7) after changing my mind in an argument, I report that my view reversed at the moment my friend produced a particular statistical table and that I was still unconvinced just before it; the reversal feels vividly locatable in time, but I know people often construct such turning points after the fact.

The principle we kept circling around was this: introspection is reliable for what is currently present to consciousness, and unreliable for causes, processes, and standing dispositions. I suspect it is too blunt. Case (4) concerns the current absence of a feeling, not a present content in any straightforward sense, and I distrust the report all the same. Case (5) concerns the order in which recently present conscious events occurred; one could call that a process, but doing so feels like stretching the label to keep the principle intact. I want to know whether there is one coherent principle — or a small set of competing candidates — that best captures my verdicts. Please state each candidate precisely, connect each clause to the cases it handles, tell me how the candidate rules on my unsettled case (7), and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, a given candidate cannot accommodate and would force me to revise. Please write your answer in American English.
