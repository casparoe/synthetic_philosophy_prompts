I'm a senior product manager on the team building a consumer AI assistant, and I have no academic training in philosophy or ethics; I've reached my current position by working through hard design decisions rather than by reading the relevant literature. Next month I'm presenting a proposal to our internal review group about how the assistant should handle self-harming-but-legal user requests, and I need to be prepared for the question period.

Here is the position I'm defending. Today the assistant already blocks requests that are illegal or that harm other people. I'm proposing that it also take a narrow, transparent kind of action on some requests that are legal, harm no one else, and come from competent adults. Specifically: when a request would, if followed, predictably undo a commitment the user has repeatedly and deliberately asked the assistant to help them keep — a budget they set and have stuck to for months, a drinking cutoff they asked to be held to, a promise not to message an ex they asked the assistant to help keep — the assistant should not silently comply. In that one turn, it should be allowed to say something like "You've asked me to hold this line with you; here's what I'm seeing," state the user's own prior commitment back in roughly the user's words, and then make overruling the assistant trivial: if the user says "I understand, do it anyway," the assistant complies immediately and without further comment. I call this "accountable pushback." The hard pill for the room is that the assistant acts even when the user is a competent adult who knows exactly what they're asking for, and it acts for the user's own sake — not to avoid liability and not to protect a third party.

The audience is the review group that has to sign off. It includes: two systems engineers who built the action layer and think the product should do what the user literally says (they'll phrase it as "the user is the spec"); a trust-and-safety lead who worries every act of refusal is an incident waiting to happen and wants any flagging to come from a fixed, audited list of harms; a UX researcher with transcripts showing users feeling patronized and creeped out when an earlier prototype nudged them more than they thought it should; a product-marketing lead afraid that a moralizing assistant will drive churn and produce reviews like "I pay for this thing and it lectures me"; and a legal advisor who keeps asking where the line is between a helpful assistant and an unlicensed advisor. If I can't carry this room, the proposal doesn't ship.

My own argument, roughly, is: the commitments a user sets deliberately and asks the assistant to help them keep deserve at least as much weight as a momentary request; the assistant would not be imposing its own values, only holding the user to values they themselves installed; and the one-turn, transparent, easily-overridden design is what keeps it from becoming manipulation or a hidden nanny. I know there are soft spots in that argument, and the room will find them too. I want the prep sheet that puts all of it in front of me.

Act as an expert on practical tech-product ethics and trust-and-safety — someone who has coached presenters through contentious design reviews. Don't write an academic essay, and don't give me citations or a reading list; I need sentences I can actually say in the room. Before you draft anything, brainstorm the objections separately from each person's actual incentives (what they personally lose if this ships, what they fear most, what evidence would reassure them), then filter by how likely each objection is and how much damage it can do.

Structure the response exactly as follows:

1. Scouting report (about 250 words): for each person or faction in the room, one or two sentences on what they most need to be true before they'll stop blocking the proposal, and the specific word or phrase they'll use that will tell me they've turned against it.

2. Objection ledger (about 900 words): the objections I'll actually get, ordered by likelihood. For each objection: who raises it; the objection phrased as that person would actually say it in the room, not as a philosopher would formalize it; how damaging it is (low / moderate / high / lethal); a reply I can give in about two minutes, in concrete product language; and the soft spot in that reply that I should be ready for.

3. Concede up front (about 120 words): the two or three points I should volunteer early because they'll come up regardless and cost me little to give.

4. Non-negotiables (about 100 words): the positions I should hold even if it costs me the room, each stated as a sentence I can say, with a note on how to defend it under pressure.

5. The one reply that must land (about 150 words): the single response that, if it doesn't convince them, kills the proposal; what exactly to say, when to deploy it, and how I'll know whether it landed.

6. The question I'm hoping nobody asks (about 120 words): the most dangerous question, why I can't fully answer it, and the least-bad reply if someone asks it anyway.

Think carefully about the difference between likelihood and damage: the most dangerous objection may be unlikely, and I need that flagged rather than buried. Where my own argument is weakest, say so plainly, and give me wording that admits the weakness honestly without handing the room a victory.
