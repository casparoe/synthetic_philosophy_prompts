Exam question (30 points):
Define an information cascade and carefully distinguish it from (i) herding driven by payoff externalities and (ii) reputational herding. Then explain the sense in which an information cascade can be individually rational while also being collectively suboptimal, and the sense in which it is fragile.

Student’s answer:
An information cascade occurs in a sequential choice setting in which each agent moves one at a time, observes the actions but not the private information of those who moved before, and rationally tries to infer that private information from the observed actions. When enough early agents have chosen the same option, a later agent can rationally follow them even when her own private signal points the other way, because the public history already outweighs one additional private signal. Once a later agent follows regardless of her private signal, her action carries no new information about what she privately knows, so later observers learn nothing from it; from then on public belief no longer updates, and the cascade sustains itself. Each individual is acting rationally given what she can observe, yet the group may converge on an option that pooled private information would have shown to be wrong. Cascades must be distinguished from payoff-based herding, in which someone follows the crowd because the payoff from an action increases the more others take it, rather than because the crowd’s action reveals information. They must also be distinguished from reputational herding, in which someone suppresses her own assessment and follows the crowd in order to avoid looking incompetent or out of step. The fragility of a cascade is therefore much the same phenomenon as conformity bias: a small change in the observed behavior of a few salient people can make everyone revise their view and reverse the cascade, especially where the early majority had made the wrong choice.
