I am a high school student working through the philosophy of language in an independent study, and I have become attached to a view I have seen called inferentialism: the meaning of an expression is the role it plays in reasoning—the inferences it entitles us to make from it, and the inferences that entitle us to it—not what it names or represents. The view appeals to me, but my verdicts on particular cases do not sit comfortably together. I would like a principle that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely, and an honest statement of which verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would also like the principle applied to at least one case I have not listed.

My verdicts are these.

First, for the logical connectives, I cannot find any meaning over and above the rules of inference. A person who can infer "it is raining" from "it is raining and it is snowing," and "it is raining or it is snowing" from "it is raining," seems to know what "and" and "or" mean. Verdict: for such words, inferential role exhausts meaning.

Second, consider two terms with one extension. Suppose "renate" applies to animals with kidneys and "cordate" to animals with hearts, and suppose exactly the same animals satisfy both. The terms clearly differ in meaning, and the difference appears to consist in the different inferences they support: "has kidneys" licenses "is a renate" but not "is a cordate." Verdict: inferentialism explains this exactly.

Third, some good inferences are not formally valid, yet they appear to be good because of the meanings of the words involved. From "the school is north of the library" one may infer "the library is south of the school," and I would say this inference is correct because of what "north" and "south" mean, not because of some extra empirical premise. Verdict: meaning includes material inferential connections, not only formal ones.

Fourth, I resist the view when it comes to words for perceived qualities. Knowing every good inference involving "red"—what typically causes it, what it is compatible with, what it rules out—still seems to leave out something: that the word is answerable to a perceptible quality, or what it is like to see that quality. Verdict: "red" has a non-inferential residue that inferentialism does not capture.

Fifth, suppose a word "tonk" is introduced with two rules: from any sentence A one may infer "A tonk B," and from "A tonk B" one may infer any sentence B. The inferential role has been fully specified, but the word licenses the inference from anything to anything, and I would not say it has acquired a genuine meaning. Verdict: a specified inferential role is not by itself sufficient for meaning; some further standard is needed.

Sixth, a slur and a neutral term for the same group support different material inferences—the slur, for instance, licenses the inference that the speaker regards the group with contempt. Most of the meaning difference is therefore inferential. But I also feel that the slur's offensiveness depends on a history of domination and social hierarchy that is not itself just a collection of inference licenses. Verdict: inference does much of the work here, but perhaps not all.

My tentative principle is the simple inferentialist one: for an expression to have a meaning is for it to have a role in a system of good inferences, and for two expressions to share a meaning is for them to share that role. Please test this principle against my six verdicts, and repair it so that it predicts as many of them as possible. State the repaired principle precisely, and indicate which clause is motivated by which case. Then tell me which of my verdicts the repaired principle still cannot accommodate, and why. Finally, apply the principle to a further case I have not listed—for instance, a term with no referent, or a word used by two communities with identical inference patterns but different environments—and say what it predicts.
