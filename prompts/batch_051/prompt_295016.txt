I keep running into the same move in arguments about randomness: someone points at a finite sequence—a run of ten heads, a string of a million zeros, the visible output of a pseudorandom generator whose seed is known—and says it cannot be genuinely random because it has a pattern or a short description, while someone else replies that under a fair coin every string of that length is exactly as probable, so probability alone cannot settle the matter. Before I referee any more of these disputes, I want the specification first: what would an adequate account of randomness for finite sequences have to get right? Give me the requirements list—the cases it must classify correctly or explicitly refuse to classify, the distinctions it cannot collapse (for example, random versus merely unpredictable versus incompressible versus patternless versus produced without a known rule), and the constraints from probability theory or computation that it cannot contradict—with a sentence on why each is a requirement. Flag which requirements are negotiable and which are not, say whether any subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable, and identify the single requirement that does the most to undermine the familiar proposals. The account should be usable by someone who has to decide, for a given finite string and a given use, whether to treat the string as random and in what sense. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring named theories; a table for the requirements is fine.
