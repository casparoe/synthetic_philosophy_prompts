I'm a student in a course on Borges and the philosophy of time, and I'm writing a paper on "The Garden of Forking Paths." Most readings I have encountered treat Ts'ui Pên's labyrinth as an early literary statement of branching time—something like a many-worlds picture in which every choice produces all of its possible outcomes in divergent futures, and the novel is the map of those futures. But in the same course we read Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time," where he argues, drawing on Berkeley's idealism and on Hume's treatment of identity over time, that time as a succession of distinct moments is not real, that the passage from one moment to the next is supplied by the mind rather than found in the world, and perhaps that all moments are in some sense copresent. I would like a careful reading of the story through the lens of that denial of time.

Does the forking labyrinth ultimately vindicate a branching realism about time, or does it undermine even that picture—so that the garden is less a map of many futures than a device for collapsing past, present, and future into one another? Where does the story, with its spy plot, its murder, and its stubborn chronological sequence, dramatize the essay's idealist argument, and where does it resist it? I want a reading that is faithful to both texts, that says honestly where the story and the essay pull apart rather than making one a mere illustration of the other. Please also distinguish, as you go, between what is clearly in the texts and what is your interpretive proposal, and flag which claims about Borges's philosophical views are settled among scholars and which are genuinely contested. I don't need quotations or citations; I want your own reconstruction and argument.
