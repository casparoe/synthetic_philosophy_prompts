In my epistemology seminar, our discussion of the regress problem has reproduced the same deadlock for several weeks, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another defense of a position. We have been taking a passage from Aristotle’s Posterior Analytics, Book I, Chapter 3, as the source of the dilemma. As I read it, Aristotle argues that demonstrative knowledge requires premisses that are prior to and better known than the conclusion; if those premisses in turn require demonstration, either the demand for demonstration travels backward without end, or else the chain must eventually stop at immediate, indemonstrable first principles. He also considers and rejects the proposal that demonstration might go in a circle, saying that circular demonstration amounts to trying to prove the conclusion from itself, which cannot satisfy the condition that genuine premisses be prior and better known.

The three live responses in the seminar are, roughly, these. Foundationalism holds that the regress stops at basic beliefs that are justified but not by further inference. Coherentism holds that the regress never needs to stop because justification is holistic: a belief’s justification consists in its membership in a mutually supporting web, and the resulting circle is not the vicious circle Aristotle rejected. Infinitism holds that the chain of reasons does not stop and need not stop, because an infinite chain of reasons can transmit justification when each reason is itself justified by another, in a way that is not inherently vicious.

What strikes me is that all three sides seem to accept Aristotle’s description of the options while rejecting a different one of his evaluations. Foundationalists treat the threat of an infinite chain as dispositive. Coherentists accept that a linear demonstration produced by proving the conclusion from itself is defective, but deny that their web is the same thing as such a demonstration. Infinitists deny that a chain’s failing to terminate is a reason to think it cannot confer justification. When we push further, each side ends up appealing to a different intuition about what makes a regress vicious, or a different notion of what it takes for a belief to be justified for a person. I suspect the debate is not over a shared set of cases but over which features of justification are the data: should we be trying to explain Aristotle’s demonstrative science, ordinary empirical justification, or the activity of being asked for one’s reasons? Or is there a hidden formal question about whether infinite chains can transmit warrant in a formal model of support? A few people in the group think it is simply a verbal dispute over the word “justified.”

I do not want a verdict on which view is correct. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement: is it substantive or verbal, do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that no side can argue for? Are they answering different questions under one heading? Then say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress—a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question—or whether no progress should be expected and why.
