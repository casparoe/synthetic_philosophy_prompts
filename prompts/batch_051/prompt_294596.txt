I am a high school junior and the student representative on my school's Discipline Code Revision Committee. The committee meets weekly during the year to hear conduct cases, and every spring it rewrites sections of the student handbook, so we are constantly doing two things at once: drafting new rules and deciding whether an existing rule was fairly applied to a particular student. Half the members are teachers and half are students; none of us has legal training; the principal must sign off on every change; and we typically have about twenty minutes to screen a proposed rule before we vote. I have been reading about the void-for-vagueness doctrine in criminal law — roughly, the principle that a law is defective when ordinary people cannot tell what it actually prohibits, or when it hands the people enforcing it nothing more than their own preferences to apply — and I want to adapt that idea into a working tool for our committee. I know a school is not a legislature and a handbook is not a statute, so part of the task is deciding which parts of the doctrine survive the transfer and which do not. Acting as a philosopher of law, please think carefully and begin by briefly brainstorming the distinctions that would actually matter in a school setting before committing to a final sequence. Then give me a reusable procedure — a checklist or a decision path in words — that non-lawyers can run on a proposed rule in about twenty minutes. It should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "send it back for rewriting," and where it must hand the matter to a person because the remaining question is one of judgment rather than structure. It should also be honest about what the procedure cannot do — for example, distinguishing a clearly drafted but bad rule from an unacceptably vague one, or setting a precise maximum on how much enforcement discretion is tolerable.

Finally, run the procedure on three rules we are currently debating: (1) "Students shall not engage in disruptive or inappropriate behavior at school or school-sponsored events." (2) "Students shall not wear clothing that promotes violence, illegal drugs, or anything the administration deems contrary to school values." (3) "Students shall not engage in bullying, harassment, or any other conduct that makes another student feel threatened or unsafe." The third rule is the one I drafted and defended most strongly, so flag it plainly if the procedure would send it back.
