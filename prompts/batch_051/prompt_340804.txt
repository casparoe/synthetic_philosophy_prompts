My teacher has given my school's literature and philosophy seminar a peer-grading exercise on a chapter we read from Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov, and I have been assigned to evaluate an anonymous classmate's response. I want to do this carefully, because I am graded on how well I justify the grade I assign, and I suspect an easy-looking response can still contain real mistakes.

The test question was:

In the Grand Inquisitor chapter, Ivan has the Inquisitor criticize Christ for refusing the three temptations in the desert. Explain the Inquisitor's central claim about the relationship between freedom and happiness, and then identify one strength and one weakness of that claim.

The student's answer reads:

The Inquisitor argues that freedom and happiness pull in opposite directions. When Christ rejected the three temptations, he was insisting on a faith that comes from a free decision, but that is a weight most people cannot carry; in their weakness they would rather be fed, relieved of decision, and told what to believe. The Inquisitor says the Church corrected Christ's work by giving people material care, miracle and mystery, and an unquestionable authority, so it is better to see the Church's action as compassion for the weak than as betrayal. The one flaw in Christ's plan, on the Inquisitor's account, is that he expected people to earn their salvation through the effort of believing, when most people are simply incapable of such a decision.

The strongest part of the Inquisitor's case is that it predicts something real: people often do surrender their judgment to institutions that look after them, and they will tolerate unfreedom if it comes with security. The weakest part is that the argument treats human character as fixed; if freedom can be learned through practice, then the claim that most people can never bear it is not settled at all. In addition, the ending undercuts the entire speech. When Christ kisses the Inquisitor and the Inquisitor lets him go, it is obvious that the Inquisitor admits Christ was right all along, which means even the Inquisitor does not really believe his own argument.

What grade would you give this answer, and what specific written comments would justify that grade? Please give a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. Be precise about where the answer is accurate, where it misrepresents the chapter, and where its evaluation is reasonable. I would especially like you to flag anything a quick peer-grader might overlook.
