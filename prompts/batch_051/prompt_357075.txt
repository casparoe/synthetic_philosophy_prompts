I keep noticing a distinction in moral life that I don't have a name for, and I want to know whether philosophy has one. On one side there is the care a nurse gives to a particular patient: noticing that he is more short of breath than yesterday, adjusting the angle of the bed, reading his silence about pain as itself a kind of report. It is directed at one person's changing condition, moment to moment, and it seems to generate concrete obligations that no general rule quite captures. On the other side there is a more diffuse attitude that also goes by the word care: someone says they care about refugees, or about future generations, or about some patient population in the abstract. It moves them to donate, vote, or feel distressed at the news, but it is not responsive to the condition of any particular person. Moral argument keeps sliding between the two, borrowing the moral seriousness of the first to defend policies that mostly operate on the second, and I do not have a term for that slide. I have also noticed a third thing that fits neither category: someone restocks the supply room, maintains the ventilator, writes a triage protocol, or keeps a care home's kitchen clean. This work sustains care but is not itself responsive to a particular person's changing condition, and it does not feel like diffuse concern about a cause either. Does this distinction, or set of distinctions, have an established name? If it does, I do not just want the label: I want to know where the boundaries lie, whether the third thing is a form of care or only a precondition for it, and which neighboring concepts it should be distinguished from. If it has no established name, what should it be called, and where are its edge cases? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the best name, or your verdict that no established name fits — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
