Heres one I keep going back and forth on. We treat resentment as basically a healthy moral emotion. If someone humiliates you in public and you resent them for it, people say good for you—you're showing self-respect, maybe you even owe it to yourself not to just swallow it. But if a friend's parents buy them a condo and you feel envy about it, we're much quicker to call that a vice: small, ugly, something to work on—even though what triggered it was a real inequality, the kind of thing that often is genuinely unfair. Both feelings are pointing at something wrong in the world, but one gets moral credit and the other gets moral blame. I want to know whether there's a principle that justifies that split, or whether it's just a prejudice about which bitter feelings we happen to respect.

The wrinkle is this. Some philosophers have said that in a society with sufficiently large inequalities, envy of the rich isn't a vice at all: it's the predictable, excusable reaction of people to a game that's rigged, and the defect is in the system, not in the person who envies. If you accept that, you can't settle the split by saying resentment responds to wrongs and envy responds to goods, because an unearned advantage can be a wrong in the relevant sense.

So: tell me the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating resentment as a legitimate moral signal and envy as a vice—or, if you cant find one, say so and tell me what we should give up. Test it on a hard pair: a worker who envies a coworker promoted through obvious favoritism, and a person who resents a stranger for an accidental, non-malicious slight. Does your principle actually put the first one in the wrong and the second one in the right? Finally, tell me how confident you are in your answer and which point you're least sure about.
