I run the trust and safety unit at a platform that hosts consumer reviews, professional ratings, and editorial forecasts about local businesses. Our users post star ratings and short written reviews, and our own editorial staff occasionally publishes forward-looking assessments: whether a restaurant is likely to survive the year, whether a contractor's current backlog signals trouble, whether a practice seems financially fragile. We receive several takedown requests a week claiming defamation. A restaurant's lawyer argues that a one-star rating combined with the sentence "the kitchen cuts corners on hygiene" is a false statement of fact; a clinic objects to a forecast that it "is unlikely to remain solvent through the year" on the ground that the prediction is verifiable; a doctor demands removal of a review calling his waiting times "criminal." Right now my moderators decide these one at a time with no shared framework, and their calls are inconsistent: some treat every negative review as opinion, others treat any concrete-sounding predicate as fact.

Write a decision procedure a non-lawyer moderator can actually run on any challenged review, rating, or forecast. It should be grounded in the structure of defamation law's line between actionable assertions of fact and protected expressions of opinion, worked out specifically for the hard cases this domain keeps producing: numerical-looking ratings, statements that are partly verifiable and partly evaluative, loose or figurative language that readers might take literally, and forecasts whose surface grammar is about the future but may imply present facts. For each step, specify the question to ask, what a sufficient answer looks like, where the procedure stops with "leave the item up as protected opinion," and where it hands the matter to counsel because the statement may be actionable or the distinction cannot be applied mechanically.

Then run the procedure on three recurring challenges: (a) a one-star restaurant review reading "overpriced tourist trap — the kitchen cuts corners"; (b) an editorial forecast that a medical practice "is unlikely to remain solvent through the year"; and (c) a one-star rating of a physician with the comment "waiting times are criminal." Flag every step at which a moderator must exercise judgment rather than apply a rule, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle. Include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer my team will dislike.
