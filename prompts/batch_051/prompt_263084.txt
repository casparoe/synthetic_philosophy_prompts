Right, I'm marking a first-year undergrad moral philosophy course and would like a second opinion on one essay. The question was:

Consider rules like tipping in countries where it's customary, queueing, and not jaywalking. Are these everyday conventions morally binding on a visitor to a place, or is a visitor only required to avoid genuine moral wrongdoing? Set out the strongest case on each side, then defend your own view.

The student's answer, in full, was:

Conventions like tipping, queueing and jaywalking are not moral rules as such, since they are local and could easily have been otherwise, whereas moral rules hold generally and do not just track local habit. Still, conventions generate expectations, and locals rely on them; a visitor who ignores these rules upsets those expectations and free-rides on a practice that works only because most people comply. That unfairness is a moral matter, so visitors acquire a genuine moral obligation to follow local conventions even though the convention itself is not a moral rule. I would allow an exception for conventions that are themselves unjust, since those expectations have no moral weight. Conventions also characteristically carry social sanctions and expressive meaning rather than the sanctions and seriousness of morality, which is our best test for telling a convention from a moral requirement in hard cases.
