I am a student in a philosophy of science course on underdetermination and scientific realism, and I have been watching two classmates argue for weeks about a single question: when two ontological pictures of a system are compatible with all observations that are accessible not just in practice but in principle, does it still make sense to say we have identified the system's ontology, or has the claim lost its content?

One classmate holds what I will call the robust-identification view. On this view, ontology is identified in the same fallible way hidden causes are identified anywhere, and the in-principle inaccessibility of some distinguishing information makes an ontological question undecidable, not meaningless. The other holds what I will call the accessibility-condition view. On this view, an ontological difference that no accessible evidence could ever discriminate has no content; the only thing that can legitimately be identified is structure, relations, or whatever is common to the rival pictures through their accessible consequences.

I want to write the document these two should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: a joint statement both could sign. Please draft that document for me.

Format it as follows. First, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, phrased precisely enough that neither side is merely tolerating a vague form of words. Second, a disagreements table that gives, for each point of difference, the robust-identification formulation and the accessibility-condition formulation, each in wording that side would accept for itself. Third, a section on cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would force at least one side to revise something it currently asserts. For each crux, propose a concrete case or piece of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, write down in advance what each side predicts the case would show, and say exactly what a positive result would and would not settle for each side. The cases may be thought experiments or toy systems, since the point is not to run an experiment but to make the disagreement tractable.

To keep the document from drifting into abstraction, it should be anchored in the following running example, which the course has used and which is deliberately stripped of all real scientific detail. Consider a finite system whose observable states and transitions constitute a fixed table. Two accounts of the system, O1 and O2, agree on the entire table and on every possible observation that could be made from inside the system. They differ only below the surface: O1 says a single hidden kind does all the work, while O2 says a plurality of cooperating hidden kinds does the work, with a division of labor that is undetectable in principle. Since observations are defined by the transition table itself, no extension of measurement from inside the system could tell O1 and O2 apart. You may replace this running example with a better one if you explain why it is better, but the crux cases should remain of this kind: a distinction that is inaccessible as a matter of principle rather than a temporary technical limit.

Finally, include a short closing statement of what the collaboration achieved: which disagreements are substantive, which are in the end verbal or driven by different epistemic standards, and where each side would have to admit that further evidence, even of the most idealized kind, could not by itself close the gap.
