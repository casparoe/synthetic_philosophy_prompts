I am a high school student in the United States, and my strongest subject is ecology. Through AP Biology and two years of Science Olympiad ecology events I have a working command of terms like succession, disturbance, disturbance regime, resilience, resistance, alternative stable states, regime shift, hysteresis, carrying capacity, keystone species, and trophic cascade. I have read only a little of the debate over whether complex societies collapse or merely change, mostly through the standard examples of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island. I am not asking for facts about those cases, and I am not asking for a reading list or citations.

What I want is a translation exercise, done carefully and honestly. Please take the role of a researcher who works where ecology meets the study of past societies.

Here is the task. Translate the main positions in the debate over whether societies fail or merely change into the ecological vocabulary I just listed. The positions I have in mind are roughly these, and you should treat them as working descriptions, not as quotations of any author:

1. Collapse as system failure. A complex society is an integrated system held together by complexity itself—administration, record-keeping, specialized production, long-distance exchange. When the returns on that complexity fall, several core functions fail together. Genuine collapse is therefore the loss of complexity as such: not merely a change of rulers or a relocation, but a many-sided breakdown of the society's operating system.

2. Transformation, not failure. The people do not vanish; they disperse, simplify, regroup, and build new arrangements. The word collapse reflects a habit of identifying a society with its elite's institutions—palaces, writing, monuments—so that when those institutions end, we call it a collapse even though ordinary life continues in other forms. What looks like failure from the center is adaptation from the edges.

3. A middle-ground position. The distinction is real but gradational. Some transitions destroy more of what people valued than others, and calling every such transition a transformation flattens the difference between a society that adapts successfully and one that loses much of its capacities. The question is which functions were lost, for whom, and for how long.

For each position, go term by term and report (a) which claims translate cleanly into ecological vocabulary, (b) which claims have to be distorted or forced to fit, and (c) which claims have no expression in the vocabulary at all. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle ecological assumptions into the positions and then call that a translation. Then give the residue report: what does not survive the translation, and is that residue a defect in my ecological vocabulary, a sign that one of the original positions was fuzzier than it looks, or both.

If the task itself rests on a confused premise—for example, if ecological disturbance is too weak an analogue for political disintegration to do real work, or if the debate cannot be posed as a translation problem—please say so plainly and tell me what a better version of the task would be. Do not write a forced translation just to be accommodating; I would rather revise the prompt than receive a polite answer that ignores the problem.

Please begin by brainstorming candidate ecological terms as a mapping exercise before you commit to the translations. Then write the response in the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. The debate, stated neutrally (~150 words). Restate the two or three positions you will translate so I can confirm we mean the same thing.

2. Mapping summary (~150 words). Show the main candidate ecological terms and note which look like exact fits, which look strained, and which clearly do not fit.

3. Term-by-term translations (~600 words). Go through the positions one by one; for each, list clean mappings, forced mappings, and what has no expression.

4. Residue report (~350 words). State precisely what failed to carry over and what that tells us—whether the ecological vocabulary is missing something or the original claim was under-specified.

5. Verdict and confidence (~250 words). Give your bottom-line judgment about whose defect the residue mostly is, and mark the points where you are confident versus where you are speculating.
