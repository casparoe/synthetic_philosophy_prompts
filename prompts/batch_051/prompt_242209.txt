I have a friend whose moral judgement I have come to trust, but who refuses to state any general principle and will say only that everything depends on the details of the case. Over the years I have kept a record of her verdicts on cases involving broken promises. She judged it right to break a promise to a new acquaintance to meet for coffee when one came across an injured cyclist who needed help reaching hospital; wrong to break a promise to act as best man at one's closest friend's wedding in order to accept an all-expenses-paid holiday; right to break a promise to stay late at work to finish a presentation when one's child had a severe asthma attack and needed to be taken to hospital; wrong to break a promise to attend a friend's single performance in an amateur play because one felt tired and wanted a quiet evening; right to break a promise to water a neighbour's plants while she was away when one had to travel unexpectedly to care for a parent who had broken a hip; and wrong to break a promise to repay a loan by the agreed date in order to bid on a rare book at auction.

I now face a case of my own, and I have been trying to work out what she would say about it. I have promised to attend a distant cousin's wedding, but my teenage daughter, who has been having a difficult term at school, has asked me to stay with her that weekend instead. She is not in any crisis and there is no emergency; she simply misses me and says she would feel considerably better if I were there. I have recently been reading about the debate between moral generalists and particularists, and I cannot tell whether my friend is quietly applying a single general principle about when promises may be broken or whether her judgements are genuinely case-by-case in the particularist manner. Predict her verdict on my case, set out the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, say what each rule would predict about my case, give your best guess as to which rule she is actually running and why, and describe a further case that would most sharply discriminate between the remaining candidates.
