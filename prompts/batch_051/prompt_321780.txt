I spent thirty years as a physical chemist, and since retiring I have been working my way through philosophical logic. I have found myself stuck on the question whether everything that exists exists necessarily, and rather than ask for another explanation of the formalism, I want to enlist your judgment in ranking the reactions one might have to the underlying difficulty. The difficulty comes from the pair of principles usually called the Barcan formulas. As I understand them, one principle licenses the move from 'there is something that could have been F' to 'it could have been that there is something that is F,' and the other licenses the reverse move. In a standard possible-worlds semantics, accepting the first amounts to holding that the inventory of objects never shrinks as one moves from a world to an accessible world, so that no actually existing object could have failed to exist; accepting the second amounts to holding that the inventory never grows, so that no object appears in an accessible world unless it was already among the actual objects. Taken together, they force a single fixed inventory across all worlds, and that is what makes the question feel so pressing. I suspect my old habit of counting which molecules are actually in a reaction vessel, rather than which molecules merely could have been, is part of why I cannot let it go.

Here are four candidate reactions, put into my own words. Please rank them from the one you would most want to defend to the one you would least want to defend, allowing ties if you think two are on a par. (i) Accept the conclusion: the apparent contingency of existence is really contingency in how a thing is — for example, whether it is concrete or located in space and time — and the domain of what there is never varies. (ii) Reject the first principle and keep a varying domain in which objects can genuinely pass out of existence; the inference from 'something could be F' to 'possibly something is F' is simply not valid. (iii) Deflate the principles by distinguishing an unrestricted, formal sense of 'there is an object' from the ordinary, spatiotemporal sense of 'exists,' so that the formulas come out true but carry no surprising existential weight. (iv) Treat the whole affair as an artifact of possible-worlds semantics: the framework is a bookkeeping device, and we should not let its formal constraints dictate conclusions about what exists. Give one sentence for each item explaining why it lands where it does, and then a brief comment on what your ranking suggests about whether everything that exists exists necessarily. When you answer, say how confident you are in the ranking overall and identify the single point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
