I'm a journalist who covers memory politics for a magazine's ideas section—museum rows, reparations debates, truth commissions, the whole mess—and I keep runing into the same deeper question underneath all the specific fights: does a political community actually have a duty to remember mass violence and its victims, or is that framing backwards? I've ended up on the remembering side, roughly the Margalit line: the victims can no longer speak, so the community has to serve as their witness; letting an atrocity fade from public memory is a second wrong, a way of finishing the erasure. Against that I keep hearing the Rieff-style worry that collective remembrance is often really about a polity's self-image and not about the victims at all, and that some forgetting is necessary if a society is ever going to stop relitigating its past.

So take the position under test to be something like: a political community wrongs the victims of mass atrocity when it allows public memory of them to lapse; remembering is a duty, not just a nice thing. I want to know exactly how exposed this claim is. What would count against it? I'm after the strongest defeaters, not strawmen: cases where an official policy of forgetting seemed to help a society function (post-Franco Spain is the one people always bring up), what psychology suggests about how groups actually remember and distort, the conceptual worry that state-sponsored commemoration is curated propaganda rather than memory, and the problem that the duty might be compatible with everything—an empty injunction to not forget that predicts nothing. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a committed defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it, and which they would dismiss as changing the subject. And be willing to tell me at the end whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a moral claim of this kind.
