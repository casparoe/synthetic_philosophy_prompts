Take the standard beauty-contest game: each of n players chooses a real number between 0 and 100 inclusive, and the player whose number is closest to two-thirds of the average wins a fixed prize, with ties split evenly. The usual treatment runs three distinguishable analyses: iterated elimination of never-best responses squeezes the guess to 0, the unique Nash equilibrium is for everyone to choose 0, and level-k reasoning generates the familiar descending sequence of guesses.

Now change one feature. The target is the median of the chosen numbers rather than two-thirds of the mean. Everything else is the same: numbers in [0,100], closest guess wins, ties split evenly.

Work through the median version under each of the three approaches. (a) Does iterated elimination of never-best responses eliminate anything, and what is the rationalizable set? (b) What is the set of Nash equilibria? (c) What does a level-k model with level-0 guesses uniform on [0,100] predict, and does that prediction stay anchored if level-0 is instead concentrated at 50? Finally, assess how much of the original lesson survives. The original game is often used to show that common knowledge of rationality forces an extreme answer without any coordination. Which of the three analyses best explains what rational actors would do once the statistic is the median, and how much of the original argument was silently being done by the two-thirds-of-the-mean functional form rather than by common knowledge of rationality alone?
