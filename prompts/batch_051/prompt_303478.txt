Suppose that in the early 2030s an international neuroimaging consortium publishes a phenomenality signature: a pattern of brain activity that, after calibration against the first-person reports of healthy volunteers across wakefulness, deep sleep, general anaesthesia, lucid dreaming, and locked-in syndrome, tracks the reported presence and intensity of felt experience with near-perfect accuracy, and then generalizes to subjects in states from which no report can be obtained. Within a few years the signature is widely accepted as a detector of subjective consciousness. Two follow-up measurements are then treated as sound. First, about one neurologically normal adult in twelve shows the signature at zero at all times, even while employed, conversing, writing, and firmly insisting that they are conscious. Second, some patients who are behaviourally unresponsive on standard clinical tests show a rich, fluctuating signature. Accept for the sake of the prompt that these measurements reliably detect whatever the signature detects.

Predict, with visible reasoning, how the next two decades play out in three domains: (1) the philosophy of mind, especially the standing of the hard problem and the explanatory gap between physical description and felt experience; (2) clinical and legal practice concerning unresponsive patients, infants, and the signature-zero minority; and (3) everyday moral life, including whether people treat signature-zero individuals differently and how those individuals respond. Throughout, keep two readings of the discovery in play: the signature as a faithful index of experience itself, and the signature as a third-person proxy for the neural machinery behind behavioural report. Do not simply dismiss either reading; say explicitly where the twenty-year prediction branches on which reading a society adopts. End by stating which effects are robust under almost any version of this scenario, which are fragile guesses, and what would have to hold for the discovery to genuinely dissolve rather than merely relocate the hard problem.

Structure your response in five titled sections:

- The measurement and its limits (~200 words): state what the detector can and cannot establish, and set out the reading under which it is only a proxy for reportability.
- Philosophy of mind over twenty years (~250 words): predict whether the hard problem is declared solved, refined, or left intact, and how the concept of a phenomenal zombie is affected.
- Clinical and legal practice (~250 words): predict changes in diagnosing and treating disorders of consciousness, in end-of-life decision-making, and in legal personhood, with the signature-zero minority as the hardest case.
- Everyday morality and self-understanding (~200 words): predict social sorting, stigma, and shifts in how much authority people accord to first-person reports.
- Robust effects, fragile guesses, and what would change my mind (~150 words).
