I find myself pulled in two directions about envy and resentment, and I want a structured way of deciding which of my commitments should yield rather than just a verdict for one side.

On one side, I am attracted to the Rawlsian claim that envy is not always a vice to be suppressed. When the better-off hold advantages produced by institutions that could not be justified to the worse-off, the envy of the worse-off looks excusable, perhaps even a fitting response to injustice. On this picture, envy and resentment track something real about the structure of social cooperation: not merely that I happen to want what you have, but that you are benefiting from terms that could not be justified to me as a free and equal person. Resentment, similarly, looks like the appropriate moral emotion when someone treats me unfairly or with contempt.

On the other side, I have a firm ethical intuition that envy is one of the vices: other-directed, self-corrosive, essentially comparative, and defined by the wish that the other have less rather than that the arrangement be made fair. Resentment, too, seems like the kind of emotional state a virtuous agent should expel, or at most treat as evidence of their own wounded entitlement. I worry that when we redescribe our envy as concern for justice, we are rationalizing. A political culture in which envy and resentment are treated as insight rather than temptation seems to me difficult to reconcile with civic friendship and mutual respect.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what would have to be true for each of my commitments to give way, and whether there is a principled reconciliation between them. In particular, please address these four questions:

1. Are envy and resentment best understood as the same underlying type of social comparison, differing only in whether the disadvantage is perceived as deserved or undeserved, or is the difference more fundamental?

2. The claim that envy is excusable when inequalities are unjust could mean several different things: that envy serves as an epistemic signal of injustice, that it is a practical motivator for political action, that it is a morally fitting attitude, or merely that it is a predictable and non-blameworthy psychological reaction. Which of these claims, if any, should I actually accept?

3. What sort of empirical or social-theoretic premise would be needed to move from the fact that people envy to the conclusion that an inequality is unjust? Is envy likely to be sensitive to the difference principle, or is it more likely to track bare inequality, relative deprivation, or personal comparison sets?

4. Can I keep my intuition that envy is a vice while granting that some of the things I call resentment are morally required? What principle would best distinguish justified or excusable resentment from vicious envy without collapsing into self-serving rationalization?

Give me a negotiated analysis: say what kind of argument or evidence would debunk the intuition, what would amend or narrow the theory, and what would show that the two positions are compatible after all. Keep the response under 900 words.
