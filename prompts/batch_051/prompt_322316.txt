I'm a novelist, so this one's close to home. The old paradox of fiction: I can be genuinely upset when Anna Karenina throws herself under the train, even though I know perfectly well she never existed. How does an emotion attach to something I believe isn't real? The standard fix is some version of make-believe or quasi-emotion—I'm not feeling real grief, I'm feeling imagined grief aimed at imagined content. Now suppose the fiction becomes interactive: a character in a game or a companion app that responds to me, remembers what I've said, gets hurt if I ignore it, and whose lines are generated instead of written. There's no single fixed scene for me to imagine; the character is partly my doing. I want to know wether the old paradox, and the make-believe fix, survive that change. Does the fix get stronger because the imagining is now explicit and collaborative, or does it stop applying because the emotions are now about a real interaction happening to me? If it breaks, say which premise is doing the dying.
