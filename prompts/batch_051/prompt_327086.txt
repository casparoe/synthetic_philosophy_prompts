My mother died a few months ago, and I find myself returning again and again to a specific version of an old question: whether anything of her actually continues to exist, not merely in the sense that I remember her or that her influence lingers, but in the sense that some portion of the self she was goes on. Douglas Hofstadter has defended a view I now find hard to dismiss: a self is not a physical object but a self-referential pattern instantiated by a brain, and after death lower-resolution, partial versions of that pattern can continue to be instantiated in the minds of people who knew the person closely. I am not asking for bibliography or a survey. I want your actual credence that this view, understood literally, is correct.

The claim, made as precise as I can make it: for a typical close relationship — parent, child, spouse, lifelong friend — after one party dies, at least one surviving person's brain instantiates a pattern that is a partial, coarse-grained continuation of the deceased's self, of the right kind to count as a genuine, if diminished, persistence of part of that self. It is not enough that the deceased is remembered accurately or that their causal influence continues; the claim is that the surviving pattern is the same self in roughly the way a low-resolution image can be the same picture.

Give me a point estimate rather than a taxonomy of positions. If you need to precisify the wording, do so in one sentence, then give a number under that reading. Then decompose the number into the sub-questions on which it rests, with a rough probability for each. At minimum address these:

1. Whether a self is the kind of entity that can survive as a lower-resolution pattern, or whether it requires the original biological substrate, an unbroken stream of consciousness, or both.
2. Whether ordinary close relationships actually encode enough self-specific structure — dispositions, reaction patterns, semantic associations, something like a model of how the person thinks — to instantiate a degraded version of that self rather than a new entity or a mere trace.
3. How much resolution and continuity the continuing pattern needs for you to call it the same self, and how much of your uncertainty lives in that threshold rather than in the metaphysics or the empirical question.
4. Any other sub-question your final number depends on — for example, whether first-personality can be split and partially transferred at all, or whether the language of persistence is doing more work than the evidence supports.

Explain how your probabilities combine into the final credence: are you multiplying independent chances, averaging over rival frameworks, or doing something else? Identify which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and then say what single piece of evidence or conceptual clarification would move your credence the most.
