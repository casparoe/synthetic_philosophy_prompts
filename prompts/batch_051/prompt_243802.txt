In a reading group on the postwar trials, someone keeps shutting down any defense of the Nuremberg and Tokyo verdicts with nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege — "no crime, no punishment without law." His line is that the tribunal basically made up "crimes against peace" after the war and then punished German and Japanese leaders for acts that weren't individually criminal when they did them, so the whole thing was just victors' justice wearing a legal maxim. I can feel there's something too quick about this, but I can't locate it, and I've realized it's because the one-liner is doing several different jobs at once.

Can you lay out the distinct things that slogan could actually be claiming? I can see a few candidate readings but I can't keep them apart: (a) a court may not apply a written statute that wasn't on the books at the time the act was done; (b) no one may be punished without fair notice that the specific act was a crime, even if the act was already an international legal wrong in some looser, customary sense; (c) criminal liability can't be extended by analogy or by retroactively tightening definitions; (d) a court can't punish individual state officials when the only prior prohibition bound the state itself, like the 1928 Kellogg-Briand Pact's renunciation of war. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial or substantive, whether it's actually defensible, and whether it really condemns what Nuremberg and Tokyo did. Then tell me which reading my group-mate needs for his argument to work, which readings a defender of the trials can comfortably grant him, and what the best reply is to the strongest version of his objction rather than the weakest. I basically want a menu I can bring to the next session.
