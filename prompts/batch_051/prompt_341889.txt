My eng org is about to adopt a new voting rule for our architecture review board, and I got asked to figure out how it will actually behave, not whether it sounds good on a slide. The rule goes like this. Any proposal has to be submitted as a small argument: a conclusion plus the premises that are supposed to force that conclusion. The proposer gets to choose how to cut it into premises. The board votes on each premise individually, majority rule. If every required premise passes, the conclusion is adopted automatically. There is no separate up/down vote on the conclusion itself — which means a proposal can pass even when most board members would have voted no on the conclusion, as long as their opposition is spread across different premises. A toy case someone showed me: Alice and Bob accept premise 1, Alice and Charlie accept premise 2, but only Alice accepts the conclusion; under this rule the thing passes even though the board is 2–1 against the conclusion. While a proposal is open, any board member can also add a 'blocking premise' of their own, which then has to pass too. And every premise that passes gets written into a shared library, so future proposals can cite it as already-established and no one reargues it.

So my question isnt whether this is a good idea in the abstract. I want to know how the thing behaves after six months. What do proposers do under this rule — do they slice arguments into many small, safe premises, and does that let more bad decisions through or just make them easier to see? What do board members do once they cant vote no on the whole proposal — do they fight over how the premises are phrased, or over whose blocking premise counts, or do they start strategically voting against premises they actually believe in order to kill a conclusion they dislike? What kind of wrong decisions does this rule specifically select for? I also keep worrying about compounding risk: if the board thinks each of five premises is 60% likely to be true, the conclusion might be only something like 8% likely, but the rule treats each premise as majority-passed and records the whole thing as settled. Does the premise library make that worse, because you can chain your way through a few previously-passed premises to a conclusion that no single member ever endorsed as a whole? And is there a version of this that keeps the part I actually like — recorded reasons, and arguments that get voted on explicitly — without the part where a conclusion passes despite majority opposition?
