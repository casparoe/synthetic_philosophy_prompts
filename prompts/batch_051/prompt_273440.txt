A friend who has been studying Chinese philosophy recently summarized Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action to me like this:

"The idea is basically that knowing and doing are the same thing rather than two successive stages. If you say you know that lying is wrong and then you lie, you did not really know it in the first place — your 'knowledge' was just words. On Wang's view, doing wrong is always a failure of knowledge: people act badly only because they have not genuinely learned the relevant truth, and the remedy is to get them to know it properly. Once someone truly knows a moral truth, they cannot act against it, so weakness of will in the usual sense is ruled out by definition. Wang illustrates this with pain and cold: you do not know what pain is unless you are actually in pain, just as you do not know filial piety unless you are actually behaving filially."

I am suspicious of the move from "not genuinely knowing" to "cannot act against it," and of treating moral failure as a simple information deficit, but I am not sure where the misreading begins. Could you assess how fair this summary is to Wang's actual position? I would appreciate a claim-by-claim audit: which parts a careful reader of Wang would accept, which are overstatements, conflations, or common simplifications, and whether the claims about moral ignorance, moral education, and the impossibility of weakness of will follow once the accurate parts are put back together. In particular, what sort of "knowledge" does the unity thesis require, and what exactly is the relation between knowing and acting if it is not the claim that sincere belief inevitably produces action?
