I want you to analyse the run-up to the 2003 Iraq War in the UK as a case study in Orwell’s account of political language. The salient facts as I understand them are these. Throughout 2002 and early 2003, the Blair government justified military action by claiming that Iraq was actively developing weapons of mass destruction and could deploy them at short notice; official communications repeatedly leaned on abstractions such as a current and growing threat, material breach, and the enforcement of the international community’s will. After the invasion, no active WMD stockpiles were found, and a later inquiry concluded that government claims had been presented as more certain than the underlying intelligence justified.

Orwell’s view, roughly, is that political speech decays in a two-way relation with thought: abstraction, jargon, and euphemism are not merely symptoms of dishonesty but tools that make dishonesty possible by allowing the speaker to avoid picturing the human facts. I have spent enough time around rationalist commentary to be suspicious of using Orwell merely as a cheap debunking move, so I want the analysis to earn its conclusions. Apply the theory to the episode in both directions. What would Orwell’s account predict was happening as officials moved from concrete plans for removing Saddam Hussein to public language about enforcing international obligations? Where does it explain something real about how actors became caught inside their own framing, and where does it moralise or flatten something better explained by institutional incentives, distributed responsibility, and genuine uncertainty about intelligence? Specifically, I want to know whether bad prose was upstream or downstream of the political failure: would plainer language have forced the public and officials to confront the actual decision, or would it merely have made the same policy look less respectable? Tell me which parts of Orwell’s account survive contact with the case and which parts need amendment once you treat language as one institution among several.
