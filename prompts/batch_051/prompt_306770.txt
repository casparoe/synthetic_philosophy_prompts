So I keep coming back to the discount-rate fights in climate economics — the Stern Review basically refuses to discount future people's welfare on pure-time-preference grounds, while Nordhaus says the model should be calibrated to how markets actually trade off now against later, which in practice makes someone alive in 2200 worth almost nothing today. Every time I read one of those exchanges I wonder what Edmund Burke would've made of it. Burke is the guy who described the social order as something the living hold in trust, with the dead and the unborn as full members of a partnership — which sure sounds like it commits him to a near-zero discount rate. But he's also the guy who thought we should be deeply suspicious of abstract principles being used to rebuild inherited institutions, which is pretty much exactly what picking a discount rate and cranking global policy out of a climate-economy model is. So the prompt: resurrect Burke, hand him the modern debate about how much to weight a person in 2200 against a person today, and write the pamphlet or essay he might plausibly have published. Does the partnership idea force him to something Stern-ish, or does the conservative skepticism about abstraction push him toward treating the distant future as something you steward rather than optimise over? Is there one stable Burkean position here, or two commitments that keep colliding depending which direction he looks? Try to actually land him somewhere — tell me roughly where he sets the discount rate, or wether he refuses the entire question and why.
