Interpretability papers are full of this move: a neuron that fires on pictures of dogs gets called a "dog detector," a direction in a language model's activations gets called a "truth" direction, and then when someone shows the neuron also fires on random junk or the direction doesn't transfer to another model, the authors say they never meant it literally. Meanwhile the field has a different, stricter standard for what counts as a real finding: you have to intervene—turn the unit off or edit the direction—and show the behavior changes. What philosophical commitments does this practice actually presuppose? Specifically, does it presuppose realism about representations (that there's a fact of the matter about what a network represents, independent of whatever probe you happen to train), or is it fully compatible with a purely instrumentalist view where "the model represents X" just means "some readout predicts X from the activations"? Reconstruct the implicit theory of representation. I'd especially like to know what the practice strictly needs, as opposed to what merely fits well with it, and what would have to change—in what counts as finding a representation, or in how disagreements about interpretations get settled—if you gave up the realism.
