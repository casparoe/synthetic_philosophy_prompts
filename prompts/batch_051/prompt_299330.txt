I've been arguing with a friend about whether the EU's so-called democratic deficit is actually a problem, and we kept bumping into an asymmetry I can't shake. Here it is. A person who would be furious if their national government changed workplace safety rules, packaging requirements, or consumer protections through a body they never elected will barely blink if the same kind of rule arrives as an EU regulation or directive. The Commission that drafts almost all of that legislation is not directly elected as a body; the directly elected European Parliament cannot initiate legislation and is one co-legislator a very long way from the average voter; the Council includes ministers who were at least elected at home, but its deals get reached in trilogues and late-night negotiations. The accountability chain is longer and thinner at every link, yet ordinary judgment is, if anything, more relaxed about the EU end of things. I don't just want "because the treaties say so." Is there an actual principle that justifies treating supranational and national governance differently — something like pooled sovereignty, pre-commitment, output legitimacy, subsidiarity, or the idea that you elect the governments who then bargain at the EU level — and does it survive a stress test where an EU rule does something that would clearly outrage people if it had been passed at home? Or is the asymmetry just habituation and path-dependence with a layer of constitutional theory painted on afterwards?

You are an expert on democratic theory and EU constitutional law. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming the strongest candidate justifications before you commit. Then give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each point should be a distinct candidate principle or a distinct reason to accept or reject the asymmetry; together they should leave me with your honest overall verdict on whether the asymmetry is defensible.
