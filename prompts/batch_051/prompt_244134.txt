I have been turning over the problem of angelic evil in something like its classic form, and I have arrived at a position I want tested rather than affirmed. Assume the standard picture: a wholly good creator brings into existence finite rational agents who are initially good, with intellect and free will, but without bodies, appetites, developmental histories, or an external tempter. One of these angels nevertheless falls, preferring itself to the good it knows. My position is that this first evil must be understood as a bare free choice: nothing prior to the choice — no defect, ignorance, appetite, or temptation — explains it, because ex hypothesi there was nothing prior that could. The possibility of exactly such an underdetermined choice is the price of a created freedom that can love the good voluntarily rather than by necessity or automation.

What I want from you is an account of what would count against this position, not a defense of it and not a recycled list of objections. I want a map of its exposure. Which intuitions, possible cases, and results in neighboring areas — action theory, responsibility, the metaphysics of evil, moral psychology, divine freedom — does it stand or fall with? I can identify the following pressure points, but I am unsure how much weight each carries:

1. Intelligibility and randomness. An absolutely first evil choice, lacking any prior defect or distorting misapprehension, looks at risk of being unintelligible, and if it is merely random the angel seems less responsible, not more. Can the defender describe a free choice that is neither caused by prior defects nor random, and how much of the position depends on doing so successfully?

2. The apparent-good constraint. The will is standardly said to choose only under the aspect of good. But in a wholly good creation, every object the angel could have chosen was genuinely good. If the first sin was the choice of a lower good over a higher one, what supplied the disordered preference? If the sin required an apparent good that was in fact illusory, where did the illusion come from in an unflawed intellect?

3. The cost of freedom. If free will is compatible with being so constituted that one always chooses the good — as with the blessed in heaven, or with God — then the ability to sin is not the price of freedom, and the creator could have given the angels an infallibly good free will. If the defender replies that created freedom necessarily includes the ability to fail, I want to know why a fallible free will is more worth creating than an infallibly good one, and what becomes of the claim that the creator is not responsible for the first sin.

4. The gap from the human case. Free-will explanations of human wrongdoing do their work against a background of bodily appetite, habit, akrasia, ignorance, and social influence. Angelic evil, by stipulation, has none of that. I want to know whether the angelic case is the same kind of explanation or only the same word, freedom, assigned a job it has not yet earned.

For each pressure point, and for any others that carry more weight, distinguish what would be decisive from what would be merely awkward. Say which of these a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against the position, and which they would dismiss as demanding something incoherent from a story about ultimate firsts. Be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated, but then say plainly whether that insulation is a feature or a symptom of a view that has stopped making contact with the hard version of the problem.

I am not asking for a bibliography, a history of the question, or an exegesis of any author. I want the strongest honest statement of what would undermine the position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward.
