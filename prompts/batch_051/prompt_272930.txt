I have been drafting a novel set in a near-future city-state that adopts futarchy, and while working out the scheme's internal logic for a secondary character, I found myself constructing an argument that now seems to prove too much for me to use without checking it. The passage I keep coming back to is from Robin Hanson's manifesto "Futarchy: Vote Values, But Bet Beliefs" (2000), where he explains what gives speculative markets their edge:

To have a say in a speculative market, you have to "put your money where your mouth is." Those who know they are not relevant experts shut up, and those who do not know this eventually lose their money, and then shut up. Speculative markets in essence offer to pay anyone who sees a bias in current market prices to come and correct that bias.

The argument I built from this runs as follows. (1) Prediction markets aggregate information well because participants must stake money on their claims; those who know they are not relevant experts fall silent, those who do not know it eventually lose money and fall silent, and anyone who spots a bias in the current price is paid to correct it. (2) That mechanism is topic-neutral: it should work for any proposition whose truth can later be settled by an observable measurement, regardless of whether the proposition is called a belief or an evaluation. (3) Futarchy applies the mechanism to policy: representatives fix a measure of national welfare, a conditional market estimates welfare with and without a proposed policy, and if the market says the policy raises expected welfare, it becomes law. (4) The choice of the welfare measure itself can also be framed as such a proposition, since one can later measure whether citizens under that regime would retrospectively endorse that measure over a rival. (5) If the mechanism in (1) justifies the policy rule in (3), it equally justifies making the welfare measure follow a market's estimate of which measure will be retrospectively endorsed. (6) Under that rule, markets would set values rather than merely betting on beliefs; democracy's role would shrink to whatever the market predicts it will later prefer. (7) That is the opposite of the division of labor futarchy was built around. So something must go—a premise, or the inference at (5). Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and the values/beliefs division of labor, but if that pair forces me to surrender some other assumption, I want to see the bill. One further request: please separate what is settled about prediction markets and futarchy from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
