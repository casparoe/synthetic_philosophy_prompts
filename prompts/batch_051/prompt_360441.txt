I'm a novelist planning a first-contact novel, and I find myself caught between the genre's two default postures: aliens as serene benefactors, and aliens as hidden predators. The frame I keep circling is the dark forest hypothesis—roughly, that the rational policy for any civilization that detects another is to stay silent or strike first, because across interstellar distances trust cannot be built, capabilities stand in for intentions, and any detected civilization is a future existential threat. I recently rewatched Arrival, Denis Villeneuve's 2016 film based on Ted Chiang's novella Story of Your Life, hoping it would show me what a peaceful-contact plot has to earn. Read the film through the dark forest lens. Where does it genuinely take the hypothesis's worries seriously—misread signals, the gap between what a species can do and what it wants, the impossibility of inspecting motives at a distance—and where does it quietly assume exactly what the hypothesis says cannot be assumed: that communication can be built up piecemeal, that vulnerability can be shown without being exploited, that both sides prefer a delayed joint payoff to eliminating a rival? Is Arrival a refutation of the dark forest logic, or a demonstration of the narrow conditions under which peaceful contact could be stable, and thus an inventory of what a novelist must construct rather than borrow from genre convention? I am less interested in whether the hypothesis is true than in what the film teaches about the architecture of a believable, non-naive peaceful encounter.
