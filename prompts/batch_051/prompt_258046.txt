I keep going back to the old line from Darwin in Chapter II of the Origin, where he says roughly that species is just a term applied arbitrarily to groups of individuals that look alike, for convenience's sake. I want to test that against a case that should make it uncomfortable.

Here are the criteria I was taught a modern species concept has to satisfy for something to count as a real species:

- reproductive isolation, or at least reproductive cohesion internal to the group
- diagnosable distinctness, usually morphological
- monophyly, as far as that makes sense for populations
- a distinct ecological niche or role

Feel free to add or subtract if you think one of these is doing no work.

Now the borderline case: Ensatina salamanders around the Central Valley in California. There are several named subspecies forming a more or less continuous ring. Adjacent populations interbreed where their ranges touch, but at the southern end the two terminal forms overlap, look quite different, and don't interbreed. The whole ring is descended from a single ancestral population in the north.

Check each criterion for the ring as a whole, for the southern terminal forms, and for a middle subspecies. Then give a verdict: is the whole ring one species, several species, or is the question somehow misframed? Does the Ensatina case vindicate Darwin's arbitrariness remark, or does it show that at least one criterion on my list can't be applied as a simple yes/no to real organisms?

Please return the response as a JSON object with these fields:

criteria: an array where each entry has "criterion", "ring_as_whole", "southern_forms", and "middle_subspecies", with each of those three being a one-sentence judgment (true, false, or mixed, plus why).
verdict: your bottom line on how many species are there and which species concept does the least violence to this case.
darwin_verdict: a short field saying whether the case vindicates or undercuts Darwin's claim that "species" is an arbitrary convenience.
contested: an array of places where practicing systematists genuinely disagree, kept separate from what is settled; don't present a contested view as the consensus.
