I've been teaching myself decision theory and keep coming back to Good's theorem on the value of information. The way I've seen it put is that for an expected-utility maximizer, costless information can never make you worse off in expectation, because you can always just ignore it. That much seems airtight, but I got into an argument with my sister over the weekend. She has an unopened letter from a genetic testing company; she says the result wouldn't change anything she'd actually do, the condition has no treatment and she isn't planning on kids, so opening it can only make her miserable if the news is bad. I told her she was making a textbook mistake, since Good's theorem shows a rational person should always be at least indifferent to free information and probably should want it. She told me I was turning a formal result into a self-help rule about curiosity, and that the theorem says nothing about whether you should open your mail.

Now I half-suspect she's right, but I can't say exactly where the theorem's authority runs out. So my actual question is: if Good's theorem does not entitle me to tell someone they should always take the free information, what does it entitle me to say, and when is avoiding information not a mistake? I'd also like to understand whether the theorem gives any bound on how much you should be willing to pay for a piece of information, or whether that takes extra assumptions too. And if you could answer in continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just paragraphs like an email, that would be ideal, since I plan to forward it to her.
