Two friends of mine spent most of a long drive arguing about whether historical “for want of a nail” stories are usually mistaken. I’ve read enough rationalist blogs to recognize some of the moves, but I couldn’t actually referee it, and I want someone to go through it point by point.

It started when Sana said that the standard examples—a lost order, a delayed ship, a random illness—are overrated, because there are almost always other nails: the same broad outcome would have happened through a different proximate trigger. She offered the usual example of the start of World War I: the assassination may have been the spark, but the alliance system, imperial competition, and mobilization schedules made a major European war extremely likely within a decade, so treating the assassination as a hinge is bad history. Jonah said that’s exactly the hindsight you’d expect from someone who already knows the outcome; before the event, the space of possible triggers is huge, and after the event, the one that fired looks uniquely overdetermined. Sana replied that unpredictability is just an epistemic problem—it tells us we didn’t know enough, not that the small event had large causal power. Jonah said no, in chaotic systems the epistemic and the causal blur together: if a tiny difference in initial conditions can produce a completely different trajectory, then small events really are load-bearing, and the “for want of a nail” story is the honest model. Sana came back that this proves too much—if every micro-event matters, then history is just noise and any explanation is as good as any other, which is absurd because we can obviously explain broad patterns like industrialization or demographic transitions. Jonah said that’s the weather/climate distinction: we can explain climate without being able to predict the weather, but the question “why did this particular war happen when it did” is exactly a weather question, and the lives are lived in the weather. They both then tried to pin down whether “broad outcomes” are even a stable object of explanation, and it went in circles.

I’m not asking for a verdict on who wins overall. I want an adjudication of the actual moves: which of Sana’s and Jonah’s claims are right, which are overreach, and whether there is a coherent position that keeps Sana’s point about structural explanations and Jonah’s point about small causes mattering for actual events without collapsing into one side. Is the epistemic/ontological distinction they kept flirting with doing real work here, or is it a red herring?
