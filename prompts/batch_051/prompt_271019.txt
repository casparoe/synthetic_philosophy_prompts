I am a novelist. Within the small circle of writers I argue with, the longest-running dispute is not about craft but about what a character's life—and, by extension, any life—goes well in. One camp holds a view I have come to call tranquilism: the only thing that ultimately matters is the absence of craving and suffering, and every other apparent good, from achievement to love, is either an instrument toward that absence or an illusion generated by the craving it seems to answer. The other camp holds that wanting is constitutive of both a worthwhile life and a tellable story: work, love, and even absorption in a novel are good partly because they are forms of directed, unsatisfied caring, and a life from which craving had been removed would not be a perfected life but an epilogue without stakes. Call them, for the purposes of the document, the tranquilist and the striver.

The two have asked me—since I earn my living arranging wants and stakes—to draft the document such a disagreement ought to have produced long ago: an adversarial collaboration written in their voices and committed to accuracy over victory. Write that document for me. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides would sign without hedging; (2) the exact remaining disagreements, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position as fair rather than slanted; (3) the cruxes on which the argument actually turns—I suspect one is whether craving is bad in itself or only in its frustration, a second is whether absorbed attention (the writer 'lost' in a draft) is a state of wanting or of wanting momentarily quieted, and a third is whether narrative without unsatisfied desire is a formal impossibility or only an unfamiliar craft problem; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. The document should let an uninformed reader see where the two genuinely disagree, where they have merely been talking past one another, and which empirical or literary facts would force at least one of them to change position.
