Earlier this year I was given a diagnosis of panic disorder after several months of recurrent panic attacks. A colleague, on hearing of this, pressed Thomas Szasz's The Myth of Mental Illness on me, and to my own surprise I found its central argument persuasive: disease in the strict sense requires an identifiable bodily lesion or established pathophysiology; no such lesion or mechanism has been demonstrated for conditions like mine; hence what is called panic disorder is not a disease but a name for a pattern of difficulties in living, and to call it a disease is a category mistake. I have therefore adopted a working policy. I no longer describe myself as suffering from a medical condition; I refuse to say anything is wrong with my brain until a lesion or mechanism has been demonstrated; and when deciding whether to take medication, enter therapy, or request accommodations at work, I treat the diagnosis as a contested redescription of my circumstances rather than a fact about my body. I notice, too, that the view flatters me—it returns a sense of agency at just the moment a diagnosis threatened to take it—and I know flattering views deserve extra scrutiny. Is this policy defensible, or is it a flattering error? In particular: is Szasz's criterion for what may count as a disease too restrictive, and does the answer to that settle what someone in my position ought to believe? Where is the policy likely to serve me, and where is it most likely to deceive me? Finally, is there a corrected version of it that keeps whatever is good in it without the distortions?
