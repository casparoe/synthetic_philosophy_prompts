I've been reading Daniel Dennett's essay "Quining Qualia" with my philosophy of mind reading group, and we found ourselves stuck on the Chase and Sanborn thought experiment. The way Dennett presents it, two coffee tasters both report that the flavor they used to enjoy now seems different, but they account for the change differently: one says his tastes have matured — his standards for judging coffee have shifted over the years — while the other says his standards are the same but his gustatory memory has changed, so he can no longer retrieve the original experience accurately. Dennett argues that introspection cannot distinguish between these two stories, and that this illustrates a deeper problem for qualia: if the supposed intrinsic, introspectively accessible properties of experience can be re-described so easily as shifts in judgment or memory, then the very idea of a determinate quale may be an incoherent posit.

Our group split in two. Some of us thought the example only shows that introspection is fallible about how we classify or explain our experiences, not that there is no such thing as the experience itself. Others thought it pushes further: it suggests there is no principled way to separate the experience from one's subsequent judgments and memories of it, so the qualia realist is left without a stable subject matter. I want an essay that reconstructs the strongest version of each side, engages the Chase and Sanborn case in detail, and then reaches a clear, justified verdict on this question: does Dennett's argument show that qualia are an incoherent posit, or does it miss its target? The essay should treat the case specifically as a test of whether the qualia realist can draw a principled line between an experience itself and one's later judgment or memory of it, rather than falling back on general defenses of consciousness.
