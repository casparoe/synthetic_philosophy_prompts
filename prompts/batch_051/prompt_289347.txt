I am trying to work out what epistemic norm governs assertion: under what conditions is a speaker entitled to assert a proposition flat out, as opposed to merely mentioning it as a possibility or hedging it. My working hypothesis is that one may assert that p only if one knows that p, but my verdicts on the cases below do not all point in the same direction. I want a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures those verdicts, and I want you to tell me where, if anywhere, I have to revise them.

The cases:

1. She glances at a reliable wall clock, sees that it reads 3:00, and tells a colleague, "It's 3:00." The clock is accurate. Verdict: plainly fine.

2. He has no idea what time it is, but on a whim says, "It's 3:00." It happens to be 3:00. Verdict: not fine.

3. She looks at a clock that stopped exactly twelve hours earlier; by coincidence, it displays the correct time. Reasonably assuming it is working, she says, "It's 3:00." It is 3:00. Verdict: not fine — her claim is true and her belief is reasonable, but something still goes wrong.

4. He checks a clock that reads 3:00, but a colleague has just told him that this clock has been running slow all week. He ignores the warning and says, "It's 3:00." The colleague was misinformed, and the clock is in fact correct. Verdict: not fine.

5. She checks a clock that stopped an hour earlier; reasonably trusting it, she says, "It's 3:00." It is actually 3:45. Verdict: not fine as an assertion, though she is far less blameworthy than the guesser in case 2.

6. She has read a forecast giving a 90 percent chance of rain and tells a friend, unhedged, "It will rain this afternoon." It rains. Verdict: I want to say this is fine — or at least much less objectionable than cases 2 through 5 — even though I doubt she knew it would rain.

The sixth case is the one I cannot reconcile with the knowledge rule. If I keep the knowledge rule, I must say the forecast assertion was improper. If I call it proper, I no longer see what is wrong in case 3, where the speaker's evidence was also strong and her belief true. Yet cases 3 and 6 do not feel alike to me. I have considered the familiar candidates — truth, justified belief, knowledge — and none seems to fit all six on its face: a truth norm that simply forbids asserting falsehoods would license cases 2 and 3; a justified-belief norm would license 3 and 5; a knowledge norm would condemn 6.

Please do the following. First, state one or more candidate norms of assertion precisely enough that a violation can be identified in each case. Second, run each candidate through all six cases and say where it matches my verdicts and where it fails. Third, tell me which of my verdicts the best candidate forces me to abandon, or how the candidate could be repaired to save them without becoming gerrymandered. Finally, say what your preferred principle predicts for a case I have not listed: an amateur birdwatcher identifies a distant bird by a brief, far-off glimpse, and is right.
