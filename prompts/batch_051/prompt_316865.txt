I have been thinking about the role of the architectural and painterly analogy in presentations of Leibniz's best of all possible worlds. In the Theodicy, in the course of defending the divine choice among possible worlds, Leibniz argues that God's action is best understood on the model of an architect choosing among plans. He adds that what looks to us like a local defect in the created world may be like a detail in a palace or a painting that seems ugly only because we are viewing it from the wrong position; from the right point of view it contributes to a whole whose excellence we otherwise could not perceive. This analogy has become the standard popular defense of the doctrine that this is the best of all possible worlds. I want a full audit of how far it actually holds. Provide the correspondence table: what in the analogy stands for the divine intellect, the set of possible plans, the act of choosing, the materials and structural constraints, the finished world, the limited human perspective, and the local evils. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks matter for the argument that local evils are justified by their place in the whole. In particular, say what the analogy silently imports about the planner's situation—for example, whether the architect must answer to anything outside the plan, whether there are costs borne by the sentient elements of the materials, and whether the spectator's judgment is ever allowed to overrule the architect's. The conclusion to assess is whether the analogy can genuinely support the move from God's existence to the acceptability of particular evils, or whether it merely restates that the whole is best in more vivid language. If it is the latter, what would a better analogy for Leibniz's position be, and what would it have to include in order not to beg the question?
