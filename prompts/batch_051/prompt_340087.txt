I'm leading a one-off reading group at a public library — mostly sharp people in their forties and fifties who don't have any formal philosophy background. The topic is Scheffler's afterlife conjecture. I dont need it explained to me; I need a plan for how to get a room of non-specialists to actually see it, since my worry is they'll come away with either "so we should just try not to die out as a species" or "isn't this just wanting to be remembered."

The conjecture as I'll frame it for them: a huge amount of what we value now depends on a background assumption that humanity will continue after our individual deaths, and that's why human extinction would be bad in a way that outruns the deaths themselves and the grief they cause. What has to be in place before I even introduce the thought experiment, so it doesn't get read as a question about self-interest? Then, between the asteroid that wipes everyone out thirty days after I die and the version where people simply stop being born after I die while everyone already alive lives out a normal span, which should carry the session, and why? I was going to build everything around an amateur astronomer who has spent years tracking a comet she knows she won't live to see return, with no children involved; tell me whether that example has a hidden defect, and what any carrying example must not have.

Then map the misconceptions in the order they'll show up, and where in the sequence to kill each one. My guesses: first, "this is just fear of death in nicer clothes"; second, "okay, so we should be longtermists trying to maximize the number of future humans"; third, "how can any of it be bad for me if I'm dead — and if it isn't bad for me, why are we calling it bad at all?" And what do I flatly refuse to get into at this level — the nonidentity problem, aggregation across populations, metaethics of value? Finally, give me one question to ask them at the very end whose correct answer can't be faked by someone who merely found the extinction scenarios upsetting.
