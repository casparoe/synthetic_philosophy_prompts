I run the giving side of a small family foundation — we make maybe a dozen grants a year, mostly $25k to $150k, in global health and education. I'm the only staff; the board is my three cousins and they mostly rubber-stamp whatever I bring them, so the real bottleneck is me. My background is operations, not philosophy, and I have about two weeks per decision. Whatever procedure you give me has to be explainable to my cousins in plain English, because I'll have to justify the no's to them.

Lately I've been reading about consequentialist cluelessness and it has kind of wrecked my confidence. The idea is that any intervention we fund has consequences that ripple out indefinitely — second-, third-, nth-order effects — and we cant estimate most of them; a few steps out the uncertainty swamps the calculation, so the advice to just maximize expected value quietly stops being operational. I find the argument convincing, but I still have to write checks. Going with my gut feels like a cop-out, and my gut gives zero guidance anyway when two proposals look good in different ways.

So: turn consequentialist cluelessness into a decision procedure I can actually run on a grant proposal. I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each before I move on, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the call to me or the board because it's a question of values rather than estimation. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on three proposals we have sitting here:

1. Free day-secondary schooling for girls in a region where enrollment drops sharply after primary school. The near-term case is strong; the long-term effects (fertility, labor markets, migration, politics) are exactly the kind of thing I can't trace.

2. A campaign for a small excise tax on sugary drinks in one US state, where the direct health effects are decently modeled but the precedent effects and industry response are not.

3. A pilot for a community-health-worker-plus-AI-triage tool in Kenya, where the tech is unproven, the near-term evidence is thin, and the founders are very good salespeople. I expect your procedure to like this one, honestly, and I think that would be wrong.
