I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist, and I came to philosophy late. Lately I have been working through the Church-Turing thesis, and I have noticed that several different claims travel under that name. The one I care about is the empirical claim: roughly, that any function on finite strings that can be computed by any physically realizable process, no matter how exotic, is computable by a Turing machine. I have come to treat that claim as about as secure as a broadly empirical claim can be. Not a theorem, and not something I expect to see fall; but I want to know what would count against it, on its own terms, before I go on treating it as settled.

Please map the exposure of the empirical Church-Turing thesis. First, keep the formal thesis clearly separated: the equivalence of the standard formalisms for computability is, as I understand it, a proven fact about those formalisms, and I do not want it attacked or defended as if it were an empirical finding. The target is the bridge claim that no finite physical process can outrun a Turing machine in the space of computable functions.

Then give me the defeaters, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. What would the world, or our best descriptions of the world, have to look like for the empirical claim to fail? I want cases I can think through without a laboratory: a device that answers the halting problem for an unbounded range of inputs without doing the search a Turing machine would have to do; a physical quantity that behaves as if it encodes an uncomputable real and is measurable to arbitrary precision; a spacetime setup in which one observer can, in finite proper time, witness the completion of an infinite computational process carried out along another worldline. For each, say whether it should move me, and whether a defender of my position would reasonably re-describe the case as smuggling in an uncomputable input, or as an idealization that no finite measurement could ever certify.

Finally, I want your honest assessment of whether the empirical version of the thesis has been insulated from refutation by the way the notion of effective computability gets precisified. If the claim becomes true by stipulation whenever we identify effective computability with Turing-computability, then it risks nothing; but if it is doing genuine work as a constraint on what physics can realize, it must risk something. Tell me which reading I am actually committed to if I treat it as an empirical claim, and whether a well-insulated claim of this sort is as much of a defect in philosophy as it would have been in my laboratory.
