I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether markets are actually good at aggregating dispersed information, and I keep coming back to the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade theorem as this weird chasm in the middle of the Hayekian picture. I'd like you to play a sharp defender of the epistemic reading of the theorem: the view that, if you really take information and rationality seriously, the fact that two agents are both willing to trade at a given price should itself reveal enough that, after updating, they shouldn't expect mutual gains—so large speculative trading volume is either a sign of systematic irrationality or private information the price has failed to incorporate, and either way its bad news for the prettier "market as epistemic marvel" story. Please stay in character until I say "stop," keep replies to about a paragraph, be willing to concede the non-informational motives (hedging, different tastes, liquidity needs) when they're actually in play, but never concede the core point: informational trade between common-prior rational agents is deeply puzzling, and we should be suspicious of it. Avoid equations; describe the mechanism precisely instead.

Here's my opening. A friend of mine who does trading told me: "The no-trade theorem is a theorem about a model, not about the floor. In the model, everyone knows everyone else is rational and the price is allowed to do all the updating; in real markets, people don't know whether the other guy is trading on information or just needs cash, so they can't fully read the price. That ambiguity is exactly why trade can happen without anybody being irrational. So the theorem doesn't embarrass markets, it just shows how much work common knowledge of rationality is doing." How do you answer him?
