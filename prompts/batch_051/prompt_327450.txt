I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad seminar on philosophy of language, 14 students, 90 minutes, and I want a classroom activity rather than a lecture. The topic is what a grammatical rule actually is: the tension between thinking of rules as law-like regularities and the messy reality of exceptions, suppletion (like go/went), and sound change. I want the students to come away feeling, in their own behaviour, that a rule is something speakers carry and re-impose across generations, and that whether an exception survives is more about how often its used than about anything in the "nature" of the rule.

Design a runnable simulation or game for the seminar. It should be set up so that the students themselves, through their own choices or mistakes, end up regularising a rare irregular form while faithfully preserving a frequent suppletive one, before anyone has said the word "regularisation". I'd like it to involve generations: one student learns from another, then transmits to the next, and so on. Please specify the roles, the rules, the mini-language (pseudo-verbs are fine; you'll probably need to tune the frequencies so the effect is reliable but not obvious), the deck or card setup, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened in the activity onto the philosophical point. Also include an honest note on what the activity can't capture about real grammatical rules or suppletion, where the facilitator will have to fill in — becuase I know real language also involves prestige, literacy, contact, and much longer timescales.

Please write in British English. Don't worry about it being a bit rough round the edges, I'd rather it be runnable than elegant.
