I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad seminar on rationality, sunk costs, and self-control — about 18 students, one 75-minute session — and I want them to viscerally get why people throw good money after bad instead of just reading about it. I'd love to run the dollar auction with them: you auction off a dollar (or a 1-euro coin, since we're in Spain) to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder also has to pay their final bid and gets nothing. It's a classic setup, but I'm not sure how to make it actually work in a classroom rather than turning into a silly bidding war everyone laughs off and immediately forgets. Could you design the activity for me? I need concrete rules, materials, a bidding procedure, and a payoff scheme that stings enough to produce real escalation but doesn't require me to actually collect huge sums from broke students (or maybe it does, up to some cap I announce in advance?). I don't want to lecture first; the shape of the trap should emerge from the game itself. Then include a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the structure of escalation of commitment — the point where staying in starts to feel rational as a way to avoid a guaranteed loss, the difference between what's already been bid and what you still stand to lose, and how two bidders can end up bidding past the value of the prize even while each one's move looks locally sensible. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (collusion, a player refusing to bid, people being too polite, someone bidding straight to the cap) and how to handle those failures on the spot. And be honest about which features of real-life escalation a classroom game can't reproduce and the debrief has to supply.
