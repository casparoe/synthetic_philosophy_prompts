I'm taking a seminar on medieval philosophy, and we've been reconstructing Anselm's ontological argument from the ground up. As I understand it, the argument runs like this. Take the idea of a being such that no greater being can be conceived. Even someone who denies that God exists grasps this idea, so the being exists in the understanding. But a being that exists both in the understanding and in reality is greater than a being that exists only in the understanding. So if the being such that no greater can be conceived existed only in the understanding, we could conceive something greater — namely, that very same being existing in reality as well — which contradicts the original idea. Therefore the being such that no greater can be conceived must exist in reality.

One of my classmates insists this reasoning is transparently broken, and he uses Gaunilo's famous parody to show it. He reconstructs a parallel argument: take the idea of an island such that no greater island can be conceived. The island exists in the understanding; an island existing in reality is greater than an island existing only in the understanding; so if the greatest conceivable island did not exist in reality, a greater island could be conceived. Therefore the greatest conceivable island exists in reality. Since, he says, no sane person believes that, Anselm's form of argument must be unsound — he calls the island example a way of "debugging" the proof, showing that it can manufacture the existence of anything you can hold in your mind.

I suspect the two arguments are less parallel than my classmate thinks, but I can't yet say exactly where substituting an island for God changes the logic rather than just the conclusion. I'd like a careful audit of the analogy. What element of Anselm's argument corresponds to what element of the island parody, and where does the mapping hold up? Where does it break — for instance, in the move from a being considered in itself to the greatest member of a kind, or in the idea that divine perfections admit of an intrinsic maximum while island-excellences do not? Does Anselm's own reply to Gaunilo survive the objection that a "greatest island" is just as thinkable as a "greatest being"? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the island parody still do the critical work my classmate wants it to do, or does it only refute a caricature of Anselm's reasoning? If it is a defective analogy for this purpose, what would a fairer test case for Anselm's argument-form look like?
