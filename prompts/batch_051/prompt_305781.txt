Two researchers I was interviewing for a feature on the philosophy of AI fell into an argument after a panel on advisory systems. One had presented a design for a model that forms and expresses moral and factual judgments about high-stakes decisions but is engineered so that it cannot act on those judgments: it has no actuators, no ability to initiate external processes, and its outputs are shown only to a human operator. Their dispute was whether such a system could count as a moral agent, or at least be blameworthy for a bad judgment.

(1) The presenter argued that it could not: agency requires the ability to make a difference in the world. A purely advisory system is closer to a scale than a moral agent; whatever it thinks matters only through the person who decides whether to follow it, and responsibility lands entirely on that person.

(2) The other researcher replied that agency lies in judgment, not causal reach. An adviser whose recommendations are always overruled is still answerable for them, and a juror whose verdict is overturned is still blamed for a corrupt verdict. We do not wait to see whether a judgment changed the world before we evaluate the person who made it. So a system that cannot act can still be the author of judgments for which it is answerable.

(3) The presenter pushed back: those humans have second-order capacities the system lacks. The adviser can misrepresent, recant, resign, or refuse to answer; the juror can deliberate in bad faith or choose leniency. The advisory system simply emits whatever its weights determine. That is not the exercise of choice but the output of a process, closer to a blood-pressure reading than a verdict.

(4) The other researcher countered that this raises the bar too high. Removing a human's capacity to act does not convert her into a measuring instrument; we hold coerced informants and tightly constrained officials responsible for what they say, though with mitigating considerations. The relevant test is whether the system's outputs are responsive to reasons — would a better moral argument change its judgment? — not whether it can lift a finger to carry that judgment out.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and note anything important that both researchers are missing.
