For a unit on truth and deception in my high school philosophy course, we have been looking at the ethics of lying, and Augustine came up as the extreme case: the view that lying is never permissible. To review, I read a short introductory article whose summary of him bothers me, though I cannot articulate exactly what is wrong. It said something like this: "For Augustine, a lie is simply a false statement: if you say something untrue, you have lied, regardless of what you happened to believe. All lies are equally sinful, because every departure from truth corrupts the soul in the same way, so a polite lie meant to spare someone's feelings is just as serious as a lie crafted to destroy an innocent person's reputation. He also held that you must never lie even to save a person's life, since the harm lying does to your own soul is worse than any earthly harm — which is why many philosophers treat his position as a reductio of itself: any ethics that forbids a small falsehood told to save a life from a murderer is plainly absurd."

I would like an accuracy audit of that summary, sentence by sentence, because I will have to explain the view and I do not want to repeat a distortion. Please sort the summary into what Augustine actually held, what is a simplification that blurs distinctions he cared about, and what is flatly wrong. I am particularly uncertain about three things: whether, for him, saying something false is enough to make a statement a lie, or whether the speaker's aim or state of mind is doing the real work; whether he actually treats all lies as equally serious, or distinguishes degrees of culpability while still holding that all of them are wrong; and whether the concluding inference — that the view refutes itself because it forbids lying in emergencies — follows from anything accurate in the summary. I am not looking for citations or a list of his works, just the conceptual structure of the position and where the popular version of it comes apart.
