I'm leading a ninety-minute session for a reading group of educated adults who have no background in political philosophy, and we're reading the section of Rousseau's Social Contract where he introduces the general will and then says, in Book I, that someone who refuses to obey the general will can be compelled by the whole body, and that this is what it means to be forced to be free. I don't want to explain the passage to them; I want a plan for how I should teach it. In particular, what idea has to be in place before that sentence doesn't sound like an Orwellian slogan? What single everyday case or running example could carry the distinction between a person's particular will and the general will, without dragging in the state or the law? What misconceptions should I expect this audience to form right at the moment I say forced to be free — probably something about the majority overriding the individual, or about being free to do whatever one's true self wants — and at what point in the sequence should I preempt each one? The session should build toward the sentence rather than opening with it, and I want the participants to understand why Rousseau thinks compulsion can be compatible with freedom here, even if they remain unconvinced. By the end, what should I ask them to see whether they can restate the problem the passage is answering, not just recoil from the phrase? Also flag what I should openly leave out at the beginner level.
