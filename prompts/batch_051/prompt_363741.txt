Me and my roommate have been arguing about Thomson's lamp ever since my philosophy of math class covered supertasks, and we've gotten ourselves stuck on where the argument actually goes wrong. We tried to tighten it into numbered steps so we could point at one of them:

(1) A lamp starts on. It is switched off at t=1, on at t=1.5, off at t=1.75, and so on, each switch halfway closer to t=2. No switch is scheduled at t=2 or after.
(2) At t=2 the lamp must be either on or off; those are the only two states a lamp can be in.
(3) If the lamp is on at t=2, then there was a time before t=2 at which it was switched on and after which it was not switched off.
(4) But every switch-on before t=2 is followed by a switch-off before t=2, and every switch-off is followed by a switch-on before t=2. So no switch before t=2 is the last switch.
(5) By (3) and (4), it can't be on at t=2.
(6) An exactly parallel argument, swapping "on" and "off", shows it can't be off at t=2.
(7) So it is neither on nor off at t=2, contradicting (2). Hence a lamp that toggles infinitely many times in a finite time is impossible.

I keep wanting to say step 3 is the trick, but I cant say why without just shrugging and saying supertasks are bizarre. My roommate says step 3 follows straight from the meaning of "on": if a lamp is on at a time, it got switched on at some earlier time and hasn't been switched off since. Is he right? Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, under exactly what reading, and what would the corrected version of that step say? If you think the argument is actually sound, say so and defend the step that people like me want to reject.
