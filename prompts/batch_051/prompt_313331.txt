Analyze the Donatist controversy of the fourth and early fifth centuries as a case study in baptismal theology, using the threefold contrast between baptism as mere symbol, as sign and seal, and as efficacious means of grace. Here are the salient facts for the analysis: in the aftermath of Diocletian's persecution, the North African church split over whether clergy who had handed over the scriptures under threat (the traditores) could validly administer baptism, and whether those baptized by such clergy needed rebaptism; Augustine argued that baptism's validity rests on Christ's action rather than on the worthiness of the minister, thereby foreshadowing the later doctrine that the sacrament works ex opere operato. Work through the dispute within that threefold framework: which view best rationalizes the Donatist position, which best rationalizes the Augustinian position, and where the framework breaks down or misdescribes what was actually at stake. Then reverse the direction of analysis: what does the controversy reveal about the adequacy of the threefold distinction itself — for instance, does it suggest that the live question was less what baptism does than who may administer it and what kind of community the church is? Aim for a disciplined two-way mapping that explicitly marks where the framework is silent rather than forcing the case into it.
