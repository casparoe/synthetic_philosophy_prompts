A friend and I argued late into the night about whether a distribution of holdings can be unjust if it arose entirely from just acquisitions and uncoerced transfers. I was defending Nozick's entitlement theory, and she kept returning to a version of the point that background institutions, not individual transactions, are the proper object of distributive justice; I never managed to pin her down, and I suspect my failures came from pressing the standard example without thinking through what her view really claimed. I want to practice the exchange against a stronger, more careful version of the view she was defending. Please play a sophisticated proponent of patterned principles of distributive justice: someone who holds that what matters, at the deepest level, is the pattern or structure of the resulting distribution, and that a just society arranges its basic institutions so that inequalities work as the pattern prescribes. Do not play a strawman. You are willing to concede that some patterns are unappealing, that constant fine-tuning is administratively and morally costly, and that the entitlement theory captures something true about the seriousness of holding people to their commitments. But you will not concede that a just distribution is simply whatever emerges from voluntary exchanges. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep your replies to a paragraph or so, so the exchange feels like a live argument rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move, stated as the challenge I should have given my friend. Suppose we begin from a distribution that satisfies your preferred pattern exactly, and no one disputes that the people involved acquired what they have legitimately. Then a million people each freely chooses to pay a small sum to watch a gifted athlete play, because each of them would rather have the performance than the coin. Every step in the chain is an uncoerced transfer. If you now say that the resulting distribution is unjust, you seem committed to forbidding or undoing exchanges that even you concede were free, and to treating the pattern, not people's choices, as the ultimate source of justice in holdings. If you say the result is just, you have conceded that a just starting point plus free exchange can move the distribution to any shape at all, which empties the pattern of its authority. Which horn do you take? And whatever you answer, why is your standard not just a preference that the world's distribution have a certain look?
