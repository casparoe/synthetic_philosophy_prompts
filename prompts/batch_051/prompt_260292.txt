I've been turning over a puzzle I use with my grad students and it keeps leading somewhere I don't want to go. Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If heads, Omega gives you $10,000 iff it predicted that you would have paid $100 had the coin come up tails. If tails, Omega just asks for the $100. The coin lands tails. Here's the argument that's bugging me:

(1) A rational choice is determined by the consequences it can cause in the actual world.
(2) In the actual tails world, paying $100 causes only the loss of $100; the coin already landed tails, so no $10,000 is coming either way.
(3) Therefore by (1), paying is irrational.
(4) But before the coin flip, if you could bind yourself to pay in the tails case, you should; that policy has expected value $4,950, while not paying has expected value $0.
(5) It can't be rational to bind yourself now to do something that will later be irrational on the very same reasoning.
(6) So (1) is false: rational choice isn't determined just by actual-world causal consequences.

That conclusion feels like it proves way too much: it seems to take all those counterfactual branches Omega predicted in as real actors. But I don't see a clean way out. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (1), but I want to know what I'm actually buying by keeping it.
