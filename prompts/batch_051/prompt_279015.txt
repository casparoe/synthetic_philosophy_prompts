Suppose a hypothetical common-law jurisdiction is considering replacing its existing approach to digital contracts of adhesion with a certification rule. Under the current approach, a consumer who clicks through to accept the terms is generally held to them whether or not she read or understood them, subject to back-end policing through unconscionability and the reasonable expectations doctrine. The proposal is as follows. For any standard-form term in an online consumer contract, the term is enforceable against the consumer if and only if one of two conditions is met: (1) the term merely restates a default rule of the jurisdiction, so that the consumer gives up nothing she would have had by law; or (2) at or before the moment of acceptance the provider, as to that term individually, (a) presented a plain-language summary of no more than three sentences, written at no higher than a sixth-grade reading level, stating specifically what right, remedy, or defence the consumer forfeits or what obligation she assumes relative to the default rule; (b) required the consumer to type a short distinctive code displayed on that screen into a separate field; and (c) retained a timestamped record of the summary shown, the code displayed, and the consumer's entry, the accuracy of which the provider must prove. Terms that satisfy neither condition are unenforceable but severable, and the remainder of the agreement remains intact. The rule covers arbitration clauses, class-action waivers, choice-of-law and forum-selection clauses, unilateral-modification terms, data-licensing and data-sharing terms, and any other departure from background law, but not terms that were individually negotiated or contracts between sophisticated firms.

Analyze how this rule would actually behave if adopted, attending to the following. What incentives would it create for providers in the drafting, sequencing, and visual design of terms, in pricing, and in the choice between standard-form and alternative contracting structures? What path would consumers realistically take — would the code requirement produce comprehension, ritual compliance, or annoyance-driven exit — and how would the burdens fall across different consumer populations? How would sophisticated parties game the mechanism, and where would the first serious gaming appear? What would happen to the shape of litigation: would disputes migrate from unconscionability toward fights over the sufficiency of summaries, the readability standard, and the integrity of records? Does the rule plausibly get closer to meaningful assent, or does it mostly add friction that firms price and route around? Finally, separate the failure modes that are fixable by amendment from those that seem intrinsic to any attempt to engineer comprehension into mass adhesion, and say whether the proposal is best understood as a consent rule or as a disclosure rule wearing the costume of consent.
