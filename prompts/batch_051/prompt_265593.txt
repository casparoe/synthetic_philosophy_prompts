Consider the following tightening of the pessimistic meta-induction:

(1) Scientific realism, as a thesis about theory choice, holds that the empirical success of a mature scientific theory is best explained by the approximate truth of its central claims and the referential success of its central terms, so that success licenses belief in such approximate truth.

(2) The history of science supplies a large and varied sample of mature theories that were empirically successful by the standards of their own day — among them the caloric theory of heat, the phlogiston theory of combustion, the luminiferous aether, and Ptolemaic astronomy — and that are now judged false in their central claims and non-referring in their central terms.

(3) Present-day successful theories are relevantly similar to those theories: they arise from and are tested by the same broad methods of theory construction, prediction, and revision, and they enjoy the same kind of empirical success.

(4) The sample described in (2) is representative of mature, empirically successful scientific theories generally.

(5) If a representative sample of mature, successful theories is largely false and non-referring, then induction warrants expecting that further mature, successful theories will likewise be largely false and non-referring.

(6) Therefore, induction warrants expecting present-day successful theories to be largely false and non-referring.

(7) Therefore, empirical success is at best a very weak guide to approximate truth, and realism is either false or rationally unmotivated.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, which premise should a realist reject, and what exactly should they replace it with? If it is invalid, identify the precise step at which the inference fails. I am especially interested in whether premise (4) can be established without already using present-day science as the standard of truth, and whether a realist can reject it while conceding every historical fact in (2).
