I am teaching a film-and-philosophy class and want to pair the Coen brothers' No Country for Old Men with the unit on whether quantum indeterminacy could help free will. The reading sets up the familiar standoff: one side says escape from determinism requires real chance in the world; the other says chance is the last thing free will wants, because a random event is not up to the agent, so indeterminism merely replaces causal necessity with luck. I want the class to read the film through that standoff, centered on Chigurh's coin tosses. Chigurh offers some victims a coin flip and treats the outcome as having taken the decision out of his hands, while other parts of the film invite a more fatalistic or predetermined reading. Does the film dramatize chance as a condition of agency or as the opposite of agency? Where does it slide between our not knowing how the coin will land and the landing's not being determined at all — and would a genuinely quantum randomizer, one with no hidden classical state, change Chigurh's moral situation, or make it worse? Is Chigurh's earlier decision to give the coin authority itself the only freedom the film leaves anyone, and if so, does the film have a better model of free will than either causal determinism or quantum chance? I want a committed reading rather than a survey: a definite statement of what the film, on its own terms, takes free will to be, and where its metaphor for quantum indeterminacy fails.
