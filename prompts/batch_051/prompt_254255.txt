There's this debate in anthropic reasoning that seems to have been completely stuck for like thirty years, and I want someone to diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. The two sides are the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). SSA tells you to reason as if you were randomly sampled from the observers in your reference class. SIA says no—you should treat your own existence as evidence for hypotheses on which observers are more numerous, because there'd be more "slots" for you to fill. The classic objection to SIA is the presumptuous philosopher: if two theories are exactly alike except that one says there are a trillion times more observers than the other, SIA seems to say you should be vastly more confident in the many-observers theory, even though you've learned nothing beyond the fact that you exist. That strikes most people as insane. But SSA has its own mess: the answer you get depends a lot on how you specify the reference class, and thats choice is often left to intuition, which can look like cheating.

My working theory is that this isn't a factual disagreement at all. The two camps might just be answering different questions: one about where I should expect to find myself among existing observers, and the other about how much my existence should surprise me under different hypotheses. Or maybe they're using "evidence" in different senses. Or maybe it's a bedrock-intuition thing where one side just refuses to let "I exist" count as an observation and the other side refuses not to, and no argument can reach it.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question down there, or a distinction that dissolves the fight? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a theorem, a new thought experiment, a different way of asking the question? Or should we just expect the stalemate to be permanent and stop being surprised by it?
