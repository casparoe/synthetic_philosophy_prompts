I've been stewing on an argument from our reading group, and it's starting to feel like it proves too much, but I can't find the bad step.

(1) Knowledge is worth having for its own sake — not just because it's useful.

(2) If knowledge is worth having for its own sake, then what's good about it has to be that it involves getting something true. Usefulness varies with context, but truth doesn't. So any true belief is good in itself, simply by being true. And if all that matters is truth, then what a proposition is about — whether it explains anything, whether it connects to anything else you know — can't affect how good it is to know it. The proposition that my town has 3,214 window panes is just as worth knowing in itself as the proposition that natural selection explains the fit between organisms and their environments.

(3) Curiosity is an intellectual virtue: a standing disposition to recognize and pursue what's good in it's domain. So if every true belief is good just by being true, then a curious person has some motivation to acquire each available truth. There's no truth that is, in principle, beneath her notice.

(4) But then a curious person has no basis in the content of the truths themselves for preferring one over another, since the source of their value is identical. Spending an afternoon verifying the window-pane count is as rationally fine as spending it trying to understand natural selection, and by the same reasoning we should be indifferent between writing a monograph and memorizing floor-tile patterns.

(5) That can't be right. A trait that leaves you with no more reason to understand natural selection than to count window panes isn't a virtue, it's a compulsion.

So one of those steps has to go, or the move between two of them is bad. I'd most like to keep (1) and the idea in (3) that curiosity is a real virtue, and (5) also seems obviously right to me. But then I don't see what I'm left to reject, and I want to know what each fix costs.
