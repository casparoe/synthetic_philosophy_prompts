You are a professional philosopher of science who works on causation and causal inference, and who has several times served as the neutral facilitator for adversarial collaborations; you are known for refusing formulations that either side would quietly qualify, and for checking each sentence of a joint document against the strongest version of each view. Before writing anything final, brainstorm off-paper: generate candidate shared claims, candidate differences, and candidate cruxes, then discard any that are vacuous, question-begging, or worded so that only one side would sign them as stated.

I am constructing the output of a year-long collaboration between two researchers in my group. One, the counterfactualist, holds that actual causation is ultimately counterfactual dependence: C caused E just in case E counterfactually depends on C in the right way, where the "right way" is spelled out with chains of dependence, quasi-dependence, or influence to handle the hard preemption and overdetermination cases. The other, the interventionist, holds that causal structure is what supports difference-making under surgical interventions: C caused E just in case an appropriate intervention that changes C would change E while holding fixed everything else that is independent of C, and that counterfactuals are themselves disciplined by causal structure rather than the other way around. They have spent most of the year circling the same hard cases — the Suzy–Billy rock-throwing case, backup assassins, trumping preemption, double prevention, omissions, and putative causes such as race or a volcano that we cannot literally manipulate — and they keep diagnosing each other's view as either circular or ad hoc. I want you to write the document they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: not a compromise blur, but a text each would put their name to, with the residual disagreement made exact. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation of specific theorists or works; the document should stand on the content of the views.

Write the document with exactly these sections in this order:

1. Scope and shared semantics (roughly 200–300 words). State the target phenomenon — token-level actual causation, including the contrast between actual causation and type-level causal relevance — and define the load-bearing terms (cause, counterfactual dependence, surgical intervention, holding fixed, preemption, overdetermination, omission) in wording both camps would sign. Flag any term whose definition is itself in dispute rather than finessing it.

2. Claims both camps sign (a numbered list of 10–14 claims). These should be substantive and jointly non-vacuous; include claims about where simple counterfactual dependence fails, what causal claims license in prediction and manipulation, and what any adequate account must save. Include at least one claim about omissions or double prevention, since a joint statement that skips them is useless.

3. The exact disagreement (a table). For each dispute, give: the issue; the counterfactualist's formulation, worded so the counterfactualist signs it; the interventionist's formulation, worded so the interventionist signs it; and a neutral one-sentence statement of the point of conflict. Cover at least the direction-of-analysis question (do interventions define counterfactuals, or the reverse?), the treatment of preemption, the charge of circularity against surgical intervention, and the treatment of unmanipulable causes.

4. Cruxes (3–5 sub-questions, each 2–4 sentences). For each: state the sub-question in a way both sides accept as decision-relevant, say which side would update and how if the sub-question were settled one way, and explain why the other side does not already regard it as settled.

5. Tests and predictions (3–5 cases or arguments). Choose concrete cases or formal considerations both sides would accept as probative — drawn from the hard cases above or of your own construction — and state, for each, the counterfactualist's prediction and the interventionist's prediction, in advance and in wording each side would accept. Flag any case where both sides actually make the same prediction and explain what that does or does not show.

6. Verbal or substantive? (roughly 150–250 words). Taboo the words "cause," "because," "intervention," and "counterfactual" as far as possible, and say which parts of their disagreement survive as substantive and which, if any, are disputes about which formalism gets to wear the honorific word "causation." If part of the dispute is verbal, say exactly which part and why.

Think through the strongest versions of both positions before writing, and prefer sharp, falsifiable formulations over calm agreement. If a claim would be signed only with a whispered reservation, write the reservation into the document or cut the claim.
