I'm drafting a paper on what happens when two or more people act together, and before I build an argument on top of it I want an audit of the position I have drifted into holding. I can't tell whether these six commitments form a stable package or a patchwork of pieces borrowed from views that pull in opposite directions. Here they are. (1) When we sing the duet or carry the sofa, there is an irreducible "we" in the content of my intention: my intending that we do it is not simply the conjunction of my intending to do my part and my believing that you will do yours. (2) But I am a compositional individualist about the machinery: everything that happens in joint action is realized entirely in the individual agents' psychological states and bodily movements; there is no group subject with its own mind, phenomenology, or executive control. (3) I take a shared intention to be fundamentally a matter of interlocking individual plans: each participant intends the joint activity partly because the other does, and each is prepared to adjust their subplans so that the joint activity remains possible. (4) At the same time, I think that when people genuinely act together — by agreeing to walk somewhere or by starting a project together — they acquire directed obligations to one another: each party has standing to rebuke the other for nonperformance, and neither can dissolve the undertaking merely by privately changing their mind. (5) Yet I also want to say that two people can share an intention to go for a walk without any promise or explicit agreement, and that neither thereby owes the other anything; either may simply stop without wronging the other. (6) Finally, I hold that only individual human beings have experiential states: there is nothing it is like to be the pair as a pair, even if each member has a distinctive experience of acting as part of it.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which are in genuine tension with which, how severe each tension is, and which apparent tensions dissolve once the key terms are disambiguated. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. Then identify the single revision that would do the most to leave the whole position defensible. In doing so, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on shared agency, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
