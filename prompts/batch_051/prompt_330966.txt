I'm a graduate student in philosophy and cognitive science, and I've been working through Newcomblike decision problems—especially counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail—for a seminar. I keep coming back to a distinction I've been using privately, but I don't trust it yet, and I'd like an expert's honest assessment.

The distinction is between **causal reach** and **predictive reach**. An action's causal reach is just the set of worlds it influences through ordinary physical consequences. Its predictive reach, as I've been calling it, is the set of worlds in which the action would be selected by the agent's overall policy, and in which some predictor or other agents condition on that policy. In counterfactual mugging, paying has almost no causal reach into the branch where Omega gives you $10,000, but it has enormous predictive reach. I've coined the term **veil-of-branch reasoning** for a stronger principle built on this distinction: an agent should evaluate entire observation-to-action policies from the standpoint before it knows which observation it has received, rather than evaluating an option by its expected payoff conditional on the observation that made that option locally available. My slogan is that in these problems, the causal branch is not more real for the purposes of deciding than the merely predicted branches.

The part I'm genuinely uncertain about is my broader claim: I think ordinary sequential choice is full of low-stakes counterfactual muggings. For example, a manager who would grant extra leave only after hearing a vivid personal story has a policy whose predictive reach shapes what employees choose to narrate. The actual lenient decision looks causally harmless, but the policy has costs across possible histories. My friend says this is just expected utility over policies with extra mysticism, and that it dissolves once you distinguish ex ante from ex post perspectives. But I suspect the problem is deeper: actual observations are not the kind of thing that should privilege a policy over its counterfactual alternatives, because every observation is itself the result of an earlier interaction between your decision procedure and the world.

Then tell me your bottom line: whether this is a real distinction and principle, whether it has a name already in the decision-theory literature, and whether the ordinary-choice generalization is true or just nice. Give me the three strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Also tell me which consideration or argument would most likely make you reverse your bottom line, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
