Me and my friend just spent an hour arguing about whether state apologies for historical wrongs are anything more than PR. He pointed at Japan and the comfort women, the US and slavery, Canada and residential schools, and said it's all cheap talk by people who didn't do the thing, to people who didn't suffer it. I wanted to defend the practice but realized I couldn't actually say what the practice is committed to. So here's my question: what does the practice of an official state apology presuppose philosophically? It seems to treat the state as a single thing that persists across centuries, that can inherit blame or obligations, and that can apologize through officials who weren't there. But the same states are really careful not to treat the apology as admitting legal liability, and we don't usually think some random citizen today is personally guilty just because a distant ancestor did something. Is the practice presupposing a theory of collective agency or group responsibility, or is it compatible with several different views if you don't push too hard? And is it internally consistent given the liability thing? Then: if someone insisted that only living individuals can owe or be owed, what exactly would the practice of state apology have to change or give up? Keep it under 500 words.
