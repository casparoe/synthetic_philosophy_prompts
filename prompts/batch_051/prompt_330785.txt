I keep being pulled toward the Aumann agreement theorem as a working epistemology because of the explanatory discipline it imposes. The setup is seductively clean: two Bayesians with a common prior update on private information, and if their posterior probabilities for a given event become common knowledge, those posteriors cannot differ; they cannot agree to disagree. That turns the fuzzy fact of persistent disagreement into a precise diagnosis. If two apparently reasonable people keep diverging after candid discussion, at least one of them is not conditioning as a Bayesian, is not fully revealing what they know, or they never actually shared a common prior. I like this because it gives me a decision procedure: stop treating the fact that reasonable people can disagree as a terminal answer and start looking for the hidden information, the processing error, or the prior divergence.

But I also have an intuition that refuses to dissolve: two scrupulously honest, intelligent people can sit together, share everything they regard as relevant, and still rationally part company on how strongly a messy body of evidence supports a conclusion. This is not a case of one holding back a private signal; it feels as though the evidence itself is ambiguous in a way that permits different but reasonable weights. I find myself wanting to say that one can treat the same studies, the same historical record, or the same dataset as more or less diagnostic without either party's being irrational. The theorem seems to say that this residual divergence must be smuggled in somewhere, and I do not know whether to accept that or resist it.

Walk me through what accepting it would cost. Is giving up the intuition just a matter of locating the divergence in priors, and if so, why does that feel like an evasion rather than an explanation? What would it cost to instead reject the common-prior assumption as the step doing all the work, and concede that rationality simply does not require convergence? And what would it cost to keep the theorem but restrict it to the narrow case of a sharp event with literal common knowledge of posteriors, treating ordinary disagreement as outside its scope? I want the strongest version of each move, and then a negotiated verdict: which one should I actually endorse, and what would justify that choice? In particular, is the claim that the theorem applies only to an idealization we never meet a principled restriction of scope, or is it a way of immunizing the theorem against any possible conflict with ordinary disagreement?
