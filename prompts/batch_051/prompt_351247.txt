I got dragged into an argument last weekend with my roommate, who is taking a philosophy of language course, about Sherlock Holmes. She kept saying that the name Sherlock Holmes is empty, like Pegasus, so a sentence like 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' is either false or just lacks a truth value. But I notice we say things all the time that seem obviously fine: 'Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any actual detective,' 'Holmes doesn't exist,' and 'In the stories, Holmes lives on Baker Street and is a detective.' Those all sound right, but it's not obvious how they can all be right at once while the name is supposed to be empty.

So here's what I want. Write an essay using Holmes as the running example that weighs at least these three positions: (i) the name is empty and the apparently true sentences are handled by some story-operator or pragmatic trick, (ii) fictional characters like Holmes are abstract artifacts, so the name does refer but not to a person, and (iii) talk about fiction is a kind of make-believe or pretense whwere truth claims are relative to a game. If there's another camp you prefer, like the Meinongian view that Holmes is a nonexistent object, you can bring it in. But don't just survey them: you have to defend one. Your view should say something specific and non-hand-wavy about each of these four sentences: 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective,' 'In the stories, Sherlock Holmes is a detective,' 'Sherlock Holmes does not exist,' and 'Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any actual detective.' I care more about the first two questions than anything else: does the name refer, and what exactly makes the sentence about the stories true? Take a definite position and justify it, including what you're willing to bite the bullet on. End with a short note on the strongest remaining objection and why it isn't fatal to your view.
