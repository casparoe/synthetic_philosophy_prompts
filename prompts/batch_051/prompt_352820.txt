I am a PhD student working on formal approaches to animal welfare, and I keep returning to what is usually called the scale argument for taking insect suffering seriously. In its standard presentation, the argument is an expected-value calculation: even if the probability p that an individual insect is sentient is low, say on the order of one percent or less, the number of insects is so vast, on the order of ten to the eighteenth and possibly larger, that the expected number of sentient individuals, n times p, is enormous, and the expected moral stakes of practices such as insect farming or pesticide use are therefore enormous. The reasoning is normally run as though those n individuals were n independent draws from a sentience lottery: each gets its own small chance of mattering, and the chances add up across the population.

The variation I want to work through concerns exactly that independence assumption. Suppose the real structure of the uncertainty is instead the following. There is a single shared unknown C, roughly the proposition that the kind of nervous-system architecture characteristic of insects, or of some large taxon of insects, is the sort of process that can realize pain, or the proposition that some particular theory of consciousness on which insects qualify is true. Conditional on C, sentience for all or nearly all members of the taxon is near-certain; conditional on not-C, none of them are sentient. Formally, instead of independent Bernoulli draws with parameter p for each individual, we have a mixture: with probability q the shared condition C holds and the per-individual probability jumps to something close to one, while with probability one minus q the condition fails and the per-individual probability is near zero. If q is chosen so that the unconditional per-individual probability is still p, then by linearity of expectation the expected number of sentient insects is exactly the same as in the independent model, namely n times p. The headline number does not move.

What I want to understand is whether the argument itself survives this change. In the correlated model the uncertainty is not pooled across many independent risks; it is a single all-or-nothing bet on C whose payoff is merely multiplied by n. That looks structurally like a Pascal's-mugging, or at least like a gamble with enormous variance rather than a near-certain aggregate. I would like a careful treatment of the following. First, spell out precisely where the two models diverge in their implications, given that they agree on the expected sentient count; relevant candidates include the variance and higher moments, value of information, risk attitudes, diminishing marginal value, and moral uncertainty about the badness of insect pain itself. Second, work through whether the practical conclusion usually drawn from the scale argument, that insect welfare should be a major priority, still follows under the correlated model, and under which decision rules it does or does not. Third, consider a partially correlated model in which a common latent component explains only part of the per-individual uncertainty and there is residual independent noise: at what degree of residual independence does the original pooling intuition recover, and does the correlated version instead favor investing in resolving C through neurobiological or behavioral work before spending resources on directly reducing harms?
