After a long argument with a friend about whether driving is actually worth it given the micromort math, I went back to Bentham’s An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, chapter IV, where he tries to decompose the value of a pleasure or pain into separate dimensions: intensity, duration, certainty or uncertainty, propinquity or remotenes, fecundity, purity, and extent. My friend kept saying modern risk analysis is just Bentham’s felicific calculus with better empirical inputs, and I can’t decide whether he’s right or whether he’s flattening something important. The part I keep chewing on is certainty versus propinquity. In Bentham these are distinct: a pain can be certain but remote, or near but uncertain. But in everyday driving risk, a small annual risk spread over decades is a different animal than a one-off one-off risk with the same expected value, even if both are equally certain in the aggregate. Does Bentham’s list already capture that difference, or does the modern micromort/QALY framework mostly elide propinquity by discounting time? I’m also not sure the parallel survives the shift from Bentham’s individual act-by-act calculation to population-level risk thresholds. Is this a deep structural identity between utilitarianism and contemporary risk analysis, or a loose analogy that breaks once you look at how each treats certainty, time, and the unit of analysis? Where does the mapping hold and where does it come apart?
