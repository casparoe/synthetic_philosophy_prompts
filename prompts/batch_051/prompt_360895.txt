Working on a novel about a failed nineteenth-century reform movement has left me with a small theory I would like tested rather than politely handled. I have come to think the quarrel between idealists and realists in political life is not best framed as a disagreement about how much hope one should have, but about the grammatical mood of that hope. Call hope plenary when it treats the desired future as already authoritative over the present, so that every setback is read as a delay, every surprise as something the plan had not yet accounted for, and the task is always to make the present conform to the ending; call it provisional when the future functions as a revisable working hypothesis, a direction one drafts toward while editing, thinning, or abandoning it as the present answers back. My claim is that what is wrong with hoping too much is not the quantity or ambition of the hope but this plenary structure, and that realists go astray when they conclude they must hope less rather than hope provisionally. As a novelist I feel the difference technically: a scene is earned when the next sentence follows from present conditions, and it is coerced when the ending writes it backward. Is this distinction coherent, has it already been drawn under another name, and where does it actually break? Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: first, the strongest reconstruction of the distinction; second, the nearest existing idea I have likely reinvented; third, the most serious objection; fourth, the best reply available to me on the evidence of what I wrote; and fifth, your verdict on whether the distinction is worth keeping and on what terms.
