I run the intake unit of a city housing agency that takes complaints from tenants — no heat, leaks, mold, vermin, illegal lockouts — and decides which ones get an inspector sent out. Our current rule is that a complaint goes nowhere unless it arrives with something beyond the tenant's word: photographs, a second tenant's report, or a record of past violations at the address. Lately I've been reading, on my own, about an old Indian school of philosophy called the Mimamsa school, which argued, from what I can tell, that a report should be treated as valid on its face — believed until something specific shows it to be wrong — rather than needing outside confirmation before it gets any credit. Their larger claim was that the Vedas are reliable because no human author could have put errors into them, and this was tied to a general view that the burden of proof sits with the person trying to show a report is false, not with the person trying to show it is true. I keep turning this over because our current screening rule seems to punish precisely the tenants who can't gather proof.

I would like you to turn that principle into a decision procedure my intake workers can actually run on a single uncorroborated complaint. Give me the questions to ask in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "send an inspector" or "shelve it," and where it must hand the decision to me because the question has become one of judgment rather than rule-following. The staff won't know the philosophy, so keep the procedure in plain language. Each worker handles roughly forty calls a day, so it has to be usable in a few minutes per call, and I will review anything the procedure marks uncertain. Then run the procedure on three complaints: a tenant who says the heat has been off for three days and the landlord has stopped answering the phone; a tenant who says there is black mold behind a bathroom wall she cannot open, based on a smell and a dark stain spreading under the paint; and an anonymous caller who says a landlord has walled off a fire escape in a building we have never cited before. I want an honest account of where the procedure gives an answer we will not like, where it cannot settle the question at all, and what it systematically gets wrong.
