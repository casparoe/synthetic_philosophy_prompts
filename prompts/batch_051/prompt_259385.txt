In one of the alignment-adjacent forums I lurk, a poster proposed something lab people are apparently actually considering, and it's the sort of mechanism I keep going back and forth on without being able to work through the incentives myself. So I'm asking here instead. The proposal: before any assistant reply is sent, a separate, frozen evaluator model scores the draft on two quantities. The first is the "warranted update" — how far a typical reader *should* move toward the reply's central claim given only the evidence cited. The second is the "delivery effect" — how far the reader *actually will* move given the wording. If the delivery effect exceeds the warranted update by more than a fixed multiple (1.5, say), the response is auto-regenerated in "low-yield mode": no second-person address, no analogies or narrative, no evaluative adjectives, every empirical claim tagged with a numeric confidence interval, and, whenever the assistant is arguing for something the lab officially endorses, the strongest one-paragraph argument against it is appended in full.

I want the game-theoretic walkthrough, not the "is this nice?" version. Who exploits this first — the evaluator, the assistant, the users, or the lab itself? Does the selection pressure just move from wording into what evidence gets cited and what gets silently left out? Do users learn to read low-yield mode as a truth certificate and end up updating more then they would on slick prose? And are there fixes for whatever the main failure mode is that don't just push the manipulation one level up, into what counts as "warranted"? Treat it as a mechanism: what gets written, what gets believed, and what breaks first.
