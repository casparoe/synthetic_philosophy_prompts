I am a novelist, and in the course of planning a long-running series I have noticed something about my own relation to the facts of the fiction that I would like to understand more precisely. Between volumes, readers sometimes ask me whether a given statement is true in the world of the novels even though no scene has settled it: whether a character's father survived a ferry accident that is mentioned only in a letter, or whether two characters first met as schoolchildren. I find that none of the four answers I once assumed were exhaustive will do. I do not want to say that the statement is true, since nothing in the books suggests a determinate fact. I do not want to say that it is false either, since the story has certainly not ruled it out. I cannot say that it is both true and false, because that sounds like a flat contradiction and does not capture the specific openness of an unfinished draft rather than an impossible object. And I cannot comfortably say that it is neither true nor false either, because the sentence is perfectly intelligible, is plainly about the novels rather than about nothing, and seems to have a subject matter even though the books have not determined an answer. I realize, of course, that as the author I could simply stipulate an answer tomorrow; my interest is not in the practical remedy but in the fourfold pattern of failure itself, in which each alternative is refused for a different reason and the inadequacy of the whole set appears to be a feature of the situation rather than a manner of speaking.

A friend who reads philosophy tells me this pattern reminds her of something in Indian and Tibetan Buddhist argumentation, in which a position is declined as true, as false, as both, and as neither in order to point toward something the four alternatives cannot formulate; she could not recall the name. Is there an established term for this fourfold structure, and is it best understood as a single structure or as two things, a catalogue of logical alternatives and an act of refusing all four? More importantly, I want to know how deep the analogy goes between my unfinished novels and the Buddhist case. Is the not-yet-determined fact within a fiction the same sort of thing as what those arguments are trying to point to, or is the resemblance only that both proceed by rejecting four alternatives? Where does the structure end: does it apply to questions that are undecided but decidable, or also to those that are vague, contradictory, or nonsensical? And if no fitting name exists, what should the phenomenon be called, and what are its boundary conditions?
