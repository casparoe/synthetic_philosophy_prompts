Two members of my research group spent an afternoon arguing about whether Fearon's rationalist explanations for war can be transferred to AI development races, and I came away unable to referee them. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

(1) Elena began by saying the transfer should be straightforward. If we treat an AI race as a kind of war, then Fearon's starting puzzle applies. A race is costly (in compute, talent, and safety corners cut), so there should exist some bargain both sides would prefer to racing: a joint lab, a share of the eventual returns, a verifiable slowdown. When rational actors race anyway, the explanation must be one of the three standard mechanisms: private information with incentives to misrepresent, a commitment problem, or indivisible stakes.

(2) Marcus replied that the analogy fails at the first step. Fearon's puzzle presupposes that war is costly ex post and that these costs open up a range of settlements both sides prefer to fighting. But an AI race may be winner-take-all: if the first actor to a decisive system captures a durable and overwhelming advantage, the loser is left with nothing, and there is no division of the prize both would accept in place of winning. Winning then dominates any bargain for each side, and no puzzle remains for the framework to explain.

(3) Elena answered that winner-take-all is not a failure of the transfer; it is simply the indivisibility mechanism, which Fearon already lists. And indivisible prizes can normally be made divisible through side payments (equity in the leading lab, guaranteed access to the resulting systems, transfers of compute or a share of revenue). If the prize is very large, the surplus available to construct a bargain is very large too, so the bargaining range should reappear.

(4) Marcus conceded the point but said side payments restore a bargaining range only if the resulting agreement is enforceable, and enforceability is precisely what fails in an AI race. Capabilities are easy to hide and hard to verify from outside: no third party can observe what a lab has trained, what its weights contain, or how close it is to a decisive system, so an agreement to slow down or share cannot be monitored. He called this the commitment mechanism, but with a difference that matters. In nuclear arms control there was at least a monitoring technology; here the object of the agreement is close to unmonitorable. The parameters of the framework therefore shift far enough that its substantive prediction reverses: war is meant to be a rare exception to a bargaining norm, but AI races would be the norm.

(5) Elena responded that a shifted parameter is still a transfer. If commitment and information problems are severe, Fearon's framework predicts exactly that races will be common. The framework has not failed; it has located the difficulty. A genuine failure would be a race that is rational with no private information, no commitment problem, and no indivisibility, and Marcus has not produced one.

(6) Marcus said he could produce one. The costs that create Fearon's bargaining range are realised more or less symmetrically by both sides of a war, but in an AI race the costs and returns are skewed: the leading lab treats the race as cheap because it expects to win, while the trailing lab treats a long-shot bid as worth making for its option value, so the two sides are not evaluating the same gamble. That structure, he said, is closer to an all-pay auction than to a war, and it can make a race rational without any of the three mechanisms.

At that point the discussion broke up, and no one settled it. Please adjudicate the argument move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or mistaken, and explain why. Then say which of the two is more right overall, and what both parties are missing.
