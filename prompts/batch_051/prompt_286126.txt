You are a philosopher of cognitive science with a working knowledge of the animal-learning and comparative-cognition literatures. I am a PhD student in comparative cognition, and my lab works with a species that solves novel mechanical puzzles at above-chance rates the first time it sees them. A collaborator wants to say these animals are genuine Popperian creatures in the sense of the familiar Darwinian–Skinnerian–Popperian–Gregorian ladder: they evaluate candidate actions against an internal model of the task and discard bad options without acting them out. I suspect the same behavior could be produced by a well-generalizing Skinnerian creature, one whose actions were shaped by a long reinforcement history plus stimulus generalization, with no inner model at all. I want to know whether this dispute is empirically decidable, and what the strongest test would look like.

Before settling on a design, think through at least two or three candidate experiments, such as: (a) a first-trial choice among never-seen options where the wrong option is irreversible and ends the task, so there is no opportunity to learn from an error; (b) pure observation of the puzzle without any opportunity to act before the test; (c) rapid transfer to a structurally analogous but perceptually dissimilar puzzle that no amount of stimulus generalization from the training items should easily cover. Then recommend the one design that best separates the Popperian from the Skinnerian reading. For that design, specify exactly which result pattern would favor the Popperian reading and which would favor the Skinnerian reading. Finally, take the strongest result on the Popperian side you can imagine, and be precise about what it would and would not establish. Would it show the animal has an internal model, or only that it behaves as if it had one? And is the residual gap a practical limitation of the experiment, or an indeterminacy built into the notion of a Popperian creature itself?
