I'm not any kind of academic, just someone who reads around philosophy online, so I might be mangling the Williams. I keep thinking about the ending of The Last of Us (the game, or the HBO show, they mostly land the same beat) against this idea from Williams about integrity and the one thought too many. The way I understand it, Williams says that if a man stands there deciding whether he's really allowed to save his wife instead of a stranger, the fact that he had to ask is itself a kind of moral failure. A persons deep commitments, the stuff that actually makes their life go, aren't just items to be weighed from a neutral point of view, and a morality that always wants you to calculate is attacking your integrity.

Now Joel. He doesn't stop to weigh Ellie against the chance of a cure, he just kills everyone in the hospital and lies to her about it, and on one read he looks exactly like the person who never has the one thought too many. But the story also gives the Fireflies a genuinely strong utilitarian case (a vaccine, and the sense that Ellie would probably have said yes), and then it doesn't exactly show Joel as clean: he lies, and he can't tell Ellie the truth because he knows what he took from her. So I want a reading that takes both sides seriously and is willing to say that the story maybe shows the ugly underside of Williams's idea, not just a vindication of it. Does the ending support his objection to stepping back and calculating, complicate it, or end up showing that avoiding that extra thought has its own moral cost? If you were setting a discussion prompt for a movie night on this, what exactly would you ask people to look at?
