I'm rereading the "Of Property" chapter of Locke's Second Treatise for a reading group, and two passages a couple of sections apart are nagging at me. In section 27 Locke gives what everyone takes as his core labour-mixing argument: "Though the earth, and all inferior creatures, be common to all men, yet every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself. The labour of his body, and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his. Whatsoever then he removes out of the state that nature hath provided, and left it in, he hath mixed his labour with, and joined to it something that is his own, and thereby makes it his property." The engine there is that the labour is *his* — property in the person.

Then in section 28, illustrating that appropriation needs no one else's consent, he writes: "Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them." And I keep tripping over the servant: the turf wasn't cut by the master at all. Locke says the master acquires it through "the labour that was mine" — which means either the servant's labour is already the master's property, or the servant is being treated as an instrument like the horse, or Locke is using "his own" in a way that quietly includes labour he has a right to command.

Can these two passages be reconciled without quietly switching theories? Is the honest reading that Locke's real principle isn't mixing one's own body's labour into things but a labour-entitlement thesis where title tracks whoever owns or commands the labour input, and the "mixing" language in section 27 is ornamental? Or is the servant case a bit of special pleading where Locke just grouped the servant with the grass and the ore? Which of those readings is honest and which is a hand-wave, and how would you draw the line between a fair reconciliation (same term, two senses, a distinction Locke never makes explicit) and changing the subject? And does the proviso ("at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others") do the actual normative work while the labour talk is doing the rhetorical work? I can't tell if thats a problem with his argument or with how I'm carving up "labour."
