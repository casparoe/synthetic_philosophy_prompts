Since retiring from experimental physics, I have been working through the philosophical literature on probability, and I keep returning to a programmatic remark by Pierre-Simon Laplace in *A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities*. He writes:

"Strictly speaking it may even be said that nearly all our knowledge is problematical; and in the small number of things which we are able to know with certainty, even in the mathematical sciences themselves, the principal means for ascertaining truth—induction and analogy—are based on probabilities; so that the entire system of human knowledge is connected with the theory set forth in this essay."

I think Laplace is right that induction and analogy are probabilistic inferences, but he does not say under what conditions distinct induction- or analogy-based arguments may be compounded. I have been trying to supply that missing rule and have arrived at a principle I call "default decomposition of evidentiary channels," or simply "default independence." It says: if two lines of evidence bear on the same proposition and were produced through causal routes that share no apparent connection, then, absent positive reason to suspect a common source of error or shared prior commitment, we are entitled to treat them as conditionally independent given the proposition and to combine their evidential force by multiplication. For example, if one laboratory reports that a soil sample is enriched in a particular element and a quite different geological method yields an independent estimate of the same depositional age, I take the two supports to multiply. If, by contrast, both laboratories calibrated their instruments against the same standard, the presumption lapses.

The principle is meant as a formalized default: it does not claim the evidentiary routes really are independent, only that the burden of proof falls on the person who would block multiplication. I realize this may smuggle in a kind of epistemic atomism, and it may collapse into something like exchangeability or de Finetti’s representation theorem, or into the condition that our evidence structures are conditionally independent in the absence of known coupling. My question is whether the principle, so stated, is coherent, whether it already exists under another name, and where it breaks. In particular, I would like to know what kinds of hidden common causes are most corrosive to it and whether a "no apparent connection" condition can carry any epistemological weight at all.
