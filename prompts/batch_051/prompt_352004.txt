I’m an undergraduate in a seminar on the ethics of war and intervention, and our professor refuses to state a general theory of humanitarian intervention. Instead, he insists that we triangulate his view from his verdicts on concrete cases. So far he has said the following. On Kosovo in 1999, he judged NATO’s intervention justified despite lacking Security Council authorization, because it was a late-stage response to ongoing ethnic cleansing after years of diplomacy had failed, though he objected to the later expansion of the bombing campaign to targets with little direct connection to protecting civilians. On Iraq in 2003, he judged the invasion unjustified, emphasizing the absence of an imminent humanitarian catastrophe, the weakness of the preventive-war case, and the coalition’s failure to plan seriously for post-invasion order. On Libya in 2011, he initially accepted the intervention as a civilian-protection operation, but later argued that the coalition went wrong when the mission drifted into regime change, and he has admitted that Libya is the case he is least comfortable integrating with his Kosovo and Iraq judgments. On Syria in 2013, he opposed the proposed punitive strikes after the Ghouta chemical attack, saying they would not have protected civilians and were disconnected from any workable strategy for ending the war.

Next week we are discussing a hypothetical country, Valdoria. The government there is engaged in ongoing massacres of a minority group, and the Security Council is deadlocked because of a permanent member’s veto threat. A regional coalition, including several NATO states, proposes military intervention without Council authorization. Its initial aim would be to stop the killings and protect civilians, but coalition leaders have repeatedly said that the dictator must go, and their post-conflict reconstruction plan is vague. Diplomacy has been attempted, and the killings have accelerated. I want to know what verdict my professor should give about Valdoria in order to remain most consistent with his earlier case judgments. Please identify the candidate principles that fit his record, say which earlier case is the closest analog, state what each candidate principle would predict for Valdoria, and say what commitment he would be taking on by choosing one rather than another. If his record genuinely underdetermines the answer, please be honest about what distinguishes the rival ways of closing it. Answer as an expert philosopher working on just war theory and the ethics of humanitarian intervention, and begin by brainstorming candidate principles before settling on a recommendation. Please write in continuous prose, as one would write a careful email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
