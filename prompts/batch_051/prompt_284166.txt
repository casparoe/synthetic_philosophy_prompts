I hold two convictions that I cannot fit together. The first is a firm deontological intuition: I may not kill one innocent person to save five, and a surgeon may not carve up one healthy patient even when five patients will die without those organs. The second is a theoretical demand: a moral constraint is not intelligible to me unless it is answerable to some good it protects or produces. But the obvious way to meet that demand — killings are bad, and a general prohibition keeps the world from containing more of that bad — seems to generate the paradox of deontology: the rationale that justifies the prohibition also favors violating it whenever the violation prevents more killings of the same kind, and the side-constraint collapses into a maximizing rule. I do not want to surrender either the intuition or the demand for a rationale. Tell me which should yield, and what each move commits me to. In particular: can the intuition be debunked as an attachment to my own causal purity? Can the constraint stand without any good-producing rationale, and at what cost to its intelligibility? Must I accept an irreducibly agent-relative rationale, and is that a stable stopping point or a relabeling of the problem? Could a rule-consequentialist rationale — justifying the standing prohibition, not its case-by-case violation — escape the paradox, or does it merely relocate it to the question of why I should follow the rule rather than its rationale when they come apart? Is there any coherent view on which the constraint binds even in the exceptional case in which violating it would bring it about that others violate it less?
