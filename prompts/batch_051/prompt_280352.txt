I am a novelist, and I am writing a book built around a public inquiry into a fictional military intervention. A retired diplomat testifies before a commission deciding whether to support an intervention in a neighbouring country to stop an unfolding massacre of a minority population, and whether, once the killing stops, the intervening coalition should press on to remove the regime responsible. The Security Council is deadlocked; the intervention would likely halt the killing but risks civilian casualties and a prolonged occupation. In the scene everything turns on, the diplomat ends her testimony with the line "Never again," and she intends it to settle both questions in favour of intervention and regime change.

I want the opposing counsel to interrogate this line seriously rather than mock it, so I need to know precisely what it might be claiming. Could you lay out, as a menu of distinct readings, the different claims "never again" can express in this setting — claims about memory, about resolve, about a historical lesson, about an unconditional duty to prevent mass atrocity, about a defeasible reason that can be outweighed, and the like — from the platitudinous to the radical? For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or implausible or false, and whether that reading, taken on its own, actually supports a duty or permission to intervene in the fictional case. I am especially interested in where the readings come apart: for instance, whether a reading strong enough to require stopping the massacre also requires removing the regime, or whether the slogan's force runs out once the immediate killing stops.

Then tell me which reading my diplomat most likely needs for her argument to work, and where that reading is weakest. And please mark where your own reasoning could fail: for each verdict, say which further argument or consideration would change your mind about a reading's plausibility or about whether it supports the conclusion, and where your judgment is most uncertain.
