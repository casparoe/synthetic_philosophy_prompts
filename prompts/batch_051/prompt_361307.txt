I work in a clinical-data group, and I keep running into a conceptual knot that I do not know how to refer to. We are regularly asked to produce a single number: the probability that this particular patient will respond to this particular drug within six months. The number is meant to guide a real decision—whether to prescribe, whether to enroll her in a trial—but every defensible way of computing it gives a different answer. If we compare her to all patients with her diagnosis, the figure comes out around 40 percent; if we compare her to patients matched on her age and biomarkers, it drops to about 25 percent; if we compare her to the subgroup treated at her own hospital, it rises to about 55 percent. There is no obvious fact that makes one of these comparison groups the correct one, and yet the number has to be a single, usable probability. I see the same shape of difficulty whenever we have to attach a probability to a one-off event, such as whether a particular loan will default or whether a particular contract will be disputed.

At the same time, the number seems to resist being interpreted in any of the three ways people around me want to interpret it. Some colleagues treat it as a summary of how often more or less similar patients turn out to respond; others treat it as a statement about how strongly one should believe that this particular case will respond; still others talk as though the drug simply has a real tendency to work in her, present before we measure it. A single-case probability sits uneasily across all three uses: it is not simply a fact about how often similar events occur, because it is applied once; it is not simply an opinion, because the data constrain it strongly and we can be demonstrably miscalibrated about such judgments; and it is not obviously a physical feature of the patient like her blood pressure, because the only way to look for such a feature would be to choose a comparison group and count, which starts the circle again.

I would like to know whether this configuration has an established name, and how it should be carved up. In particular, I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, how it relates to the nearest established concepts in the philosophical treatment of probability, and where its edges are. If no single term captures the whole entanglement—the unrepeatable case, the shifting comparison groups, and the unresolved interpretation—I would rather have a precise definition and a term for it, with boundary cases, than a bare name that covers only one corner of the problem.
