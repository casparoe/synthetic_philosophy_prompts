I recently joined my hospital's triage-review committee, and the work has forced on me a problem I now feel pulled in two directions about. We had to settle a policy for allocating a single scarce resource — for concreteness, the last staffed ICU bed — when two groups of patients present at once and their medical need is equal. I believe, and still believe, that the policy should send the bed to the larger group. Five patients should not die because we refused to let the number of patients count. Yet the moral theory I find most convincing seems to forbid precisely this, and I cannot tell which should give way.

The theory is broadly Scanlon's contractualism: an act is wrong when it could not be justified by a principle that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. What draws me to it is that it treats persons one by one. It explains, with one idea, why we may not harvest a healthy patient's organs to save five, and why a greater aggregate good cannot simply be spread across people as if their claims were a sum. Each person has a complaint against a principle that imposes on her a loss she could reasonably refuse to accept, and the complaints of many do not, by themselves, outweigh one person's equal complaint. That individualist restriction feels like the theory's whole point.

But the triage case is a forced choice between imposing a loss of the relevant kind on one patient and imposing it on five. Each of the five can say that a principle sending the bed to the one leaves him with a complaint he could reasonably reject. The one can say exactly the same of a principle sending the bed to the five. If reasonable rejectability is the whole story, then neither policy will be acceptable to everyone it burdens, and a procedure that flips a coin between saving one and saving five looks no less well justified than a policy that determinately saves five. I find that bewildering. I also cannot find a difference between the triage case and the transplant case that does the needed work: the obvious candidate difference, between using someone as a means and merely not saving him, feels as though it should matter, but I cannot say why the one patient's complaint against being left to die is not already a complaint against the allocation itself.

I am not asking to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict. Specifically: what would have to be true for my conviction that numbers count to be debunked — an artifact of visualizing aggregates rather than a moral insight? What would have to be true for the individualist restriction to be amended, and what is the least revision that admits numbers without collapsing the view into utilitarianism? Is there a reading of the restriction on which the theory and the intuition both survive, or would such a reading be ad hoc? Say what each resolution costs, and do not pass silently over any cost because it is uncomfortable.
