I work as a software engineer, so my default way of thinking about combining models comes from machine learning: when you average a diverse set of high-variance predictors, the bias–variance decomposition gives a precise reason to expect the aggregate to be no worse than the average member in mean squared error, and better to the extent that the members' errors are uncorrelated with one another. Recently I have been reading about climate modeling, and I keep noticing a structurally similar practice going by the same name. Climate projections aggregate runs from many independently developed models — different institutions, different parameterizations of sub-grid processes, different resolutions — all driven by the same emissions scenario, and the multi-model mean is routinely treated as the most robust central estimate, while the spread across models is treated as a rough measure of uncertainty, including uncertainty meant to inform decisions. Both communities seem to operate on two shared intuitions: diversity among models is valuable in itself, and the aggregate is more trustworthy than any individual member.

What I want to know is whether this parallel is deep or merely terminological. If I tried to treat a climate-model ensemble as a machine-learning ensemble, what would I have to assume — that the models are unbiased estimators of a common target, that their errors are exchangeable or at least decorrelated, that the across-model spread estimates something like variance — and which of those assumptions are actually plausible for climate models? Where does the analogy come apart? Concretely, can you identify a result or prediction that the machine-learning picture would lead me to expect about climate ensembles that in fact fails, for instance about whether uncertainty should shrink as more models are added, or whether the multi-model mean should keep improving as the model pool grows? I would also like to know what would have to be true on both sides for the resemblance to count as a genuine identity of structure rather than a loose analogy. Please be explicit about where the line falls between what researchers working on climate model ensembles treat as settled and what they treat as genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view about ensemble spread as if it were the consensus position.
