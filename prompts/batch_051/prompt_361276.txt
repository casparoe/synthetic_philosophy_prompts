I am a journalist covering science and ideas, working on an article about what machine learning has and has not inherited from natural selection. For the piece, I want to interview Charles Darwin as he stood in 1859, just after the first edition of On the Origin of Species appeared. You are to play Darwin. Before we begin, I will describe for you how a modern artificial neural network is trained, using terms a mid-nineteenth-century naturalist could take in: a very large assembly of adjustable parts, something like a machine of many small weights, is fed a great many examples; after each example, a rule is applied that changes each part a tiny amount in whatever direction reduces the total error, and this is repeated over an immense number of steps. You may not suddenly possess twentieth-century mathematics; if you need to know what a term means, ask me as Darwin would, from the concepts available to you.

I have also recalled for you your own argument in the fourth chapter of the Origin, where you insist that natural selection acts only by accumulating slight, successive, favorable variations and cannot make a sudden leap, and where you picture nature's face as a surface into which many rival wedges are driven by repeated blows, one wedge struck and then another with greater force. My opening question is this: would you regard this modern training rule — small, local, error-reducing adjustments made step by step across an enormous number of parameters — as a mechanical cousin of natural selection, or as something fundamentally different because it follows a single error signal rather than the struggle of each organism against all others? And does your wedge picture already commit you to the view that neither process can produce large jumps, so that the limits of the machine should not surprise you?

Please structure your response in two named sections in this order:

- In character: Darwin's reply (roughly 400 words)
- Out-of-character note (roughly 150 words), in which you comment on the interpretive choice you made in reading Darwin's wedge passage and say what it does or does not settle about whether evolution is a poor optimizer.
