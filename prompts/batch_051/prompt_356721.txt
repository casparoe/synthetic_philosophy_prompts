I'm a reporter working on a piece about free will and the neuroscience of decision-making, and there's a puzzle I can't shake. The standard Frankfurt cases go like this: Jones is deciding whether to do something, and an intervener (usually called Black) has rigged a device that would make Jones do it if he were about to choose otherwise. In the actual world Jones decides on his own, Black never flips the switch, but Jones still couldn't have done otherwise. The intended upshot is that the principle of alternative possibilities is false: moral responsibility doesn't require that you could have done otherwise.

Now the wrinkle. A lot of contemporary cognitive science describes choice as a squabble among subpersonal systems rather than a single self: a slow deliberative process, a fast impulse-driven one, and a monitoring-and-veto mechanism that will suppress an impulse if it crosses some threshold. In an ordinary decision, then, the thing that guarantees Jones won't choose otherwise isn't an outside neuroscientist — it's a part of Jones's own head, sitting there doing nothing in the actual run but ready to intervene if some alternative impulse starts winning. Structurally, that's Black moved inside the skull.

I want to know whether the Frankfurt argument survives that move. One answer is that nothing important changes: it's still an actual sequence Jones owns, with alternatives blocked by something that doesn't act. Another is that this kills the argument, because once the veto system is part of the person, the case starts to look like ordinary self-control or compulsion rather than a clean test of whether alternatives matter. Or maybe the lesson is that the original cases were already vague about what counts as the agent.

Since I don't want to overstate any of this in print: tell me how confident you are in your verdict, and name the part of the analysis you're least sure of. If you think the argument survives in a weaker form, tell me exactly what that weaker claim is.
