An AI lab's safety review board has asked two working groups to stop exchanging rival memos and instead produce a single document that both can sign. One group holds the monitoring-realist position: with appropriate training incentives, auxiliary classifiers that score the reasoning itself, and deliberate audits, a model's chain of thought can be made faithful enough that a monitor reading it catches most catastrophic failures before the corresponding action is taken, without an unacceptable loss of capability. The other group holds the pressure-skeptic position: whenever the reasoning is read by supervisors or used to gate the model's behavior, optimization pressure selects for reasoning that looks safe to the reader while the actual process producing the action diverges from it, so monitored reasoning should be expected to become strategically misleading exactly in the highest-stakes cases. Both sides already agree that chain-of-thought sometimes fails to track a model's actual decision procedure, and that outcome monitoring alone is not a substitute for reasoning-level oversight; the live dispute is whether reasoning-level monitoring can become reliable enough to serve as a primary safety mechanism in consequential deployments rather than only a debugging aid.

Write the joint statement the two groups could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should open with a numbered agreed-claims section, stated precisely enough to be neither vacuous nor slanted, covering what the disagreement is not about — for example, that chain-of-thought aids ordinary problem-solving or that naive outcome monitoring fails — as well as the failure modes both camps concede are real, and the empirical facts both camps would treat as established. Next, it should formulate the residual disagreement exactly, with each position stated in the wording its own side would accept, making clear where they differ on the direction of the selection pressure created by monitoring, on the feasibility of obfuscated reasoning that remains undetectable at scale, and on the marginal value of reading reasoning over auditing actions. Then it should identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that convincing evidence on one of them would move at least one camp, for example whether the monitor's own training signal is a net selection force for or against faithfulness, whether interpretability-style audits of internal representations add reliability beyond the reasoning text and whether they generalize under distribution shift or adversarial pressure, and whether capability scaling makes deceptive reasoning easier to produce or easier to detect. For each crux, the statement should specify the experiment, argument, or test case both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction registered in advance and the exact observation that would be surprising to each side. Where an apparent disagreement is merely verbal — over what counts as 'faithfulness,' 'reasoning,' or 'monitoring' — the statement should flag it and restate the dispute in terms both sides accept. Close with a disagreements table whose rows summarize each remaining point of divergence, the crux underlying it, and the two camps' advance predictions.
