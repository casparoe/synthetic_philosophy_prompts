At the start of David Hume’s essay *Of Money* in his *Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary*, Hume suggests that money ought not to be classed as one of the things commerce is actually about, but rather as a lubricant—like oil that makes the wheels of trade turn more smoothly. I want to know whether that idea can be subjected to a real empirical test, or whether it is best read as a conceptual proposal about what wealth and commerce should include. Design the strongest test you can of the distinction between two readings: (a) money is a neutral facilitator, valuable only for what it buys and for its reduction of transaction costs, and (b) a nation’s money stock is itself a component of its commercial wealth, so that an increase in money is an increase in economic power even after prices adjust. Use a real or realistically specified historical episode in which the quantity of money changed without any matching change in goods, labor, or technology. State exactly which observable outcome would favor each reading, and then say what a positive result for either side would and would not settle—especially whether any apparent failure of the lubricant reading can be explained away by transition effects, by a distinction between money as unit of account and money as durable asset, or by the possibility that the claim is partly a stipulation about how we use the word wealth. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
