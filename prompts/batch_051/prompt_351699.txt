I've been outlining a novel set in a small fishing town, and I keep tripping over a philosophical problem that's started to feel like the actual subject of the book. Every character acts sensibly: the boat owners work the stocks as hard as they can, the cannery owner cuts costs, the mayor chases tourist money, and each of them can give you a perfectly good reason for what they do. But the harbor ecosystem is slowly collapsing, and by the end it's obvious that this is something that should not have happened. The strange part is that the reason for the harbor not collapsing doesn't seem to belong to anyone. No particular character has that reason, because for each of them, doing their bit is a real loss with only a tiny, dispersed benefit. The reason just sits there, unattached, like smoke over the water. A friend who reads philosophy tells me this is close to a real debate about free-floating rationales — reasons without reasoners — and that a lot of philosophers thinks it's confused talk.

Here's what I want. Please argue, tightly and in your own words, that these unattached reasons are real: that a consideration can genuinely count in favor of some outcome or state of the world even when there is no individual agent for whom it is a reason. I don't mean just repeating the usual moves from the literature, and I'm not interested in a survey. I want you to actually face the strongest objection my friend raised: that a reason is always a reason for someone, and that sentences like "there's a reason for the harbor not to collapse" are just loose ways of saying the outcome would be better, or that whoever is in a position to help has a reason to help. If it helps, stay with the harbor as a running example, or reach for others — an unowned meadow, a dead language, a beautiful thing where no one will ever see it. Write it so it would land for a novelist, not just for someone already inside the debate.
