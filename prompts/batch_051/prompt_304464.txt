He keeps saying corporate blame is just shorthand and the real blame always lands on individuals. I don't think that's obviously right, but the standard cases people reach for always seem to have at least a couple identifiable people who could have stopped the thing, so they don't really pressure his view.

I want a pair of matched cases for an essay I'm writing on corporate moral agency. Both cases should involve the same midsized logistics company and the same kind of harm — call it a delayed shipment of temperature-sensitive medication that ends up harming patients. In Case A, the company should come out blameworthy but no individual employee is blameworthy. In Case B, every member of the relevant team is individually blameworthy but the company as a whole should come out not blameworthy. The cases should be realistic: no evil CEO, no conspiracy, no deliberate sabotage, nothing that would make a normal reader think I rigged it. And the blame in Case A has to fall out of the structure of the organization — its routines, decision procedures, incentives, information flows — not out of anyone's bad intentions. Ideally the two cases differ on exactly one structural feature so we can see what's doing the work.

After you lay out the cases, walk through why each one meets its half of the spec, say which feature seperates them, what happens if you move that feature from one case to the other, and then honestly say how someone who denies corporate blame would attack each case.
