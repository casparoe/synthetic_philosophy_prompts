Suppose someone drives eight thousand miles a year and, without much reflection, accepts the accompanying mortality risk of roughly one hundred micromorts — a micromort being a one-in-a-million chance of death. Suppose this same person campaigns against an industrial plant that is reliably estimated to add five micromorts per year to every nearby resident's chance of dying. The exposure is measured in the same units, the driving risk is twenty times the larger, and driving is not a purely self-regarding risk, since it also endangers passengers, pedestrians and other motorists. Ordinary judgement nonetheless treats the driving as a private choice and the plant as a wrong demanding remedy. Is there a defensible justification for this asymmetry between risk one tolerates because one is its source and risk one rejects because someone else is its source? Weigh the usual candidates — consent, control, dread, familiarity, the concentration or diffusion of the risk, whether the risk is a by-product or an end, and who benefits — and then test the best of them on nearby cases: (i) a motorist whose motorway driving raises the ambient risk to other road users by one micromort a year without their consent; (ii) a household that buys a discounted house beside the plant after the risk is disclosed and still protests; (iii) passengers who give informed consent to a light-aircraft flight carrying ten micromorts. End by stating the principle that correctly locates the asymmetry, and whether ordinary practice and public policy should preserve, abandon, or restrict it.
