I teach a seminar on the value of culture, and last meeting we got stuck on a question about endangered languages that I want to think through more carefully than I could on the spot. The familiar argument for preserving them, charitably reconstructed, is that a language is not just an alternative code for the same thoughts: it carries a distinctive way of carving up experience—lexical distinctions, grammatical categories, narrative forms, the sort of observations that get sedimented in idiom—so when the last speakers die, an irreplaceable conceptual resource goes with them, and no amount of translation afterward recovers it. I'm paraphrasing the line rather than quoting anyone.

The development that's been bothering me is this. We now have large language models and surprisingly good machine translation between many low-resource or endangered languages and major ones. Suppose a community's language is documented in a large corpus, and the resulting model can translate it, explain its idioms, and produce fluent text in it more or less indefinitely, long after the last fluent speaker has died. My question is about the argument, not about policy. Does the old argument for preserving endangered languages survive that changed asumption? Does it now instead support preserving corpora and building translation layers while letting living languages shift or disappear as communities themselves choose? Or does it just become inapplicable, because the development removes the premise that made conceptual loss so serious?

I'd like you to work through this the way a professional philosopher of language or cultural value would, not as a policy analyst. Start by brainstorming the distinct premises the argument might really be running on, including ones that are often left implicit. Then answer in these sections: "The argument, reconstructed" (one short paragraph), "What the new development changes" (one paragraph: which premise or premises it applies pressure to, and why), "Repairs" (one or two paragraphs: the most plausible weakened or revised version that still supports some preservation, if any), and "My verdict" (one paragraph: does the argument survive, survive in modified form, or is it dead—and why). Keep the whole thing under about 900 words, and don't pad with survey-of-views material; I want the argument's structure, not a bibliography.
