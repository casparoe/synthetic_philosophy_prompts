So I've been going back and forth with a friend about same-sex marriage — not really about what the law should say, but about the underlying philosophical issue of what marriage is for in the first place. I keep coming back to an older line of argument, the kind you find in natural-law defenses of marriage, which goes roughly like this. Marriage isn't just a legalized close friendship or a formalized romantic relationship; it's a specific sort of union. What makes it specific, the argument says, is that it joins two people in the one kind of bodily act that is, by its biological structure, capable of producing a child. Because that's the only kind of union with this built-in orientation toward reproduction, that, on the argument, is what marriage as an institution is for, and what justifies a society in singling it out. A same-sex couple can love each other and even jointly raise children, but on this view their relationship can't be a marriage in the relevant sense, since two bodies of the same sex can't form the sort of union whose characteristic act can make new people. (Some versions add a mother-and-father point, but I'm mostly interested in the argument's backbone, not the add-ons.)

Heres what I'm stuck on. Suppose safe, widely available artificial wombs — ectogenesis, where the entire pregnancy happens in a machine rather than inside anyone's body — become real in the fairly near future. And suppose it also becomes possible to derive gametes from any adult's cells, so that two men or two women can produce a child biologically related to both of them, without a third person's body entering the picture. The old argument seems to have been built for a world where only an opposite-sex sexual act could produce a child. Does the argument survive that change? And if it does, what exactly is doing the surviving? I want to know which premise the development actually bears on: the premise that marriage is essentially a bodily union oriented toward procreation, the premise that the special value of marriage comes from the one kind of act that can make new people, or some deeper premise about children needing a mother and a father. Is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still meaningful — say, that the state should support stable child-rearing units whatever their makeup, or that marriage is really a public commitment to a shared life rather than a biological category — or does the whole line collapse into "marriage is whatever we decide it is" once the procreative link is loosened? I'm after the fate of the argument, not a policy verdict, and I'd appreciate it if you flagged where my framing of the argument is uncharitable or sloppy. No citations or surveys needed — just work with the version of the argument I've sketched. Keep it under 700 words.
