I'm trying to work out what I actually think about introspection — when my word about my own mind should settle something, and when it shouldn't — and my gut reactions to cases don't obviously hang together. On the one hand, I think that if I'm in pain I can't be wrong about it; someone who says "you only think you're in pain, but maybe you're not" has misunderstood what pain is. Same for what I'm currently thinking: if I report that I'm replaying a conversation in my head, that isn't a hypothesis about myself I could turn out to be mistaken about. On the other hand, I'm convinced people confabulate the reasons for their own actions all the time. There's that experiment where people chose one face over another and then, when the experimenter swapped the face, they gave fluent, confident explanations for a choice they never actually made. So I don't take myself to be authoritative about why I do things.

But it isn't just causes. I once insisted I was merely annoyed at my partner, and he said no, you're scared about the interview; I told him that was ridiculous, and two hours later I concluded he was right. So I think I can be wrong even about an emotion I'm having at the time. And I think I can be wrong about what I believe: I've had friendships where I was convinced I was fine with something, but then my own behavior — avoiding the topic, finding excuses not to show up — slowly made me realize I wasn't fine, and that I'd been wrong about my own belief before the behavior clued me in. Yet if a therapist insisted I "really" want something I sincerely say I don't want, I'd call that overreaching. There isn't, it seems to me, a hidden want with determinate content that I simply lack access to.

My hunch is that the line has something to do with what's currently present in consciousness — pain, the thought I'm having — versus causes, standing attitudes, and interpretations. But the emotion case troubles that line, because the fear presumably felt like something even while I was mislabeling it as annoyance. Is there one principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures all these verdicts, or nearly all? Please state it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me what it predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on: a friend who says "I'm not anxious" while their leg jiggles under the table and they've been dodging every conversation about their father for months. Be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle can't accommodate. And please seperate what's settled among people who work on introspection from what's genuinely contested; don't present a contested view as the consensus.
