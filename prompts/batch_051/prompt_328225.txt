I have gradually come to accept a form of moral particularism of the kind associated with Jonathan Dancy. As I hold it, the view is not merely that our inherited moral principles admit of exceptions, but the stronger pair of claims that a feature can be a reason in one case and no reason, or even a reason on the opposite side, in another, and that moral thought does not consist in applying a finite stock of principles because no finite codification of morality is in principle available. I know the standard objections: that this makes moral disagreement and moral education inexplicable, that it cannot make sense of the role of rules in law and institutional life, and that some prohibitions look as close to exceptionless as anything does. What I have never seen is a systematic map of what would actually have to be true for the position to fail, or to be damaged badly enough that I should abandon it.

Please provide that map. Identify the distinct possible defeaters: particular cases whose structure particularism cannot describe, arguments from moral psychology or metaethics, and results about the possibility of codification that would amount to a collapse into a more sophisticated generalism. For each defeater, say whether a committed particularist would concede that it counts against the view or would instead try to absorb it, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by its own qualifications, especially the distinctions between enabling and disabling conditions, between default reasons and invariant principles, and between the claim that morality cannot be codified and the claim that there are no true moral generalizations at all. If particularism has been qualified until it risks nothing, say whether that insulation is itself a reason to be suspicious of it. Finally, identify what you regard as the single strongest piece of counterevidence and say whether you take it to have been met. The consideration I would personally find most persuasive is a demonstration that central cases of moral learning or moral transfer require exactly the sort of stable, context-independent generalization that particularism says we can do without.
