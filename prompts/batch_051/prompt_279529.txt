A colleague has been pressing me on the argument below, and I cannot find the fault in it, though its conclusion seems to me unacceptable. We had been arguing about whether the state should license parents, and I had been invoking the child's right to an open future in defence of parental licensing. He replied that the same right, taken seriously, proves far too much. His argument runs as follows.

(1) The child's right to an open future is the right not to have one's future options substantially foreclosed by others before one is capable of choosing among them for oneself.
(2) When an activity predictably and routinely violates such a right, the state is justified in restricting that activity.
(3) Ordinary child-rearing predictably and routinely violates it in exactly that way. A monolingual upbringing forecloses easy entry into some careers and communities; a religious upbringing forecloses ways of life the child has not had occasion to assess; the choice of a particular school or town forecloses friendships, intellectual habits, and ambitions that a different choice would have kept open.
(4) Therefore, by (1) to (3), the state is justified in restricting the great majority of significant child-rearing decisions.
(5) But a state standing in that relation to child-rearing could legitimately direct the language a child learns, the faith, if any, in which she is raised, and the ambitions that are cultivated in her. Parents would become executors of state-approved plans for their children's futures, with no protected sphere in which their own judgement about the good of the child is authoritative.
(6) That conclusion is untenable. Hence either children do not have the right described in (1), or the argument goes wrong somewhere before (5).

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I should like to keep both the child's right to an open future and the rejection of (5), but I worry that this is wishful thinking. Identify exactly which premise or inference you would reject; explain why that step looked plausible before it was rejected; and set out what a defender of the child's right to an open future must commit to, and what she must give up, in order to resist (5) without merely stipulating that the argument fails.
