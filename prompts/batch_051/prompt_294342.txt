For a paper I am drafting, I have been defending a Dennettian account of the kinds of minds, ordered by how an organism generates and tests candidate behaviours: Darwinian creatures, whose repertoires are selected only across generations; Skinnerian creatures, shaped within a lifetime by reinforcement; Popperian creatures, which test hypotheses in an inner environment rather than in the world; and Gregorian creatures, which inherit mind-tools, chiefly language, from their surrounding culture. I want to treat this not as a convenient pedagogical taxonomy but as a substantive claim about the evolution and architecture of cognition.

What I would like is a map of this position's exposure. Which findings in comparative cognition, which theoretical difficulties about learning or cultural inheritance, and which plausible redescriptions of the hierarchy would show that it is false, or that it is merely cosmetic? A good answer would distinguish developments that defenders of the view should concede to be genuine defeaters from those that can be absorbed by redrawing a boundary, and would say where the first serious crack is likely to appear. If, after all the qualifications, the hierarchy turns out to be unfalsifiable, I would also like to know whether that is a defect of the view or a virtue.
