I'm in a graduate seminar on causation and the instructor had us write out our current take on the Woodward-style interventionist account so we can stress-test each other's positions before the next meeting. I'd rather find the cracks now than get cornerd in front of everyone. Here's where I've landed, in no particular order:

(1) X causes Y roughly when, for some set of background circumstances, there is a possible intervention on X that would change Y (or its probability distribution).
(2) An intervention on X has to be a causal process that is exogenous relative to the system, that directly controls X, and that screens X off from its other causes, so it overrides them.
(3) I know (1) and (2) look circular if you read them as a reductive definition, since interventions are themselves causal; I lean toward thinking the account doesn't need to be reductive, so the circularity is non-vicious, but I'm not fully sure.
(4) What draws me to the account in the first place is that it ties causation to manipulation: a causal claim feels empty unless there's some sense in which you could wiggle X and watch Y move.
(5) But I don't want causal claims to depend on what humans, or any actual agent, happen to be able to manipulate.
(6) I also think what marks a generalization as genuinely causal is that it is invariant under a range of interventions, rather than merely being a true accidental correlation.
(7) And I think there are real causal facts about large-scale social and economic variables — for example, the recession caused the electoral swing — even though nobody can run a clean, surgical intervention on a recession.

I have a nagging feeling these don't all hang together, but I can't tell wich ones are the real problem. Go through and tell me: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only seem to be?
