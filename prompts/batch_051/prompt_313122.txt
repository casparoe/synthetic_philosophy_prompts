Below is a note toward an essay on computer-assisted and unsurveyable proofs. I want your critical comments before I expand it into a full draft. Please confine yourself to the internal dialectic of the note; do not suggest reading lists or evaluate prose style, and engage the specific moves below rather than the topic in general.

Nearly every philosophical discussion of computer-assisted proof starts with the worry that such proofs cannot be surveyed by a single mathematician. I suspect this is the wrong starting point. The word "surveyable" bundles together at least three different properties. Step-surveyability is the ability of a human to check each inference in turn. Global surveyability is the ability to hold a proof's architecture in view at once and see how its parts fit together. Self-containedness is the absence of any empirical premise: the proof's authority should rest only on definitions, axioms, and rules of inference. Once these are separated, much of the supposed novelty of computer proofs looks like a difference of degree. Finite mathematics already contains globally unsurveyable proofs in the form of long enumerations and exhaustive classifications, and few people deny that those are proofs. Step-surveyability, on the other hand, is not obviously lost when a computer checks cases: the program specifies the steps precisely enough that a human could in principle, with unlimited time, check them, just as one trusts a collaborator's arithmetic without redoing every sum. The genuine break, I want to argue, is in self-containedness. An ordinary proof purports to show that a theorem is true; an actual computer-assisted proof, as opposed to an idealized computational derivation, also depends on the empirical claim that a particular physical machine executed a particular program without a hardware fault. That claim is an engineering fact, not a mathematical lemma, and it cannot be established by mathematical reasoning alone. The philosophically interesting question is therefore not whether a proof may be unsurveyable, but whether empirical evidence about a machine's behaviour can transmit mathematical warrant. Formal proof objects and independently checked implementations are the most promising answer, because they convert the empirical claim into an object for further computation; but that only pushes the question one level back. The four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture matter less for having been proved originally by computer than for showing, through later formal verification and reimplementation, how the empirical element of computer proof might be tamed.

I especially want the following three moves pressed: (i) whether the three-way distinction is exhaustive or hides a fourth sense of surveyability; (ii) whether the claim that step-surveyability survives in computer proofs is tenable, given that the code, compiler, and hardware are themselves human artifacts subject to undetected error; and (iii) whether the appeal to self-containedness establishes a difference in kind, or merely a difference in degree from ordinary proofs that already depend on memory and testimony.
