I keep noticing people in AI safety use 'worst-case' and 'average-case' the way complexity theorists do, and I can't tell if it's a real analogy or just a shared vocabulary. In complexity theory you can ask whether a problem is hard on all inputs, or only hard on a few contrived inputs while staying easy on random ones, and 'average-case hardness' is a precise notion with it's own distributions and reductions. In alignment, 'worst-case' seems to mean the system never does anything catastrophic on any input, even under adversarial optimization, while 'average-case' means it's fine on the distribution it will actually be deployed on and thats all you can honestly guarantee. I want to know whether the parallel is deep or superficial. What would the alignment side need to have for the transfer to work—an actual distribution over inputs, a definition of average-case guarantees, maybe an analogue of a reduction? And where does it break? A model isn't a fixed computational problem: it keeps changing during deployment, and the worst inputs are partly generated by people trying to exploit it. So: is this a useful import of a rigor concept, or mostly cargo-cult math talk? State the mapping precisely, and tell me one thing the parallel would predict that's actually false.
