I have been trying to work out whether the harms people suffer inside virtual worlds are real harms, and what it is about a virtual object that makes damaging it wrong when it is wrong. I have recorded my own verdicts on a series of cases, and I am not sure they hang together. I would like you to find the principle behind them, if there is one.

Here are my verdicts:

1. In a massively multiplayer online game, a player persuades another player to hand over a rare sword that took roughly three hundred hours to obtain, then destroys it. The sword usually sells for about $900 on an unofficial marketplace. My verdict: a real harm; the platform should restore the item.

2. In a shared sandbox game with no formal property system, a griefer demolishes a monument that about forty players spent six months building together. The structure has no resale value. My verdict: a real harm to the builders, though I am not sure the platform owes restoration.

3. At a social event inside a virtual reality platform, one user moves their avatar so that it gropes another user's avatar without that user's consent. The target reports acute distress and stops using the platform. My verdict: a real harm, and wrong even though no physical body was touched.

4. An automatic update corrupts a single-player game save representing two hundred hours of progress. No other person is involved. My verdict: a genuine loss, but I hesitate to call it a harm in the same sense as the others.

5. In the final of a tournament with a $100,000 prize, a team loses after its opponents exploit a physics bug that the tournament rules did not address. My verdict: the result is unfair, but the losing team was not harmed by their opponents in the way the target in case 3 was.

6. A developer rebalances its game economy, and a virtual currency that many players bought with real money loses about ninety percent of its exchange value overnight. My verdict: a real financial harm, though it may be a permissible one if the terms of service reserved that right.

7. A player deletes a procedurally generated object that no one has ever seen, used, or valued. My verdict: no harm at all.

8. A platform shuts down and deletes a user-created virtual library that one person had spent years assembling, with no export tool available. My verdict: a real harm, and the platform owes at least a chance to retrieve the material.

I cannot tell whether these verdicts express one coherent view. Please state, precisely, the principle about virtual objects and virtual harm that best captures them. Then apply it to each case, and say what it implies about three cases I have not settled:

(a) a visitor in a virtual reality gallery defaces a virtual artwork that the artist intends to sell as a unique NFT;

(b) a user's AI companion, to whom they confide daily, is deleted by a housemate who briefly accesses the account;

(c) a griefer repeatedly kills another player's avatar in a game where death costs only a few minutes of progress, solely to make the target quit the server.

Finish by saying which of my original verdicts the principle forces me to revise or abandon, if any, and whether you would prefer a simpler principle that overturns one or two of my verdicts over a more complex one that saves them all.

Please begin with the candidate principle in one or two sentences, and keep the entire response to at most 1,000 words.
