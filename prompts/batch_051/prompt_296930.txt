I am the editorial director of a mid-sized literary imprint. My team receives roughly four thousand submissions a year and can acquire perhaps forty, and these acquisition decisions happen weekly: an editor prepares a one-page recommendation, an acquisitions committee signs off, and a wrong call is either a costly advance on a book that fails or a missed author. Ten editors work with a larger pool of outside readers, and almost every decision is made after one editor has read at most a few chapters plus someone else's report; asking everyone to read every promising manuscript in full is impossible. But I worry that we have drifted into making irreducibly aesthetic judgments — that a novel's prose is extraordinary, that its ending is devastating, that the book is genuinely funny, that it deserves to be published as art rather than merely sold as product — on the strength of other people's say-so. Philosophers have long defended something like the acquaintance principle: the view that first-hand experience of a work is required if one is to legitimately judge its aesthetic character, and that aesthetic testimony cannot be handed from one person to another the way factual testimony can. I do not want an acquisitions process that treats that principle as either obviously true or obviously irrelevant.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my editors could actually apply when they sit down with a manuscript and a reader's report. The procedure should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with a clear account of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and it should sort the claims in a report into those that may be accepted on testimony (for example, claims about length, genre, plot content, comps, production history) and those that may only be treated as advisory until an editor has had direct experience of the relevant part of the text. It should also distinguish coarse aesthetic judgments that a trusted reader's word may be enough to support in a low-stakes commercial decision from fine or verdictive aesthetic judgments that it may not, and it should say where the procedure stops with a no and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Mark explicitly the points at which judgment cannot be eliminated.

Approach this as a professional philosopher of aesthetics who has also worked in trade publishing, and think carefully before writing; it may help to begin by distinguishing the kinds of claim that actually appear in a reader's report. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) two outside readers independently describe a literary novel's prose as extraordinary, but the acquiring editor has read only the plot summary and the first five pages; (2) a trusted reader describes a thriller as a compulsive page-turner with a genuinely satisfying twist, and we are weighing a commercial acquisition where the committee cares mainly about reader enjoyment rather than literary prestige; (3) a commercially promising manuscript by an author with a large platform gets uniformly glowing scout reports, but the one editor who began reading it found the prose flat and stopped after fifty pages, and the committee is leaning toward acquiring it anyway. For each case, say what the procedure recommends, what residual risk remains, and whether the recommendation is one I am likely to dislike. End by saying what the procedure cannot do — which parts of an acquisition decision are no more governable by a procedure than a person's taste.
