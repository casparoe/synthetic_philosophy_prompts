I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy and I've been going in circles on the aggregation debate inside Scanlonian contractualism, and what I actually want is someone to tell me why this debate is so weirdly stuck, not another argument for one side.

Here's the debate as I see it. Contractualism says wrongness gets settled by asking whether an act is permitted by some principle for how we're generally to treat one another, where the test is that no one could reasonably reject that principle if they were trying to find terms they could all live with. The aggregation problem is whether this machine can count heads. The no-aggregation camp says it structurally can't: the reasonable rejection test is individualist, so you compare each person's complaint against each candidate principle one at a time. Five complaints of you-let-me-die can't be added together into a thing that outweighs one person's you-let-me-die. That gives you the Taurek-ish result in the drug case, where you can save the person who needs the whole dose or the five people who each need a fifth, and the framework refuses to say the five matter more, which leaves you flipping coins or admitting the numbers are morally inert, take your pick. The numbers-matter camp says that's a misreading: the thing that gets rejected is a principle, and principles can encode patterns, so something like save the greater number in rescue cases might pass the reasonable rejection test even though no single one of the five, by herself, outweighs the one. Plus there's the second front, the transmitter-style case where saving one trapped person means cutting power to a broadcast that millions are enjoying. Everyone agrees something changes when the harms are tiny and numerous, but they disagree about whether the theory's refusal to let a million small complaints swamp the one big one is a feature (finally, a principled anti-aggregationism) or a bug (really, can't a million people's avoidable misery buy anything).

Maybe I'm wrong, but it doesn't read like a normal first-order moral fight. Nobody is confused about the cases; the moves are all mapped out. The people who think contractualism must count numbers keep producing mechanisms — tie-breakers, pairwise weighing, principles that mention numbers — and the no-aggregation people keep saying the mechanisms are either disguised aggregation or they don't do any justificatory work. Meanwhile the revisionists make peace with coin flips and the anti-revisionists cite that as proof the reading went wrong somewhere. Nobody changes there mind.

My half-baked diagnosis is that the two sides disagree about what contractualism is for. One side treats it as a criterion of rightness that has to track our considered verdicts in rescue cases, so flipping a coin for one-versus-five is close to a reductio. The other side treats it as an account of what it is to justify a principle to each person, so cases are just probes for what justifiable-to-each commits you to, bullets and all. If that's right, it's partly a metatheoretical disagreement and partly a disagreement about which intuitions are evidence. But I also suspect there's a buried disagreement about how complaints are individuated — whether the same person has the same complaint under two different candidate principles, or whether the principle partly constitutes what counts as a complaint — and until that gets sorted the two sides are just passing each other's arguments in the dark.

So what kind of disagreement is this? A substantive normative one, a verbal one about the word aggregation, a metatheoretical one about what the theory is for, a disagreement about what counts as data, or some mixture? And what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a new case — or is this just permanently load-bearing vagueness in the idea of reasonable rejection? And please don't tell me to go run a vignette study; I'm pretty sure this one isn't settled that way, but I'm willing to be argued out of it.
