I'm putting together the view section of a paper on epistemic arguments for democracy, and I've ended up with a package I'd like pressure-tested before I commit to it. I can't tell anymore which bits are pulling against each other.

Here's where I've landed:

1. The Condorcet jury theorem is a real (if partial) epistemic argument for majority rule: if all voters have the same probability p > 0.5 of being right, and their votes are conditionally independant given the truth, then the majority is more likely to be right than any single voter, and as the group grows the probability the majority is right tends to 1.

2. The independence condition is the fragile one. In real life the main threat to the epistemic story is correlated votes — people sharing the same cable-news segment, party cue, or school curriculum.

3. But I also think real electorates are shot through with those correlations and it usually still works out: for a large enough electorate, majority rule is still more reliable than the typical individual voter, because once you discount for correlation there are still enough effectively independent signals left.

4. I also think you don't need informed voters for the theorem to work. p = 0.51 is enough, and with a million voters that tiny edge gets amplified to near-certainty that the majority is right.

5. At the same time, I think there are cases where we should defer to a small panel of genuine experts over a large electorate on epistemic grounds — even when every voter in the electorate clears the p > 0.5 bar individually.

6. And I don't think correlated votes can make a majority worse than a coin flip when each individual voter is more likely right than wrong. Correlation can shrink the majority's edge but not push it below 50/50.

Go through the pairs that are in tension, rank them by how bad the tension is, and say which single revision would clean up the most. Also flag any apparent tension that actually dissolves. And please seperate what's mathematically settled about the theorem from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present a contested point as the consensus.
