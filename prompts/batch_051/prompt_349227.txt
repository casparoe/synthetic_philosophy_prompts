I am a novelist beginning a book about the decade-long run-up to a financial panic and its aftermath, and I have been trying to make the novel's implied theory of social explanation explicit before I commit it to every page. Over the course of the work I have drifted into six commitments, and I want an audit of whether they form one coherent position. Here they are:

1. Explanatory individualism. Social events are to be explained, in the last analysis, by the beliefs, desires, choices, and interactions of individual persons; sentences like 'the market panicked' are acceptable only as shorthand for many individual acts and states.

2. Supervenience of the social. There is nothing to a currency, a bank run, or a social class over and above the individuals involved and their relations; fix every fact about the individuals and you have fixed every social fact.

3. Institutional realism. Money, contracts, and property are nonetheless real, causally consequential features of the world: they do not depend on any particular person's attitude toward them, and they can outlive everyone now alive.

4. Systemic explanation. The best account of the panic runs through liquidity spirals, leverage, and self-amplifying expectations — mechanisms that are properties of the trading system as a whole, and I do not think they can be restated, even in principle, as the practical reasoning of any finite set of individual traders.

5. Narrative corollary. The novel will not employ collective narrators or attribute intentions to 'the market' or 'the city'; social forces will be shown only through individual scenes and individual consciousnesses.

6. Strategic unpredictability. Even perfect knowledge of every individual's preferences and endowments would not let me predict the panic, because what each person does depends on what each expects everyone else to do.

I ask you to tell me whether these six hang together, and to do the work of separating real conflicts from merely apparent ones rather than flagging every pair. Please structure the response in four numbered sections: (1) 'Mapping the commitments', about 150 words, placing each of the six relative to methodological individualism and holism; (2) 'Real tensions, ranked', about 450 words, the genuine conflicts ordered by severity, each with the smallest revision that would resolve it and what that revision costs; (3) 'Dissolving apparent tensions', about 200 words, one or two pairs that only seem inconsistent and the distinction that dissolves each; (4) 'The cheapest revision', about 200 words, the single change that relieves the most tension for the least loss, with a brief justification.
