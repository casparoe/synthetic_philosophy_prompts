I want you to work through a counterfactual in the philosophy of time: how Dōgen, the thirteenth-century Japanese Zen philosopher, would have responded to the presentism/eternalism debate if he had encountered it.

Here is the part of his view I have in mind, as I understand it. In his discussion of uji — being-time — Dōgen denies that time is a container within which beings occur. Time and being are not two things: each being just is a time, and each time is a complete configuration of things rather than a thin slice of a passing present. He draws this out with the example of firewood and ash. Firewood does not become ash the way one later phase of a single continuing thing succeeds an earlier phase; firewood rests in the dharma-position of firewood, ash rests in the dharma-position of ash, and the before-and-after relation we ordinarily project from one to the other is, on his account, severed. He extends the point to life and death: neither becomes the other, and each is its own time. I read him as claiming that each being-time coincides with the whole of time, not with one segment of it.

Against that, consider the debate that took shape in the twentieth century, after McTaggart's distinction between tensed and tenseless time and under pressure from special relativity. Presentism holds that only the present exists, while the past and future do not. Eternalism holds that all times exist in the same way, standing in unchanging earlier-than and later-than relations — the block universe. Relativity added a further consideration: if observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are simultaneous, then there is no single cosmic now to serve as the privileged present, and many philosophers took this to favor the block picture.

I want you to predict, as rigorously as you can, where Dōgen would have landed if handed this menu — and, just as importantly, what he would have thought was wrong with the menu itself before choosing from it. In particular: does his claim that each being-time is the whole of time amount to eternalism, or to a more radical view that is neither presentist nor eternalist because it rejects the shared assumption of a single timeline in which moments are ordered? Would he have seen the block universe as the modern mathematical expression of his own position, or as still half-inside the container picture — a four-dimensional container in which moments merely sit side by side rather than pass? And would the relativity of simultaneity, which undermines a single cosmic present, strike him as a vindication of his rejection of the now, or as a confusion that arises only from within the very picture of time-as-container he was trying to dismantle?

If you think the exercise is ill-posed — that Dōgen's framework and the presentist/eternalist framework are too incommensurable for a determinate prediction to be made, or that I have mischaracterized one of the positions — say so plainly instead of pretending the question can be answered. Where the prediction goes indeterminate, tell me the nearest answerable question and answer that.
