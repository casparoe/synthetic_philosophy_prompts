Most companies have a policy—sometimes informal, sometimes written—against employees discussing their pay with one another. The offical rationale is that pay talk breeds bad feeling and envy. But I'm not sure that rationale holds up: it seems to only make sense if you assume that a worker who gets upset about a coworker's higher salary is merely envious, wanting to bring the better-paid person down a notch, rather than resentful about an injustice in the wage structure itself. What view of envy and resentment does a pay-secrecy practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be clear about whether it's really committed to treating wage comparisons as mere envy as opposed to a signal of distributive injustice. How does it sit with the Rawlsian line that some envy is excusable—a predictable byproduct of inequalities that undermine self-respect—and what would a pay-transparency policy be presupposing instead? Finally, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could someone defend pay secrecy on other grounds, like privacy or smooth cooperation, while rejecting the envy-as-vice picture—and what would distinguish those justifications from the official one?
