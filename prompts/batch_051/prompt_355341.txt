A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Boethius on divine foreknowledge, and by the end we had reconstructed from our discussion an argument that both of us found intolerable but could not see how to escape. I want a diagnosis of the argument, not a summary of the view.

The argument runs like this:

(1) If God is omniscient, then for every action I will perform in the future, God already knows now that I will perform it.
(2) God is omniscient.
(3) So for every future action of mine, God already knows now that I will perform it.
(4) If God knows something, it cannot turn out to be false; God is infallible.
(5) Therefore, for every future action of mine, it cannot turn out that I fail to perform it.
(6) If it cannot turn out that I fail to perform an action, then my performing that action is necessary.
(7) If my performing an action is necessary, then I do not perform it freely.
(8) Therefore, no future action of mine is performed freely.
(9) If no future action of mine is performed freely, then I can deserve no blame for any future action.
(10) But some people are already worthy of blame for what they are about to do; my friend granted that a person who has settled on committing a murder tomorrow is blameworthy even before he acts.
(11) The argument therefore yields the conclusion that even deliberate future wrongdoing is blameless. Since that seems to prove too much, something in the argument must give.

Which step or steps should I reject, and why did the relevant step look compulsory while we were arguing? I most want to keep the connection between knowledge and truth in step 4, and the connection between freedom and desert in step 9; I feel less protective of the middle of the argument, though I do not yet see whether the middle can be given up without the whole thing collapsing. I also want to know exactly where Boethius's own suggestion enters: the idea that God does not strictly foreknow anything because God is outside of time and sees all of history in a single eternal present. Does that move block step 1, by rejecting the picture of God as knowing the future from the past's vantage point, or does it enter only after the damaging step has already been taken? Finally, say honestly what rejecting the offending step costs: what remains of free will, of moral responsibility, and of the claim that God knows future acts at all?
