I have been trying to evaluate an argument I recently encountered, and although each step looks plausible, the conclusion is one I cannot accept. (1) If the familiar is-ought principle is right, no moral conclusion can be validly inferred from purely descriptive premises alone. (2) Therefore, any argument that establishes a moral judgment must include at least one moral premise. (3) But a moral premise is itself a moral judgment, so it stands in just as much need of justification as the conclusion it was introduced to support. (4) Thus an argument for a moral judgment can never reach a foundation outside moral judgment; at every turn it appeals only to another moral judgment. (5) If moral argument can never reach a foundation outside moral judgment, then no moral judgment is supported by, or answerable to, any fact about how the world is. (6) If no moral judgment is answerable to any fact about how the world is, then no moral judgment is rationally preferable to its denial. (7) Yet the judgment that torturing children for amusement is wrong is rationally preferable to its denial. The argument must fail somewhere. I would like to keep the is-ought principle in (1), read in its usual way, and the datum in (7). At which step or transition does the argument fail, and what would it cost to reject that step? Please write your answer in American English.
