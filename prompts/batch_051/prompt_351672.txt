You are a professional philosopher who works on institutional design, incentive systems, and the contrast between governing through virtue and governing through rules. Please work through the question slowly and carefully: begin by brainstorming the pressures the scheme would create, then trace how those pressures would compound over time, and only then settle on a judgement. Do not assume the scheme is either sensible or perverse.

I have been thinking on my own about an idea from Han Feizi, and I want to know how a modern version of it would actually behave.

As I understand Han Feizi, he believed a ruler cannot safely rely on officials' virtue, loyalty, or good intentions, because those qualities are easy to fake and expensive to verify. What the ruler can verify is whether an official's results match what the official said they would produce. So Han Feizi wanted officials to state their undertakings in advance and then be rewarded or punished according to whether their deeds matched their words. The part that interests me is his insistence that the mismatch itself is the offence. A minister who promises a small thing and produces a large one is punished just as much as one who promises a large thing and produces a small one, because the ruler's deepest need is for speech and action to line up, not for occasional good results.

Now suppose a large public bureaucracy adopted the following scheme.

1. Each year, every senior official must file a public statement of quantified, dated undertakings: licences to be processed, inspections to be completed, projects to be finished, costs to be saved, and so on, for everything within their remit.
2. At the end of the year, an independent board compares each statement with measured results. Officials receive a fixed salary, but a large bonus is paid only for exact correspondence between statement and result, within a narrow tolerance band.
3. Any deviation from the statement, whether a shortfall or an over-delivery, costs the official the whole bonus for that item and triggers a disciplinary review. The rules say the review may not consider intentions, effort, extenuating circumstances, or unannounced good works.
4. Hitting the stated undertakings exactly, item by item, earns the full bonus and priority for promotion, however modest the undertakings themselves were.
5. Statements are published, and other officials and the public may compare promise with delivery. Revisions are allowed only twice a year and only with a written explanation.

I am not asking whether this is moral; I want to know how it would work. Walk through the incentives it creates for the individual officials, for their superiors who set their remits, and for the independent board. What kinds of people get promoted after several rounds? Where does the scheme get gamed first: cautious underclaiming, hiding over-performance, relabelling work so the numbers land inside the tolerance band, collusion with the evaluators, or something else? Does the rule that over-delivery is also punished make the system more truthful, or just more timid and wasteful? And in the end, does a scheme like this vindicate Han Feizi's bet that a ruler can dispense with virtue, or does it quietly reintroduce the very virtue it was meant to replace?
