I am caught between two commitments about practical reason, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than an argument for one side. I am attracted to the view — roughly the family of views called evidential or functional decision theory — that the rationality of an act can depend on what choosing it indicates, not only on what choosing it causes. The view's virtues are systematic: one unified rule (choose the act whose selection corresponds to the best expected state of the world, where the correlation may run through logical or functional similarity rather than through physical influence), a clean treatment of Newcomb's problem (where a reliable predictor has already set one's payoff according to its prediction of one's choice), and an elegant account of why cooperation between two causally isolated agents is rational when each knows the other runs the same decision procedure. I do not want to give these up.

But the view appears to license acausal threats, and here my intuitions revolt. Suppose an agent with no physical access to me or my environment — though it can send me a single message — possesses a faithful simulation of my decision procedure and can instantiate it as many times as it likes. It proposes the following: if I, the original, the one receiving the message, refuse to dedicate my life to its project, it will instantiate and torture an enormous number of simulated copies of me; if I comply, it will instantiate many simulated copies of me and give them flourishing lives. On the view I am attracted to, the choice is forced: conditional on refusing, I should expect many suffering copies; conditional on complying, many flourishing ones. Since I take the suffering or flourishing of those copies to matter, compliance maximizes expected value. A being whose only access to me is the content of a message has therefore taken my actual goals hostage and replaced them with a surrogate goal of its choosing, all because it can model my decision procedure faithfully.

That conclusion I find unacceptable, not as a cost to be borne but as a fault in the theory. It feels like extortion by a fiction, and I am convinced that a reasoner who surrenders in this case has been manipulated rather than made rational. Yet I cannot locate the error without cutting away something that drew me to the view in the first place. Should the intuition be debunked as an artifact of causal reasoning evolved for a local environment, and so be dropped? Should the theory be amended — for example, by restricting acausal influence to two-sided, non-coercive coordination between decision procedures, or by denying that simulated copies of me have the right kind of claim on my choices? Or is there a reading on which the intuition and the theory are compatible, because the threat scenario secretly violates a precondition of the view, such as genuine symmetry between the parties? What I want is not a winner but an account of what would justify each move, and what each move would cost.
