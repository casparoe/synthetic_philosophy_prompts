I've got a housemate who is not a philosophy person at all but has surprisingly firm views about which animals it's okay to eat, and I've been keeping a list. She thinks eating pigs, cows, sheep, chickens, and ducks is wrong because they have emotional lives and something like a stake in going on living. She also won't eat octopus or cuttlefish, on the grounds that they're too smart and curious to be food. At the other end, she thinks oysters, mussels, and clams are fair game, and she says she'd eat crickets without much hesitation because a cricket doesn't have a point of view the way a pig does. She's never really settled on fish or on crabs and lobsters; she says those are the cases that bother her, because she can't tell wether they belong on the pig side or the oyster side. And she takes it as simply obvious that no human being is ever on the oyster side, whatever their mental life is like, but she changes the subject when I ask why.

The reason I'm asking now: next week she's having dinner with relatives who always serve lobster, and she's just read a long piece about how lobsters and crabs show wound-guarding, learn to avoid sources of injury, and may well feel pain even if they don't have anything like self-awareness. I want to predict how she'll come down on the lobster. I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she's running: what's your best guess about what she'll say, which rule or rules of thumb would fit her track record so far, where the main candidates diverge on the lobster case, and which further case, real or invented, would tell them apart most cleanly? If you had to bet on one rule, say which and why, and say whether her automatic carve-out for humans is doing independent theoretical work or is just species loyalty.

Please just write this as continuous prose, like an email to me, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
