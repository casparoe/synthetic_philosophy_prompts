I'm leading a two-hour seminar for third-year philosophy undergraduates on the wrong kind of reasons problem — roughly, the problem of explaining why considerations about the costs or benefits of holding an attitude (a threat that makes it prudent to admire someone, a reward for believing a proposition) seem not to bear on whether the attitude is correct, and what the difference between those two kinds of reason consists in. The students can already distinguish normative from motivating reasons and accept that beliefs can be evaluated as correct or incorrect, but they have not seen the attitude-focused literature. Plan the seminar's explanatory arc: what must be in place before the problem can be stated cleanly, which single example should carry the idea and what features it must have and must lack, the misconceptions the students are likely to bring or form and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, what you would deliberately leave out at this level and why, and a final check that would show they understand the problem itself rather than merely the example. Commit to one definite plan and defend its ordering; do not offer a menu of alternatives.
