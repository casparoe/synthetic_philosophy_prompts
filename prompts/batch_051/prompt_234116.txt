I am a historical theologian preparing a seminar paper for a faculty workshop at a confessional Reformed seminary, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defence of my argument. The paper's thesis is this: the classical Protestant claim for the clarity of Scripture — the claritas Scripturae, that the text is clear in everything necessary for salvation — cannot do the adjudicative work it is usually asked to do, because the claim itself is one more interpreted assertion about the text. Consequently, sola scriptura, if it is to amount to more than a private right of judgment, must be supplemented by an explicit account of the church's ministerial authority to settle disputed readings; that account would be fallible, but it would still be genuinely binding in a way that functions like tradition, and I will argue that Protestant practice already relies on such a tradition under other names. My sketch of the argument runs through the regress that opens whenever someone asks who decides what a contested passage means: an appeal to Scripture alone yields an interpretation, an appeal to the clarity of the relevant passage yields an interpretation of where clarity applies, and so on, which is why the existing Protestant machinery — the analogy of faith, Scripture interpreting Scripture, the confessions, and the ordinary means of grace — ends up doing the very work that a public interpretive authority does, only without acknowledging it.

The audience is mostly systematic theologians whose working assumptions are those of post-Reformation orthodoxy: they affirm the full inspiration and authority of Scripture, they treat the Westminster standards as a faithful summary of biblical teaching, and they regard any appeal to tradition as a second source of revelation with deep suspicion. The room also includes two analytic philosophers who work on testimony, authority, and the epistemology of interpretation, plus a historical theologian who specializes in how early Reformed orthodoxy actually handled exegetical disagreement. I need to know how this audience, not a different one, will push back.

Please prepare the objections in roughly the order in which they will be raised, ordered by their likelihood or damage, and phrase each one the way a member of this faculty would actually put it — the systematician's version, the philosopher's version, and the historian's version where they differ. For each, give me a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, and the point where the reply is softest. Then close with the question I am hoping nobody asks. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, as in a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic text, no tables.
