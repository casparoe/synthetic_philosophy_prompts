Consider the Ship of Theseus, but with the following change: the puzzle involves no gradual replacement of parts and no rival vessel. A ship sails each day; every evening it is completely disassembled, and each plank, nail, and rope is catalogued and locked in a separate crate, so that no original part is lost, none is added, and no two parts touch overnight. At dawn the very same parts are refastened in precisely the same configuration, and the ship sails again. This routine continues for a century. At three o'clock in the morning, while the constituents lie disconnected in their crates, is there a ship in the warehouse, or only a scattered collection of ship parts? And when the parts are reassembled at dawn, is the resulting ship numerically the same ship that sailed the previous day, or is it a new ship coincidentally composed of the same materials?

This version of the story is usually taken to raise a question about persistence through time, but I want it to press the special composition question instead: under what conditions do some parts compose one thing at a given moment? Work through what the main rival answers to the special composition question would say about the 3 a.m. case, including mereological nihilism, mereological universalism, a contact-based or fastening-based restriction on composition, and van Inwagen's answer on which the only composite objects are living organisms, so that a so-called ship is really just some simples arranged in a ship-like manner. Then explain how those verdicts bear on the dawn question of sameness over time: if a restricted view says no ship exists at night, can it nonetheless identify yesterday's ship with this morning's ship, or is it forced to say the ship is ceasing to exist and being reconstituted daily? Finally, assess whether this diurnal variant gives any of the competing answers to the special composition question a clearer advantage than the original replacement version of the Ship of Theseus does.
