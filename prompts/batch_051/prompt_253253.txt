I've come to hold the following view about what counts as a legitimate mathematical object. There is no principled, time-invariant line between the objects mathematics may legitimately talk about and the ones it may not. What a community counts as a real object is a function of the construction procedures or definitional resources it accepts at a given moment — straightedge-and-compass constructibility, solvability by radicals, expressibility in closed form, definability in ZFC, and so on — and each of these standards was relaxed not because a deeper criterion was discovered, but because the old standard made some problem or object practically unavoidable. On this view, the passage from Euclidean constructions to transcendental curves, and then to functions, arbitrary sets, and large cardinals, is a history of ad hoc line-drawing under local problem-solving pressure. To call something a legitimate mathematical object is to describe a community's current consensus, not to track a real joint in nature.

I want a map of what would count against this view. Give me the defeaters — observations, arguments, formal results, developments in mathematical practice — that should move someone who holds it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a committed holder of the view would concede that it genuinely cuts against the position, or would dismiss it as not bearing on the claim. I am especially interested in defeaters that come from inside mathematical practice — for example, the surprising stability of some standards, reverse mathematics (the program of asking exactly which existence axioms a theorem requires), the indispensability argument (the idea that objects doing work in scientific explanation are legitimate however they were introduced), or the way certain set-theoretic constructions keep looking pathological to working mathematicians — rather than from armchair metaphysics. I would also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and whether the view can be qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Where possible, distinguish evidence that would force abandoning the position from evidence that would only force restricting or refining it.
