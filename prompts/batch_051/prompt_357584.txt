A friend is habitually twenty minutes late. It is rarely an emergency; it is mostly optimism and poor planning, and the friend knows it. After each incident they do what a reasonable person could ask: they warn as soon as lateness becomes likely, they apologize without excuse, and they make the lost time up in some concrete way. I have a firm intuition that something is still being subtracted from the friendship even after that repair. Now consider an account that says this intuition must be false. On an expectation-repair account of small promises, the whole wrong of committing to arrive at three and arriving at three-twenty is the induced expectation one fails to meet; once one supplies timely candor, takes responsibility for the inconvenience, and provides a make-good that restores the other person's time and confidence, the debt is paid. The account is attractive for systematic reasons, not because it flatters my habits. It unifies small promises with punctuality, deadlines, and casual commitments; it yields a clear decision procedure—never break a commitment without reason, but judge punctuality against the cost of repair, and do not mistake apology for compensation—and it explains why the moral weight of a promise varies with the reliance it invites. But the account's verdict on the habitual friend is that, after adequate repair, nothing further is owed and nothing further is wrong. That verdict conflicts with my intuition that recurring lateness of this sort changes what the relationship itself is: the friend is not simply someone with more repair-debts, but someone one can no longer count on in the same way, and that change is not fully captured by any discrete loss that can be made whole. I do not want a bare decision between these; I want to know what each would have to establish in order to require the other to yield. What sort of argument would debunk the intuition as a moral-bookkeeping illusion rather than a reliable perception of relationship-constituting reliability? What sort of argument would show that the expectation-repair account needs amendment or replacement because it leaves out something constitutive? And is there a reading of repair—perhaps distinguishing making someone whole from restoring a standing—on which the account and the intuition are compatible, and what would each side have to give up under that reading?
