I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how committees and courts aggregate the reasons behind their decisions. I want to test a variation on the discursive dilemma, and I'd appreciate a philosopher's analysis of whether the standard reading survives the change.

Here is the baseline. A three-judge panel must decide a contract case under a two-element rule: the defendant is liable if and only if the contract was valid (P) and the conduct breached it (Q). The familiar problem profile is this:

- Judge A accepts P but rejects Q, and therefore votes: not liable.
- Judge B rejects P but accepts Q, and therefore votes: not liable.
- Judge C accepts both P and Q, and therefore votes: liable.

A majority of judges accepts P (A and C), and a majority accepts Q (B and C). So if the court first aggregates the premises by majority and then applies the two-element rule — the premise-based procedure — it must find the defendant liable. Yet a majority of the judges individually votes not liable, which is what the conclusion-based procedure returns. That divergence is the discursive dilemma, also called the doctrinal paradox.

Now the variation I want to examine. Expand the panel to five judges, and change only the premise-based procedure: a premise is adopted only if it commands a four-fifths supermajority, meaning at least four of the five judges must accept it. The conclusion-based procedure remains what it was: a simple majority on the final verdict. Consider this profile:

- Judge 1: P yes, Q yes, and votes liable.
- Judge 2: P yes, Q yes, and votes liable.
- Judge 3: P yes, Q yes, and votes liable.
- Judge 4: P yes, Q no, and votes not liable.
- Judge 5: P no, Q no, and votes not liable.

Under the modified premise-based procedure, P passes with four votes, but Q fails with only three, so the two-element rule returns not liable. Under the conclusion-based procedure, the final-verdict majority is liable by three votes to two. The two procedures diverge, but this time the premise-based outcome is the one that contradicts the majority on the verdict, and it does so because of the supermajority threshold rather than because of the conjunction structure by itself.
