I help run a small, non-academic discussion group that has spent the better part of a year arguing about constitutional monarchy. One side defends it as a workable and even attractive part of parliamentary democracies: a hereditary head of state trained from birth for a ceremonial role, kept out of partisan politics precisely by having no electoral mandate, and able to represent continuity and national identity without governing. The other side thinks this is the problem in polite form: a democratic state should not have any public office distributed by birth, even a mostly powerless one, because it embeds an aristocratic status hierarchy and teaches citizens that equal access to public office does not really matter. Both sides accept constitutional democracy, the rule of law, and civilian control of the military; nobody is defending absolutism or a monarchy with real veto power over legislation. The examples we keep returning to are the United Kingdom, the Scandinavian monarchies, and Japan, but we do not need deep historical detail, just a clear account of the institutions as they operate today.

I would like you to draft the document our two camps should have produced if they had run a properly disciplined adversarial collaboration: the joint statement that a committed constitutional monarchist and a committed democratic republican could both sign after good-faith work. Please include (a) a numbered list of claims both sides accept, including claims about what these monarchs actually do, what democracy and equal citizenship require, and where both sides agree the institution is mostly symbolic; (b) the exact disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position and would not call the other side's wording a caricature; (c) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, for example whether a ceremonial office can be genuinely separated from its heredity, whether abolishing a monarchy tends to improve or worsen democratic stability in practice, or how much symbolic representation matters for civic equality; and (d) for each crux, a concrete test, case, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and if you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why at the end.

Please keep the document self-contained: no footnotes, citations, or reading list, and do not invent statistics or quote any text from memory. Where a factual claim about how, say, the Swedish or Japanese system actually works is uncertain or contested among informed observers, mark it as uncertain rather than stating it as settled. Above all, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
