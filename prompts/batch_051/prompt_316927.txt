I'm a journalist on a science-and-ideas beat at a general-interest magazine, and I have a recurring problem. Roughly once a month I write a piece that could be headlined "why is Gen Z so anxious" or "millennials are killing the napkin industry" or "boomers are hoarding all the houses." My editors love the generational hook. My sources—demographers, sociologists, the occasional philosopher of social science—keep telling me the hook is fake, that generations aren't real kinds, just arbitrary cohort bins, and I usually end up writing the story anyway with a vague hedge. I'd rather have a procedure than a hedge.

I know the rough shape of the debate: some people treat generations as real kinds, others as arbitrary bins; there's something about how classifying a generation can help make it real (looping effects?); and there's the demographic problem that one cross-sectional survey can't separate age effects, period effects, and cohort effects. But I don't know how to turn that into a reusable decision procedure for someone on deadline.

Give me the procedure: a checklist, or a sequence of tests with a flowchart in words, that I can run before I file any story with a generational claim. It should tell me when I can defensibly write "Gen Z is X" or "Millennials did Y," and when I have to write something weaker like "young adults today" or "a Pew survey found." It has to be usable by someone who isn't a philosopher or demographer, in maybe an afternoon between the draft and filing, and it has to survive the magazines fact-checkers and my editor. Keep the distinctions that actually matter under those constraints and tell me which ones I can drop.

Then apply it to three cases I'm likely to hit:
1. A new survey finds adults under 30 report more anxiety than adults the same age did in 2004, and my editor wants "Gen Z is the most anxious generation yet."
2. Government data shows the median Boomer household has far more wealth than the median Millennial household had at the same age, and the pitch is "Boomers won the wealth game."
3. An industry report says canned tuna sales are down among people born 1981–1996, and the pitch is "Millennials killed canned tuna."

Show how the procedure plays out on each, including at least one case where it gives an answer my editor won't like. Flag the points where the procedure runs out and a human editor has to make the call anyway.

And don't hand me a menu of options or a balanced survey. Take a definite position: tell me which tests to run, in what order, and make actual calls on the three examples, with reasons. I'd rather have a procedure that's defensible and occasionally wrong than a hedge that's always safe.
