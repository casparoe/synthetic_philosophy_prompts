The law of armed conflict, as standardly understood, draws a sharp line between two attacks that kill the same number of civilians. A bomber may strike a munitions factory while foreseeing that nearby civilians will be killed, provided the civilian deaths are not excessive in relation to the military advantage; the deaths are classified as collateral damage. But if the same bomber instead strikes a residential area in order to terrorise the civilian population and break its will to fight, the act is classified as a war crime. What account of action, intention, and moral responsibility does that distinction presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits naturally with, and say whether the various parts of the practice presuppose consistent things. Then tell me what would have to change in the law and in its underlying theory if that view of intention were replaced by a straightforwardly consequentialist outlook, under which only outcomes matter. Write the answer in American English.
