I am a PhD student in decision theory, and this term I am running a weekly 90-minute section for an intermediate philosophy course on practical rationality. The section has about 16 undergraduates who have met expected utility theory in lecture but have not yet felt why its axioms are meant to be requirements of coherence rather than expressions of taste. I would like a runnable in-class game that makes them feel the force of the coherence argument for expected utility maximization: that a person whose preferences violate certain consistency conditions can be led, by their own choices, into a series of trades each of which they accept individually but which together leave them strictly worse off, and that the axioms are what rule out exactly this kind of exploitability.

I do not want to lecture the argument first. I want them to make choices, discover from inside the activity that they have been money-pumped or Dutch-booked, and only then have the formal structure named. The payoff scheme should use points toward a trivial prize rather than real money, and it should be designed so that at least some students predictably fall into the trap without being humiliated by it.

Please give me a complete plan: the setup and materials; a timeline for the 90 minutes; the exact sequence of choices or trades; how to use pairs or small groups if that helps; and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the formal features — the per-choice reasoning that seemed sensible at the time, the pattern of preferences that permitted the pump, the specific axiom violations (transitivity, or the independence-style failures behind Allais-type choices) that opened the door, and the conclusion toward which the debrief should build: that the coherence conditions of expected utility theory are motivated precisely by closing off such exploitable patterns, and that conforming to them is what permits representation as maximizing expected utility. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students who decline the bets, students who notice the trap and strategically guard their preferences, students who treat the game as a joke — and how I should handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the coherence argument the activity cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply myself in the debrief. I suspect the game can show that incoherent preferences are exploitable, but cannot by itself show that exploitability is the right standard of irrationality; if that is right, tell me what to say to bridge that gap without undercutting what the activity showed.
