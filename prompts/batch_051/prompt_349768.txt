Until last year, the governance of marine cloud brightening was, for me, a problem I argued about in the abstract. It has since become personal: my country has asked me to serve on a delegation that will advise whether we should join a regional feasibility consortium, and the coastline where my parents live appears, by name, in the consortium’s own risk-sharing simulations. I am now scheduled to present the paper described below, and I want to be ready for the questions this particular audience will actually raise.

The paper defends a conditional and explicitly precautionary thesis about marine cloud brightening (MCB): not that it should be banned, but that it must be governed as the kind of intervention it actually is. The gravest risk of a regional MCB program is not the direct effect of the brightening but the commitment it creates. Once a program has been running long enough to suppress a meaningful amount of regional warming, stopping it abruptly would return that masked warming over years rather than decades, producing a termination shock worse than the no-intervention baseline in several impact models. My thesis is that a decision to begin such a program is therefore a decision to make an open-ended, hard-to-reverse commitment, and that this commitment cannot legitimately be made by a single state or a small consortium. It requires, before deployment begins, an upfront pact that includes a funded and institutionally credible termination path, liability for transboundary harm (including harm caused by the interruption of a program), and the consent not merely of the states that would receive the cooling benefit but also of the states that would bear the largest termination risk.

The argument has three parts. First, the asymmetry claim: deployment is cheap and quick to begin, but ending it safely is expensive, slow, and politically fraught, so the ex ante decision carries the full weight of the exit problem. Second, the second-order moral hazard claim: my worry is not the familiar one that the mere availability of MCB will weaken emissions cuts, but rather that the institutions needed to make a termination pact credible are the very institutions that have so far failed to secure comparable commitments on decarbonization. Third, the standing claim: consent to alter the heat budget of a shared atmosphere cannot be inferred from local benefit or from the absence of a presently attributable harm.

The audience is a working group of our regional climate security council. It includes two aerosol–cloud physicists who are sympathetic to experimentation but skeptical of drawing governance conclusions from forcing estimates they regard as badly underdetermined; a development economist who works on adaptation finance and has written on moral hazard in climate finance, though not on geoengineering as such; two diplomats from low-lying states, one who wants research accelerated and one who suspects any regional deployment lets major emitters off the hook; a representative of a reinsurance consortium who will be thinking about whether the liability I want priced can be priced at all; and a civil-society observer from a large coastal city who campaigns against what she regards as technofixes. They have read the paper.

Please give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. I want the seven or eight objections I will actually receive from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging each would be, most dangerous first, each phrased the way a member of this audience would actually put it. For each objection, give a reply that would fit in two minutes, a note on where that reply is weakest, and a clear indication of what to concede and what to hold. End with the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
