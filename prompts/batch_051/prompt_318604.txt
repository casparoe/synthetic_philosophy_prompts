I retired a few years ago from a career in glaciology, and I've been spending my time on philosophy and game theory. I keep running into the same problem: my gut instincts about one-shot prisoner's dillemmas don't line up with any single principle I've read. Hofstadter's superrationality — the idea that in a symmetric one-shot dilemma you should pick the move that would be best if the other player, reasoning exactly as you do, lands on the same move — made it clearer and more confusing at once. I want to know what I actually think, so I'm going to give you my verdicts on some cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them.

Throughout, by the standard payoffs I mean: mutual cooperation gives each player 10; if I defect while the other cooperates I get 11 and he gets 0; if I cooperate while he defects I get 0 and he gets 11; mutual defection gives each player 1.

A. Me against an exact duplicate of me, same information, same instructions, same everything. I cooperate. Not close.

B. Same payoffs, but the other player is a stranger the experimenter has truthfully described to me as a perfectly rational, purely self-interested money-maximizer, and he's been told the same about me. I defect. Also not close.

C. Same payoffs, other player is just some anonymous person recruited online, one shot, no communication, no reputation. This is where I flip-flop. Most days I lean defect, but when I imagine the other person sitting there reasoning the way I reason, cooperating pulls at me.

D. Same payoffs, but the experimenter tells me the other player is 'very similar to me' — same age, same education, same kind of person who reads the same sort of stuff — though we've never met and there's no causal link between our choices. I cooperate, but I want there to be a justification, because my scientific reflex says that if there's no causal channel, his move is fixed relative to mine and I should just defect.

E. Now soften the payoffs so the temptation and the sucker's payoff are both mild: mutual cooperation is 10 each, defecting against a cooperator is 11, cooperating against a defector is 8, and mutual defection is 9. Here I cooperate even against a total anonymous stranger, and I feel settled about it. The dominance structure is exactly the same — defecting is always 1 better for me than cooperating, whatever he does — but my gut flips.

F. The case I keep going back and forth on. Two players with different stakes: player A's payoffs are ten times player B's, but the dominance structure is the same. If both cooperate, A gets 100 and B gets 10; if A defects while B cooperates, A gets 105 and B gets 0; if A cooperates while B defects, A gets 0 and B gets 11; if both defect, A gets 5 and B gets 1. Both players have a dominant defect, and mutual cooperation is the jointly best outcome — 110 total, versus 105, 11, and 6. I have no stable gut here. Part of me says superrationality tells both to cooperate since it's jointly best. Another part says that's just demanding the low-stakes player pass up 11 for 10 for the high-stakes player's benefit, and I don't know whether the principle is doing real work or just dragging in a symmetry assumption.

So: is there a principle — superrationality, team reasoning, evidential decision theory, some rule about how similar to me the other player has to be before I treat his reasoning as a mirror of mine — that gets A through E, or close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to overturn, and what does it say about F? I'd rather have the cleanest honest statement of the principle, even if it means killing off one of my verdicts, than a baroque rule that saves all of them.
