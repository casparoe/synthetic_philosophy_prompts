I have been working through Fernand Braudel's conception of historical time, and in particular his insistence that history becomes more, rather than less, intelligible once one turns away from the fast time of events, decisions, and named individuals and attends to the longue durée: the nearly immobile time of geography, climate, and slowly altering social and economic structures. On his picture, the event is at best froth on the surface of slower currents, and the intermediate rhythms of conjunctures — price cycles, demographic movement, institutional drift — are the fastest objects that deserve sustained historical treatment.

A problem keeps tugging at me when I put this framework next to the present. The Anthropocene debate — the now-common proposal that human activity has become a force operating at the geological scale and that we have entered a new epoch — cuts in two directions at once against Braudel's hierarchy of times. On one reading, it vindicates the longue durée: the relevant object of study is no longer the campaign, the treaty, or the biography, but the slow accumulation of a species-wide way of extracting energy and transforming landscapes. On another reading, it threatens to collapse the hierarchy itself, because things that look like events — industrialization, the mid-twentieth-century acceleration of fossil-fuel use, the signing or rejection of an international agreement — now appear capable of altering the slowest-moving registers of all, within timescales that living agents can actually notice and contest.

I would like you to reason through, as concretely as you can, how Braudel would have responded had he lived to encounter this debate. Would he have welcomed the Anthropocene as a new object of longue-durée history, or would he have suspected the concept of smuggling an implausibly unified, quasi-individual agent — "humanity" — back into a framework designed to dethrone such agents? And would he have had to revise his low estimate of event-centered history once fast time and slow time begin to feed back on each other, or does his framework already contain the resources for that complication? Argue for one specific predicted position rather than merely canvassing the alternatives.
