I'm a novelist, about three drafts into a book about a real but pretty obscure 19th-century woman astronomer, and I've been going around with myself about how much I'm allowed to invent. At a residency last fall an older historical novelist kept making the same point: he said a novel about a real person is like a portrait painted after the sitter is dead, worked up from letters and old photographs instead of a sitting. The painter gets to choose the light, the clothes, the expression; the painting doesn't stop being a portrait just because it isn't a photograph. What the painter can't do is paint a face the person never had. He used it to draw a line I actually find pretty attractive: inventing the inner life—thoughts, feelings, conversations nobody recorded—is just choosing the light. But inventing an outward event that changes the documented record is like painting in a scar they never had, so it isn't a portrait of that person anymore, just a picture of someone else with their name on the plaque.

The problem is that the hinge of my book is a scene I invented where she, during a fight with a male colleague over credit for a comet, deliberately misfiles his observation notes so his numbers won't reconcile. There's no record anything like that happened, but its exactly the kind of thing the archive wouldn't record, and it does a lot of work for the book's whole argument about her.

So audit the analogy for me, because I can feel it doing more than it's licensed to do. Lay out what corresponds to what—sitter, painter, canvas, viewer, light, wardrobe, scar, photograph, the whole lot. Then tell me where the portrait picture breaks. Which of those breaks actually matter for the inside/outside line he drew, and which are just noise? What does the portrait picture smuggle in that a novel about the dead doesn't have—consent, for one, but I want your list. And the real question: does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table—is inventing inner life defenitely safer than inventing an outward act like the misfiled notes? If the analogy is carrying more than it can, what's a better one?
