I want you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two people who disagree about whether pure time preference is defensible. Call them the Discounter and the Time-Neutralist.

The Discounter's view, in wording they'd sign: other things being equal—same size of benefit, same number of people, same certainty—a benefit that occurs later is worth less than one that occurs sooner, just because it's later. The Time-Neutralist's view, in wording they'd sign: temporal position is like spatial position; a life doesn't matter less because it begins later, so the only legitimate reasons to discount future well-being are uncertainty, opportunity costs, and the chance the relevant people won't exist—never the sheer passage of time.

Write the document this collaboration would have produced. It should include:

1. A numbered "agreed claims" section both can sign. Make these neither vacuous (not just "future people matter somewhat") nor slanted. Include what each means by discounting, what they agree the dispute is not about (e.g., neither is denying that uncertainty matters), and any substantive convergence they reached.

2. A disagreements table: each row states the issue neutrally, then the Discounter's position in wording the Discounter would accept, then the Time-Neutralist's position in wording the Time-Neutralist would accept. Watch out that the "neutral" statement of the issue doesn't already smuggle in one side's framing.

3. The cruxes: the subquestions such that a settled answer to one of them would move at least one side. I suspect one is whether temporal partiality is more like spatial partiality (which they both treat as a bias) or more like partiality to one's own children (which the Discounter at least thinks may be legitimate); another is whether "the future is later" and "the future contains people who don't yet exist" can be pried apart; another is whether a small pure time preference, applied consistently across centuries, compounds into the far future counting for almost nothing.

4. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Include this one: a two-planets case where a fixed resource can produce exactly the same certain welfare gain either for presently existing people or for people who will exist in 500 years, with no overlapping population, no uncertainty, and no way to help both; each party has to say in advance what they predict their own and the other's response to that case reveals.

Keep it constructive: the deliverable is something both could sign while still disagreeing. Where you judge part of the dispute to be merely verbal—e.g., because one is talking about policy evaluation and the other about what's fundamentally valuable—say so and seperate it from the substantive disagreement. Don't adjudicate who's right and don't write it as a dialogue.
