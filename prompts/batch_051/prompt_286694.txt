Two colleagues of mine got into it at lunch over how much we should lean on the AI tools our office now uses. (1) The first said he has the AI draft reports from the numbers our team hands over, and he reviews each draft carefully before it goes out, so the arrangement is no different in kind from a calculator or a spellchecker: the machine does the mechanical part and he supplies the judgment. (2) The second said that is exactly what worries him. A spellchecker never decides what the message should say, but the AI is already choosing how to interpret the numbers, and reviewing its output cannot catch a slant he would never have thought to look for; either he could already have done the analysis himself, in which case the tool saved him little, or he could not have, in which case he is certifying work he cannot verify. (3) The first replied that this is true of every tool we trust: when a spreadsheet computes a figure, it is doing arithmetic we do not redo, and when we run a statistics package, we sign off on steps we could not reproduce from memory; so the second's principle would force us to abandon all of our software. (4) The second said there is still a line between a tool whose steps you could in principle inspect and a tool whose steps are not written down anywhere for you to inspect; with the AI, "I reviewed it" collapses into "it sounded right." (5) The first answered that "in principle inspectable" is doing no actual inspecting, since nobody opens the source code; the only test that ever actually happens with either kind of tool is whether its output holds up against the data and the client later. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right and briefly justify that verdict; then say what you think the best overall view of the matter is, including anything both of them are missing.
