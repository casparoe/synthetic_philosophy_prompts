I am presenting next month at a national-security law institute to an audience of mostly constitutional lawyers and continuity-of-government planners, plus two political theorists who work on democratic legitimacy. My paper defends this thesis: the designated-survivor convention is justifiable only as a temporary placeholder whose purpose is to buy time for reconstituting elected leadership, not as a stable emergency executive, so the survivor's authority should carry a hard sunset and codified, public selection criteria rather than quiet executive discretion. My argument runs three steps: (1) continuity of government serves democratic values only if it eventually restores elected leadership rather than merely preserving an unelected one; (2) the survivor's authority should therefore extend no further than the restoration task requires; and (3) current practice, in which a survivor is selected by secretive executive choice and then treated as an ordinary successor, exceeds that warrant. Prepare me for the question period. Draft a prep sheet as a table with one row per objection, ordered by how likely it is to be raised times how damaging it would be. Columns: (a) the objection phrased as this audience would actually phrase it, and who raises it; (b) the damage it does to my argument; (c) what I should concede; (d) my two-to-three-sentence reply; and (e) the softest point in that reply. After the table, write one paragraph naming the question I most hope nobody asks and explaining what makes it dangerous. Make the objectors strong rather than strawmen, with the constitutional lawyers pressing on legal authorization, the planners on operational reality, and the political theorists on democratic delegation.
