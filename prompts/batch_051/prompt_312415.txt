I have been running an informal experiment with a friend who is fond of puzzle-book pattern tasks, using miniature Bongard problems instead of the usual drawn figures. Each item is a set of black squares on an otherwise empty three-by-three grid, and I name the cells as follows: top-left, top-middle, top-right; middle-left, centre, middle-right; bottom-left, bottom-middle, bottom-right. I first showed her six grids that belong to the concept and six that do not, and she classified all twelve without hesitation. Her "yes" grids were: (1) top-left, centre, bottom-right; (2) top-right, centre, bottom-left; (3) top-left, middle-left, bottom-left, bottom-middle, bottom-right; (4) top-left, top-middle, top-right, centre, bottom-left; (5) top-left, middle-right, bottom-middle, bottom-right; (6) top-left, top-middle, top-right, middle-left, bottom-left, bottom-right. Her "no" grids were: (1) centre alone; (2) top-left and bottom-left; (3) top-left and top-middle; (4) top-left, top-middle, middle-left and centre; (5) top-left, top-right, bottom-left and bottom-right; (6) top-left, top-middle and bottom-left.

The next three grids I intend to put to her are: (i) top-left, top-middle, middle-left and bottom-middle; (ii) top-left, top-right and bottom-middle; (iii) top-left, middle-left, bottom-middle and bottom-right. I doubt that twelve training grids pin down a unique rule, which is rather the point of the exercise. Predict how she will classify each of the three new grids, lay out the candidate rules that are consistent with all twelve of her earlier calls, say which rule you would back as the one she is implicitly running and why, and specify the single additional grid that would most efficiently settle the matter.
