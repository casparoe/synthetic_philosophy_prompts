I find myself pulled in two directions about mental privacy, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a winner.

On reflection, I am attracted to a deflationary theory: mental privacy is not a distinct kind of right but an application of ordinary informational privacy. What makes certain uses of neural data wrongful—discrimination, covert persuasion, blackmail, trading on intimate information, violating a reasonable expectation of confidence—is exhausted by the wrongness of analogous uses of highly sensitive behavioral or physiological data, such as search history, location traces, or health records. The fact that the data is recorded from the brain adds nothing to the wrong-making features; the skull is not a normative boundary, and positing a special category of mental privacy seems to smuggle in a dualism I otherwise disclaim. The theory's virtues are real: it unifies mental privacy with an existing framework, avoids mystery, and gives me a decision procedure—ask what an equivalent use of sensitive behavioral data would justify.

But I also have a firm intuition that will not go away. Consider a brain-computer interface, or a noninvasive neural decoder, that records the neural antecedents of a person's speech, or the subvocal rehearsal that occurs before a thought is endorsed, without altering anything and without any downstream use of the recording: no one reads it afterward, nothing is stored beyond the session, and the person never learns that it happened. I still want to say that something normatively important has occurred—that a space has been entered, not just a piece of information collected. I want to say that the wrong, if there is one, lies in being read at the neural level before one has chosen what to express, and that a person has a right to be the first audience of their own nascent thoughts, or not to have such thoughts turned into objects at all. If mental privacy is just applied informational privacy, this intuition should be a confusion: with no use and no downstream harm, there is nothing to complain about. Yet I do complain.

I am not asking which side wins. I want to know, with the strongest version of each on the table: should the intuition be diagnosed and dropped, for instance as a lingering folk-dualism or as an artifact of how vivid we find brains; should the theory be amended or abandoned because it cannot capture a distinctive wrong of unauthorized access to the pre-endorsed mind; or is there a reading on which both are preserved—for example, because the intuition is tracking cognitive liberty, a right over one's inner activity, rather than mental privacy, a right over information, or because informational privacy can be extended to treat mere extraction as a harm when the target is the unexpressed mind? Specify what would justify each move, not just which move you would make.
