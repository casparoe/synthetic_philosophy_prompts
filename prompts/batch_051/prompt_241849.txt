So I got into a three-hour argument with a friend last night about whether games are art, and I ended up defending this thing I've been calling the shadow space of a game. I want to know if it's any good before I bring it up again.

Here's the idea. When I think about the games that have actually felt like more than a toy to me, what sticks isn't just what I did in my playthrough. It's the feeling of all the other playthroughs i didn't do. In a good game, at any moment I can sort of feel the shape of the options I'm not taking — the door I didn't open, the move I almost made, the version of my character I let go. I'm calling that the shadow space. The claim is that what we're appreciating when we appreciate a game as art is the aesthetic quality of this shadow space: the way the game makes unchosen possibilities present, not just as menu options but as something with weight. A game is artful to the extent that it makes me feel the loss, or the curiosity, or the dread, of the versions I didn't pick. I guess that's a claim about the aesthetics of agency — the art is in the choosing, not the chosen. Which sounds kind of pretentious when I say it out loud, but it's the only thing that tracked what I actually felt.

This is why a game whose whole gimmick is that a voice narrates the choices you're making and the paths you're skipping is interesting even though the choices are mostly fake. And I think it's why a game with ugly graphics and no story can still be beautiful, if the choice architecture has that quality of making you feel the life you aren't living. I'd even say this about chess — the tragedy is in the moves you saw and didn't play, not the moves that actually happened.

My friend said three things. One, that I've just reinvented the idea of a possibility space and added a mood to it. Two, that by my defintion a Choose Your Own Adventure book, or a well-arranged salad bar, has a shadow space too, so either those are art or the definition is way too broad. Three, that the unchosen branches are in my head, not in the game, so I'm giving the game credit for my own imagination.

So: is this a real concept or just a metaphor getting too big for its britches? Does it already exist under another name? What does it get right, if anything, and where does it break? Give me the strongest objection he didn't make, and tell me whether any of it is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. Be mean if you have to.
