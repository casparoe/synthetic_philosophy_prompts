Spent last night arguing with a friend about Arrow's impossibility theorem, and I came away stuck on an asymmetry I cant get out of my head.

If one voter says they prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, everyone treats them as irrational, unserious, a crank. Their preferences are incoherant and that's the end of it. But when three voters, each with perfectly ordinary transitive rankings, produce exactly that same cycle in the aggregate -- A beats B, B beats C, C beats A under majority rule -- nobody says the electorate is irrational. They say the rule has a Condorcet cycle, the agenda was rigged, or (if they know Arrow) that no ranking rule can satisfy a few innocent-looking conditions at once. The same intransitive pattern is called a defect when it lives in one head and just an unavoidable artifact when it lives in a group.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry, or whether it's a double standard we've learned to accept because the group case is mathematically unavoidable. The obvious candidate is that a group isn't a single mind, so there's no "it" to be rational or irrational. But we do talk about the will of the electorate, hold committees responsible for incoherent decisions, and treat parties as agents when they flip-flop, so that line only sometimes gets used -- which is exactly the kind of thing my friend and I went in circles about.

Give me the strongest principle you can find that makes intransitivity irrational in an individual but not in a group of rational individuals. Then test it on the nearby cases that came up. A committee of three people who literally share the same cyclic preferences: is the committee irrational, or just three people each with the same defect? A single person whose cycle comes from context-dependence -- a different self in each pairwise comparison -- rather than a stable all-things-considered ranking: still irrational? And a group that cycles not because of coherent disagreement but because one member controls the agenda: does that group now have the kind of defect the plain majority cycle lacked? Then give me an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.

On the very last line, on its own, write \answer{justified} or \answer{double standard}.
