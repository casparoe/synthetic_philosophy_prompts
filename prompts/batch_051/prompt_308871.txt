I've been going around and around on when thinking about your own thinking actually helps and when it actively hurts, and I'd like someone to help me extract a principle instead of just shrugging. Here are the cases as I actually feel them.

A tennis player has hit forehands fine for years; mid-match he starts monitoring his wrist angle and weight transfer on every shot. His forehand falls apart. I think the self-monitoring made him worse.

A chess player after losing a game sits down and reconstructs what he was thinking at each blunder — why he misevaluated the position, what he overlooked. That seems clearly helpful, not harmful.

Someone lying in bed thinks "am I asleep yet?" over and over, checking whether she's drifting off. She stays awake longer. I'd say the checking is doing harm.

A person in an argument realizes he's starting to feel defensive; he pauses and thinks "why am I reacting this strongly, what exactly is the threat I'm registering?" Then he actually hears the other person better. That's helpful, and it feels structurally pretty similar to the tennis player's monitoring — it's online, in the middle of the activity — but I judge them opposite.

A diarist writes every evening about her own mental life — moods, trains of thought, why she did what she did. She says it helps her run her life. I want to count that as helpful.

A guy can't decide between two jobs. He starts tracking his own deliberation: "am I being fair to the salary factor? am I ignoring my gut? what is my gut, anyway?" and after a week he's more tied in knots then before. I'd say the meta-deliberation made it worse, even though the chess reconstruction feels similar in form.

An anxious person keeps asking "do I still feel anxious right now?" and rating it 1 to 10. The feeling gets worse. I think the monitoring is feeding it.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn and why? And what does the resulting principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a surgeon who, while operating, narrates each step in her head — "now I'm checking for bleeders, now I'm deciding whether to widen the incision" — when she normally operates fluently without any inner narration?
