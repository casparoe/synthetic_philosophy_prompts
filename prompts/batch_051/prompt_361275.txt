For a course on early modern political philosophy, I have to lead a fifty-minute discussion section for first-years on Locke's account of how labour grounds private property and the proviso he places on it. I don't want an explanation of the view itself; I want a teaching plan. Which commitments have to be secured first — a person's ownership of their own body, the earth being given to humankind in common, the idea that working on something changes its normative standing — and what would break if I presented them in the other order? What single concrete example should carry the session, and what features must it have and lack — in particular, would the obvious case of gathering wild acorns teach something subtly wrong, and if so, what should replace it? Which misconceptions will first-years most likely bring or form, and at exactly what point in the sequence should I head each off? I suspect two: that Locke is offering a labour theory of value, and that the enough-and-as-good condition only needs to hold at the moment of first acquisition. I would also like to know what should be deliberately left out for this audience and why. Finally, what one closing question would show whether they have understood the proviso rather than merely restated it? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
