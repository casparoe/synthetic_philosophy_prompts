Some years ago I adopted a policy about my own decisions, and I have started to suspect that the policy may be doing more to protect my self-image than to protect my reasoning. The policy is this: while a choice is still genuinely open to me, I decline to assign any probability to what I will in fact choose. Last month I was deciding whether to leave my university post for a position at a startup, and when a friend asked what I honestly thought the odds were that I would go, I told her the question was ill-posed. As I explained it, a decision that is still mine to make is not yet an event in the world with a standing chance of occurring; to say that I would probably leave was not to predict my decision but to half-make it, and it would displace the very act of deliberation I was trying to perform. A mutual friend pushed back. She said I predict other people's decisions with reasonable success, that a third party who knew my history, my preferences, and my fear of disruption would be happy to give odds on my staying put, and that my refusal looked like a tactic for never facing how predictable I am. And indeed, in the past year I have refused to give odds on three significant choices, and each time I eventually took the safe, conventional option.

I want a serious assessment of this habit. Is my refusal to assign probabilities to my own impending choices defensible, or is it simply a bias dressed up as a principle about agency? Under what conditions, if any, is it appropriate to treat one's own future choice as an object of prediction, and under what conditions does doing so undermine deliberation? Is there a defensible version of the policy — for example, one that permits third-personal or conditional predictions while forbidding first-personal ones made in the middle of deliberating — and how would I tell, from the inside, which situation I am in?
