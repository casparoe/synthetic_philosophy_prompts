I am a novelist writing a campus novel about a hospital ethics committee chaired by Dr. Ellery, a philosopher whose published work applies T. M. Scanlon's *What We Owe to Each Other*, especially its treatment of aggregation. In the book, she frequently paraphrases a passage from that work: a technician in urgent need of medical attention is not to have his rescue traded away for the disappointment of millions who would miss a television broadcast, because many small complaints cannot be summed to outweigh one serious one. In an upcoming chapter the committee must vote on a new case, and I want to know how Dr. Ellery would vote if she were consistent with her own past rulings.

Her recorded committee votes so far:

1. When a donated liver could go to one patient who would die without it or to five patients who would also die without it, she voted to give it to the five, saying the death claims were of the same kind and could simply be counted.
2. When a research proposal would have caused one participant a severe allergic reaction in order to spare ten thousand participants one day of mild nasal congestion, she voted against it, saying the many mild complaints could not be added together to outweigh one severe one.
3. When the committee had to choose between treating one patient's severe chronic pain and running a program that would relieve fifty patients' mild stiffness, she voted to treat the one patient, again rejecting aggregation.
4. When a triage decision involved one patient with a severe but nonlethal head injury and one patient with a moderate wrist fracture, she voted to treat the head injury first, though she said the wrist fracture was not a trivial claim.

The new case is this: an operating room must be assigned to either one patient with a severe pelvic fracture requiring eight hours of surgery, or fifteen patients with simple wrist fractures each requiring forty minutes. The wrist fractures are painful and temporarily disabling but routine; the pelvic fracture is far more serious for that individual but not life-threatening if treated within the day.

What should Dr. Ellery vote if she wants to be consistent with her own precedents? Which of her past cases is the closest analog? I can see at least two rules she might be running: a strict no-aggregation-of-unequal-claims rule and a threshold rule that allows aggregation once each lesser claim is sufficiently serious. Where do these rules come apart in the new case, and which does her record better support? If her record genuinely underdetermines it, what principle would she be adopting with each vote, and what further hypothetical case would most clearly reveal which rule she actually holds?
