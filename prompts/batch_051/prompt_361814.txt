A friend of mine who works in AI alignment keeps insisting that building a "low-impact" agent is basically an engineering problem: you just add a penalty for changing the world. I don't buy it, but I've been struggling to articulate why beyond the standard examples everyone already knows. I'm writing an explainer for a reading group and want one clean, original case to hang the whole thing on. Can you build me a thought experiment that isolates exactly this issue?

Here's the spec. Set the scenario in a mundane setting—a home, an office, a warehouse; no trolleys, no sci-fi, no strange physics. There's an agent with a competant model of its environment, and its objective is to get reward for completing an ordinary task, minus a penalty proportional to how much it changes a small, human-chosen set of state variables, the kind of "side effect meter" a sensible layperson would design. The penalty is a purely formal proxy for "avoid side effects"; the designer has no full specification of value. I want a case where the agent completes the task successfully and keeps the formal penalty very low, but does something a normal person would call obviously bad—and the badness has to come from the avoid-side-effects term specifically, not from a badly specified task, not from a simulator bug or wireheading, and not because the agent was dumb about how the world works. After the scenario, walk through the agent's options in the agent's own terms: why the bad action is the argmax given the penalty, and why a human recoils from it. Then say which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if you remove that feature, and how a defender of the naive penalty would try to dismiss the case—and whether that defense actually succeeds.

Then do a second pass. Replace the naive penalty with an attainable-utility-preservation measure: roughly, penalize actions insofar as they reduce the agent's ability to later achieve a wide range of possible goals. Tell me whether the same case still goes bad, goes bad in a different way, or comes out fine. I want the last line of your answer to be a short verdict in this format: \answer{...}, where the contents are either "operationalizable", "not operationalizable", or "only in toy cases", depending on the upshot of your analysis.
