I commission a weekly science-and-ideas podcast for a British broadcaster. About once a month, something resurfaces about a guest we've already booked — an old blog post, a decade-old tweet, a clip from a conference Q&A — and we have to decide in a day or two whether to keep them, drop them, or keep them with some sort of on-air note. We can usually get four producers and me in a room for half an hour, and my editor has to sign off, but that's about all the process we have. I've read a bit of the punishment and social sanction literature — desert, proportionality, expressive condemnation, reintegration, the difference between a court punishing and a public doing it — but I can't turn it into something we can actually run.

Could you write me a reusable decision procedure for these cases, not an essay on cancel culture? I want a checklist or flow of questions we can work through in the meeting, in order. For each question, say what counts as an adequate answer, where the procedure should stop with a 'no', and where it should hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Mark the judgement calls honestly rather than hiding them inside formal language. In particular, I want the procedure to force us to seperate three things we keep blurring: whether the conduct was wrong, whether a social sanction is warranted as punishment, and whether keeping the guest would be read as endorsement. And work through how social sanctions differ from legal ones — no due process, no fixed sentence, no proportionality check, and a pile-on can be effectively permanent. It should also say what it can't do — e.g., it can't tell us how the audience will react, or settle the underlying culture war.

Then run the procedure on these three:
1. A scientist we booked to discuss reproducibility wrote a 2016 blog post alleging that harassment complaints in a prominent lab were careerist and had bypassed proper channels. She retracted it in 2021, apologised to the people involved, and has since done a lot of work on research-integrity processes. A campaign group is now circulating the old post and demanding we drop her.
2. A philosopher booked to talk about free speech published, two months ago, a deliberately provocative essay comparing deplatforming to a village mob, except the village is global and never forgets. Some listeners say it trivialises harm; he says it's about proportionality, not impunity, and he won't take it down or apologise.
3. An early-career researcher we'd like to feature wrote a flippant thread during a controversy, was quote-tweeted into a pile-on, and lost a position over it. She has apologised and is trying to move on. Dropping her might avoid reigniting the pile-on, but keeping her might add another sanction on top of one that already looks disproportionate.

Finally, please do two meta things. Say which steps in the proceedure are treated as settled even among people who disagree about cancel culture, and which rest on contested views of punishment or free speech — and don't present a contested view as the consensus. And if the procedure returns an answer that would make my life harder, don't soften it.
