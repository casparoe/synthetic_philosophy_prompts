I have been thinking about desuetude — the idea, as I understand it, that a law which has gone unenforced for a long time can simply lapse — and I cannot make it fit together with the rule of law. Suppose a town still has an 1890s ordinance against swearing in public. No one has been charged under it in living memory, the police call it a historical curiosity, and residents would be shocked if it were suddenly enforced. Is that ordinance still law? If non-enforcement can extinguish a law, then every time a police officer or prosecutor declines to enforce a statute, they would seem to be starting the clock that could eventually erase it; with enough patience, officials could nullify laws they dislike merely by looking the other way. But if non-enforcement can never extinguish a law, then the ordinance is just as much law today as it was in 1895, and the fact that everyone treats it as dead is legally irrelevant. What I want to understand is whether desuetude can be a coherent middle position between these two alternatives, or whether it just collapses into one of them. If there is a middle position, what is doing the work — the passage of time, the non-enforcement itself, or something about the community's attitude toward the rule?
