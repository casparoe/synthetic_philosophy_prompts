i keep getting pulled in two directions by what Nietzsche says about diet and health, and i'd rather have someone map the conflict than hand me a verdict. the view i'm drawn to is something like this: a philosophy is in part a symptom of the philosopher's body — what they ate, how well they digested, the climate they lived in, the state of their health. he seems to mean this fairly literally, not just as a figure of speech: heavy food makes for heavy thinking, bad digestion produces bad arguments, and a philosophical system expresses the physiology of the person who built it. read that way, it becomes a debunking tool — when a system seems wrong you get to ask whether it is an expression of sickness or of health, and the answer is supposed to bear on whether you should accept it.

but i also can't shake the conviction that none of that should matter to whether the arguments are any good. an inference is valid or invalid on its own; what the author ate is a fact about the causal history of a belief, not a reason for or against it. if i had to check a philosopher's digestion before taking their view seriously, argument would be theater, and i dont think it is. so here's the clash: should my intuition about the autonomy of reasons be the thing that goes? or should the symptom-reading get weakened until it becomes only a vivid metaphor, and if so, whats left of the debunking force? or is there a third reading where a thought can be both a physiological event and still answerable only to reasons? and i'm not even sure how you'd sort the healthy from the sick physiology without already using the very standards of good thinking the body was supposed to explain. walk me through what each move commits me to, what gets lost on each side, and how i'd actually tell them apart.
