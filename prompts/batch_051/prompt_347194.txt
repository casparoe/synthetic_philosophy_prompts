Consider the argument that gives the handicap principle its force. When a receiver cannot directly inspect the quality that a signal advertises, and low-quality signalers would profit from mimicking high-quality displays, the argument runs, any display that is equally affordable to all will eventually be ignored; a display can therefore remain informative only if it is differentially costly, in the sense that producing or maintaining it is systematically harder for low-quality signalers than for high-quality ones. On this view, the signal's reliability is explained by the sender's cost rather than by the receiver's checking. The peacock's tail is the canonical illustration: it is taken to be honest because a low-condition bird pays more for a given level of ornament than a high-condition bird pays.

Now consider a later development. A growing share of human signaling now moves through cheap, scalable verification: cryptographic authentication, tamper-evident credentials, third-party certification, public audit trails, and aggregated reputation systems. In these environments a receiver can often establish that a signal is accurate without the honest sender paying a continuing, display-specific cost. The signal is trustworthy because it is hard to counterfeit, and the expense falls mainly on would-be fakers or on the verification infrastructure itself rather than on the sender who uses it. A credential is cheap to display and expensive to forge; a seller pays nothing at the margin to display a large repository of third-party reviews. Here the honest signal is cheap to send, but low-quality signalers are still screened out.

Does the argument behind the handicap principle survive this development, or does it need revision, or does it simply cease to apply once verification becomes cheap? Focus on what happens to the argument itself, not on any particular example or policy. Identify the premise the cheap-verification development bears on, and say whether the principle can be restated—perhaps with the relevant cost relocated to the forger or to the verification system—in a way that preserves its original explanatory work.
