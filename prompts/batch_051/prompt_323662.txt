I'm putting together a seminar unit on the extended mind thesis, and I want to test whether the parity argument at the heart of the Otto case is genuinely sensitive to its conditions, or whether it can tolerate weakening them one by one. The familiar baseline: Inga stores and retrieves facts in ordinary biological memory. Otto, who has a memory impairment, always carries a notebook in which he writes down new information, and he consults it whenever the information is needed. The standard argument holds that the notebook is functionally equivalent, in the relevant respects, to Inga's biological memory — it is reliably available, directly consulted without effort, automatically endorsed, and its contents were endorsed by Otto at the time of entry — so that if Inga's memory is part of her cognitive system, Otto's notebook is part of his.

Now consider a modified Otto. His notebook has been replaced by a device that behaves exactly like the original notebook except in one respect: each day, every stored entry has an independent one-in-ten-thousand chance of being silently corrupted — a word replaced, a number altered, a name changed — with no signal that anything has gone wrong. Otto consults the device exactly as before and automatically endorses whatever it tells him. He has used it for years and has accumulated a large number of entries, so he is almost certainly carrying many false entries even though any particular entry is unlikely to be corrupted on any particular day. Assume, to keep the comparison fair, that Inga's biological memory is also imperfect, but with a different failure profile: ordinary forgetting and occasional misremembering, somewhat less frequent than the device's corruption but not zero. The modification is small by design; the question is how much it disturbs the parity argument.

Work through the standard analysis of this case rather than just giving an intuitive verdict. Specifically: (1) does the corrupted device still satisfy the four criteria, and do the criteria need to be restated in terms of typical availability and reliability rather than constant access and truthfulness? (2) Does the problem scale with the error rate — that is, is there some per-entry corruption probability at which the parity argument should stop counting the device as part of Otto's cognitive system even though it remains a constantly available, directly consulted, automatically endorsed store? If so, where is the threshold, and what principle sets it? (3) Is there a difference between asking whether the device is part of Otto's cognitive system and asking whether the contents it delivers are things Otto knows, and does the error rate force these two questions apart? I want the answer to identify exactly which part of the original parity argument the modification puts pressure on, and to say whether that part was doing more work than the standard presentation of the case admits.

Please structure your response in the following named sections, with approximate lengths:

1. Load-bearing claims of the original case (about 15%): restate the parity criteria and say which of them the modified case tests, and which it leaves untouched.
2. Point-by-point analysis (about 35%): evaluate each criterion against the corrupted device, carefully separating availability from reliability, direct access from accuracy, and automatic endorsement from truth.
3. The threshold question (about 25%): work out how the parity conclusion depends on the corruption rate; if you think there is a principled cutoff, derive it, and if you think any cutoff is arbitrary, explain what that shows about the original argument.
4. Mind versus knowledge (about 15%): analyze whether Otto knows the corrupted entries, and whether "the device is part of his mind" and "the device delivers knowledge" come apart in this case.
5. What survives the modification (about 10%): state which version of the extended mind thesis is left standing, and which popular gloss of the Otto case turns out to be false.
