Here's a policy I've caught myself following for a while now. Whenever someone in a discussion backs a philosophical point by appealing to a study that supposedly found cross-cultural differences in intuitions — say, that people in one country are much more willing to say someone "really knew" something even when the evidence was lucky, or that another group doesn't share the usual trolley-case intuitions — I basically tune it out. My reasoning is that these studies are bound to be messy: translated vignettes, tiny samples of the usual kind of participant, and no way to check whether the subjects were even using the same concept. So I treat all of them as noise until proven otherwise.

But my friend pointed out that I don't hold studies that find cross-cultural agreement to the same standard; I'll happily cite those to support universality. She's also right that I once dismissed a study for reasons I barely remebered, mostly because it would have forced me to rework a view I like. So what I want to know is whether the discounting itself is a defensible epistemic shortcut, or just a rule that launders motivated reasoning. Are there conditions under which I should actually update on a cross-cultural disagreement about intuitions, and what would a more defensible version of the policy look like?
