I've been trying to cut down on phone scrolling, so I downloaded one of those habit apps where you build a streak. You know the kind: the counter ticks up every day you don't do the thing, and if you slip once it slams back to zero, usually with a sad little animation. Last month I lost a 40-day streak, and the app didn't say I'd succeeded 39 of the last 40 days — it told me I was back at day zero. And weirdly I believed it; the whole 40 days felt erased. The same logic shows up in sobriety counters, Duolingo, gym challenges. One lapse and you're back to nothing, even though lapsing after 40 days seems like a very different fact about a person's self-control then lapsing after one day.

I'm trying to pin down what theory of temperance this design presupposes. It seems to treat self-control as a binary — you're either clean or you've relapsed — and to value uninterrupted continuity over something like your overall rate of success. That sounds less like the virtue of temperance (which is about the mean and about getting back up) and more like a purity rule, or maybe a crude bet about slippery slopes. So my question: what exactly does the streak practice commit itself to about self-control — virtue, prudence, repression, or something else? Does the practice strictly need that theory, or is it compatible with several? And I keep wanting to draw a distinction between aggregate self-control and continuous self-control; is that a real distinction or just two ways of scoring the same thing? If someone built a habit app on the opposite theory, what would it have to change?
