I'm a third-year undergraduate taking epistemology this term, with a philosophy of religion module, and I keep noticing a tension between how the two courses treat experience. In epistemology, perceptual experience gets a kind of default status: other things equal, if I have the visual experience of a mug on my desk, I am prima facie justified in believing that there is a mug on my desk, and anyone who wants to deny that needs a specific defeater such as bad lighting, a known hallucination, or a drug in my system. The same default is extended to memory and to ordinary testimony. But when the experience is religious, the usual treatment in much secular academic and public reasoning is the reverse. If someone reports an overwhelming sense of being in God's presence, a feeling of being addressed or commanded by something outside themselves, or a sudden and durable experience of forgiveness and peace that they attribute to God, the default is to withhold evidential weight until the report can be independently grounded: first show that God is likely to exist on other grounds, or explain the experience psychologically or neurologically, or assimilate it to culturally scripted emotional experience.

So the asymmetry I want analyzed is this: a class of experiences treated at face value as evidence for their apparent objects when the objects are ordinary, and a class of experiences treated as evidence only if their apparent object has already been established by other means when the object is God or something supernatural. Is this asymmetry justified, or is it an epistemic double standard that we keep because the stakes feel higher?
