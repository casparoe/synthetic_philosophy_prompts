So a friend of mine is taking a criminal law theory seminar and somehow our whole dinner group spent an hour on strict liability. One person kept saying that punishing someone who took every reasonable precaution is a moral scandal, full stop. Another kept saying we're missing the point: in a lot of regulatory and corporate settings liability without fault isn't really punishment in the classic sense, it's more like a price you pay for choosing to run a risky activity at scale. I don't think either of them actually landed the hard points, and I'm curious what the argument looks like when neither side is phoning it in.

Write a dialogue between two criminal law theorists who disagree about whether strict liability — and vicarious liability, if you think it's relevant — can ever be a justified basis for legal punishment. One should defend the claim that liability without any finding of fault is justified in at least some domains (product safety, environmental spills, corporate offenses, or a made-up example you come up with). The other should defend some version of the fault principle: a genuine criminal conviction and punishment requires culpability, at least negligence, even if that cost us some deterrence. Make both of them sharp; neither should be a strawman, and they should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other. A good version of this debate should eventually get into the "you're confusing punishment with a licensing fee" objection, the idea that negligence is just a finding that your precautions fell short of a standard you reasonably could have met, the expressive or stigmatizing function of a criminal conviction, and the question of whether fault comes in degrees or is more like a threshold. Set it wherever you like — a faculty office, a bar, a courtroom hallway. Aim for roughly 12 to 18 exchanges, and try to have at least one character partly concede or narrow their view in response to the other, instead of everyone just digging in.
