I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have come to suspect that we run a double standard in how we receive quantitative forecasts. A demographer projects a fiscal crisis from population aging and fertility trends, and reporters cover it as a finding, uncertainty intervals and all. An epidemiologist projects a disease wave from contact rates, and nobody calls that unscientific. But when a researcher builds a model from long-run historical data — real wages, state fiscal capacity, and something its practitioners call elite overproduction, meaning more aspirants for a fixed number of elite positions than the system can absorb — and forecasts a wave of political instability or state breakdown, the same editors tend to call it speculation, or insist that history is not the kind of thing one predicts. The second researcher works in the cliodynamic tradition, in the structural-demographic approach associated with Peter Turchin's work on secular cycles: long waves in which population growth compresses real wages, elite numbers expand faster than elite positions do, intra-elite competition intensifies, and state fiscal strain raises the odds of collapse. The model's variables are no less quantitative than the demographer's.

The question I want to work through is whether anything justifies that asymmetry in reception. The distinctions people reach for include: the established disciplines have a better out-of-sample track record; social and political systems are reflexive, strategic, and open-ended in a way demographic or epidemiological systems are not; outcomes like political instability are poorly defined and the historical record is selectively preserved; forecasts of disorder can be self-fulfilling or self-defeating in ways projections of aging or warming are not; and readers simply hold politically charged forecasts to a higher standard. I would like you to do three things. First, identify which of these distinctions, if any, genuinely separates the accepted forecasts from the dismissed ones, and say whether there is a better justification I have not listed. Second, test the best candidate against nearby cases where the asymmetry looks less stable: recession forecasting, which has a mediocre record yet retains authority; disease forecasts that themselves change behavior; climate projections intended to prevent the very outcome they predict; demographic projections that rest on assumptions about individual choices. Third, deliver a verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up? Please also tell me what would change your mind — which argument or evidence would push the verdict the other way — and where in your reasoning you think it is most likely to break.
