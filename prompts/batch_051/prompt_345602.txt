I'm making a "which theory of group minds do you actually hold?" quiz for my blog, and I want it to be the version that starts from the ant-colony question in GEB's Aunt Hillary / Ant Fugue dialogue and then pushes past the stock examples. My readers are the kind of people who read Hofstadter once, remember the anthill with the pheromone trails and the aunt who keeps saying the colony has a kind of mind, and have strong vibes but probably can't reconstruct the original argument.

Please write the quiz itself: 8-10 questions, each built around a concrete case or variation, with 2 or 3 answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions. Don't sort people by which label they like; each question should be a case where positions genuinely come apart, phrased so someone can answer without knowing it's really a functionalism-vs-emergentism question.

The cases I want in the pool are roughly: a mature leafcutter ant colony doing trail maintenance and nest repair; a single human brain considered as a society of neurons; the old nation-of-China setup where a billion people pass brain-implementing messages; a big transformer LLM running one forward pass per token; a decentralized prediction market aggregating forecasts; a slime mold solving a maze; and a company whose decisions all have to pass through one executive team. You can add or drop one of these if you have to, but say why if you drop one that matters.

The distinction I actually want the quiz to cut along is whether saying the group has a mind means (a) it has the right causal or information-processing organization, (b) it has some unified valence or phenomenology that isn't reducible to the members', (c) it has a serial bottleneck or global-workspace-ish integration point, or (d) it's loose talk and only the individual ants/neurons/traders have minds. I suspect there's also a mostly-empty cell for full group-level phenomenology with no group-level access consciousness, and another for boundary/homeostatic-individuality mattering more than integration; include those as leaves or explicitly note them as empty.

For the scoring, each option should be scored as evidence toward or against each leaf, and the key should say which patterns really separate positions and which leaves the quiz can't pull apart (I'd be surprised if the bottleneck view and the boundary view don't cling together on most items). End with the leaves as name-or-unnamed plus one sentence each on the position's most awkward commitment.

Keep the questions concrete and not leading. And its fine if the intro says this is an adapted, not-exegetical Ant Fugue, so I dont get mail about what Hofstadter really meant.
