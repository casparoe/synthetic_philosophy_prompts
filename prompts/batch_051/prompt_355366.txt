An argument broke out in my high school game theory study group over iterated deletion of dominated strategies, and I was not able to referee it. I would like a move-by-move adjudication. We had just read the rule that a pure strategy may be deleted if it is strictly dominated, where the dominating strategy may be a mixed strategy of the player's own actions. Both sides accepted the pure-strategy version. The dispute was over what the mixed-strategy version actually does, and over a mismatch they found in games with more than two players.

(1) Eli argued that mixed-strategy deletion is suspect. A rational player randomizes only when indifferent among the actions in the support, so the mixed strategy used as the "dominator" is not an action the player would ever deliberately choose. Using a lottery over one's own actions as the dominator, he said, lets you eliminate a strategy that no single pure strategy beats in every contingency. That is using an impossible choice to prove that another choice is impossible.

(2) Sasha replied that the mixed strategy is not a recommendation but a proof device. If some mixture of a player's actions yields a strictly higher payoff than strategy s against every profile of the opponents' actions, then against any probability distribution over those profiles the same mixture still yields a strictly higher expected payoff than s. Therefore s cannot maximize expected utility against any belief at all, and a player who maximizes expected utility cannot rationally play it. The absence of a single pure strategy that dominates s is irrelevant: different pure strategies can beat s in different contingencies, and the mixture covers all of them at once.

(3) Eli came back with what he called the deeper problem. The textbook also says, he pointed out, that this deletion procedure captures what is consistent with common knowledge that everyone maximizes expected utility. But he had seen a three-player example in which the procedure leaves a strategy that would be removed if each player's belief about the other two had to treat their choices as independent. If rationality is just maximizing expected utility against one's belief about what the others will do, why should independence of that belief matter? A strategy either is a best response to some belief or it is not.

(4) Sasha answered that the textbook's equivalence claim holds only if beliefs over the other players are allowed to be correlated. In a finite game, deleting strategies that are strictly dominated by mixed strategies is equivalent, at each stage, to deleting strategies that are not a best response to any distribution over the other players' remaining pure action profiles, with no independence condition. In a two-player game this distinction collapses, because there is only one opponent, and independence has nothing to bite on. With three or more players, treating the opponents' choices as independent is an extra assumption about how they are related, and common knowledge of rationality alone does not force it. So the textbook's equivalence claim has to be read with the correlated-belief interpretation; under the independent-belief interpretation that yields the rationalizable strategies, iterated dominance leaves too much and stops matching rationalizability.

Neither would budge, and I did not know enough to referee the dispute. Please adjudicate it move by move. For each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right, and briefly justify the assessment. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them missed or left ambiguous.
