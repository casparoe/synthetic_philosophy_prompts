I have recently been appointed a trustee of a small grant-making foundation. Its written guidance urges trustees to draw on their personal networks to bring strong applicants forward, and the board counts how many applications each trustee has personally sourced as a mark of good stewardship. The same guidance states that any trustee who knows an applicant socially or professionally must declare the relationship, withdraw from the room when the application is discussed, and refrain from discussing it with other trustees. The same relationship is therefore treated as our best source of knowledge about who is worth considering and as a contaminant to be excluded from our deliberations. Reconstruct the theory of partiality this pair of practices presupposes: is partiality a virtue during the discovery of candidates but a bias during their assessment, and can that line be held? Say whether the two elements are consistent, which rival views of partiality and impartiality they quietly rule out, where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and what would concretely change if the foundation consistently adopted the view that partiality is always a bias or, alternatively, that it is a legitimate source of reasons.
