I cover music and tech, and I've spent the last few weeks trying to write a story about why some kinds of musical borrowing are basically free while others can cost a fortune. The thing I can't get past is this: in the U.S., a single recorded song is two pieces of property at once. There's the copyright in the "musical work" — the composition, the song behind the record — and there's a separate copyright in the "sound recording," the actual audio captured on that master. You can see it on a label: the © next to the songwriter and the ℗ next to the label. The practical consequences are strange. A band can record a cover of your song without asking you; they just buy a compulsory mechanical license and pay a statutory rate. But if someone samples two seconds of the actual record, they have to clear the sound recording, and the label can charge whatever it wants or say no entirely. So the same two seconds of music are either nearly free or eye-wateringly expensive depending on whether the law treats them as "the song" or as "the recording."

I want to understand what picture of what a piece of music is, is doing the work underneath that system. It seems to assume the song is an abstract kind of thing that survives any number of performances and recordings, while the recording is more like a concrete object — the recipe versus a particular plate of food. Is that a fair reconstruction of what the practice presupposes? And does it stay coherent when you push on music where the split breaks down: electronic tracks whose whole identity is the production and timbre, works that were never notated and only exist as one particular studio artifact, the Blurred Lines mess where a jury ended up treating something like a "feel" as part of the composition. I'd also like to know which parts of the system are actually forced by that assumption and which are just the residue of bargaining between publishers and labels. And here's the part I'd really like sharpened: if we swapped in a rival view — say, that a musical work just is it's concrete recordings, or that timbre and production are essential to the work rather than incidental — what would actually have to change about how covers, samples, and royalties work? Reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me where it's coherent and where it isn't, and be honest about the places where the practice is underdetermined enough that several different theories would license it.
