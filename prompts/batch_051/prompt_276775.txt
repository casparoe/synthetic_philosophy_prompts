During our department’s Hume reading group, a senior faculty member defended what he called a “spectator” reading of Hume against the stronger regularity reading, and his analogy has been nagging at me. He asked us to imagine spending one’s whole life observing a closed watch from the outside: one can record which movements of the hands follow which motions of the winding stem, and one can build up expectations through repeated sequences, but one never sees the internal springs. It would be a mistake, he said, to infer from this ignorance that the watch contains no internal machinery; all one has shown is that the machinery is not among one’s data. That, he claimed, is exactly Hume’s procedure with causal powers: Hume is explaining what we can observe and what the idea of necessary connexion can be based on, not denying that something in the objects might answer to it. He then cited this passage from David Hume’s An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section VII, Part I, as the clearest statement of the point:

“When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other.”

His conclusion was that the New Hume reading is basically correct: Hume leaves room for mind-independent causal powers, and he denies only that we can discover them. My worry is that the watch analogy is doing a great deal of the work, and I would like to know whether it can genuinely carry that load. Could you treat the analogy as an argument and audit it? Specifically: lay out the explicit mapping between the watch and Hume’s target—what plays the role of the observer, the watch-face, the winding, the hidden springs, the relevant sense of ignorance, and the inference from ignorance; identify which disanalogies matter for the intended use and which are merely superficial; say what the analogy quietly imports about whether the hidden item is the same kind of thing as its observable effects, and whether that import is licensed by Hume’s text; and assess whether the New Hume conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time.
