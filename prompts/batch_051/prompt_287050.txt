I am an undergraduate in a course on rational choice and the philosophy of action, and I have been trying to name a pattern that keeps appearing in different guises. The basic structure is as follows. A person confronts a long sequence of options ordered by some burden, where any two neighboring options differ so little that the move from one to the next seems rationally unproblematic, especially since each step carries a fixed benefit; yet the ends of the sequence are so far apart that the same person would reject the final option outright if it were offered directly against the initial one. The local, step-by-step comparisons all point forward, while the global comparison points back. One version I keep coming back to is a freelance writer who accepts a long series of small contractual concessions, each individually negligible and each compensated, until she holds terms she would have refused from the start. Another is a patient who consents to weekly increases in a medication's side effects, each increase too small to matter on its own and each bringing a benefit, ending with a regimen he would never have accepted as a single choice. I can see that this resembles the sorites paradox, since the sequence works by increments beneath a threshold of notice; but the sorites cases I have studied concern whether a predicate applies, whereas these cases concern what an agent should choose, and the agent seems to choose well at every step even though the result is one she would never choose. It also seems distinct from akrasia, since at no point does the person act against his own better judgment about the particular step before him. My question is whether this configuration has an established name in the literature on practical rationality, and, if it is usually treated under a broader heading — a failure of transitivity, a preference cycle, or a form of dynamic inconsistency — how exactly the relevant concept is delimited, which of those headings captures this case least misleadingly, and what distinguishes it from ordinary instances of the others. If no established concept matches it precisely, I would prefer a careful definition of the phenomenon and its boundary cases to a bare label.
