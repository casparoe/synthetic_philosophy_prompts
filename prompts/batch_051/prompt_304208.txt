For a paper I am drafting, I have been working through the analysis of meaning in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach — specifically the claim that meaning does not reside in the physical marks or sounds themselves but in an interaction among levels: a frame message that tells the receiver how to decode the signs, an outer message that serves as the carrier, and an inner message that the sender intends to convey. The paradigm examples are a message in a bottle, a vinyl record, and DNA, all of which presuppose something playing the role of an intentional sender or at least a designed channel, so that the receiver's job is to recover the inner message under the right frame.

I would like to test whether that picture survives a changed assumption. Suppose a large language model, trained only to predict likely continuations of human text, produces a passage that a careful reader finds genuinely illuminating — it manages to articulate, for instance, an insight about grief that the reader recognizes as true and thereafter acts on. The system undeniably supplies an outer message, and the reader applies a frame that yields what looks like an inner message; but there is no obvious candidate for the sender whose inner message this was. The apparent source is distributed across the training corpus, the training objective, and the prompting. Does the frame/outer/inner analysis still go through in this case, does it need repair, or does it collapse? More specifically, which premise of the original argument is under pressure, and is there a repaired version that keeps the core point — that meaning is not in the symbols themselves — while conceding something weaker about the need for an intending sender? If a system with no inner message to send can reliably trigger one in a reader, does that make the inner message a construction of the receiver, or can it still be located somewhere in the prompt, the corpus, or the model?
