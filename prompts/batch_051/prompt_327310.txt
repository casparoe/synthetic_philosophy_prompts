Next month I am giving a departmental seminar defending the claim that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are retrospective constructs rather than periods with discoverable boundaries in the historical record. By "constructs" I do not mean that nothing changed in Europe between roughly 500 and 1700, or that the labels are useless. I mean that they are organizing categories imposed later to serve later disciplinary, pedagogical, and cultural needs, and that their boundaries and contents shift with the interests of the historian using them. My argument, briefly, runs like this. First, the boundaries are chronically unstable: the Middle Ages ends in 1453, 1492, 1517, or nowhere in particular depending on whom you ask, and the Renaissance begins at different dates in Italy, France, and England. Second, the features standardly used to mark a Scientific Revolution — mathematization, experiment, instrumentation, new institutions — have long medieval and late-scholastic careers, and only look revolutionary when earlier work is read as their dark prehistory. Third, the Renaissance carries an unstated value judgment: it treats one regional elite's revival of classical letters as the rebirth of civilization full stop, which is as much a nineteenth-century story about modern identity as a neutral description of the fifteenth century.

The audience is a mixed history department, and I want to be ready for what these people will actually ask, not for a generic "science wars" audience. In the room will be an early modernist who works on Florence and is professionally invested in the Renaissance as a category; a historian of science who teaches an undergraduate course called "The Scientific Revolution"; a medievalist who is tired of the "Dark Ages" cliché but also distrusts postmodern debunking; and a historian of Byzantium with no stake in any of the three labels and a reputation for sharp questions.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I am likely to get, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased roughly as those colleagues would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. End with the question I should most hope nobody asks, with a sentence on why it is the dangerous one. The deliverable is a prep sheet, not the talk itself and not a defense of the thesis.
