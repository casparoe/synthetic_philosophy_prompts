I am writing a paper on moral deference and have committed myself to the following five theses. I would like a consistency audit of the set before I circulate the draft: which of these commitments are actually in tension with one another, which are only apparently in tension, and what is the least costly way to make the set coherent?

1. Moral understanding — grasping why a moral claim is true — cannot be acquired by trusting another person's say-so; it requires working through the considerations oneself.
2. The point of moral inquiry is understanding rather than merely true moral belief; a true moral belief acquired purely on another's say-so is a deficient substitute for understanding.
3. In some delimited domains there are genuine moral experts, people substantially more reliable than most at reaching correct moral verdicts. When a person reasonably judges that someone else is more reliable than they are on a particular moral question, they are justified in adopting the other person's verdict on trust, even without reconstructing the reasoning.
4. A person who habitually outsources their moral convictions to well-chosen experts is doing something wrong. The wrongness, however, is not epistemic: their adopted beliefs need not be false or unjustified. What is missing is self-governance, the failure to take responsibility for one's own ethical outlook.
5. A nonexpert generally cannot reasonably determine that someone else is more reliable than they are on a moral question without already exercising the very moral judgment that consulting the expert was meant to replace.

Please order the genuine tensions from most to least severe, and for each say what would have to be revised and what the revision would cost. Then identify any apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection, and say briefly why they should not worry me.
