You are to act as a professional decision theorist who works on choice under prediction, especially the tensions among causal, evidential, and policy-based decision principles. Take your time. Begin by brainstorming several candidate principles, test them against each case below, and only then commit to a proposal. I am interested in the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

Here is a set of decision problems and my own gut verdicts. I am trying to figure out whether they are coherent and what principle, if any, captures them.

Case 1 (coin mugging). Omega, a predictor that is effectively infallible about your decision policy, flips a fair coin. If heads, Omega pays you $10,000 if it predicted that you would have paid $100 had the coin landed tails. If tails, Omega asks you for $100. You observe tails and are asked for the money. My verdict: pay, though with some reluctance.

Case 2 (logical mugging). Same as Case 1, but instead of flipping a coin, Omega uses the 10,000th decimal digit of pi. If the digit is odd, it plays the role of heads; if even, the role of tails. Omega tells you the digit is even and asks for $100. You do not know the digit from memory but could in principle compute it. My verdict: pay, but noticeably less confidently than in Case 1. I suspect the two cases should be treated the same, but I cannot say why they feel different.

Case 3 (imperfect prediction). Same coin mugging, except that Omega’s predictions are right 70% of the time in each direction, and you know this. My verdict: pay. At 51% I still pay. At 50.1% I would still pay if the stakes remained $100 and $10,000, though I would feel like I was following expected value rather than any deeper principle. I do not know whether that feeling matters.

Case 4 (settled-file blackmail). A blackmailer with a similarly flawless model of your policy tells you this: it has already acquired a damaging file about you. If its model predicted you will pay, the file has been destroyed. If its model predicted you will refuse, the file has been left on a timer to be released. You must choose to pay $1,000 or refuse. My verdict: refuse, even though paying is strongly correlated with the file’s having been destroyed. I recognize that a naive expected-value calculation says pay if the damage is large, and I do not trust that calculation. This is not a moral objection to being blackmailed; I would happily pay for a service that actually protected the file.

Case 5 (contingent-file blackmail). The same blackmailer tells you instead: it has the file but has not yet decided what to do. It will release the file if and only if its model predicts you will refuse; it will destroy the file if and only if its model predicts you will pay. You choose now. My verdict: refuse, just as in Case 4, and here I am slightly less puzzled by my own refusal, though I cannot say why.

Case 6 (repeated coin mugging). Omega runs Case 1 every month for a year, with a new coin flip each month. You know the sequence will continue. My verdict: pay every time, and feel almost no reluctance. It seems obvious to me that the one-off and repeated cases should be related, but I do not think they are merely the same case scaled up.

Case 7 (precommitment offer). Before flipping the coin in Case 1, Omega offers you a device that will force you to pay the $100 if tails, at a cost of $1 for the device itself. My verdict: buy the device. Yet this makes me suspect that my refusing in Cases 4 and 5 might simply be weakness of will, because if I could precommit to pay in the blackmail cases, the blackmailer would have predicted payment and I would never face the choice. I do not know how to reconcile the precommitment intuition with the refusal intuition.

Tentative principle: I have been toying with something like this: pay iff your choice is an instance of a policy whose adoption across the relevant counterfactual situations you take yourself to be in would maximize expected value. That says pay in Cases 1, 2, 3, and 6. It may also say pay in Cases 4 and 5 if I take the blackmailer’s conditional at face value, which I do, and that is exactly the problem. Maybe the principle needs a clause about causal reach, but I cannot state one that does not also rule out Case 1.
