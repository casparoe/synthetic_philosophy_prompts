For a unit in the philosophy of language and religion, I am preparing a model exercise on the concept of an inspired text, and I would like your response to serve as the model answer. The question is self-contained; no outside reading or citations are needed.

Let us define an inspired text provisionally as a text whose wording is regarded by a community as divinely authorised in its own right, rather than as a merely human record of something divine. As a working characterisation, I have been teaching three criteria for membership in the category:

(1) Divine provenance. The text's content originates in a divine communicative act, not solely in human inference or construction.

(2) Verbal authority. The authority attaches to the text's actual words, so it would not survive a paraphrase or correction that preserved the sense while altering the wording.

(3) Functional supremacy. The text functions as the community's final norm for doctrine and practice, such that appeals beyond it are, by the community's own lights, out of order.

Now consider a borderline case, described in the abstract but modelled on real traditions. A community possesses its sacred text only in translation. The translation was made from source texts that the community no longer uses and now largely treats as superseded or inferior; in several places the translated wording departs from those source texts in ways that affect narrative, doctrine, and what the community takes to be historical fact. The community nevertheless holds that the translated wording is itself divinely guided or preserved, and it treats challenges to that wording as challenges to revelation itself. Two familiar historical analogues are the standing of the Septuagint in certain strands of early Christian thought and the position of some English-speaking communities that the King James Version is itself an inspired rendering; I want your answer to engage the abstract case rather than any contested historical or textual claim.

My question is whether the translated text qualifies as an inspired text under the three criteria. Work through the criteria one by one, and say honestly where a criterion underdetermines the verdict rather than forcing one. Then tell me whether the case exposes a defect in the criteria themselves—for example, whether they need a separate criterion of semantic fidelity to an antecedent source, or whether they conflate the provenance of a text's content with the question of which words a community may treat as authoritative, or with the question of whether a translation can be faithful while departing from its source. If you think the case is not genuinely borderline but clearly qualifies or clearly fails, say so, but justify that verdict against the strongest statement of the opposite judgement.

Write in British English, and keep the entire response to no more than 1,000 words.
