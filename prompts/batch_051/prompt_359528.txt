I'm a TA grading an upper-level philosophy of biology exam and I'd like another set of eyes on one students answer. The question was:

Explain the tautology objection to the principle of natural selection understood as survival of the fittest. How does the propensity interpretation of fitness answer the objection, and does that answer fully succeed?

The student wrote:

The tautology objection says that if fitness just means actual reproductive success, then survival of the fittest amounts to the individuals who reproduce the most are the individuals who reproduce the most, which is empty and untestable. The propensity interpretation solves this by defining fitness not as actual offspring number but as an expected number of offspring in a given environment—a probabilistic tendency rather than an outcome. That lets fitness and actual success come apart: a fitter organism can still get unlucky and leave fewer offspring, like a biased coin can come up tails. Since fitness is now a cause of reproductive success rather than identical with it, selection claims become empirically testable, and we can in principle measure fitness independently of actual outcomes by looking at an organism's genes and environment. So the interpretation fully resolves the tautology worry.
