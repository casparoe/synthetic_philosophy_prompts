I write about technology and governance for a general-interest publication, and at least once a year someone approaches me with a proposal for a floating community, a privately developed special economic zone, or a new city built from scratch under its own legal framework, promising that it will at last let people leave bad government the way one leaves a bad phone carrier. Over the past decade I have developed a fixed policy for handling these pitches. I do not write about the proposal, and I do not seriously engage with the underlying argument, until the project has acquired all three of the following: a population of actual residents, legal recognition in some real jurisdiction, and several years of operation that is not dependent on subsidies or donor money. Before that threshold is met, I treat the argument for exit-based governance as an intellectual essay rather than a live political possibility, and I assign it no coverage.

A colleague on the ideas desk contends that this is the mirror image of the credulity I claim to dislike in technology journalism: I am installing my own prior as a filter and calling it triage. He argues that the analytically interesting question is not whether most of these projects fail, but whether exit is a coherent alternative to voice at all, and whether existing states could be made more contestable if new jurisdictions were cheaper to found. He says that by waiting for a seastead or private city to prove itself, I guarantee that I will never report on the argument while it is still malleable, which is precisely when reporting might do some good. I want to know whether my policy is defensible as a rule for allocating scarce professional attention, or whether it is a bias dressed up as professional judgment. When is it legitimate for a journalist to decide that an entire family of arguments, rather than an individual claim, receives no hearing until it clears a real-world bar—and, if so, is my particular bar the right one?
