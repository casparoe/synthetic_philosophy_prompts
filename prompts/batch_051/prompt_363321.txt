I am a teaching assistant for a course on American pragmatism, and I have just come from a long argument with a friend who maintained that Peirce's pragmatic maxim is only a nineteenth-century version of verificationism, and that his fallibilism leaves him with no principled reason to prefer the method of science over any other way of fixing belief. I am now grading an exam on exactly these points, and I do not want the argument with my friend to make me either too lenient or too harsh, so I would be grateful for an independent assessment of one student's answer.

The exam question I set was: "Explain how Peirce's pragmatic maxim is meant to clarify concepts, and contrast it with a verificationist account of meaning. Then explain the four methods of settling opinion Peirce distinguishes in 'The Fixation of Belief' and his reasons for thinking the method of science is superior to the other three. Finally, say whether his fallibilism undermines that judgment of superiority, and why."

The student wrote: "Peirce's pragmatic maxim says that the whole meaning of a concept consists in the effects we expect it to have, so clarifying a concept requires setting out the observable consequences of the object's existing or the proposition's being true. This is close to verificationism, but Peirce differs from verificationists by talking about effects that could conceivably have practical bearings rather than only effects we have actually observed. Even so, the maxim is ultimately a criterion for sorting meaningful empirical claims from meaningless metaphysical ones. In 'The Fixation of Belief' Peirce distinguishes tenacity, authority, the a priori method, and the method of science. Tenacity fails because it cannot survive contact with other people's contrary beliefs; authority is more stable but it cannot regulate opinion on every question, and it eventually confronts the fact that other societies believe differently; the a priori method fails because it makes belief answer to what is agreeable to reason, which is really just taste. The method of science is the only one that places an external, public constraint on belief, and that is why Peirce takes it to be superior. Peirce is, however, a fallibilist: he denies that we can ever know for certain that a present opinion is the final one. If every claim to superiority is itself fallible, then no method can be shown to be better than the others at reaching the truth, so Peirce's preference for science rests on a regulative ideal that he must accept on faith rather than on an argument."

Please grade the answer on the 0–20 scale and give the specific comments that justify that grade. I am less interested in a general impression than in a point-by-point verdict on which parts of the answer are correct, which parts are subtly mistaken, and exactly why. Write the comments in continuous prose, as you would in an email to the student; do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
