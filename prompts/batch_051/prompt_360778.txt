Here is an asymmetry in the way mathematical ability gets labeled. Two people confront the same difficult theorem. The first can trace the proof line by line, catch any error inserted into it, and certify it correct; but when asked what the proof is really doing, she can only start again at line one. The second cannot get through the proof without small mistakes, but she can say in a few sentences how the argument works, where the hypotheses do their work, and how the proof would change if one of them were weakened. In much of mathematical practice, the first is described as careful, or as a good checker; the second is described as someone who understands the theorem. The striking thing is that the second is the less reliable guide to whether the theorem is true. A version of the same asymmetry appears in classrooms: a student who executes a procedure flawlessly but cannot say why it works is suspected of not understanding, while a student who can articulate the idea but botches the execution is credited with understanding despite getting the answer wrong. I want a principle that generates both judgments, or else an admission that the practice is inconsistent. What does the second person have that the first lacks, if it is not reliability? Bear in mind that a proof assistant can check lines far more reliably than either human, yet many mathematicians would hesitate to say the machine understands the theorem. Should we keep, reverse, or refine the asymmetry? If you defend it, defend it against the case in which the line-checker can also recite what is generally regarded as the standard explanation of the proof yet still seems not to understand.
