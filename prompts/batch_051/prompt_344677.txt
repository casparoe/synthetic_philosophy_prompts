I've spent a while reading popular discussions of the distinction between honor cultures, dignity cultures, and victimhood cultures, and I keep running into a version of the following argument against victimhood culture. I find its conclusion intuitively right, but when I tried to lay it out as a sequence of steps I noticed it can be turned against the alternatives, and now I suspect it proves too much.

(1) A culture can be evaluated in part by the behavior it rewards, because people predictably do more of what is rewarded and less of what is punished.
(2) Victimhood culture rewards people for publicly establishing that they have been harmed and deserve sympathy or support; being a victim becomes a source of moral standing.
(3) This gives people an incentive to interpret ambiguous or minor slights as serious injuries, to hold on to grievances rather than resolve them, and to bring conflicts before audiences that will confirm their injured status.
(4) A stable social setting that rewards those behaviors will tend to produce more conflict, more unresolved resentment, and more reliance on third parties to adjudicate personal life.
(5) Therefore victimhood culture is morally worse than a culture that does not reward grievance in that way.

The problem is that the same reasoning seems to go through for the rival cultures. Honor culture rewards people who refuse to back down when they are disrespected; that creates incentives to read ambiguous remarks as insults and to escalate them, sometimes into violence. Dignity culture rewards people who stay calm, decline to take slights personally, and keep conflicts out of the public eye; that creates incentives to perform indifference and swallow serious wrongs, allowing real abuse to go unredressed. If steps (1) through (4) are enough to single out victimhood culture as worse, then the parallel arguments seem to single out honor culture and dignity culture as worse too.

So either the argument proves too much — and cannot support the special conclusion about victimhood culture — or there is some hidden asymmetry the argument leaves unstated. I would rather keep the conclusion that victimhood culture has a distinctive incentive problem than abandon the general principle in (1). Which step should I reject or modify, and what do I pay if I insist on keeping the conclusion? If the argument needs an extra premise to avoid proving too much, what exactly should that premise say, and can it be defended? Finally, does the best repaired version show that one culture is morally superior overall, or only that one has a worse characteristic failure mode than the others?
