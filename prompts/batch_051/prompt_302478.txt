I have no formal background in any of this; I have just been reading about the backward-induction puzzle in the centipede game on my own, and I want someone to referee a disagreement I keep having with myself rather than tell me that one side wins.

The theory, as I understand it, runs like this. Two players take turns deciding whether to take a growing pot of money or pass it to the other player. The game has a known finite number of rounds, and taking ends the game. The pot grows each time it is passed, and the longer the players keep passing, the more each of them stands to receive in the end. But at every move, taking gives the player whose turn it is more than they would receive if they passed and the other player took at the next move. Since taking is strictly better at the very last move, the player at that move would take. The previous player, knowing that, should expect to come out worse by passing, so they should take one move earlier; and that reasoning unwinds all the way back. So the first player should take on the very first move, even in a game with a hundred rounds left and a large pot, and even though both players would end up with far more if each passed many times. The result is supposed to follow from almost nothing: self-interested players, a finite game, no uncertainty about the payoffs or about the other player's rationality, and all of that common knowledge, meaning everyone knows it, everyone knows everyone knows it, and so on.

I find the theory compelling because it gives one definite answer in every finite game of this kind, with no psychology and no guesswork about what the other person will do. But I cannot shake the intuition that it is wrong about what a sensible person should actually do. If I were the second player and the first player passed on the opening move of a long game, I would not treat it as a move that should never have happened. I would treat it as a signal that the other player might be willing to keep the pot moving, or doubts that I will take, or simply does not accept the whole backward chain. And I would think passing back was reasonable, not a failure of rationality. The instruction to take on move one feels like a proof about a game nobody is really playing, not advice I would give to a friend.

I do not want you to pick a side and defend it. I want the strongest version of each side, an account of what each concession would cost, and then a negotiated verdict.

On the side of intuition: can the sense that passing back is reasonable be saved without giving up on the players' rationality? If the backward-induction result unravels as soon as we add even a small chance that the other player is a cooperative type, or might make an error, or might doubt my rationality, then how much is the original result telling us? Is the real work being done by common knowledge of rationality rather than by rationality itself, and if so, what should a rational player infer when the game reaches a node that common knowledge said should not be reached?

On the side of theory: what would it mean to give up the intuition? Is the claim only that my intuition comes from imagining real people playing a game whose assumptions I do not literally accept, and that once I accept the assumptions the prediction is unavoidable? Or is the stronger claim that real, intelligent people in a one-shot centipede game should also take early, and what would show that?

Then I want the negotiated verdict. Is there a stable reading on which both survive: backward induction tells us what follows from a set of idealizations, while the intuition tracks what reasonable players should do once those idealizations fail; or the theory is correct but its scope is narrower than it looks; or the intuition tracks a real strategic consideration the theory ought to include? I am aware that such readings can be a way of refusing to admit the theory made a false prediction, so I want you to say how we would tell a genuine reconciliation from a face-saving one.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument would most likely make you drop the intuition as an artefact, which would make you abandon or seriously restrict the theory, and where in your own reasoning you think the failure is most likely.
