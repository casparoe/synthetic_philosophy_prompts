I write novels for a living, so this one got under my skin. A writer friend backed me into a corner about the duty to be legible — the idea that we owe other people sincere, readable accounts of ourselves — and now I can't find the seam. Here's his argument as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) If I owe it to you to be legible, all I can actually give you is a narration of my inner life, an account you can take up and use. There's no other channel; nobody can hand over his feelings directly.

(2) But an account only makes me legible if it's answerable to the inner material. If it isn't constrained by how things actually are with me — if it's guided just by what's convenient to say — it's performance, not legibility.

(3) The trouble is, when I try to locate "how things actually are" inside me, I don't find a finished book. I find drafts: a mood here, two conflicting impulses, a motive I only hit on afterward and half-convinced myself I'd always had. And choosing a narration changes what I can honestly say was going on next.

(4) So the inner material I'm supposed to be answerable to is underdetermined, and partly produced by the very act of narrating it. There isn't a settled fact of the matter for the telling to track, not in the way (2) demands.

(5) If there's no settled fact of the matter, then being sincere about myself can't be a matter of getting my account to correspond to the inner facts. The inner facts aren't there in the right way.

(6) So the only standard left is fidelity to a draft. Does the account cohere with the scraps I've got? Does it make me more available without inventing? But those are exactly the questions I'd ask about a character in a novel.

(7) And if that's the standard, the difference between sincerity and good self-narration dissapears. Sincerity is just craft applied to your own draft, and a duty to be legible is no more a moral duty than a duty to write a convincing character.

I badly want (7) to be wrong, but I can't spot which earlier step is weak enough to pay for it. I'd most like to hang onto (1) and the idea in (2) that being answerable is part of what separates legibility from performance. Wich step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
