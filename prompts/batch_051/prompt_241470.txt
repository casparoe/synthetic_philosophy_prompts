I’m moderating an undergraduate reading group on labor history, and we’re spending two sessions on the Boston police strike of 1919. The students quickly start debating the line then-Governor Calvin Coolidge sent to Samuel Gompers of the American Federation of Labor. The commonly quoted version runs: “There is no right to strike against the public safety by anybody, anywhere, any time.” But I’ve seen colleagues argue that the telegram’s actual force comes less from that line than from what Coolidge says before he gets to it. The full passage, as reproduced in the Massachusetts state documents from September 1919, reads:

"Your assertion that the Commissioner was wrong cannot justify the wrong of leaving the city unguarded. That furnished the opportunity; the criminal element furnished the action. There is no right to strike against the public safety by anybody, anywhere, any time."

I want to push the students past the slogan. Write me a set of fifteen to twenty interview questions for Samuel Gompers, as if he were being interviewed just after the strike collapses. The questions should be organized into four titled sections, with a short note under each section heading saying what that round of questions is designed to expose. The sections should be: "The status of police labor," "What a right to strike is," "Who bears the public safety argument," and "After the defeat." The questions should force Gompers to confront the weakest points of the AFL’s position after Boston rather than allowing him to retreat to platitudes about solidarity or the dignity of labor. Include at least three follow-up questions that anticipate the likely AFL replies and press further. End the whole set with a brief section, titled "What the passage assumes," of no more than 250 words, assessing which claims Coolidge packs into the quoted passage and which of them the earlier questions have actually succeeded in challenging.
