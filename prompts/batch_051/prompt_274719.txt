In my moral psychology course, a classmate has been defending Moral Foundations Theory against the charge that it reduces political disagreement to differences in taste and thereby makes it impossible to say that either side is actually right. Her analogy, as I understand her, is that the moral mind is like the tongue. Evolution supplies everyone with a small set of moral taste receptors — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, and liberty/oppression — and cultures activate and combine these receptors the way cuisines activate the shared biology of taste. On her telling, a liberal's morality is cooked mostly with care and fairness, while a conservative's draws on all six more evenly. Just as it would be a mistake to ask whether French or Japanese cuisine tastes the world more accurately, she argues, it is a mistake to ask whether the liberal or conservative moral profile is more correct; each is simply what happens when the same perceptual equipment is trained by different environments. The psychology of left and right therefore tells us nothing about who is right.

I find this compelling but also suspicious, because the analogy seems to be doing all the normative work. I would like help auditing it. Please give me: (a) an explicit mapping of correspondences — what plays the role of the taste receptors, the tongue, the cuisines, an acquired taste, and tasting more accurately; (b) the places where the analogy breaks down, and which disanalogies matter for the normative conclusion as opposed to being irrelevant surface differences; and (c) a verdict on whether the conclusion — that the psychology of left and right tells us nothing about who is right — survives once the disanalogies are in view. If there is a better analogy the theory should be using, say what it is and whether it helps or hurts her argument.
