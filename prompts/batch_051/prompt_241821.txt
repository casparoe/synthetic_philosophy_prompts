For our seminar on Turing's account of education, I want you to do a conceptual design problem before turning to the secondary literature. In "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), section 2, Turing writes:

No engineer or chemist claims to be able to produce a material which is indistinguishable from the human skin. It is possible that at some time this might be done, but even supposing this invention available we should feel there was little point in trying to make a 'thinking machine' more human by dressing it up in such artificial flesh. The form in which we have set the problem reflects this fact in the condition which prevents the interrogator from seeing or touching the other competitors, or hearing their voices.

Later in the same paper, when Turing turns to learning machines, he sketches something like a child machine that is educated by a teacher through what he describes as rewards and punishments. I want you to write the desiderata a theory of machine education must meet if it is to take both of these moments seriously. For each requirement you propose, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and state whether it is a non-negotiable constraint or a preference one could drop without losing the phenomenon. Pay close attention to what the quoted passage does to embodiment, perception, and display in education: if the target is a machine that can handle the imitation game, what exactly has to be learned, and what can be treated as irrelevant to the learner's education? Then, briefly, score Turing's rewards-and-punishments proposal against your list, and say whether any pair of desiderata is in tension. A table would be welcome, but do not let it replace the explanations.
