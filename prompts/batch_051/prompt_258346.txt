You are a moral philosopher advising the Wainford Civic Valour Committee. The Committee awards the Grey Medal, founded in 1921 to recognise civilians who show in peacetime the qualities its founders associated with soldiers: the deliberate acceptance of bodily danger, presence of mind, and loyalty to others in extremity. By its own rules, the medal is reserved for acts in which a person voluntarily risked life or limb to save or protect another person, so purely moral courage, however admirable, is outside its remit.

Over the past decade the Committee has decided the following cases. It awarded the medal to a lorry driver who pulled six passengers from a burning coach and required skin grafts; to a retired teacher who, armed only with an umbrella, delayed a knife attacker outside a school until police arrived and was herself badly stabbed; and to a farm labourer who could not swim but waded into a flooded culvert, kept a trapped child's head above water until the fire service cut through a grate, and was later revived. It denied the medal to a young man who chased a pickpocket across a railway line and was killed by a train, on the ground that the object sought did not justify the risk and that the pursuit endangered passengers; to a woman who had twice before had to be rescued while entering a burning barn to look for animals and who died in a third attempt; and to a fit but untrained passer-by who tried to descend an unstable cliff to reach a fallen walker with no rope and no workable plan, fell, and had to be winched out with a spinal injury, after which the Committee observed that his attempt had diverted rescuers from the very person he meant to help.

A new case is on the agenda. A man who could not swim saw a car swept into a flooded river at a ford, with a woman trapped inside. Although onlookers urged him to wait for the fire service, which was already on its way, he waded out and was almost immediately carried off by the current, never reaching the car. His body was recovered downstream the next morning. The fire service freed the woman unhurt half an hour later, and the Committee's fact-finding report finds that his attempt neither helped nor hindered her rescue. Some members argue that a willingness to risk everything for another, without regard to skill or odds, is exactly what the medal exists to honour; others reply that the medal cannot become an award for drowning oneself needlessly, and that valour involves some steadiness and some prospect of success. Before you recommend a decision, brainstorm the candidate principles that could explain the Committee's past awards and denials. Then say how each principle would treat the new case, identify the closest past precedent and where it does and does not bind the Committee, give your best recommendation as to what the Committee should decide, and state explicitly what principle it would be adopting with each possible outcome if the precedents underdetermine the decision. Conclude by describing one further hypothetical case that would cleanly separate whichever principles remain in contention. Please write your answer in British English.
