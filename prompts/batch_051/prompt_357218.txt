Consider two cases. In the first, pricing managers at rival firms never communicate, but each notices the other matching price increases; they settle into a pattern of parallel increases, acting only on each other's observed behavior. Our reaction to this is ambivalent: the behavior is anticompetitive, but we hesitate to call it collusion, and we are unsure whether any secret coordination has taken place. In the second case, two independently deployed pricing algorithms learn exactly the same pattern from repeated interaction. We are much quicker to say the algorithms colluded, that they secretly coordinated, and that this is a distinctive failure mode of multi-agent systems. The resulting prices are the same, the information available to each agent is the same, and in neither case is there an explicit agreement or instruction to fix prices. What, if anything, justifies the sharper judgment in the machine case? I want the strongest principle that makes machine coordination collusion while leaving the human case genuinely gray—a principle about intent, opacity, speed, scaling, or something else—and an honest verdict on whether it survives the case of two humans who achieve the same speed and scale by deliberately writing the algorithms and letting them run.
