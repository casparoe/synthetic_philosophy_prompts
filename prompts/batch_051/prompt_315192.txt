Act as a moral philosopher who works on both professional ethics and the ethics of close personal relationships. I spent my career as a research biochemist and have been retired for a few years; I have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, so I would value a careful, structured analysis rather than labels.

I have been trying to work out what I actually think about loyalty and whistleblowing by attending to my gut reactions to cases. Please think through these slowly, sorting the cases and noting patterns before you commit to a principle.

1. A nurse repeatedly notices a senior surgeon making the same medication dosing error. She raises it with him informally and then with her supervisor; nothing changes. She files a report with the state licensing board. I think she did the right thing.

2. A mid-level engineer finds that her firm has falsified emissions data for a product that is still in use. Internal complaints go nowhere. She gives documents to a newspaper. I think she did the right thing, though I wish it had not come to that.

3. A policy analyst leaks a classified memorandum showing that a government repeatedly misled the public about the reasons for a military deployment. I lean toward saying this was right, but with real unease about the oath of secrecy.

4. A man discovers that his closest friend is having an affair and tells the friend's wife. I think this was wrong: it was not his to disclose, even if the wife had an interest in knowing. He should have pressed his friend to come clean himself.

5. The adult daughter of a couple who own a small manufacturing business discovers that the business has underreported its income for years — tax evasion on a moderate scale. She reports it to the tax authority, and the resulting penalties destroy the business and her parents' retirement. I think what she did was wrong, or at least not what she should have done first, even though I cannot point to a clean difference between this and the nurse's case.

6. A police officer reports his partner for planting evidence on a suspect. I think this was clearly right, and that he would have been wrong to stay silent.

7. A junior employee at a charity learns that the director has been misusing funds on a small scale. She confronts the director privately; he admits it, repays the money, and the misuse stops. I think she handled it rightly, and that going to the board at once would have been worse.

I keep going back and forth on a further case: a software engineer discovers her company is selling users' location data despite having promised not to, in a way that is legal but plainly violates the company's privacy commitments to its users. Internal complaints are ignored, and she is deciding whether to report it to a regulator or to a journalist.

Is there one principle — or a small set of principles — that captures my verdicts, or close to them? Please state it precisely, say which distinction is doing the work in each case, tell me what it predicts for the case I am undecided about, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If saving all the verdicts requires an ugly or ad hoc principle, I would rather know that, and which verdict is the least defensible.
