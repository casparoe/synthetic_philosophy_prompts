In the color phi phenomenon, two brief flashes are presented in quick succession, one red and one green, and normal observers report a single moving spot that appears to change color before the second flash could have occurred. Daniel Dennett and Marcel Kinsbourne used this kind of case to distinguish two ways a cognitive system could produce such a report: a Stalinesque revision edits the experience before it reaches consciousness, while an Orwellian revision leaves the original experience intact but later alters the memory of it.

Suppose an artificial visual system is being designed to reproduce this sort of report. In the Stalinesque implementation, raw sensor events are held in a short buffer for a fixed interval. If a later event conflicts with an earlier one, an editor may alter or replace the earlier event before anything is admitted to the system's central workspace. In the Orwellian implementation, events are passed to the workspace immediately, but a separate memory-revision process may later overwrite the workspace's record when a conflicting event arrives, so that future reports reflect a corrected timeline.

Treat these as mechanisms, not as labels. What observable differences would you expect in the system's reaction times, confidence judgments, ability to report colors at intermediate times, and susceptibility to post-hoc suggestion? Under what conditions would an outside engineer with complete access to the code and logs nevertheless be unable to tell which implementation was running? Does adding more internal probes resolve the question or merely relocate the boundary between pre-conscious editing and memory revision? Suppose the project's sponsor pays a bonus for versions that pass timing-accuracy and coherence tests without flagging internal edits. What would that incentive do to the choice between the two implementations and to the way edits are logged, and how might the project be gamed by reporting artifacts as conscious percepts? Does the engineering analysis support the claim that there may be no fact of the matter in the human case, or does it show that the distinction has practical consequences?
