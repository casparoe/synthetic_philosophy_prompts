I'm a retired molecular biologist, and I've only started reading philosophy seriously since I left the bench. A few weeks ago I wrote out an argument that now bothers me a lot, because the conclusion seems obviously wrong, but I can't find the bad step. Every premise on its own is something I would have said without blinking during my career. Here it is.

(1) If the genome literally codes for proteins, then there has to be a standard of correctness: some readings of the sequence are right and some are wrong.

(2) The cell has no such standard. A ribosome that puts in the wrong amino acid isn't getting anything wrong; it's just doing what its chemistry does in those conditions. There's nobody in there trying to read the codon correctly, and no intended protein sequence to get wrong.

(3) So the whole vocabulary — genetic code, transcription, translation, proofreading — can only be shorthand for ordinary causal regularities linking sequence to product. It can't be a literal code.

(4) But ordinary causal regularities are everywhere. Snowpack thickness causally records snowfall. Tree rings causally record wet and dry years. A scar causally records the cut that made it.

(5) If the genome's information is just a causal regularity, then a fossil is as much a code for its organism as a gene is a code for its protein, and a stop codon means stop in exactly the same sense a wet sidewalk means rain.

(6) So the central claim molecular biology has been built around — that the genome contains a code — is, taken literally, false. The genome no more contains a code then a tree ring does.

I can't accept (6). It would make my discipline's founding idea into a category mistake, and I'm not prepared to believe that. I very much want to keep the view that genetic information is real and does explanatory work, and that a stop codon means stop in a way a snowpack doesn't mean anything. But I can't see which step to reject without giving up something else I want. Which step is wrong, and what do I give up by rejecting it?
