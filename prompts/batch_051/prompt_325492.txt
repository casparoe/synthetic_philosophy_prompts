Adam Smith, An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter vii, opens the chapter on colonies with this sentence:

“The interest which occasioned the first settlement of the different European colonies in America and the West Indies was not altogether so plain and distinct as that which directed the establishment of those of ancient Greece and Rome.”

I am trying to understand why the debate over whether Adam Smith was a critic of empire or a realistic defender of some imperial projects has remained unresolved among readers who know the texts well. Some emphasize his attacks on mercantile colonial policy and his worry about the treatment of indigenous peoples; others emphasize his admission that the utility of the European colonies has been very great and his pragmatic proposals for gradual adjustment. The opening of the chapter on colonies seems to set up the problem directly: ancient colonization had motives that were “plain and distinct,” while modern European colonization did not. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about what Smith said, but about how to weigh his descriptive political economy against his normative judgments, and about whether the relevant standard is commercial utility, justice, or something else.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than another verdict. Is it a textual disagreement, a methodological disagreement about how to read an eighteenth-century political economy as part of moral philosophy, or a substantive disagreement about which of Smith’s evaluative commitments should dominate the reading? What, given your diagnosis, would actually move the debate forward—a distinction, a different reading of a neglected passage, or an acknowledgment that the interpretive question is ill-posed? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested interpretive line as the consensus.
