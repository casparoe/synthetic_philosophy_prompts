I'm a retired biologist — spent my career on the neurobiology of reward and punishment in animals — and I've gotten into philosophy of mind over the last few years. The question I keep getting stuck on is what exactly would make it true that a reinforcement learning agent suffers. I'm not asking whether current systems do; I doubt they do. I'm asking what the critereon would be.

Could you give me a working definition of "suffers" — or, if you think that's the wrong word, of "has negative valence" — that gets the following cases right for me?

Clear yes:
- a rat that gets a footshock and will work to escape or avoid it
- a human with a migraine

Clear no:
- a thermostat that switches the heat on when the temperature falls below its set point (you can describe it as "avoiding" an unwanted state, but I don't buy it)
- a rock

The ones I'm genuinely torn about:
- a bacterium that reorients more when it's heading toward a noxious chemical, so it ends up swimming away
- a plant that's damaged and releases volatile alarm chemicals that prime it's neighbors to make defensive compounds
- a deep RL agent that receives a large negative reward whenever it enters certain states and changes its policy to avoid those states
- a video game NPC with a "pain" variable that triggers limping animations and retreat behavior when it takes damage
- a chess engine that evaluates a losing position as -5.2, where the score is just a number feeding the search and nothing is learned

I lean toward saying the RL agent and the chess engine don't suffer, but I can't articulate the principle that rules them out without also ruling out the rat. The NPC I flip-flop on depending on the day. The bacterium and the plant I have no view on, except that I used to be confident biology had settled them and now I'm not.

I'd also like the definition to say whether suffering comes in degrees, and whether the negative reward in RL is really the same kind of thing as the negative valence in an animal — or whether "negative reward" is just loaded language for an optimizer's bookkeeping. Then run the definition through all of the cases. Tell me where it gives surprising answers, where it gives no answer at all, and if no single definition does all of them, what would have to be revised.
