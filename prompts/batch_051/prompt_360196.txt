Here's a short essay I've been kicking around and I'd like your comments on it, especially on whether the operator-vs-model move in the last paragraph is sound and whether "counterfeit assertion" is doing real work or just relabeling. Feel free to be blunt.

For a while I've thought the debate about whether LLM hallucination is lying, bullshit, or error has a frame problem, because all three categories assume a speaker. Lying requires someone who knows the truth and says otherwise. Error requires someone who takes themselves to be saying something true, stakes themselves on it, and gets it wrong. Bullshit, in Frankfurt's On Bullshit (the essay, later a book), is less about the world and more about the speaker's stance: the liar at least has to keep track of the truth in order to contradict it, while the bullshitter has stopped caring how things actually are and cares only about how his words land. On this telling, the bullshitter isn't primarily trying to get you to believe a falsehood; he's trying to get you to see him in a certain light, and truth is simply irrelevant to the project. That's what makes bullshit, in one respect, worse than lying.

An LLM hallucination fits none of these, because there's no speaker in the picture. The model isn't knowingly contradicting the truth, isn't staking itself on anything, and has no motives, so a Frankfurt-style motivational account can't get a grip. Calling the output bullshit looks like a category mistake if it's aimed at the model. But I also don't want to fall back on error, because that smuggles in a truth-aiming operator. Error isn't just any false output; it's a false output inside a practice where someone was answerable for the truth.

So I propose a fourth category: counterfeit assertion. A hallucinated answer has the full surface grammar of assertion—declarative form, confidence, the kind of sentence that would carry a truth-commitment if a person said it—but there's no one who stands behind it. That's not bullshit, because there's no motive and no impression management; the operator may be indifferent to truth, but the model isn't anything.

Which pushes the interesting question up a level: maybe the bullshit is located in the operator, not the model. If I deploy a system that I know produces fluent, confident falsehoods at some nontrivial rate, and I present its output as if it were an answer, my act may be bullshit in Frankfurt's sense even though the token output is not. On this view, hallucination isn't a speech act gone wrong; it's counterfeit money, and the question is whether anyone is knowingly passing it.

Comments I'd especially like:
1. Is my reading of Frankfurt fair, or am I stacking the deck by making bullshit motivational and agentive by defenition? Could a non-agentive process still count as bullshit in a natural extension of his view?
2. Is "counterfeit assertion" earning its keep, or is it just a fancier way of saying false output from an unreliable process?
3. Is the move of relocating the bullshit from model to operator too neat? Are there cases of LLM bullshit that don't relocate so easily—like when the model hedges, argues, or qualifies its confabulations in ways that look like impression management?
4. What's the strongest objection you'd raise to this essay in a seminar, and what one change would most improve it?
