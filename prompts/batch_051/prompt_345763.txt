You're a professional philosopher who works on epistemology, especially questions about cognitive outsourcing, expertise, and how we rely on other minds and tools. Even if you have some particular philosophical views, I want you to approach this as an adjudicator. I'm especially interested in who is right about which specific point, where each person overreaches, and who ends up more right overall.

Two people in my department, Mara and Dev, got into an argument last week about a third person, Lena, and they asked me to referee. I honestly couldn't, so I'm bringing it to you.

Lena is writing a dissertation on a nineteenth-century philosopher. She doesn't read the original language well, so she works mostly from published translations, but increasingly she uses an AI assistant to translate long passages, paraphrase difficult sections, and "unpack" arguments for her when she gets stuck. She also has the assistant summarize scholarly debates and occasionally draft outlines of her chapters, which she then rewrites heavily. Everyone agrees she's not breaking any rules and her work is good. The argument was narrower: does Lena actually understand the material she's writing about?

Mara said no. Her line was that if you can't sit down with a text, follow the argument in your own head, and get yourself unstuck, then the understanding is happening in the tool, not in Lena. She said Lena could produce a perfectly good chapter and still not know her way around the source material, the way someone can give directions from a phone without knowing the city. The phone knows the city; the person knows how to use the phone.

Dev disagreed. He said this draws the boundary of Lena's mind in the wrong place. All serious intellectual work uses scaffolding: notes, translations, colleagues, search, citation managers. If the AI is functioning as a fast research assistant, then Lena-plus-the-AI understands, and that's what matters for the quality of the work. He said it's a kind of snobbery to reject new cognitive prosthetics while quietly depending on older ones nobody questions.

Mara came back: the difference is that with a human research assistant or a published translation, Lena has some independent grip on how reliable the source is. She can ask the assistant how they reached a reading. She can notice if a translator seems to be smoothing over something. With the AI, she's taking outputs she can't interrogate in that way. So the problem isn't that understanding is outside her; it's that she has no calibrating access to it.

Dev said that's a fact about current interfaces, not a fact about AI in principle, and it's also more rigor than most scholars apply to their translators. If Lena compares multiple translations, cross-checks the AI against published renderings, and prompts the model to justify its paraphrase, then she's at least as entitled to say she understands the argument as someone with shaky German who leans heavily on translations and footnotes. Nobody denies that that person understands Kant, or whoever.

Mara replied that comparing outputs doesn't help much when you lack independent competence, because the outputs can be correlated with each other and with your own prior ignorance. And there's a difference between scaffolding that extends an ability you already have and scaffolding that replaces an ability before you develop it. A calculator doesn't stop me from knowing arithmetic, but relying on the AI before Lena can parse the texts means she never builds the skill that would let her check anything later. So she's not using a tool; she's permanently dependent on one.

Dev said that's a fair point about skill development but it doesn't show Lena doesn't understand. She isn't claiming to be a philologist. The object of understanding is the philosophical argument, and arguments can be conveyed through reliable translations and summaries. Lots of excellent philosophers understand authors they can't read in the original. He then said the deeper issue is that Mara keeps smuggling in a requirement that understanding must survive the removal of all external aids, but no understanding works like that. Take away my notes, my colleagues, my books, and my phone, and I understand much less too.

Mara said the relevant test isn't removal of all aids. It's whether Lena could answer a novel interpretive follow-up when the AI is off. Not perfectly, maybe, but better than random. She predicted Lena would be fluent in the AI's glosses but would falter when asked to weigh two claims about the text that the AI hadn't already juxtaposed for her. That, she said, is what reveals the difference between having understood and having access to something that understood.

Dev said that's an empirical bet about Lena's particular habits and metacognition, not a principled objection. And it would be just as true of a lazy scholar using a human translator. The question is whether the AI makes the relevant kind of understanding impossible, and Mara hasn't shown that.

They went around like that for a while. Neither convinced the other. I think they were partly using different notions of "understanding." But I'd like you to sort out exactly where the arguments land, what each person gets right and wrong, what distinctions they're missing, and which view is more defensible in the end.
