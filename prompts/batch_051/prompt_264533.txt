I'm a PhD student in metaphysics, and I have settled into a standing policy for reading disputes in ontology—about composite objects, numbers, possible worlds, and the like. Before I commit to either side, I try to restate each side without using its contested vocabulary, and I ask which non-verbal differences would remain: downstream predictions, formal or inferential commitments, or other consequences each side assigns to the world. If the two views converge on everything I can elicit and differ only in how they apply the contested term, I classify the dispute as merely verbal and decline to take a side; if they still diverge, I treat it as substantive. This filter saves me time, but I am unsure whether it is defensible or just deflationism disguised as method. Evaluate the policy as a policy: where it is reliable, where it fails, and whether it can be repaired. In particular, can it distinguish a merely verbal dispute from a genuinely substantive dispute between theories too underdeveloped to yield distinct predictions yet? Does it beg the question against someone who holds that the contested term tracks a real joint that my neutral restatements are too coarse to detect? And what should the policy say when the two sides disagree precisely about what the non-verbal facts are—for example, about whether there is any fact of the matter about composition?
