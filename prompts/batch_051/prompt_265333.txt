I direct the evidence-review office of a state public-health department. Several times a year I convene an expert panel to turn a contested body of evidence into an operational recommendation — a screening threshold, a coverage decision, a treatment interval. The panels are rarely unanimous. Typically two or three camps form, each composed of reasonable people, each reading the same studies, and each reaching a different number or even a different sign. The statistically literate members tell me this should matter less than it instinctively does: the standard Bayesian convergence results say that, under the right conditions, agents who begin with different priors will come to near agreement as shared evidence accumulates, because the priors eventually wash out. I am not interested in the theorem as a seminar topic. I need to know how to convert it into a decision rule for a Tuesday afternoon, because some of our panels never converge, and in some cases the split has nothing to do with priors at all. What I want is a reusable procedure, not another lecture on the asymptotic properties of rational agents.

Please write a decision procedure my staff could run in an afternoon using only the briefing packet in front of them: the included studies, each camp's point estimate and stated uncertainty, and a page of each camp's reasoning. The procedure should state which questions to ask, in what order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it directs us to issue a recommendation with the dissent recorded; where it directs us to issue no guidance this cycle; and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the crux is evaluative rather than evidential. Be precise about where the Bayesian washing-out idea is genuinely doing work, and where applying it to a room of experts would be a category mistake. Then run the procedure on the following cases: (1) a panel split over whether to recommend universal dyslexia screening at age six, with both camps reading the same five trials and disagreeing about the practical importance of a small average effect; (2) a panel split over the projected effect of a sugar-sweetened beverage tax, with one camp forecasting a meaningful decline in consumption and the other forecasting little effect because of substitution to untaxed drinks; and (3) a panel split over the duration of preventive tuberculosis treatment, where the camps share the same efficacy data but disagree about the acceptable risk of drug-induced liver injury. At least one of these should come out as a recommendation the procedure says we should not issue but may badly want to issue, and you should say plainly that this is what the procedure commits us to. Throughout, take a definite position: do not reply with a menu of defensible alternatives. Choose one procedure, defend its choices, and identify the largest foreseeable way it could go wrong.
