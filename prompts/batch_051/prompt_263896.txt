“Generation” gets used in at least two ways: a stage in a line of descent (grandparent, parent, child) and a social cohort (“Baby Boomer,” “Gen Z”). I mean the second sense. Give me a working definition of a social generation that gets the following cases right. (1) Americans born between 1946 and 1964, the standard Baby Boomer window. (2) The bare set of Americans born in 1987, which everyone treats as a birth-year slice and no one treats as a generation. (3) The “Xennial” micro-cohort, roughly 1977–1983, whose members claim to fit neither Generation X nor Millennial. (4) A child who immigrates to the United States at age nine and shares her American classmates’ adolescent touchstones but not their early-childhood ones. (5) Iranians who were teenagers during the 1979 revolution, often spoken of at home as a distinct generation although the label does not travel. (6) “Generation Alpha,” applied to children born from roughly 2010 onward before they have any shared formative memory of their own. I am confident (1) is a generation and (2) is not; I lean toward saying micro-cohorts like (3) are not real generations; I have no settled view on (4)–(6). Propose the definition, test it against all six, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer, no answer, or a verdict you would have to defend at the cost of revising the definition. Lead with your bottom line — the definition you are willing to defend after testing — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
