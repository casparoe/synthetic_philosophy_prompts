Two people in my program got into it at a party last week, and I got stuck as referee. Eli was pushing a line he was very proud of: there's an actual result in decision theory, I think its called Good's theorem, which says costless information can never have negative expected value. His gloss was that information only changes what you choose, and a rational agent can always ignore a free signal, so having the option can't make you worse off in expectation. Mira said that can't be right as a general claim, because people pay real money not to learn things; her case was someone who refuses a genetic test for a disease with no treatment, since finding out would poison whatever healthy years are left. That's not a person misusing the information, she said — the information itself is the harm. (1) Eli replied that her case was outside the theorem: the despair isn't the test changing which action is best, it's the state of knowing being unpleasant, and the theorem only covers cases where you care about states and actions, not about your own mind. (2) Mira called that a dodge, saying that if the theorem only holds in cases where information doesn't hurt then it's vacuous, and the claim she actually cared about — that avoiding information can be rational — is left standing. (3) Eli said it isn't vacuous: it tells you that if you are genuinely neutral about the information except as a guide to action, it literally cannot hurt you in expectation, so any case where it does hurt must be one where the information itself carries a cost you haven't counted. (4) Mira then gave what she called a cleaner case: a reporter who can learn a source's name. If she doesn't know it she can honestly say she can't reveal it under a court order; if she does know it she can be compelled to testify and someone gets hurt. The information doesn't make her unhappy, she said — it changes what other people are able to do to her, so even as pure information it can be bad to have. (5) Eli admitted that one didn't fit, but said that only shows the theorem is about a single agent choosing among her own actions, not about strategic situations where other people use what you know. (6) Mira said that's exactly the point: once you carve away the direct harms, the strategic ones, and the cases where you can't simply choose to ignore what you've learned, the theorem can't carry the weight Eli put on it. Nobody declared victory; they asked me and I changed the subject.
