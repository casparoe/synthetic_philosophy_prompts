Write a pantoum of sixteen lines, in four quatrains, about the fact that no year can be assigned to the change from Latin to Italian. Let the poem's form carry the argument rather than state it. Use the pantoum's standard recurrences — a stanza's second and fourth lines return as the next stanza's first and third, and the closing stanza loops back toward the poem's opening lines — but allow each returning line to drift slightly: a Latin case ending softens into a preposition, a verb loses its final consonant, an inversion is flattened into modern word order. Adjacent stanzas should remain mutually intelligible, like adjacent generations of speakers, while the first and last stanzas, read side by side, should no longer look like the same language. Aim for the effect of a temporal sorites — a chain whose neighbouring links understand each other and whose endpoints do not — without resolving it into the lesson that the distinction was an illusion.

Do not let the poem become a lecture or close with a thesis; the gradually altered refrains should do the philosophical work.

Give the poem first, then a single section titled Commentary, about 200 words. In the commentary, discuss the poem's main choices and its intellectual risks rather than glossing it line by line: which claims about language change the poem quietly assumes — for example, that every generation could understand the one before and after it, or that spoken descent and the persistence of written Latin can be treated as one continuous object — which of these claims is most contestable for the actual Latin-to-Italian case, and what kind of historical or linguistic evidence would most force a revision of the poem's underlying picture.
