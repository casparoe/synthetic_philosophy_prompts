I am chairing the Q&A session of a philosophy department colloquium on animal ethics. The visiting speaker defends a version of what is sometimes called the logic of the larder: raising animals for food and killing them is permissible, and indeed positively good, when the animals are given lives that are on the whole worth living, both because the practice brings into existence creatures who otherwise would not have existed and because animals that lack a temporally extended sense of their own lives are not additionally harmed by death. I want the session to be a sustained internal critique of this view, not a repetition of the familiar speciesism dispute.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. They should be direct challenges, with no biographical or scene-setting questions. Include at least one question on each of the following four pressure points, and give each a follow-up calibrated to the reply the guest is most likely to offer:

1. Creation and benefit. Ask whether an animal can be benefited by being brought into existence, or whether the view must fall back on an impersonal claim that total welfare in the world increases. If he says a life worth living is better for the animal than no life, press on what the comparison can mean when there is no subject before creation; if he takes the impersonal route, ask whether he is committed to breeding the largest possible population of lives that are only barely worth living, and what on his account restrains that conclusion.

2. Replaceability. Ask whether the view licenses painlessly killing a contented animal and replacing it with another animal that will have a comparable life, and whether the same inference extends to any being at the relevant psychological level, including humans whose self-conception is episodic or otherwise reduced. If he draws the line at self-consciousness, press on whether the line is a sharp moral boundary or a matter of degree, and what exactly does the normative work at that point.

3. The argument and actual practice. Ask how the view handles the fact that much contemporary animal agriculture does not produce net-positive lives. If he replies that the logic of the larder is therefore a criticism of factory farming rather than a defense of it, ask what the view actually permits in practice, whether it collapses into a defense of a small class of high-welfare farms, and how much animal use it still licenses once the welfare premise is taken seriously.

4. Wild animals. Ask whether the axiology driving the view implies that most wild animal lives are net-negative, and what obligations, if any, would follow. If he says we owe nothing to wild animals because their suffering is not of our making, ask whether that makes moral status depend on our causal relation to the animal rather than on the animal's welfare, and whether the creation-based justification has become a relational theory in disguise.

Arrange the questions cumulatively so that later questions can invoke commitments the guest has already had to make, and end with a question about what observation or finding would, on his own account, show that the view fails on its own terms.
