I'm preparing a unit for a course on philosophical method and interpretation, and I want to build it around Jorge Luis Borges's story "Death and the Compass." The story transplants the old problem of symbolic numbers in scripture — the question of where interpretation ends and pattern-hunting begins — into a murder mystery. The detective, Erik Lönnrot, reads a series of apparently unconnected murders through kabbalistic numerology and the four-letter name of God (the Tetragrammaton), extends the pattern to predict a fourth location, and discovers that the pattern was in one sense genuinely there: the criminal, Scharlach, had planted it precisely because he knew Lönnrot would treat any such design as meaningful. Read the story against this issue. I'd like you to say what the story shows, dramatizes, or complicates about the difference between responsibly interpreting a numerical-symbolic scheme and merely projecting a pattern onto coincidences — and where it helps, or refuses to help, an instructor who wants to distinguish the two in class. Which scenes or turns would you point students to? Where does the story resist a comfortable moral: for instance, is its lesson simply that the rationalist should have been more skeptical, or does the story make that lesson unavailable, since a skeptic would have missed a real though malicious design? Finally, does it support or subvert the thought that there is a principled line between interpreting a sacred pattern and pattern-hunting? I want the reading to be usable in class, and faithful in both directions: it should not reduce Borges to an illustration, and it should not soften the philosophical problem to fit the story.
