Produce the co-signed statement that a fair-play theorist and a scheme-preservation theorist could both put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on what, if anything, is wrong with free riding. The fair-play camp thinks the free rider wrongs the co-contributors themselves: he takes a benefit that exists only because others accepted burdens he declines, and this wrong survives even when his non-contribution changes nothing at the margin. The scheme-preservation camp thinks free riding is wrong only insofar as it tends to unravel the cooperative scheme (for instance by triggering conditional cooperators to withdraw, or by leaving the good underproduced), so that in a genuinely robust scheme the free rider is selfish or distasteful but not unjust.

Anchor everything in an invented running case: a small town keeps a volunteer flood-watch during storm season. Warnings are non-excludable (you can't stop a non-volunteer from hearing the siren and moving his car), shifts are easy to shirk, and one resident, Kem, never takes a shift. Use four variants: the roster is always overfull and nobody's behavior changes; the volunteers would keep doing it even if they knew Kem never showed up; one volunteer says she'll quit if she finds out Kem is free riding and she's likely to find out; and Kem's elderly father also benefits while being unable to take a shift.

Give me the document itself, formatted as (1) a numbered agreed-claims section that says something substantive, not just definitions; (2) a disagreements table with two columns, each cell worded so the camp it describes would accept it; (3) for each genuine disagreement, the crux sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp; (4) for each crux, a concrete test or case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded ahead of time; and (5) a note on any part of the dispute that turned out to be merely verbal, including whether "social parasite" adds anything beyond "free rider" or is just a rhetorical upgrade. Don't resolve the debate in either side's favor, and don't let the fair-play phrasing smuggle in a debt owed to co-contributors.
