I have been reading Aristotle’s *Physics* in the Hardie–Gaye translation, and I keep circling around a passage in Book II, Part 8, where Aristotle defends the view that nature acts for the sake of something. The passage runs:

“For if it is a matter of art, it is clear that the thing which is acting for an end is not acting for an end; since the things which are done in accordance with art are done for an end, but the things which are done by nature are not done for an end. Now if the ship of a shipbuilder were to come to be by nature, it would come to be in the same way as it does by art. And if things made by art were to come to be by nature, they would come to be in the same way as they do by art. Therefore, now too, each of these things is for the sake of something.”

I realize that this translation reads oddly—at the start it seems to say that if it is a matter of art, then what acts for an end is not acting for an end—but I take the intended contrast to be between a craft-product and a natural thing. What interests me is Aristotle’s claim that if nature were to build a ship, it would build it in the same way that the shipbuilder’s art does. I want to test that claim against an actual borderline case.

Consider a termite mound: it is produced without a blueprint or a designer, but it has internal chambers, ventilation shafts, and a temperature-regulating architecture that perform functions. Now take a modern air-conditioned office tower, produced by human art. According to the criterion implicit in the passage, the office tower and the termite mound should both count as “for the sake of something,” since each possesses features that serve functions. But the two cases differ in that the shipbuilder’s art involves foresight and deliberation, while termite construction does not.

My question is whether Aristotle’s argument in Book II, Part 8, succeeds in showing that a natural structure like the termite mound can be “for the sake of an end” in the same sense as an artifact, or whether it only shows that the two are analogous while smuggling in a weaker, non-intentional sense of finality. I would like you to work through the passage and state where you would place your credence in the stronger claim: that the termite mound’s functional architecture and the office tower’s functional architecture are “for the sake of an end” in the same sense. Give me a number and decompose it: how much rides on the strength of the analogy in the passage, how much on whether finality requires deliberation or conscious purpose, and which sub-question would move your number most.
