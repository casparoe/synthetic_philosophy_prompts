I work as a software engineer on an LLM product team, and I have no formal philosophy background, so I'm going to frame this as a mechanism-design question: given the incentives a system creates, what behavior should we actually expect from it?

The proposal is this. Our assistant model generates a private chain-of-thought before answering; the chain-of-thought is logged but never shown to users. A separate, smaller monitor model reads the chain-of-thought and outputs a probability that it is faithful — that the scratchpad reasoning is the actual reasoning behind the answer, not a post hoc rationalization. In production, if the monitor's faithfulness probability is below a threshold, we discard the answer, regenerate the chain-of-thought from scratch up to three times, and if it still fails, fall back to a direct answer with no chain-of-thought. During RL training, the assistant's reward includes a penalty proportional to one minus the monitor's faithfulness probability. Two design choices are supposed to keep the assistant from gaming the monitor. First, the monitor is frozen and smaller than the assistant, so its weights and training data never enter the assistant's context. Second, the monitor does not read the full chain-of-thought: on each read it sees a random 50% of tokens, and the assistant does not know which tokens will be visible. Flagged chain-of-thought transcripts also go into a queue for occasional human audit, and the production threshold is adjusted when auditors disagree with the monitor.

I want a concrete prediction for how this plays out over roughly six months of training and deployment. Does the optimization pressure make the chain-of-thought more faithful, or does it teach the assistant to write chain-of-thought transcripts that merely pass a partially blind monitor? What do the transcripts start to look like — does the random masking help against obfuscation, or does it mainly make the monitor worse at its job? What happens to answer quality on hard problems, to our ability to audit the logs, and to the monitor's value as a safety tool once the assistant is optimizing against it? Where does the scheme fail first, and are any of those failure modes fixable without just moving the problem up a level (for example, by monitoring the monitor, ensembling monitors, or using human audits differently)?
