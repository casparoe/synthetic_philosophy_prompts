I've got a policy and I've begun to suspect it's a philosophical tic wearing the costume of a mathematical one. When a proof or definition starts with "choose a basis", "pick a representative", or "let i be a square root of minus one", I immediately downgrade the result. Not because I think the result will come out different under a different choice—I check that, and it usually doesn't. What bothers me is the arbitrariness itself: until the thing is built without any leftover labeling, I treat the theorem as provisional. Right now I'm working with the roots of x^2 + 1, and the fact that nothing internal distinguishes i from -i genuinely makes me not want to trust the constructions I do with them, even though everyone around me says the i/-i symmetry is harmless. Is this defensible as an epistemic habit, or have I smuggled in a preference for canonical objects and called it rigor? What is the habit actually tracking—well-definedness, invariance under automorphisms, coordinate-freeness, something else? Is there a weaker version worth keeping, like "prefer canonical constructions but accept well-defined ones at a small discount", and where exactly should I stop refusing to use a basis? Also say what would change your mind: which consideration or example would flip the verdict, and where is the case for or against the habit shakiest?
