I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums forever — LessWrong, Overcoming Bias back when it was actually active, the decision theory megathreads — and I basically run my epistemology on a subjective Bayesian plus expected utility picture. Lately one conflict keeps messing with me, and I want you to help me figure out which side is actually load-bearing.

The conflict is about naked statistical evidence. Take the blue bus case: someone is hit by a bus and has no memory of its color; the only evidence at trial is that Blue Bus Company operates 80% of the buses on that route at that time. On that evidence, the probability it was a blue bus is 0.8. Standard decision-theoretic factfinding, applied to proof by a preponderance of the evidence, says find Blue Bus liable: the posterior is above threshold, and expected accuracy is maximized by finding against them. Same with the gatecrasher: 1000 people attend an event and only 499 paid, so a randomly selected attendee is 50.1% likely to have crashed. Expected accuracy says make them pay. But I would not find Blue Bus liable, and I would not make the gatecrasher pay, and I suspect most people wouldn't either. And I don't mean beyond a reasonable doubt, where you could try to rescue things by saying the threshold is much higher; these are civil preponderance cases, the easiest possible case for the Bayesian. The intuition is that legal proof has to be about this defendent and this incident in a way that bare base rates are not. Individualized evidence, or whatever it is, matters, so much so that I would rather find against someone on a 75%-reliable eyewitness than on an 80% market-share statistic, even though the second is better evidence by my own lights.

So here's the clash. I hold a theory that compresses legal factfinding into probabilistic decision-making, and it gives clean, non-arbitrary answers in every case. I also hold a firm intuition that it gives the wrong answers in exactly these two cases, and the intuition is not loss aversion or a rounding error; it survives reflection. I don't want you to pick a side and defend it. I want a negotiated verdict. Walk me through, as precisely as you can, what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes: what would the Bayesian debunking story have to establish before I should classify the blue bus intuition as a bias and drop it, and what would I then be committed to in adjacent cases, like a 99% gatecrasher or punishing a company on market-share evidence alone? Then walk me through what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes: what exactly has to be added to pure expected-accuracy reasoning to make it refuse naked statistics while still accepting a decent eyewitness, and can that extra ingredient be stated without smuggling in a legal fiction or a demand for certainty? Finally, say whether there is a reading of proof, of liability, of what more likely than not amounts to, on which both the probabilistic machinery and the intuition survive, or whether one of them genuinely has to be given up.

When you answer, tell me how confident you are in the main claims, and explicitly single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of. And write it as continuous prose, like an email or a comment, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables.
