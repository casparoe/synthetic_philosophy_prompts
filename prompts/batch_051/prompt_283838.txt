I've been reading some philosophy since I retired — I was a sensory physiologist, so when I came across Quinn's self-torturer it landed pretty close to home. The setup, as I understand it: a person can turn a device up one notch at a time, each notch brings a large sum of money and a pain increase too small to notice, but by the final step he is in agony for a sum he would never have accepted as a single trade. At each step the trade looks good, so he walks himself into a state he would have rejected from the start. The usual diagnosis seems to be that his pairwise preferences are intransitive, and that's meant to be trouble for any account that takes rational agents to have transitive preferences.

I've been tinkering with a homemade idea I call "preference voids." The thought: when two options differ by less than the agent's discrimination threshold, the agent doesn't prefer either over the other in the way transitivity cares about — the pair is just a void, like a balance that can't resolve a milligram. Transitivity constrains genuine preferences, not the noise you get when a choice is below resolution. So the self-torturer's cycle is an artefact of treating instrument noise as data. The same idea would explain everyday cases: you can't feel the difference between a room at 20.0°C and 20.1°C, and so on up to a temperature you obviously can feel, without your insensitivity being transitive. I realise this might just be Quinn's own lesson restated, or some old psychophysics idea, or that I may have moved the puzzle rather than solved it.

Could you give me an honest assessment of this: what it gets right, what it gets wrong, whether it already exists under some standard name, and where it actually breaks? Please make it exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, and in British English.
