I'm a retired geochemist — after forty years of taking laboratory results seriously, I've finally started reading epistemology. I keep coming back to the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, because they feel less like seminar puzzles and more like an accurate description of how I actually worked. But I've never managed to state the principle I was using. I'd like you to do it: I'll give you my verdicts on some cases, and I want you to work out the best principle, or small set of principles, that captures them. State it as precisely as you can, run it on the two extra cases at the end, and then tell me which of my verdicts I'd have to give up if I want the principle simple — and what each sacrifice costs. I don't need the history of the problem or citations; just the principle and the repairs.

Here are the cases.

1. A charity raffle with 5,000 tickets and one guaranteed winner, fairly drawn. For any individual ticket, I'll assert it won't win: ticket 2,147, yes; ticket 4,001, yes; each one. But I won't assert that this raffle will have no winner, and I accept that some ticket will win. I know the set of assertions is inconsistent, but the attitude feels entirely ordinary.

2. A two-ticket fair raffle. For either ticket, I won't accept that it loses; it's fifty-fifty. So the big-raffle verdict seems to rest on high probablity, but I can't feel where the line falls between two tickets and five thousand.

3. A batch of 800 geochemical analyses from a commercial lab I used to trust, with blanks and duplicates all looking fine. If you point at any one result, I'll accept it. But I won't accept that all 800 are correct, and I'd think less of anyone who wrote the paper as if the batch were error-free.

4. Three replicate measurements from the same homogenised sample, same lab, same methods. I'll accept that each is within tolerance, and I'll accept that all three are. This and case 3 are the pair that bothers me most: I accept each member of both sets, but I accept the conjunction for the small set and refuse it for the large one, and I can't say which feature does the work.

5. A chapter I wrote years ago for a regional geology volume. Sentence by sentence, I believed each sentence was correct. I also asked the editor to put a line in the acknowledgements saying I had no doubt errors remained. Both were sincere. I know it is the preface paradox; it still seems to me like a correct report of my state of mind rather than a confusion.

I once tried a simple rule: accept a claim when its probability is above some threshold — 0.99, say — and accept a conjunction only when it independently clears the same bar. That handles case 1 (each losing ticket is at 0.9998; the no-winner claim is at zero). It handles case 3 on paper. But it gets case 4 wrong: if each replicate is 0.99, the conjunction of three is about 0.97, below the bar, so the rule says don't accept all three — but I do. And in case 5 I don't actually think every sentence in that chapter cleared 0.99; I accepted plenty of moderately probable sentences.

For the principle to earn its keep, I'd like it to give verdicts on two further cases I still haven't settled:

A. A hundred independent instrument checks, each 99.5% likely to be correct. Do I accept that all hundred are correct? I don't know — the probability all are correct is only about 0.61, which feels too low, but refusing feels pedantic.

B. A single big groundwater-flow model with maybe fifty parameters, each individually well-enough supported, but the model as a whole has many places to fail. I used to think: acceptable for planning, not acceptable as a truth claim. Does the principle say anything like that, or is that just me changing the subject?

And please write the answer in British English.
