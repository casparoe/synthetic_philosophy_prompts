You are a professional philosopher who works on practical reason and deontic logic. I want you to evaluate a step-by-step argument whose conclusion is that Sidgwick’s dualism of practical reason is internally inconsistent, and I want you to take a definite position rather than just surveying possible responses.

The dualism, as I’ll characterize it, is the view that practical reason contains two ultimate and rational first principles that can conflict: rational egoism, which says each agent is rationally required to pursue her own good, and rational benevolence, which says each agent is rationally required to pursue the good of all. Consider a conflict case in which the act that best promotes the agent’s own good (call it E) and the act that best promotes the general good (call it B) cannot both be performed. The argument runs:

(1) In such a conflict case, the agent is required by rational egoism to do E and required by rational benevolence to do B, and E and B are mutually exclusive.
(2) To be required by a genuine first principle of practical reason is to be subject to an all-things-considered ought. Hence, in the conflict case, the agent ought all things considered to do E, and ought all things considered to do B.
(3) All-things-considered oughts agglomerate: if an agent ought all things considered to do E and ought all things considered to do B, then the agent ought all things considered to do E-and-B.
(4) Ought implies can: if an agent ought all things considered to do E-and-B, then the agent can do E-and-B.
(5) But E and B are mutually exclusive, so the agent cannot do E-and-B.
(6) Since (4) and (5) contradict, the dualism must be false: practical reason cannot house two ultimate principles that issue conflicting all-things-considered requirements.
