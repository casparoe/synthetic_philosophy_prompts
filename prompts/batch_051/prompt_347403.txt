Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether every statement must be true or false. I was defending the idea that some statements are neither; she kept insisting I was confusing our inability to decide with there being nothing to decide, and that giving up bivalence would force me to give up ordinary logic. I am now preparing an hour-long session for a first-year philosophy tutorial introducing truth-value gaps, and I want a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. The students will mostly arrive with my friend's assumptions: bivalence is obvious, and rejecting it is either epistemic modesty or intellectual chaos.

Please lay out the explanatory arc: which distinction has to be in place first (for example, the difference between a sentence and the claim it is used to make, or between being false and failing to be true), and which example should carry the session — whether one of the usual candidates (the present King of France, a borderline heap, a future contingent) or another of your choosing — and what that example must have and lack to do its work. I also want the misconception map: the predictable confusions these students will bring or form (conflating bivalence with excluded middle, treating "not true" as automatically "false," thinking vagueness is just ignorance, thinking future contingents are only about determinism), and where in the sequence each should be headed off before it entrenches. End with the check by which I could tell the difference between a student who now sees why gaps are a live option and one who has merely memorized that some philosophers reject bivalence. State explicitly what to leave out at this level even if it matters to the full debate, and why.

Keep the plan to at most 850 words.
