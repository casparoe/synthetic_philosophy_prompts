I have been developing a notion I call trace-linking and want a candid assessment of it. The motivating case is the blue bus: the plaintiff cannot identify the bus closely, but the Blue Bus Company operates 80 percent of the buses in town, so it is 80 percent likely that a Blue Bus is responsible. The standard teaching is that this does not suffice for liability even under the civil preponderance standard, which requires only that the claim be more likely true than not; and the resistance runs even deeper in criminal law, where statistical evidence alone is thought unable to support conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. My hypothesis is that evidence may support a verdict only if it is the causal trace of the particular act it is offered to prove — a signal that ran through the event or the defendant's conduct itself. The market-share statistic fails because it runs through the reference class of buses in this town rather than through the accident. DNA profiling falls, I think, on the admissible side for the right reason: the random-match probability is statistical, but the sample is a trace of the very act, and the chain from sample to defendant runs through the defendant's body and conduct rather than merely through a class. So far the criterion tracks the standard intuitions. But two cases loosen it. First, an eyewitness identification is a noisy trace, reliable only to some degree; its structure looks statistical too, with the probabilities buried inside the witness's psychology. Second, the rodeo gatecrasher: we know that 501 of the 1000 attendees entered without paying and that the records identifying which ones are destroyed, so any particular attendee is more likely than not to have gatecrashed. There the damaging fact is caused by the individual actions of the very people in the room, and a destroyed roster looks like lost trace evidence rather than a bare base rate — yet nearly everyone classifies it as naked statistical evidence that cannot ground a verdict. I want to know three things. First, is trace-linking a real distinction or only a vivid metaphor? Does it collapse into a familiar idea from legal epistemology, and if so, what does trace-linking add? Second, where exactly does it break: does it wrongly admit some bad statistics, wrongly exclude some good evidence, or both? Third, does it illuminate the criminal reasonable-doubt standard, or does it merely reorganize the civil cases? Give me the strongest reconstruction of the idea, the strongest objection to it, and a verdict on whether the notion earns its keep.
