Here's an epistemic rule I've been using a lot at work, and I'd like a sanity check on it. I'm a backend engineer, and whenever someone claims "the deploy caused the latency spike" or "the cache change caused the error-rate drop," I basically refuse to accept it until they can answer: what exactly would you intervene on, and what would you expect to change if you did? The experiment doesn't have to be practical to actually run; I just want the hypothetical toggle, flag revert, canary rollout, whatever, plus a statement of what you'd hold fixed while flipping it. If there's no possible intervention that would make a difference to the outcome, I treat the causal claim as confused or circular. Lately I've caught myself applying the same rule to big non-technical claims, like "social media caused the teen mental health crisis" or "the 2008 crash caused the rise of the gig economy." Is this rule defensible as a general test for causation, or am I just overfitting a debugging heuristic to the whole world? I know the obvious complaint is that some causes can't be manipulated—genes, historical events, your childhood. But I'm more worried about a deeper problem: the instruction to "hold other things fixed" already seems to smuggle in a bunch of causal assumptions, so my supposedly neutral test might be circular. Where does the rule break down, and is there a patched version I'd be justified in keeping?
