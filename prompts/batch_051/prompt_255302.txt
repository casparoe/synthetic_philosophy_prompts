Im getting suspicious of how often people talk about "the objective" of some system—a chess engine, a language model, a company, a person—as if you could read it straight off the behavior. Before I believe any of that, I want the spec. What does an adequate theory of inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior actually have to get right? By search space I mean the set of options it even considers, not just the ones it ends up picking.

Give me the desiderata list. For each requirement: the case or little thought experiment that forces it, and why a theory that misses it goes wrong. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just taste or a particular camp's commitments. And the part I most want: tell me which pairs or larger subsets of the desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied—like, if you want unique recovery of the objective, what do you have to import, and if you want to stay assumption-light, what do you give up. Also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular ways people actually do this.

The list has to cover, at minimum: a system that never tries an action (do we read that as low value, or as an option outside its search space?); a system under time pressure whose cautious moves look like a different goal; a behavior stream that's optimal for a thousand different reward functions at once; exploration that looks like a taste for noise; the difference between an objective and a constraint; a system that stops when things are good enough rather than maximizing; an optimizer that can edit its own search process; and a "system" that's really a truce between subagents, or not an optimizer at all. I care mostly about claims people make about AI systems, but the same machinery should handle the economics and biology cases too.

Keep the scorecard of existing approaches short—the criteria are the deliverable.
