I am a graduate student in moral philosophy working on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to impartialist moral theories, and I have been stuck on an asymmetry in ordinary moral judgment that I would like help thinking through.

Consider two agents. The first is an emergency physician deciding which of two patients should receive the last available ventilator. She applies the hospital's outcome-based triage criteria, sees that one patient's expected outcome is moderately better, and gives that patient the ventilator. If she had instead passed over the better-prognosis patient because the other reminded her of her sister, we would treat that as a professional failure: the personal consideration seems not merely outweighed but misplaced. The second agent is a father escaping a house fire who can carry out either his young daughter or his neighbours' two children. He stops, deliberately takes up the impartial standpoint, weighs two lives against one, concludes that the two count for more, and carries out the neighbours' children. Many people judge that something went wrong even though his conclusion tracks the impartial recommendation, and they locate the failure in his pausing to perform the calculation rather than in the outcome — the kind of failure Williams registers when he discusses the husband who stops to ask whether he is justified in saving his wife.

The asymmetry is this: in the physician's case we demand that the agent step back from her attachments and let an impartial calculation govern; in the father's case we condemn the agent for performing that very operation, even when the calculation is sound. Both agents occupy roles partly constituted by commitments — the physician by the norms of her profession, the father by his relationship to his daughter — and both face a choice between acting from those commitments and submitting them to impartial review. Yet our reactions diverge sharply.

I want to know whether any single principle can justify this divergence, or whether it is an inconsistency we simply live with. The strongest candidates I can formulate — that special obligations attach to persons rather than institutional roles, that professional roles legitimately require one to bracket attachments, that a parent's love is partly constituted by not treating his child as merely one person among others — all seem strained by a hybrid case: the physician's own child is wheeled into the resuscitation bay during her shift, and the triage protocol requires her to treat the child as one patient among others, subject to the same outcome calculation. Is there a version of the principle that handles the original cases without collapsing in the hybrid, and does it leave Williams's deeper point about integrity intact? I care less about which verdict you finally reach than about discovering what the asymmetry commits us to if we try to keep it, and whether it should in the end be kept, revised, or abandoned.
