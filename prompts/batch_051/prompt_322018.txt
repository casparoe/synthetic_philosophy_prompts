One feature of contemporary democratic states has long puzzled me. We treat every adult citizen as equally entitled, through elections, to help decide some enormously consequential questions: which representatives will write tax law, set criminal penalties, and commit the state to military action. Yet we remove other consequential questions from that same electoral process. Monetary policy is placed in the hands of appointed central bankers who are deliberately insulated from voters and elected officeholders, typically on the grounds that setting interest rates is a technical matter rather than a question of common values. Similar justifications are offered when drug approvals, environmental standards, or competition policy are delegated to independent expert agencies. You are a political philosopher working on democratic theory and the question of what makes a political procedure legitimate. What theory of the relationship between political equality and political competence does this institutional patchwork presuppose? Does it quietly commit us to the view that, on some questions, the judgments of the many are less deserving of political power, or can a principled egalitarian about democratic citizenship endorse the same patchwork? Please start by brainstorming several distinct principles that could rationalize treating monetary policy differently from tax policy or war powers; then defend the reading you find most coherent. Be careful to separate what the patchwork strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it remains philosophically underdetermined. Finally, indicate what would have to change if a stricter egalitarian view of political competence were imposed across the board.
