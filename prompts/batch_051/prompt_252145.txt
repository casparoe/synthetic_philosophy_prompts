I'm leading a one-off, 90-minute graduate reading group on the family of anti-mechanist arguments associated with Lucas and Penrose: the claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems give us reason to reject computationalism about the human mind. The room is split between philosophers who have worked through the first incompleteness theorem exactly once, and computer scientists who know the theorem and suspect that any anti-mechanist use of it is a category mistake. I want the session to give the strongest version of the argument a serious run before the objections arrive, without making the argument look better than it is.

I'm asking for a session design, not a rehearsal of the argument. Work out the dependency order: which ideas have to be secured before the argument can even be stated with its real force, and where a different ordering would make it look weaker or stronger than it deserves. Pick one concrete toy case — a particular formal system or diagonal construction — to carry the demonstration, and say what features it must have and must lack for the point to land. Give me a misconception map for the two halves of the room separately: for the philosophers, the temptation to move straight from "this system cannot prove its own Gödel sentence" to "therefore we can, and that settles mechanism"; for the computer scientists, the assumption that Gödel's theorem concerns machines directly, or that a refutation of one proposed formalization would refute all of them. Say exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be defused rather than corrected after it has hardened. Decide which of the standard difficulties — the consistency assumption, the mind's not being a single fixed formal system, our inability to know our own formal specification, the differences between Lucas's and Penrose's routes — I should raise myself and which I should let the group discover, and defend that division. Tell me what to set aside for the session and how to say so. End with one check that would distinguish someone who merely followed the diagonal argument from someone who understands what the anti-mechanist conclusion would need to establish.

Finally, treat the plan as fallible. Tell me which step in your ordering, or which feature of your toy case, you would defend most confidently; where this particular group is most likely to force a revision; and what argument or reaction in the room would change your mind about how central a piece is.
