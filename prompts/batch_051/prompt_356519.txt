I'm a managing editor for a company that writes high-school and first-year biology textbooks, and we keep fighting about how to talk about DNA. One author wants to say 'the gene codes for the trait'; our reviewers say thats backwards, since a stretch of DNA doesnt code for anything on its own — the cell supplies the decoder (ribosomes, tRNAs, the codon table), and they keep citing some distinction between causal information (smoke carries information about fire) and message-like information that needs a sender and a receiver. They also think 'the genome is an instruction manual for building an organism' is the worst offender, because nothing in the cell reads the whole genome back as a set of instructions; development is just biochemistry. I'd like a reusable editorial procedure our copyeditors can run on any sentence about DNA in about ten minutes, something like a short checklist, that ends in one of three verdicts: keep the code/information language, keep it with a qualifier, or rewrite. It should say which steps are real distinctions and which are just house style, and where a person has to make the call. Then actually run it on our three worst sentences: 'DNA stores the instructions for making proteins,' 'The genome is a blueprint for the organism,' and 'Mutations are random typos in the genetic code.' I want to know what it does with a sentence that's technically false but pedagogically useful, and at least one case where it tells us to cut something the authors will hate.
