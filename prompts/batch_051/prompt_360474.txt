Consider the following pattern in contemporary conservation. Much of the field frames its goal as restoration: returning species and ecosystems to a prior baseline and protecting ecological integrity. At the same time, serious research programs pursue de-extinction, attempting to reconstruct species that humans destroyed by editing genomes and using surrogate relatives, with the eventual aim of releasing the result into the wild. In parallel, some of the same institutions and funders are moving toward gene drives: engineered genetic elements that bias inheritance so that a trait spreads through a wild population to fixation. A representative proposal would use a gene drive to spread sterility through an invasive rodent population on an island until the population collapses. This is not a cull of some individuals; it is an intervention designed to rewrite or erase a wild gene pool, with effects that may be irreversible and difficult to contain across generations or borders.

The same institutional world thus speaks the language of restraint, continuity, and fidelity to natural baselines while also advancing interventions that deliberately alter or eliminate wild populations and manufacture substitutes for extinct species. What theory of environmental value, and of the human relationship to nonhuman nature, does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view or views that would make these commitments coherent. In doing so, address whether the pattern requires treating species or ecosystems, rather than individual organisms or natural evolutionary processes, as the fundamental unit of value; whether it presupposes that human intention changes the moral status of an ecological disruption; and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same mix of policies but diverge in their next steps.
