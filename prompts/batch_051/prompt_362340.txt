I'm a journalist covering ideas and the humanities, and I'm working on a piece about whether the meaning of a literary text is fixed by what its author intended. In my notes I'm compiling the standard arguments against actual intentionalism — the view that a text means what its author actually intended it to mean. So far I have these:

- Authors' intentions are private. A reader cannot inspect them directly, and an author's later statement of what they meant is fallible evidence, not the meaning itself.
- Authors can fail to execute their intentions, so what they meant to write and what the text actually says can come apart.
- Textual meaning is carried by public linguistic conventions rather than private mental states; once the words are set down, they mean what the language makes them mean.
- If intended meaning were constitutive of textual meaning, a text's meaning would change whenever the author changed their mind. It does not.

I suspect there is at least one more kind of consideration that is not a variant of these — something that does not turn on the privacy, fallibility, or instability of intention, and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of the above. What is it? Give me one more argument against actual intentionalism, say whether it is any good, and explain how it relates to the four on my list.
