You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaphysics and the philosophy of science. Please think through the position carefully before drafting, and begin by brainstorming four or five possible lines of questioning.

I am a high school student who helps produce the philosophy segment of my school's podcast. Next week we are interviewing a philosopher who defends a Humean best-system account of laws of nature, in the tradition of Mill, Ramsey, and Lewis. On this view, the laws are the axioms or theorems of the deductive system that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit in systematizing the totality of local, categorical matters of particular fact across all of space and time. Our audience has already heard the standard statement of the view, so the interview should be critical rather than introductory; the guest has said he is happy to defend the position against serious objections.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do more than ask him to restate the view. I want at least one question, plus a follow-up, on each of the following challenges. The follow-ups should be keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give first.

1. The language-dependence worry: how simple or strong a system is seems to depend on which vocabulary or predicates we use to describe the mosaic, so different vocabularies could yield different 'best' systems and different laws. If the guest replies that the correct vocabulary is a privileged set of perfectly natural properties, press him on whether that appeal gives up the fully Humean reduction.
2. The problem of vacuous or uninstantiated laws: if a law is never instantiated in the actual history of the world, it seems not to summarize any actual regularity, so it is unclear how the best-system account either yields it or explains what it is for it to be a law. If he replies that such laws are still theorems of the best system, ask what distinguishes lawlike vacuous theorems from the countless other vacuous truths that the system could generate.
3. The explanatory-direction worry: a summary of the mosaic seems to describe the regularities rather than produce or explain them, and it is hard to see how a mere summary can perform the usual work of laws in supporting counterfactuals and guiding explanation. If the guest replies that the best system can underwrite the right counterfactuals, push him on where that modal force comes from once laws are nothing more than an economical summary of actual history.
4. The worry that the account depends on substantial objective structure: a privileged vocabulary of natural properties, and a determinate weighting of simplicity, strength, and fit, may smuggle non-Humean or modal facts into the picture. If the guest accepts natural properties as an extra primitive, ask him how much of the Humean view is left, and whether the view has only moved the problem rather than solved it.

For each of the four areas, please provide the main question and the follow-up (eight questions in total), and then add one or two further questions of your own, for eight to ten questions altogether. Make them sound like an interested and well-prepared high-school interviewer rather than a professional seminar participant. Do not include generic biographical or ice-breaker questions. Ensure every question is answerable only by doing real philosophical work, not by repeating the textbook formulation of the view.
