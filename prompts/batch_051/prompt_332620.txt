I am a retired natural scientist who has lately been reading philosophy, and the problem I keep circling is whether transformative AI has already begun. The discussion seems to me to slide among at least three thresholds — systems that change the scientific enterprise itself, systems that reorder economy and institutions, and developments that alter the long-run trajectory of the species — and a definition that quietly moves between them can make the claim true in one paragraph and false in the next.

A passage by Samuel Butler has become a fixed point for me, from his letter "Darwin among the Machines," published in The Press (Christchurch, New Zealand) on 13 June 1863:

“We have used the words “mechanical life,” “the mechanical kingdom,” “the mechanical world” and so forth, and we have done so advisedly, for as the vegetable kingdom was slowly developed from the mineral, and as in like manner the animal supervened upon the vegetable, so now in these last few ages an entirely new kingdom has sprung up, of which we as yet have only seen what will one day be considered the antediluvian prototypes of the race.”

Butler is not asking when a machine will outthink a human being. He is asking when an entire mechanical order — a new kingdom — has come into being, and he treats the machines of his own day as merely its antediluvian prototypes. That seems to me a hard constraint on any account of the subject: it must either make good sense of Butler's kingdom and locate its beginning somewhere in real history, or say precisely which feature of the analogy is doing the misleading work.

Before I take a position, I want the specification. Write the desiderata that any account or definition of transformative AI must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement; mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp; and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the most common definitions in circulation. The list should issue a non-arbitrary verdict in at least these cases: Butler's own 1863 machinery, the electrification of industry, the systems now in wide use, and a hypothetical later system that exceeds human performance across all scientific work. Then, working from the list alone, say whether Butler's claim that we have so far seen only antediluvian prototypes forces the conclusion that the transformation has already begun, or leaves that question open. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of who said what; the criteria, and the cases that motivate them, are the deliverable.
