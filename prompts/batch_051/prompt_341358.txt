Every few months my local rationalist group relitigates whether digital minds have moral patienthood, and it's always the same three people talking past each other because they don't actually disagree about the cases, they disagree about which cases to weight. I want to make a "which theory of digital mind moral status do you actually hold?" quiz for the group blog, eight to ten questions, each one a concrete scenario (a slowed-down whole-brain emulation, a from-scratch RL agent that reliably avoids its own damage states, a copy that gets deleted and restored from a backup, a lookup table that passes every conversation test, that sort of thing) with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto leaves: substrate-independent computationalism, biological chauvinism, sentientist gradualism, behavioral functionalism, and the "treat them as if they mattered for coordination/acausal reasons even if we're not sure" instrumental view. The point is to sort people by what they say about the cases, not by which label they self-identify with, so no question should just ask whether digital minds are conscious or which position sounds right.

I care more about the diagnostic questions than about a clean final taxonomy. The distinctions I want the questions to actually separate are: (a) whether the implementation substrate matters, (b) whether the mind has to be descended from a biological original or can be designed de novo, (c) whether the evidence for valenced experience has to be behavioral, self-reported, or architectural, (d) whether the possibility of copying and restoring changes duties because identity stops being a background assumption, and (e) whether subjective clock speed changes how duties aggregate or just how we schedule them. Please build the tree from the logical space of answers first and then locate actual positions in it, rather than starting from the names. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, say so in one sentence and diagnose whether it's empty because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely underexplored. Also flag pairs of leaves the questions can't really separate; I suspect behavioral functionalism and the instrumental view will blur on the copy/restore case.

Audience is long-time LessWrong readers who don't know the academic literature, so define any jargon the first time it appears. Aim for nine questions and at most seven leaves, make each answer option a real structural commitment rather than a vibe, and write the questions so someone can answer them without already knowing which leaf they're in.
