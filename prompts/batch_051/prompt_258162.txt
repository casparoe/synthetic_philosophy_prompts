Construct a short scene for a novel that makes the paradox of wu-wei concrete, and then analyze the scene as a designed case. The scene must include two characters attempting the same difficult mundane action—such as an emergency medical procedure, a high-stakes musical performance, or a demanding craft task. The first character has set out deliberately to attain wu-wei, using an explicit technique of trying not to try: self-monitoring, breathing rules, inner injunctions, or the imitation of a master. The second character simply performs the action with absorbed, unforced competence and has never formed the goal of becoming effortless. The first must fail in a way the narrative traces to the very effort not to be effortful, not merely to insufficient skill, poor training, or bad luck; the second must succeed without the concept of wu-wei ever entering the scene.

Then do the philosophical work. Analyze the scene under two interpretations of wu-wei: (a) a byproduct interpretation, on which the state the first character seeks cannot be made the direct object of effort because it is constitutively a byproduct of attending to the action itself; and (b) a cultivation interpretation, on which the first character's failure is only a wrong training method, so a different regimen of practice could produce the state. Identify the single structural feature of the scene that makes (a) and (b) deliver different verdicts on the first character rather than merely offer two descriptions of the same failure. State how the scene would have to be altered for (b) to win the case, and give the strongest objection a defender of (b) would raise against treating the scene as evidence for (a). The analysis should not depend on any particular translation or scholarly provenance.
