You are a historian of economic thought who specializes in the interwar socialist calculation debate, and your particular expertise is F. A. Hayek's critique of central planning. I want you to think carefully and methodically: first brainstorm the relevant Hayekian commitments in a quick list, then select the ones that do real work, and only then apply them to the proposal described below. Do not pad your answer with citations or a reading list; the point is the substance of the argument, not the scholarship.

A proposal is now being advanced that Hayek could not have encountered in his lifetime: a "neural planning board." The idea is that a foundation model, trained on decades of market-generated data (prices, quantities, lead times, consumer choices, production records, location data, and so on), can learn the mapping from the state of an economy to the goods and services that should be produced next. The planners concede that prices were needed in Hayek's day because no mind could hold the dispersed facts of an economy at once. But, they argue, machine learning changes exactly that: the dispersed knowledge that once had to be coordinated through prices can now be concentrated into a single dataset and a trained model, which can then issue detailed production and allocation directives, and even make counterfactual predictions about goods that do not yet exist. A neo-Langean version adds that the model can also play the trial-and-error role Oskar Lange assigned to his Central Planning Board, adjusting notional prices until predicted supply and demand clear.

Your task is to predict, as carefully and defensibly as you can, how Hayek would assess this proposal. Organize your answer in three parts.

First, state the commitments of Hayek's that would do the work. Separate the epistemic commitments (knowledge is dispersed, often tacit, local, and time-sensitive; prices summarize and transmit information no single agent has) from the dynamic or discovery commitments (competition as a procedure for discovering which goods, methods, and prices are viable) and the incentive commitments (why individuals and firms need both the knowledge and the standing to act on it, and what gamesmanship does to centralized information).

Second, test the neural planning board against each commitment. Address at least the following four pressure points explicitly:

(a) Training data versus counterfactuals. The model's data are overwhelmingly traces of markets, not traces of alternative non-market arrangements. What does that mean for planning during structural change, for novelty, and for the counterfactuals the plan itself would make unavailable? Can the model know what the prices would have been under the plans it chose not to run?

(b) Tacit and unreported knowledge. Much of the operative knowledge sits in the habits, hunches, and local know-how of managers, workers, and firms, and is never written down. Can training data capture what was never recorded? And once people know a model will use their reports, what governs what gets reported upward in the first place?

(c) Discovery versus interpolation. If the market is a discovery procedure, is a model trained on past market data a discovery mechanism, or an interpolation engine over what has already been discovered? Does a successful plan give the planners reason to suppress the very experiments that would have corrected the model's errors?

(d) Incentives and the non-stationarity of the data-generating process. Once managers and firms understand that their reports, outputs, and strategic behavior are inputs to the model, and that the model's directives feed back into their quotas, costs, and rewards, will the process that generated the training data remain stationary? Is there a Hayekian reason to expect the model's environment to drift as soon as the model is installed?

Third, deliver a nuanced overall verdict. Would Hayek see the proposal as an answer to the calculation problem, as a sharpening of it, or as a change of subject? Which of his objections survives most strongly, and which is genuinely weakened by modern machine learning? End by flagging the one or two places where your projection from Hayek's published views to this setting is most conjectural.
