I am a PhD student working on Peirce's epistemology, and for the past year I have been testing a distinction in seminar papers that I would like checked before I build a dissertation chapter around it. I call the distinction the specification requirement, or specified fallibilism for short. It begins from Peirce's contrast between the methods of fixing belief. Tenacity, authority, and the a priori method each collapse, on Peirce's account, once their adherents notice that other people in other places and periods have fixed their beliefs differently; the method of science survives this reflection because it alone stakes its beliefs on an external permanency that will eventually settle opinion. But I have come to suspect that the distinctive feature of the method of science is not the bare postulation of such a permanency, nor the willingness to utter fallibilist disclaimers. It is the maintenance of a specification: a standing, revisable description of the observations, outcomes, or hard cases a thinker would recognize as counting against the belief and would treat as a reason to reopen inquiry. My central claim is that fallibilism without a maintained specification is not the method of science at all but tenacity wearing the vocabulary of science, and that the real content of the injunction not to block the road of inquiry is the obligation to keep such specifications in good repair even when, or especially when, a belief is confidently settled.

Two examples from my own corner of the field. An economist who holds that expanding credit reduces extreme poverty and can say which trial results, replication failures, and patterns of heterogeneity would move her is, on my criterion, holding the belief scientifically, whether or not she turns out to be right. A colleague who holds that institutions are causally prior to economic growth but answers, whenever asked what would unsettle that belief, that history is too complex to say in advance is, on my criterion, not holding it scientifically; the belief may be true, but it is being fixed by a method closer to tenacity than to science. Likewise, a philosopher who asserts that all observation is theory-laden yet cannot say what experience would force him to reconsider is, on my view, advertising a fallibilism he is not actually practicing.

I am aware this sounds Popperian, and I want to know whether it is something better, worse, or already fully worked out under a familiar name. The three objections I keep hearing in my own head are these. First, a specification is itself a belief, so the requirement seems to generate a regress, and I need to say where the chain of specifications stops or why it need not start. Second, Duhem-style holism suggests that no single specification ever captures exactly what is at stake, because what gets revised under pressure is a whole web of beliefs rather than one isolated claim. Third, Peirce also seems committed to a kind of critical common-sensism: inquiry has to begin from some beliefs so entrenched and so vague that we cannot spell out in advance what would dislodge them, and demanding specifications everywhere might make inquiry impossible rather than honest. Give me the strongest version of each objection, tell me which of them is fatal and which merely uncomfortable, and say plainly whether the specification requirement is a genuine extension of Peirce, a disguised restatement of a familiar position, or a criterion that fails on its own terms.
