I'm retired now, after a working life in the natural sciences, and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously. Two essays I've now read three times each seem to talk straight past one another: W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" and William James's "The Will to Believe." The first camp, the Cliffordian evidentialist, holds roughly that we have a moral duty to proportion belief to evidence and that believing on insufficient evidence is wrong in itself. The second, the Jamesian pragmatist, holds roughly that when an option is living, forced, and momentous and the intellect cannot settle it, the passional nature may lawfully decide, and a belief may be judged partly by the life it makes possible.

The Clifford sentence I keep stubbing my toe on, where he draws the moral of the shipowner case, is this: "The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

Please draft the joint statement the two camps would produce after a genuine adversarial collaboration, the kind both sides could actually sign. Not a debate or a dramatized exchange, and not your own verdict on who wins. Start with the claims both can accept, and be specific about how much of that Clifford sentence each side signs: would James sign it as applied to the shipowner? Would he sign it as a fully general principle? Would Clifford accept a Jamesian caveat that in a forced and momentous option — where waiting for more evidence is itself a choice with costs — the duty comes out differently? Then set out the residual disagreements, in wording each side would accept for its own view. Then give me the cruxes, the sub-questions whose settlement would actually move at least one side, and for each crux a concrete case or test both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I'd especially like one test involving a clinician who cannot wait for more evidence before acting, and one about whether doubt itself has costs that change the duty.

One formatting constraint: continuous prose only, like a long letter. No headings, no numbered lists, no bullets, no bold, and no tables. I know a disagreements table is the natural way to present this, but I want to see wether the whole thing can be written as something an actual person might sign. And if part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, or mostly about which cases count as forced and momentous, say so plainly and identify which part.
