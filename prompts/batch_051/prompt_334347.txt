I keep going back and forth on something from our department's AI-and-philosophy reading group, and I'd like a careful second opinion. The old Löbian obstacle says, very roughly, that a consistent formal system of the right kind can't prove its own soundness: if it proved the schema "if my rules prove P, then P," Löb's theorem would force it to prove P for every sentence P, including 0 = 1, so it either stays consistent and can't certify its own reliability, or it proves everything and is useless. People in AI safety talk about this as a principled reason a self-trusting agent hits a ceiling: it can't bootstrap a proof that its reasoning is reliable.

But I've started to wonder whether that argument survives the agents we actually build now. An LLM-based agent isn't a deductive system demonstrating theorems; it's a statistical model emitting tokens, and when it "checks its own reasoning," it is just emitting more tokens, not carrying out a proof in a formal language with a real provability predicate. So the premise that the agent is the sort of object Löb's theorem applies to looks false straight away. But I don't want to conclude to fast, because the agent does still have to decide whether to trust its outputs, and if it ever treats its own outputs as authoritative evidence about the world — or about its own outputs — there might be a self-referential structure that recreates a cousin of the old problem in probabilistic or decision-theoretic form.
