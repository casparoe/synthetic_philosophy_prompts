Act as an expert in philosophy of religion and in literary criticism, and take as much time as the problem needs before you settle on a reading. I keep coming back to a question that the rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums I read tend to handle badly: can a translated text be divinely inspired, and what exactly is it that bears the property of being inspired? I want to approach it through Jorge Luis Borges's story "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote," because I suspect the story gets further into the underlying theory of texts than the standard theological arguments do.

Set up the two poles of the dispute for me. On one side is the ancient status of the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures: some Jewish and early Christian communities treated it not as a mere rendering of an inspired original but as carrying its own authority, and legends grew up that the translators themselves had been divinely guided. On the other side is the KJV-only movement, which holds that the English text of the King James Bible is the inspired and preserved word of God for English-speaking believers, so that a translation can bear full scriptural authority in its own right. The live question underneath both is where inspiration resides: in the original act of composition, in the text considered as a sequence of words, in the translator's act, or in a community's reception and recognition.

Now bring in Borges. The story imagines a twentieth-century French writer who sets himself the task of composing — not copying — several passages of Don Quixote that coincide word for word with Cervantes's text. Borges's narrator insists that the two verbally identical texts are nevertheless different works with different meanings and different literary value, because who wrote them, when, and under what historical circumstances are intrinsic to what they mean. The "same" words can constitute a new work.

I want your reading to do four things. First, state what the story's implied theory of textual identity and meaning, taken at full strength, entails for the Septuagint and KJV-only positions: does it make an inspired translation easier to formulate (a translation is a new text issuing from a new, unrepeatable act) or harder (if the words underdetermine the identity of the work, a translation cannot simply inherit authority from its source and must earn it independently)? Second, mark where the analogy is illuminating and where it breaks down. Menard's project is secular, deliberately anachronistic, and framed by a narrator who may be ironizing throughout, whereas a doctrine of inspired translation requires a divine agent and a community capable of recognizing the result. Third, take up the authentication problem: if a translation can be inspired, by what signs could a community tell an inspired translation from a merely competent or tendentious one, and does Borges help with that question at all? Fourth, resist the reading where it should be resisted: say what the story cannot reach in the theological debate, and then say what the theological debate can borrow from Borges that its own standard examples do not supply.

Before you write the final essay, brainstorm at least three distinct ways of mapping the story onto the theological positions, identify the one you find most defensible, and then present that as a continuous, argued reading. Avoid bibliographic digressions; the point is the reasoning, not the literature review.
