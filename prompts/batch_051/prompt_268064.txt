I'm presenting a paper next month to our social psychology workshop. My thesis is that national character is a coherent notion only if you treat it as a loose, probabilistic generalization about aggregate tendencies, mediated by institutions, laws, education, and selective migration, rather than as a set of fixed individual traits. I want to claim it's sometimes acceptable to say things like "country X is, on average, more rule-punitive then country Y" if you state the base rate, institutional context, and within-country variance, but the moment you reify it into an essence or use it to predict an individual you've made a category mistake.

The audience will be mostly cultural psychologists, a couple of people who work on implicit bias, and one historian who thinks any national-character talk is just warmed-over Herder. They're not hostile to me personally, but they think this kind of generalization is how stereotypes get laundered through social science. Prepare me for objections in roughly the order I'll get them, phrased as they'd actually phrase them. For each: what to concede, what to hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I'm probably hoping nobody asks and why it hurts. Don't give me a general defense; give me a prep sheet for question period.
