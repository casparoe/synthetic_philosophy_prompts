Here's an asymmetry I keep noticing in how people talk about influence. Take two people in the same week. One works in a company's benefits office and switches the retirement plan from opt-in to opt-out, with automatic yearly increases in contributions. Everyone calls that smart choice architecture: using inertia to get employees to do what, by their own lights, they already want to do but can't be bothered to start. The other works for a subscription company and makes cancelling require a phone call, a long hold, and a retention script, while the marketing team anchors the monthly price against a fake regular price. Both of them are exploiting the same two biases, inertia and anchoring, to change behavior without changing anyone's options or giving anyone new information. But one is called a nudge and the other a dark pattern, and almost nobody thinks that's a double standard.

I get the obvious reply: one is mostly for the target's benefit and the other is mostly for the company's. But I'm not sure who benefits does the work. Suppose a well-meaning spouse hides vegetables in their partner's dinner so the partner eats better. If the partner found out they'd be annoyed, and most of us would say its manipulative even though it's for their benefit. But we don't say the opt-out retirement plan is manipulative. And it's disclosed doesn't clean things up either: a charity fundraiser who openly says most people in your building have already chipped in is using social proof on you in plain sight, and that still feels more manipulative than the retirement default, even though it's just as transparent.

So here's my question: is there a principle that actually sorts these cases into legitimate persuasion versus manipulation, or are our judgments just tracking vibes about which influencer we happen to like? I want the strongest candidate principle you can find. It might be about consent to being influenced, about whether the influence runs through our rational capacities or around them, about whether the influencer would endorse the mechanism if the roles were reversed, or something else entirely. Then stress-test it against the vegetable-hiding spouse, the opt-out retirement plan, the cancellation runaround, and a couple of cases of your own that are as similar as possible but pull in opposite directions. At the end, tell me whether the principle is strong enough that we should reorganize our judgments around it, or whether we should just admit the asymmetry is a prejudice and either get stricter about defaults or loosen up about the dark patterns.
