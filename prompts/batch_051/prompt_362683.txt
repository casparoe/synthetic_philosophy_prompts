Ok so in my econ class we did the Ellsberg-paradox demo, but with bags of poker chips instead of urns. Bag A had exactly 50 red and 50 black chips, where you could see the mix. Bag B had 100 chips, each one red or black, but nobody would tell you how many of each. Basically everyone in the room, when asked which bag they'd draw from to win $10 on red, picked Bag A, and then when asked which bag they'd draw from to win $10 on black, also picked Bag A. My teacher said this is the classic sign of ambiguity aversion: people prefer the risk they can put a number on over the risk they can't, even though choosing A for both colors can't be justified by any single belief about Bag B's mix.

Here's the version I haven't been able to stop thinking about. Change Bag B so that before you draw, a computer picks the number of red chips uniformly at random from 0 to 100 (all 101 possibilities equally likely), fills the bag accordingly with that many red and the rest black, and then you draw one chip without ever learning the number the computer picked. Now in some objective sense your chance of drawing a red is exactly 50%, same as Bag A. My friend says the paradox is just over: at that point the two bags are identical gambles, and no one could rationally still prefer Bag A. But I still get the same uneasy feeling about Bag B that I got in the original demo, even though I know the rule that generated it, and I can't tell if that feeling is tracking something real or is just a leftover cognitive glitch.
