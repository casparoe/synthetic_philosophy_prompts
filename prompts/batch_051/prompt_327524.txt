I run the verification desk at a news organization. Several times a week we receive a contested video — an official apparently taking a bribe, phone footage of police violence, a candidate's offhand remark — and must decide, on deadline, whether to treat it as genuine enough to publish and to use as a check on the people denying it. The old assumption that video settles such disputes is no longer available: the clip might be a deepfake, and even a genuine clip can be waved away as one, a move some call the liar's dividend. I need a reusable decision procedure my desk can follow, with no tools beyond the file and publicly available context, for deciding when a contested video can still settle a factual dispute in the way video evidence once did — serve as an epistemic backstop — and when it cannot. The procedure should list the questions to ask in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where it terminates in a decision not to rely on the video, and mark which steps are editorial judgments rather than routine checks. Then run it on three cases: (1) an anonymously leaked video of an official accepting a bribe, recompressed through messaging apps, with no original metadata, which the official calls deepfaked; (2) multiple independently uploaded phone videos of police violence at a protest, mutually consistent in time and place, each from an unknown uploader, which the police call fabricated; (3) a single-source video of a candidate making a racist remark, sent by an anonymous account, on which our forensic checks are inconclusive. Include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer the desk would rather not accept, and say what the procedure cannot settle.
