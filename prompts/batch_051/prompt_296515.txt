I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a column about what, if anything, makes something funny. I would rather not argue downward from a theory; I want to start from my own recorded reactions to concrete cases and see whether any principle captures them, or whether some of my reactions have to be revised. The standard positions, as I understand them, are these: humour as superiority (laughter at another's flaw, failure, or misfortune, pleasurable because it puts us above them); humour as incongruity (laughter at a violated expectation or a clash of frames); humour as relief (laughter as the discharge of accumulated tension or nervous energy); and humour as benign violation (something is funny when it registers as wrong or threatening and simultaneously safe or playfully framed).

Here are my cases and verdicts, written down as honestly as I can manage.

1. A silent-film comedian slips on a banana peel, both legs fly upward, he lands flat on his back, then springs to his feet smiling. Verdict: funny.

2. A security-camera clip of an elderly pedestrian slipping on ice, falling hard, and lying still as blood spreads across the pavement. Verdict: not funny; I flinch and close the tab.

3. A political cartoon of a powerful cabinet minister drawn with a tiny head on a bloated body, seated on a throne built from taxpayers' banknotes. Verdict: funny, and the fun sharpens because the target is powerful and the mockery feels earned.

4. A stand-up comedian who does not stutter performs a bit in which the entire joke is an exaggerated imitation of a stutter; the punchlines are the blocks themselves. Verdict: not funny; I stop watching the stage and start watching the audience.

5. A comedian who stutters performs a structurally similar bit, from within their own experience, at a fundraiser for a stuttering association, before an audience many of whom stutter; the length of the blocks becomes comic timing, and the room laughs with recognition. Verdict: funny — and not just mildly; it lands as honest. I cannot say why these two should land so differently, since in both the stutter itself carries the joke.

6. An estate agent's board outside a houseboat reads at the bottom, "A rare liquid asset." Verdict: mildly amusing in a groan-inducing way; it makes me smile, not laugh. There is no butt, no harm, and no tension released.

7. A false fire alarm empties our building, and after everyone files back to their desks a colleague deadpans, "Next time, I'm taking the stairs for the cardio." The room laughs far harder than the line deserves, and by the next morning the same line wouldn't raise a smile. Verdict: funny in the moment, flat on recall.

8. An adult wiggles their fingers at a toddler in a game, the toddler shrieks and collapses with laughter, and the same gesture from an unfamiliar adult produces alarm rather than laughter. Verdict: not a joke, but evidently the same machinery; the play frame seems to be doing all the work.

The two pairings that trouble me are (1) against (2) — the same physical mishap, opposite verdicts — and (4) against (5) — nearly the same material, opposite verdicts depending on who says it and to whom. And then (3) sits uneasily with (4): both hold someone up as the object of laughter, yet I only enjoy one.

What I would like from you is this: state the principle (or the small set of candidate principles) that best captures my verdicts; say precisely what each clause predicts and which case forces it; apply the principle to three further cases — (a) a deepfake video of a ruling-party minister slipping on a banana peel at a press conference and popping up unhurt, (b) the same dark joke about a company's layoffs told at a bar by a fired employee versus by the CEO who ordered the layoffs, and (c) an AI-generated image of a horse in a suit reading a newspaper on a train, with no victim and no punchline; and finally tell me plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether that is a reason to complicate the principle or a reason for me to abandon the verdict.
