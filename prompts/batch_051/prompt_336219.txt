Take the relevant facts as given: in 2012 a Colorado baker refused to design and create a custom wedding cake for a same-sex couple because of his religious views about marriage; the couple brought a complaint under the state's public-accomodations antidiscrimination law; the Colorado Civil Rights Commission ruled against the baker, and later proceedings raised real questions about whether the Commission had shown anti-religious animus; in 2018 the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 7-2 for the baker on the narrow ground that the Commission had not treated his religious objections neutrally, and it explicitly did not decide whether religious objectors get a general exemption from such laws. What I want is to run this episode through Rawls's views on religion and public reason: the idea that coercive state action is legitimate only if it can be justified by reasons all reasonable citizens could accept, that religious reasons by themselves are not enough for constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, and the proviso that citizens may bring their comprehensive doctrines into public debate so long as in due course they also offer properly public reasons.
