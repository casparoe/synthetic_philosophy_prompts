Set up: Rousseau's general will. The idea is that each person has a private will (what they happen to want as an individual) and a general will (what they'd want insofar as they think as a citizen, in common with everyone else). And Rousseau has that weird slogan about people being forced to be free: if the sovereign compels you to obey the general will, that compulsion is really freedom because you yourself willed it qua citizen, even if your private will is kicking and screaming.

Now cash that out with a simple n-person prisoner's dilemma. Everyone is better off if everyone cooperates (that's the general will made concrete), but each individual prefers to defect whatever the others do, so without something added, the general will loses to the private will. Add a sovereign that can punish defectors, making cooperation individually rational, and in the standard Rousseau-y version the sovereign's commands just are the general will, so being punished for defecting is being forced to be free.

Here's the variation. Say the sovereign isn't identical to the general will; it's an actual institution that tries to track it and has a publicly known error rate: for any given policy, with probability .8 it picks the option that really is in everyone's common interest, and with probability .2 it picks something that benefits only a faction (and is worse for everyone else). Everyone knows these probabilities, and each citizen has a private hunch, not common knowledge, about which kind of policy the current one is. The punishment technology still works mechanically: once the sovereign picks an option, complying is made individually rational.

Does the forced-to-be-free defense survive that change? I want you to go through it piece by piece. The ex ante consent part (you'd sign up for this institution behind some veil or as a citizen), the epistemic authority part (the sovereign knows better than you what you really will), and the self-legislation part (when you're punished you can tell yourself the rule is one you made). Where does the .2 error rate bite hardest? If you're punished under a policy that is actually the faction's option, can that punishment still be redescribed as freedom, or is it just coercion with a probability attached? And is there some error rate where the whole phrase stops making sense, in a way that can be made precise rather than just lower is better?
