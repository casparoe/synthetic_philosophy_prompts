I recently found myself trying to assign a probability to a decision I was still in the middle of making — whether to accept a job that would require my family to relocate — because my partner and I needed to plan around both outcomes. The attempt immediately produced a familiar-looking tension: if I treated my own choice as an event about which I could be uncertain, I seemed to be predicting myself rather than deciding; but if I refused to assign any probability to it, I seemed to make planning artificially blind. I would like a sharp argument that makes sense of this conflict rather than merely restating it.

Please argue for the following position. A deliberating agent may rationally assign probabilities to her own future choices, but only under what I will call the planning stance, and those probabilities must be quarantined from her first-person evaluation of her options: they may guide downstream planning, but they cannot be fed back into the question of which option is better or into the question of how likely she is, while still deliberating, to choose each option. In defending this position, you should (1) articulate the difference between the planning stance and the deliberative stance, where the latter is the stance from which one is deciding among options one still treats as live; (2) explain why the familiar thought that deliberation crowds out prediction, if accepted, is a constraint on the deliberative stance rather than a global ban on all self-assigned probabilities; and (3) answer what seems to me the strongest objection, namely that a probability that is rational at all should be usable in every calculation, including in the evaluation of the very options over which one is deliberating.
