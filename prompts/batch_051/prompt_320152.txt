I help run an adult discussion group at a parish, and last month the group split sharply over a case one member described: a hospice nurse let a dying woman believe that her estranged son had called to say he loved her, when he never did. Half the room thought the nurse had done something plainly wrong; the other half thought it was plainly kind. I want an exercise that sorts people by how they actually judge cases, rather than by the position they claim for themselves.

Please write a "which view of lying do you actually hold?" quiz with eight to ten questions. Each question should be a concrete case — the murderer at the door, a false résumé, comforting a dementia patient, a lie to a frightened child, protecting a fugitive — with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key. Build the questions from three distinctions so that answer patterns land in a logical space rather than in a fixed list of names: (1) whether lying is ever permissible; (2) whether some lies are graver than others; and (3) if lying is sometimes permissible, whether it is permissible only to prevent serious harm that the truth would foreseeably cause, or whenever lying is expected to do more good than harm overall.

The scoring key should map response patterns onto at least these positions: strict absolutism (lying is always wrong, and ranking lies is beside the point); graded absolutism in roughly Augustine's sense (every lie is a sin, but some lies are far graver than others — a lie about religious teaching far above a lie meant only to shield someone from bodily harm); a limited exception for serious harm; and a consequentialist case-by-case view. Note any response pattern that has no leaf, and say briefly whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely neglected. Also flag any leaves your instrument cannot reliably separate, and state the non-case question that would separate them. Avoid jargon, and avoid any question that simply asks whether lying is always wrong.

Finally, tell me where the instrument's reasoning is most likely to fail: which question you are least confident actually tracks its intended distinction, and what pattern of answers would make you redesign the scoring key. Keep the whole response under 900 words.
