At a standing philosophical reading group last month, two of the other members fell into a dispute that none of us could referee cleanly. The topic was whether, in a polarized public sphere, one is ever rationally justified in describing one's political opponents as arguing in bad faith. The exchange, as I recorded it, went roughly as follows.

(1) Mara began with a skeptical point. The charge of bad faith, she said, is unfalsifiable in practice: we cannot inspect motives, and across a partisan divide the very same conduct gets read as sincere when it comes from our own side and cynical when it comes from the other. She added that polarization has a more economical explanation — people inhabit different informational environments, with different sources and different social penalties for dissent — so someone can be perfectly sincere while looking, from outside, like she is refusing to engage. The bad-faith charge, she concluded, converts an epistemic problem into a moral verdict.

(2) Leo replied that this cannot be right as a general matter. In ordinary life we do make warranted bad-faith ascriptions: when a person ignores a clear correction, shifts the goalposts, wields an objection he would dismiss if his own side used it, or refuses to accept a plain consequence of his own argument, we are not merely guessing at his inner states. If none of that ever licenses the charge, he said, then no one ever argues in bad faith, which is absurd.

(3) Mara responded that those behaviors are fully explained by motivated reasoning and self-deception. The person is not cynically performing an argument he disbelieves; he is honestly biased, and an outsider cannot distinguish honest bias from deliberate insincerity. So, she claimed, the accusation still does no diagnostic work beyond condemnation.

(4) Leo answered that Mara had fixed on the wrong level of description. Good faith, he said, is not a report about a speaker's private feelings; it is a norm governing the exchange of reasons. One violates that norm when one's reasoning is systematically one-sided in a pattern one would not accept if the roles were reversed — whatever one privately believes. And because the best evidence about partisan reasoning shows exactly such systematic asymmetries, calling the pattern bad faith is a factual description of broken argumentative norms, not name-calling.

(5) Mara came back that Leo had saved the accusation from refutation only by relocating it. Once good faith becomes a matter of which argumentative norms a person honors, she said, the accusation becomes symmetrical: each side can point to the other's selective charity toward itself. A standard that licenses the same charge for every participant is no guide to who is actually reasoning well.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, state what is right in it, what is mistaken, and whether the point survives the reply that follows it. Then say what both participants are missing. Finally, offer your own view about what would have to be established before attributing bad faith across a political divide is justified.
