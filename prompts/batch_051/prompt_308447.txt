I'm a journalist who mostly writes about science and ideas, not a historian, but I've spent the last few weeks poking at the way people talk about "war guilt" without noticing what the word guilt is actually doing. I keep coming back to the First World War because it seems to be the hardest case. The old quarrel among historians about whether Germany's leaders deliberately risked a continent-wide war (the Fischer controversy, roughly) made me think that documents can change who people blame, but it left something else unclear: what it actually is to hold a nation responsible for a war at all. So I'd like a working definition of national responsibility for a war — "war guilt", if you prefer — that you then test against the following cases. The German blank cheque to Austria-Hungary after Sarajevo. The assasination itself, carried out by a Bosnian-Serb nationalist with ties to Serbian military inteligence. Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia in July 1914. Russia's decision to order general mobilisation rather than a partial one. France's financial and diplomatic backing for Russia's military build-up. Britain's decision to enter the war after the invasion of Belgium. And finally the view, which I at least half believe, that the whole thing was a systems accident: railway timetables, alliance blocs, and generals who didn't understand what they were setting loose.

I want a definition that separates causing the war, intending a war, wanting a limited war, and being most to blame for the general one, and that produces a clear yes or no for each case — or at least a principled reason to refuse a verdict. My own suspicion is that the word is doing two jobs, one causal and one moral, and I'd like the definition to say what happens when they come apart. Then tell me which of your verdicts is least secure. Please also mark what is settled history and what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present one interpretation as the consensus when it isn't.
