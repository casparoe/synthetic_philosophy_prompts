I have been thinking about whether species are real features of the world or only convenient groupings imposed on organisms by taxonomists. Here is an argument that seems to show they are merely imposed, although I distrust the conclusion.

(1) If a biological grouping were part of the real structure of the world, then it would have to be a natural kind: the members would share some underlying property, or stable cluster of properties, in virtue of which they belong together.
(2) Species fail this test. The leading candidate properties—interbreeding capacity, morphological similarity, common descent, and shared ecological role—do not converge; in many cases they sort organisms into different groups, and each has recognized exceptions.
(3) Therefore, species are not real features of the world.
(4) If species are not real features of the world, then they can only be convenient, conventional groupings imposed by taxonomists.
(5) Therefore, species are convenient, conventional groupings imposed by taxonomists.

If the argument goes through, then species are invented rather than discovered, which many taxonomists would deny. So I suspect a false step, but I cannot locate it. Does the argument go through? If not, identify exactly which step fails and state what the correct version of that step would be.
