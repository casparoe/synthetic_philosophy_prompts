There is a standing dispute among people who think about Moloch-style traps—situations in which individually reasonable competitive moves drive a group into an outcome that all parties regard as worse—about what kind of failure the trap fundamentally is. One view treats such races to the bottom as principally a problem of incentives and credible commitment: the participants have intelligible ends and do not want the collective result, but any unilateral forbearance exposes them to being outcompeted, and they cannot bind themselves, or one another, to a better equilibrium. On this account the remedy is structural—precommitment, regulation, reputation, altering payoffs through taxes or standards, or installing an enforcer—and the participants' underlying values are taken as largely fixed. The rival view objects that in the deepest cases the race does not merely operate on existing desires; it trains them. It teaches participants to experience the race's own metric (citations, engagement, prestige, growth) as simply what it is to be good at the thing in question, and to hear calls for restraint as naivety or a confession of inadequacy. On this view, a solution that treats preferences as given is likely to move the race rather than end it, because part of the trap is a failure of self-formation as much as a failure of coordination.

Write a dialogue between two proponents of these views. Give each the best version of their argument rather than a strawman; have each one raise the hardest objection to the other's position, and have each concede at least one point that costs them something. They should work with a single running example—academic hiring and publication metrics, say, or local journalism and clickbait—and they should argue over whether the escape lies in changing the rules of the game or in changing the players' sense of which games are worth playing well. At some point they should tangle with the question whether the second view can be quietly absorbed into the first by treating the formation of preferences as just another variable inside the game, and what would be lost if it were. The dialogue should read as two people reasoning aloud together rather than as two position papers colliding; later remarks should answer earlier ones specifically, and the piece may end unsettled so long as the exact point of disagreement has come into sharper focus.
