I keep going back and forth on an argument I've seen floating around that tries to use computational complexity against expected utility theory, and I can't tell if it's deep or just a category mistake. The rough idea is that maximizing expected utility in any realistic decision problem can require solving NP-hard (or worse) search problems, so the theory is telling us to do something no finite agent can actually do. The standard reply I've heard is that complexity theory is about worst-case resource use on particular formal models of computation, and that's a descriptive/computational matter, whereas a norm of rationality is a standard you can fail to meet—it isn't supposed to be cheap. But I worry that reply proves to much: it'd let a normative theory demand a literally uncomputable procedure and then just call that an "ideal." Can you help me pin down what the two sides are actually disagreeing about, and weather the complexity-based objection to expected utility maximization survives that reply?
