I am preparing a short statement of my views on introspection to circulate to a philosophy of mind reading group, and I would like it audited before I send it out. The position feels coherent to me, but I suspect that is because I find each piece individually plausible; I want to know whether the pieces in fact add up. Here is the profile as it stands.

First, introspection is a genuine and sui generis source of knowledge about my own mind, neither reducible to inference from behavior nor to ordinary outward perception, though I take it to be fallible in the way both of those are. Second, the classic confabulation findings are real and important: subjects can be brought to offer sincere but false explanations of the reasons for choices or judgments they demonstrably did not make. But I want to restrict the lesson. In my view, those findings tell us about our access to the causes of, and reasons for, our decisions and attitudes; they do not undermine reports about the phenomenal character of one's current experience. Third, for current phenomenal states, at least in the bodily and sensory cases, appearances are authoritative in a narrow sense: if it introspectively seems to me that I am in pain, or that my pain feels a certain way, then it is true that I am in that state and that it feels that way; there is no gap between its seeming and its being. Fourth, I think cognitive phenomenology is real: there is something it is like to understand a sentence, to judge that something is the case, or to search for a word, over and above any imagery and emotion that may accompany the thought. Fifth, introspection of sensory experience is transparent: when I attend to what my visual experience is like, the only qualities I find are qualities presented as belonging to the world, such as the redness of the tomato, and not further intrinsic qualities of the experience itself; there is no introspectable mental paint over and above the represented properties. Sixth, introspective access is best modeled as a form of attention, and attention is selective: it enhances, filters, and can even alter what it picks out. Seventh, introspective evidence is what gives the standard thought experiments in philosophy of mind — the zombie, Mary the color scientist, the inverted spectrum, the explanatory gap — their force; if introspection is not a reliable way of knowing what experience is like, those arguments lose their claim on us.

I would be grateful for an audit of this set. Which commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each conflict? Which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and why? For each genuine tension, what would the cheapest repair be, and what would it cost elsewhere in the view? Finally, if I were willing to revise exactly one of these commitments in order to make the whole set most defensible, which single revision should I make, and what is the main philosophical cost of making it?
