I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of economics — about 24 students, 75-minute sessions — and we are entering the unit on expected utility and risk. The misconception I keep failing to dislodge by lecturing is this: students compute the expected value of a multiplicative gamble, see that it is positive, and conclude that repeatedly taking the gamble with the proceeds reinvested must be a good idea. But there are gambles in which the ensemble average over many parallel players is strongly positive while almost every individual path decays toward nothing, because the mean is carried by a tiny minority of astronomically lucky paths. I would like a classroom game in which the students run into this themselves before I name it.

As a starting point, I am imagining something like the following, but please treat it as seed material and improve on it. Each student begins with 100 chips. Each round, a fair coin is flipped independently for each student: heads multiplies that student's current stack by 1.5, tails by 0.6, and the entire stack is always at risk. After 25 or 30 rounds, the group average should have risen while most individual stacks have fallen; I want the activity to surface that gap starkly. Then I would like a second phase in which each student chooses, round by round, what fraction of their current stack to stake on the same coin, so they can discover that staking everything is the strategy that maximizes expected value each round but not the strategy that grows a typical path over time. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the ensemble/time-average distinction and the non-ergodicity of multiplicative wealth dynamics, and it should be honest about which parts of the idea the game cannot do justice to on its own.
