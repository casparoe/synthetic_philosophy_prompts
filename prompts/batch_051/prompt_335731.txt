Write a proposal for a philosophy-of-biology term paper (about 6,000 words, one semester) on a question about biological individuality. The assignment is to produce a research plan, not to defend a thesis, and the proposal will be read by a course instructor; no citations or bibliography are expected or wanted.

The proposal should be developed from the following half-formed idea. The debate over whether holobionts are organisms treats a holobiont as a host together with its persistent, often coevolving microbial partners (for example, a mammal and its gut microbiome, or a coral and its symbiotic algae and bacteria). A standard approach runs through candidate criteria for organismality and then argues about which ones holobionts meet. The usual candidates are: functional integration (the parts work as a coordinated whole); metabolic interdependence (each part depends on the others for necessary metabolites); common fate (the parts flourish or fail together); a reproductive bottleneck that transmits partners vertically from parent host to offspring; being a unit of selection (a level at which natural selection tracks the whole association's fitness); and a bounded physiological or developmental system that distinguishes itself from its environment.

The "yes" camp says holobionts do well enough on the integration, interdependence, and physiological-boundedness criteria to count as organisms. The "no" camp says they fail the developmental and evolutionary criteria, because host and microbial lineages remain partly separate, retain partly divergent fitness interests, and are only loosely coupled in fate. A third camp says the answer merely depends on which criterion one chooses, and takes this to vindicate a pluralism about biological individuality. In the background there is a comparative agenda: social insect colonies are often called superorganisms, siphonophores are treated as one organism despite being clonal colonies of zooids, and holobionts are sometimes presented as the next case along the same spectrum.

I suspect the live question is not whether holobionts are organisms, but whether the disagreement among the three camps is generated by different explanatory projects — physiological, evolutionary, developmental, immunological — quietly selecting different criteria while continuing to use the single word "organism." My half-formed thought is that the defensible conclusion will be a constrained pluralism: holobionts are organisms relative to some explanatory projects and not others, and this is a substantive claim about how different biological fields individuate their units rather than a semantic retreat. But I am not sure this is right; in particular, I worry that constrained pluralism collapses into triviality unless we can say when two explanatory projects are entitled to give different answers to what sounds like the same question, and when one project's answer should override another's.

Turn this into a two-page proposal. It should include:
1. A precise statement of the question, framed so that it has a determinate answer or a determinate small set of possible answers.
2. Why the existing positions — strong holobiont individualism, host-centric individualism, and unconstrained pluralism — leave the question open.
3. The approach: what I would actually do, step by step, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives (a survey of the criteria literature, or a case-study adjudication of a single holobiont system).
4. What a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing.
5. A realistic plan for the semester, including where the project could be cut if it grows too large.

The very last line of the response, with nothing after it, should begin with "Summary:" and be exactly one sentence summarizing the proposal's question, method, and expected result.
