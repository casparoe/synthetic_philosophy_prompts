I run the market-making desk at a small betting exchange. We quote prices on roughly forty markets a day—sport, politics, and entertainment—and for every line we quote two decimal prices: a back price, at which a customer may back the outcome (we are then the layer), and a lay price, at which a customer may lay the outcome (we are then the backer). A trader on my desk typically has twenty minutes to check a proposed set of quotes before it goes live, and a mistake can be exploited within minutes by automated accounts. Our risk committee has one non-negotiable rule: our quoted prices must never allow a customer to combine backs and lays across our markets so as to guarantee a profit against us whatever happens. They call this opening a Dutch book, and they forbid it.

I want a reusable desk procedure for deciding, before publication, whether a proposed set of quotes is coherent in that sense. The procedure should take decimal back and lay prices as input and return one of three instructions: publish, do not publish, or escalate, where an escalation must state the exact question for the head of risk, since only the head of risk may sign off an escalated set of quotes. Write the procedure as ordered questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and mark the steps at which judgement cannot be eliminated. The traders using it are numerate but have no formal training in probability theory, so each step must be expressed in the language of odds, stakes, and sure profit rather than in the language of the Dutch book theorem.

Then run the procedure on the following three cases.

(i) A football match quoted with back prices of 2.00 (home), 3.50 (draw), and 5.00 (away), and lay prices of 2.10, 3.70, and 5.30 on the same three outcomes.

(ii) A politics book quoting, for one candidate, a nomination market with back 3.00 and lay 3.20, and a presidency market with back 2.50 and lay 2.60.

(iii) A bundle of markets on the same football match—match winner, total goals over/under, and both teams to score—each carrying its own healthy margin and each passing its individual checks, but never checked jointly. In this case I want to see not just what the procedure does, but where it stops and hands the bundle to a person because the relevant state space has become too involved for a twenty-minute check.

Finally, say plainly what the procedure does not protect us against. In particular, distinguish an incoherent set of quotes, which can be turned against us for a certain profit, from a coherent set of quotes that will merely lose us money in expectation if our own probability estimates are poor. The deliverable is the procedure and its application to the three cases, not a derivation of the theorem and not a reading list.
