A recent essay summarized Arendt's account of the Eichmann trial this way: "Arendt's thesis in her book on the Eichmann trial is that the Holocaust was carried out not by moral monsters but by ordinary men who approached mass murder as a bureaucratic assignment. Eichmann himself, on her account, was not an ideological fanatic; he was a mediocre, cliché-ridden careerist, more concerned with his own advancement and with following the rules than with the people he was deporting. Arendt called this the banality of evil, and she intended it as a general claim about the modern world: wherever institutions divide labor, demand obedience, and cover individual actions with administrative routine, otherwise decent people will do terrible things without malice, without full awareness, and without ever deciding to do harm. Her deeper conclusion was that the real culprit in such cases is the bureaucratic machine itself, which makes individual culpability beside the point." Something about that final move strikes me as off given what I know of Arendt, but the essay is by a professional intellectual historian. To what extent is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what a careful reading of Arendt would treat as a distortion or an outright denial of her position. Then tell me whether the essay's concluding inference about the machine and individual culpability survives the corrections. In doing so, please distinguish the points that are settled from those that are genuinely contested among people who know the topic well, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. I would like the answer in American English.
