I chair the prizes and honors committee of a research foundation that awards an annual Medal for Distinguished Achievement in Mathematics. The medal's stated purpose is to honor work of outstanding originality that has deepened the discipline. This month we must rule on a posthumous nomination.

The nominee, a mathematician at a university whose archive now holds her papers, published little and spent the last twenty years on a single longstanding open problem. Shortly before her death she deposited roughly two thousand pages of notebooks in the archive under a deed with strict terms: for seventy years, no one may read, copy, digitize, or quote from the notebooks, with one exception. The deed permits three referees selected by her estate to examine the notebooks in confidence, solely for the purpose of assessing a posthumous prize nomination. The three referees have now done so and have each submitted a confidential report. They agree that the notebooks contain a complete, rigorous solution to the problem, worked out at, in one report's phrase, a level of elegance that would place it among the finest achievements of the century. No one outside the three referees, and no member of this committee, has seen the proof, and under the deed the field will not see it for seventy years, if ever.

The committee is split. Some members say the medal is for the achievement itself: she actually solved the problem, the solution is certified by qualified referees, and what happens to the notebooks afterwards is a separate matter from how good the mathematics is. The others say the medal exists to honor work that enters and enriches the discipline, and a solution that cannot inform, teach, or be scrutinized by any living mathematician, for generations, is not the kind of excellence the foundation exists to celebrate; they also worry that awarding a medal on the basis of sealed, privately refereed notebooks would set a precedent in which the prize becomes hostage to uncheckable testimony. The first group replies that this conflates excellence with dissemination; the second replies that a wholly unshared advance is excellent only in a thin formal sense.

The split tracks a deeper disagreement: whether excellence retains its value when no one else can learn from, use, or even inspect it. I need to send the committee a written decision memo before next week's meeting. Draft it. The memo should open with a clear recommendation: award the medal, decline the nomination, award a special citation separate from the medal, with or without conditions, or postpone the decision pending conditions you specify. It should then give the reasoning in a form the committee can act on, including an honest engagement with the strongest consideration against your recommendation and a statement of what general principle, if any, your recommendation would commit the foundation to.
