i've been trying to get clear on when a coincidence should actually move me toward believing something's going on, and when it's just noise, and my gut keeps giving different answers to cases that look kind of similar. Here are my verdicts:

- At a party with 23 people, two people discover they have the same birthday. I shrug. I know the birthday-paradox math: with 23 people a shared birthday is more likely than not, so I don't reach for an explanation.
- A stranger I start chatting with on a train has my exact birthday. I feel a little shiver of meaning, even though I also think 1-in-365 events happen all the time and I shouldn't be surprised. I suspect my feeling is the thing that's wrong here.
- A friend calls five seconds after I'd just been thinking about him. I roll my eyes. Given the hundreds of times I think about him and he doesn't call, this is just selection bias, and remembering only the hit doesn't make it evidence of anything.
- A roulette wheel comes up red ten times in a row. I'd start suspecting the wheel is rigged, even though an exact sequence like R-B-R-B and so on also has probability 1/1024. Why does one sequence feel loaded and the other doesn't?
- The same coworker wins the lottery twice in one year. I don't care what the exact odds are; I'd assume fraud or some kind of fix.
- Four people on my street get diagnosed with the same rare cancer within five years. I'd push for an environmental investigation, not chalk it up to chance, even though with enough streets in the country one street probably looks like that by accident.

My half-baked principle is that I should only be surprised when the coincidence was specified in advance, and that the birthday paradox shows most anecdotes are just failures to count the misses. But that can't be the whole story: when I walked up to the roulette wheel I didn't specify ten reds in advance—I noticed the pattern and then got suspicious—and the cancer cluster was definitely noticed before it was explained.

So: is there one principle, or a small set, that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please give me the best candidate, state it precisely enough that I could apply it, say which clause is motivated by which case, and then run it on a few cases I haven't listed: a friend who announces the exact score of a football match an hour before kickoff; three people in my town struck by lightning the same summer; a dream about my sister getting engaged that comes true the next day. Be honest about which of my original verdicts the principle can't accomodate, and whether I should revise them.
