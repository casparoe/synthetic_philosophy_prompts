For a seminar I'm teaching on Simone Weil's political thought, I'm compiling a list of her distinct reasons for thinking that political parties should be abolished outright, not reformed. So far I have these four: (a) parties are machines for manufacturing collective passions, and thereby suppress the individual's capacity for disinterested attention to what is true; (b) the party form is inherently totalitarian — democratic parties are just a milder stage of the same disease, not a different kind of thing; (c) party competition does not produce truth because it is a collision of wills, not a genuine exchange of reasons; and (d) parties introduce a criterion of action, namely the party's own growth and survival, that has nothing to do with justice. I want one more distinct reason from her work, one that is not a variant or special case of any of those. I suspect there may be something that draws on her metaphysics of attention or her account of value, rather than on her diagnosis of collective psychology. Give me that argument, or tell me if I'm looking for something that isn't there. And if it does exist, say whether it does independant work — i.e., whether it would still have force for someone who doesn't share her theology or her broader metaphysics.
