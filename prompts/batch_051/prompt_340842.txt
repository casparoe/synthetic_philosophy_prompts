I'm a transportation safety analyst for a regional planning agency. Every few weeks I receive a proposal — a speed-limit change, a new signalized crossing, a bike-lane installation or removal, a revised school route — and I have to produce a one-page recommendation for a board of elected officials and lay members. The board's question is always some version of: is the expected reduction in deaths and serious injuries worth the cost, delay, and political friction? They are suspicious of numbers that look precise but aren't. I want to ground my recommendations in micromorts (one-in-a-million probabilities of death), but I need something more operational than "estimate expected deaths." Please give me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart-in-words, with steps in order — for deciding whether a transportation intervention, or the status quo activity of driving itself, passes a micromort-based test. I will use this for every proposal, so it has to be a repeatable procedure rather than a one-off memo. It must be runnable in about three hours per case by someone with my background (comfortable with arithmetic, but no formal training in decision theory or philosophy), and it must produce a verdict I can defend to that skeptical board.

The procedure should tell me how to set the baseline risk of the current situation; how to treat injuries versus deaths; how to handle voluntary versus imposed risks; how to handle risks concentrated on a few named people versus spread across thousands of anonymous ones; how to handle risks to children versus adults; and how to handle risks people dread versus risks they barely notice. It should flag the points where quantification stops being honest and the decision becomes a value judgment, and it should not launder those judgments into made-up numbers — for example, how to weigh a child's life against an adult's, or how to price a minute of delay. It should also say explicitly what it cannot do. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) lowering a 30 mph speed limit to 25 mph along a five-mile corridor, (2) installing a signalized crossing outside an elementary school, and (3) requiring helmets on a city-run bikeshare system. Make sure at least one case reaches an answer the board — or I — will dislike, and show why the procedure is right to be annoying there.

I realize this whole request may rest on a confusion: micromorts were designed for comparing individual activities, and they may break down at the policy scale, or the question "is it worth it?" may not be the sort of thing a checklist can settle. If so, tell me that plainly rather than giving me a procedure that pretends to work. Please approach this as an expert in risk theory and the philosophy of quantification who is also a clear practical communicator. Before writing the procedure, spend a little time thinking about where micromort reasoning quietly changes meaning when it is scaled up from an individual choice to a public decision, and mark the places where you are least confident in the resulting procedure.
