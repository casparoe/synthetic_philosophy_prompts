I'm a novelist and I realized I've been using two different theories of causation interchangeably, and I want to know if that's sloppy or fine. The counterfactual picture (Lewis, rougly) is: A caused B iff in the nearest world where A doesn't happen, B doesn't happen. The interventionist picture (Woodward, roughly) is: A causes B iff there's an ideal intervention on A, a kind of surgical wiggle from outside the system, that would change B. For me these feel very different in practice. Counterfactuals are what I do when I read my finished draft and ask what had to be true for the ending to happen; interventions are what I do when I redraft and change one detail to see what ripples. I want a real comparison, not just a summary of each. Do these two theories genuinely disagree anywhere, or is interventionism just counterfactuals with variables and a better story about experiments? Shove them both at the cases that matter in fiction: omissions (the letter never sent), preemption (the first poisoner gets there before the second one would have), overdetermination (two gunmen fire at once), and double prevention (a missile shoots down an incoming bomb and so the city survives). Which one better captures what I mean when I say a scene is causaly motivated? Be candid about where each breaks or goes silent.
