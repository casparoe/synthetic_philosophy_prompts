I'm a PhD student in econ, mostly market design, and I've gotten myself attached to the no-trade theorem as more than a technical result—I think it's an epistemological claim about what markets can and can't do. The picture I'd defend is this: if you've got Bayesian agents with a common prior (they'd have assigned the same probabilities before they saw their private information) and they all know each other to be rational, then a purely informational trade can't be common knowledge that both would want. The second someone offers to sell to you at a price, their willingness is itself a signal about what they know, and once you condition on it the expected gains wash out. So the standard story where trading volume is the market pooling dispersed information is, on this view, mostly wrong: the trade itself shows the two sides haven't updated on each other, and high speculative volume is a sign of inconsistent priors, overconfidence, or plain irrationality rather than healthy aggregation.

What I want is not another person telling me the theorem's assumptions are unrealistic—I know that. I want a defeater map. If my picture is wrong, where exactly does it break first? Which of its assumptions being false would do the most damage: common priors, common knowledge of rationality, common knowledge of the inference everyone makes from a trade offer, the starting allocation being ex ante Pareto efficient (nobody thinks they can improve on their holdings before the info arrives), or the idea that the relevant trades are pure information trades rather than hedging or liquidity trades? And what would the world have to look like for each to be the real escape route—what sort of price discovery, volume patterns, or cross-sectional returns among active traders should I take as actual evidence against the no-trade picture, rather than stuff the theorem can quietly accommodate?

One thing I can't decide about: if enough smart traders really internalized the no-trade logic and stopped trading on their own signals, then those signals would stop getting into prices at all, and the market would get less informationally efficient, not more. So the position seems to have a weird self-undermining flavor—believing it too hard might make its picture of prices less accurate. Does that count as a serious problem for the view, or is it just a twist on the point that information and trade can't both be what the folklore says? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, including that one, and be straight with me about whether the common-prior-rationality framework has been stretched so far that no amount of real-world trading could ever count against it. If it has, tell me whether that's a problem for a claim that was supposed to have epistemological bite.
