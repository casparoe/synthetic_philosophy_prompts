I am a teaching assistant for a survey course on aesthetics and the emotions, and I am grading a midterm. The question I set was:

Present the paradox of tragedy as sharply as you can. Then explain how Hume's essay Of Tragedy attempts to dissolve the paradox, and assess whether his account can handle cases in which the subject matter is especially painful or morally repellent.

Below is a student's answer. I want you to grade it as you would grade a serious undergraduate essay answer, on whatever scale you think appropriate, and give the specific comments that justify the grade. Please lead with your grade in the first sentence, followed by your point-by-point reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

Student answer:

The paradox of tragedy is that we normally avoid painful emotions, yet we voluntarily go to tragedies that arouse pity and fear, and we report liking them. This seems irrational unless some feature of tragedy changes the valence of the emotion or adds a source of pleasure. Hume solves this by saying that tragedy's eloquence and artistic representation convert the painful passions into pleasure. The audience feels real sorrow for the characters, but the manner in which the sorrow is produced is so beautiful that the pain is swallowed up in the pleasure of the aesthetic activity. Because Hume thinks this conversion is automatic whenever a painful passion is expressed with sufficient eloquence, he predicts that even real events related in a beautiful narrative would produce the same pleasure. This shows that it is the awareness that the events are fictional, rather than the eloquence, that does the real work. In this respect Hume is really offering a version of Aristotle's catharsis: both hold that pity and fear are purged or transformed into a pleasurable state by the end of the experience. The limitation of Hume's view is that it cannot explain why we might appreciate a tragedy whose subject matter is morally repugnant, because the moral repugnance is too strong to be converted by aesthetic means. A better view would say that such tragedies give us the pleasure of understanding something important about human life, and that this cognitive pleasure outweighs the painful emotional cost.

Please attend especially to whether the student has accurately represented Hume's actual mechanism, as distinct from Aristotle's catharsis, the mere relief of tension, or the view that fictionality alone makes painful affect pleasant; whether the assessment in the final paragraph actually follows from the exposition; and whether the student has conflated distinct solutions to the paradox.
