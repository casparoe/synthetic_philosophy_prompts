I've been arguing with a friend who insists that the value of a conscious state is simply the product of two quantities — how good or bad it feels per moment, and how long it lasts — and that a life's value is the sum of those products. "Consciousness times happiness," he calls it. The arithmetic is appealing because it cleanly explains why we trade intensity against duration, and why a sufficiently long stretch of mild contentment can seem to rival a short burst of intense joy. But when I draw out the consequences, I seem to have an argument that proves too much, and I can't find the step I'm willing to abandon.

Here is the argument:

(1) Valence is continuous: for any experience that counts as positively valenced, no matter how faint, there is another possible experience that is still positive but fainter; there is no smallest positive amount of felt good.

(2) The value of a temporally bounded experience for its subject is its valence multiplied by its duration, with painful experiences taking negative values.

(3) The value of a whole life for its subject is the sum of the values of the experiences composing it.

(4) From (1)–(3): for any finite amount of suffering, however intense, and for any positive target level of well-being, there is a possible life containing exactly that suffering, followed by a sufficiently long stretch of experiences that are positive but so faint that the subject could not reliably tell them apart from indifference, whose total value exceeds the target. An arbitrarily intense finite pain can always be outweighed by enough barely-positive filler.

(5) So the difference between a mild irritation and an hour of torture is, in principle, exactly compensable by a difference in the duration of some barely-positive filler. Severity collapses into a duration discount: pain is not different in kind, only in how long a mild pleasure must run to cancel it.

(6) But then a life consisting of an hour of torture followed by an astronomically long twilight of moments barely distinguishable from neutral is better, all things considered, than a much shorter life of ordinary genuine contentment. That seems to me absurd, or at least a conclusion I do not want to accept without a fight.

Which premise or step should I reject — and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (2) and (3), because abandoning them abandons the arithmetic of value; but the only alternatives I can see are denying (1) by positing a threshold below which faint positives count for zero, or treating some values as lexically prior to others, and each of those looks arbitrary or costs more than I would like.
