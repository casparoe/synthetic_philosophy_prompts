I’m taking a history of economic thought class and got annoyed at how quickly everyone writes Malthus off—the standard line is that he was wrong because he didn’t foresee the Industrial Revolution. But that feels like a cheap shot, and the actual 1798 work is more interesting than the caricature. The passage I keep coming back to is from the preface to An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798):

“It is an obvious truth, which has been taken notice of by many writers, that population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence; but no writer that the Author recollects has inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected: and it is a view of these means which forms, to his mind, the strongest obstacle in the way to any very great future improvement of society.”

What strikes me is that he’s not making a one-off forecast. He’s saying the permanent, unanswered question is the check—the mechanism that keeps population down to whatever subsistence currently is—and that this mechanism, not the current level of technology, is the real obstacle.

So I want a number, not a summary. Give me your credence that Malthus is right but early in this specific sense: over roughly the next 150 years, the long-run binding constraint on human welfare will turn out to be the relation between population (and consumption) pressure and the means of subsistence, with the check taking some mix of mortality, deprivation-driven restraint, or conflict over scarcity—rather than technology and institutions cleanly outrunning that pressure forever. Decompose it. I can see at least these terms: (1) the probability that the demographic transition (fertility falling with development) is a stable, permanent change in how the check operates, rather than a one-time historical episode; (2) the probability that innovation can keep raising the effective resource ceiling faster than population and consumption rise over that whole window; (3) the probability that “subsistence” should be read as a socially defined moving target, which would make Malthus much harder to refute; (4) the probability that the whole “right but early” idea is unfalsifiable in practice—that any outcome could be reconciled with the mechanism after the fact. Give each term a rough probability and one line of justification, show how you combine them, and tell me which term is doing the most work and what evidence would move it most. For calibration: I’m around 0.6, a friend who thinks Malthus is just outdated is around 0.15, and we’d like to know where the disagreement actually lives. And if I’ve set this up so that no number could be well-defined—if “right but early” is doing something weird that I haven’t noticed—say so plainly instead of pretending the question is fine.
