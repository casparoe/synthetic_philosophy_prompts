Here's an argument I keep going back and forth on, from a discussion about whether automatic enrollment in retirement savings is objectionably paternalistic.

(1) A policy is objectionably paternalistic only if it either overrides a person's judgment about their own good, or removes or penalizes an option they had.
(2) Automatic enrollment merely changes the default: it doesn't remove the opt-out, it doesn't penalize opting out, and it doesn't tell you that you're wrong about what's good for you.
(3) So automatic enrollment is not objectionably paternalistic.
(4) The same reasoning applies to any nudge that just changes a default, reorders a cafeteria line, sends a reminder, and so on, so long as it satisfies the conditions in (1).
(5) Therefore the standard "liberal" objection to paternalism gives us no reason to object to nudges of this kind.

I half-suspect step (2) is doing more work than it admits: that turning the "wrong" choice into the opt-out rather than the opt-in changes what it means to decline, or that "not telling you you're wrong" is compatible with deliberately exploiting the fact that you'll predictably fail to act on your own judgment. But maybe the argument is fine and the people who hate nudges really do just want to restrict options. Is the argument valid?
