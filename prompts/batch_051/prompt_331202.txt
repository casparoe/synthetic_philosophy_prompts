Philosophy PhD student here, working on the badness of death. At a departmental workshop someone trotted out the old Epicurean one-liner — “death is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not” — to shut down an argument about whether dying young is bad for the person who dies. He said it shows there’s no time at which the dead person is worse off, so the question is ill posed. I’m pretty sure that one sentence is smuggling in several distinct claims at once. Can you pull apart the precise theses it might be expressing — e.g. about when a harm has to occur, whether being harmed requires experiencing it, who the subject of the harm would be, maybe the symmetry with prenatal nonexistence — and lay them out as a menu with verdicts: which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false. And tell me which reading(s) actually do the work in his argument, and which ones a deprivationist about death’s badness could accept without giving up their view.
