I have been trying to pin down the concept of a Ulysses contract, and I keep finding that the usual formulation — voluntarily binding one's own future self because one does not trust that self — separates into several ideas when it is pressed. The paradigm case is Ulysses, having been warned about the Sirens, ordering his crew to tie him to the mast and ignore his later protests, so that when the song reaches him he will be unable to steer the ship toward them even though he will desperately want to. I would like a working definition of a Ulysses contract that correctly classifies the following cases:

(a) Ulysses and the mast, as described.
(b) A person who opens a savings account with a substantial early-withdrawal penalty, specifically so that their later self will not raid the account.
(c) A person who sincerely resolves never to gamble again, but puts no external constraint of any kind in place.
(d) A person who bets a friend one hundred dollars that they will not drink for a month, and authorizes the friend to keep the money if they do.
(e) A person in the early stages of dementia who signs an advance directive refusing life-extending treatment for a later state in which they will be content, will ask to live, and will have no memory of the directive.
(f) A constitution, ratified by now-dead generations, that requires a supermajority to amend, so that a clear current majority cannot revise it even when it wants to.
(g) A person who signs over their house because someone is holding a gun to their head.

My suspicion is that the ordinary idea of a Ulysses contract does two jobs at once: it marks a voluntary, self-imposed external constraint on one's own later conduct, and it presupposes enough continuity between the binding self and the bound self that the later self's rebellion still counts as the same person being held to their own earlier choice. Please propose a definition, run it through (a) through (g), and indicate where the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. I am especially interested in whether (e) and (f) are genuine Ulysses contracts or only loose metaphors; I do not have a settled view on either, and I would like to see what in the definition forces the answer one way or the other.
