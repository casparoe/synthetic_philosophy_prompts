I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and I've somehow ended up giving a short talk for our department's film-and-philosophy series next month. We're screening M. Night Shyamalan's Signs, and I want to use it to get at the later Wittgenstein on religious belief — not as a cute illustration but as something the film genuinely has something to say about, or maybe gets wrong in an instructive way.

The thing I keep circling is this. Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief (the lectures on the topic, plus the scattered later remarks) keep insisting that religious belief is not a hypothesis about how the world is, the kind of thing evidence could confirm or disconfirm. Believing in God, or in providence, is more like living inside a picture — a framework that determines what counts as a sign, a coincidence, a test, a punishment — rather than a claim you check against the data. The believer and the skeptic can look at the same events and not really be disagreeing about the facts; they're taking the facts differently. Signs seems almost built for this: Graham Hess is an ex-Episcopal priest who lost his faith after his wife died in a car accident, and over the course of an alien invasion the film piles up details — his son's asthma, his daughter's habit of leaving glasses of water around the house, his brother's old baseball career, his wife's dying words — and at the end Graham re-reads all of it as providential and puts his collar back on. My worry is that the film can't decide whether its doing the Wittgensteinian thing or the exact thing Wittgenstein warns against: treating the events as evidence for a religious hypothesis, as if the question whether there's providence gets settled by how neatly the signs fit together. And I keep going back and forth about whether the film knows the difference between those two readings.

What I'd like from you is a reading of Signs through the later Wittgenstein on religious belief that takes both seriously and is honest about where the film resists the reading. In particular: does the film dramatize a change in framework — a different way of seeing the same events — or does it stage the confirmation of a hypothesis, where the events are data that make providence more probable? Where do the two readings come apart in specific moments — the early scene where Graham divides people into those who see lucky events as signs and those who see them as mere luck, the night in the basement, the final fight where the wife's words turn out to be literally useful? And what does the film add to the philosophical point, if anything, that the bare Wittgensteinian remarks don't? Keep it under 1,200 words — I have to fit it into a lunchtime talk.
