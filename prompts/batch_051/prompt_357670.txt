Last week I had a long argument with a friend who defends a broadly Thomist account of the Eucharist, and the discussion left me stuck on a puzzle about whether transubstantiation can even be stated coherently. By transubstantiation she means this: at the consecration, the entire substance of the bread is converted into the substance of Christ's body, while the accidents of bread — its look, taste, smell, texture — remain in existence, though no longer inhering in any substance of bread. After the consecration, she is therefore willing to say that the consecrated host is Christ's body. I tried to press my doubts into a formal objection, and I keep thinking the argument is sound, but she insists I am misapplying Leibniz's law. Here is the argument as I reconstructed it afterward:

(1) According to transubstantiation, after the consecration the substance present under the appearances of bread is the substance of Christ's body. (2) So the consecrated host — the thing one receives — is Christ's body. (3) The consecrated host has the property of looking, tasting, and smelling like bread. (4) Christ's body does not have the property of looking, tasting, and smelling like bread; in its natural mode it has the sensible qualities of a human body. (5) If x and y are identical, then any property of x is a property of y (Leibniz's law). (6) If the host is identical with Christ's body, then by (5) Christ's body must have the property of looking, tasting, and smelling like bread. (7) By (4) and (6), the host is not identical with Christ's body. (8) But (2) says it is. So transubstantiation lands in an explicit contradiction.

My question is whether the argument goes through. I suspect the only escape is to deny Leibniz's law for the Eucharist, which strikes me as ad hoc. My friend says the mistake is in step (6) and has something to do with the accidents not being properties of the substance after the consecration. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, which step exactly fails, and on what reading of 'the host' and 'has the property'? If it is valid, say which premise a defender of transubstantiation is forced to reject, and whether that rejection can be made principled rather than ad hoc.
