Please produce a decision tree, for a handout in an undergraduate philosophy of mathematics course, that sorts positions on what numbers are by whether they give the question "Is 2 an element of 4?" a determinate answer. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: work out the diagnostic questions first, then locate the actual positions within that space, then note which combinations are empty because they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The branch nodes should be questions a student can answer before knowing any labels, and they should turn on the real fault lines: whether arithmetic needs objects at all; whether Benacerraf's two set-theoretic reductions (Zermelo's and von Neumann's) show that the "real" reduction is indeterminate or only that set-theoretic reductionism was the wrong project; whether what arithmetic cares about is shared structure rather than the particular relata; and whether a structure is a free-standing abstract object, a pattern only in systems that instantiate it, or merely a way of speaking about possible systems. Make the leaves include at least set-theoretic reductionism, ante rem structuralism, in re structuralism, eliminative structuralism, and fictionalism, and flag any two leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: walk the reader through each branch and leaf in sentences. If you think the tree distorts the debate, or that "2 ∈ 4" is the wrong diagnostic question to build around, say so plainly and say what you would use instead rather than forcing a taxonomy around it.
