I cover science and ideas for a living, and right now I'm poking at the whole responsibility gap debate about autonomous systems—the worry that when a learning system does harm, neither the designers nor the operators nor the machine itself is really responsible. Most of the examples people reach for are either fatal crashes or science fiction, and I want something more surgical. Can you build me a thought experiment to spec?

Here's what I need. The harm should be caused by a genuinely learning-enabled system, like a delivery robot whose navigation policy keeps updating after deployment, but the harm itself should be mundane—a broken leg, a ruined livelyhood, that sort of thing—and the tech should be something a city could plausibly deploy next year. No AGI, no perfect predictors, no demon scientists. The case has to work like this: under a control-based account of responsibility (you're on the hook only if the relevant behavior was under your control in the right way), no human is responsible for the particular harm—the designers set the objective and the training environment but didn't control the specific navigation choice, and the operator or owner acted reasonably throughout. But under a forward-looking answerability or tracing account (whoever deploys a risk-imposing system can be called to answer for what it does, and responsibility traces to choices that made the harm foreseeable as a class), the deploying company clearly is responsible. Same case, two different verdicts.

Then show your work. Walk through the reasoning each account gives, and be straight with me about which feature of the scenario is doing the real work—is it the post-deployment learning, the opacity, the lack of a human in the loop, or what? If you remove that one feature while holding everything else fixed, does the disagreement collapse into a standard negligent-design case where both accounts agree? And how would a defender of the no-gap position attack the case—by saying the control condition is too demanding for humans too, by relocating control to the training objective, or something else? I want to find out whether the responsibility gap is a real structural possibility or just an artifact of how tightly we draw the control condition.
