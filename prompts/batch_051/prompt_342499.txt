I've been thinking about the Pasadena game and I'm genuinely stuck. The setup is: flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first heads is on toss n, you win (−1)^(n−1) · (2^n)/n dollars—so win $2 on the first toss, lose $2 on the second, win $8/3 on the third, lose $4 on the fourth, and so on. The probabilities are 1/2^n, so the expected value comes out to 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + … = ln 2, about 69 cents. Fine. But that series is only conditionally convergent. If you reorder the same terms—without changing the game, just listing outcomes differently—you can make the sum come out to any real number, or diverge. So expected value is apparently undefined for this game. But the game itself doesn't give a damn about the order of rows in a spreadsheet. I feel like there has to be a fact of the matter about whether this gamble is worth, say, $0.50, even if the theory can't see it.

I'm pulled both ways. On one side, expected-value maximization is a beautiful theory. It's simple, unifying, and handles every ordinary finite gamble without breaking a sweat. Maybe the right lesson is just that infinite games with alternating signs are pathological, and we shouldn't demand a precise answer from a decision rule in such edge cases. On the other side, if rational choice theory tells me that this very same gamble has no determinate value just because the payoff series is conditionally convergent, that seems to mistake an artifact of representation for a property of the gamble. I could literally imagine a casino offering this game; someone would buy tickets, someone would sell them, and the universe wouldn't shrug.

Can you act as a decision theorist and give me the strongest version of both sides? Specifically: what would justify saying the intuition is a finitary illusion and we should accept that some gambles have no well-defined expected value? What would justify amending expected-value theory so it gives a determinate answer here—say by requiring absolute convergence, or by picking a privileged summation order, or by weakening the decision rule? And is there a compatibilist reading on which the theory never promised to handle conditionally convergent cases anyway, while the intuition survives as a norm about well-behaved gambles? Don't just pick a side; tell me what it would take for each move to be the right one. Start by laying out the options carefully, then give your negotiated verdict.
