I am a retired physicist who spent most of my career using statistical models in experimental work, and I have recently begun reading the philosophy of science in earnest. A local seminar has asked me to lead a ninety-minute session on the interpretations of probability—frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity—for an audience of working scientists and philosophers, most of whom are competent with probability but have never compared these interpretations. I want a plan for the session, not the text of a lecture.

Please lay out: the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why, with attention to what depends on what; the single concrete example that should carry the session through all three interpretations, and what that example must have and must lack if it is to do the work; the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form (for instance, that subjectivism means probability is arbitrary, that the interpretations are rival calculi, or that propensity is just long-run frequency under another name) and the point in the sequence where each should be defused; and a check of understanding that would show whether participants have grasped the distinctions rather than merely learned the labels. Conclude by saying what you would deliberately leave out for this audience and how you would flag the omission.

Lead with your bottom line—the single most important structural decision about how the session should be built—in your first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
