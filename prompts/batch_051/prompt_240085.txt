I am an undergraduate student currently taking a philosophy of science course, and I also work as a manuscripts editor for our faculty's undergraduate research journal. We receive around a dozen submissions per cycle, three cycles per term. Each editor has about forty-five minutes per manuscript and must return a recommendation of accept, revise and resubmit, or reject on methodological grounds; a faculty advisor must sign off on every rejection, but the advisor is usually not available to coach us before we have to decide. The editors keep disagreeing about when a methodological departure is fatal. Some treat any deviation from the standard template, such as a hypothesis specified in advance, a preregistered design, and conventional null-hypothesis testing, as grounds for rejection. Others defend ad hoc or exploratory papers because the authors found an interesting result. Others admit they have no workable rule at all.

In the course we have been reading Feyerabend's anything-goes position. On the reading we are working with, it is not the claim that every method is as good as any other or that methodological criticism is impossible. It is the claim that no single context-free set of rules, whether inductivist, falsificationist, or otherwise, has ever succeeded in describing or governing scientific inquiry, and that the methodological constraints that do improve inquiry are local, revisable, and earned within a discipline rather than imposed from a template. I would like to turn that reading into an editorial procedure rather than leave it as a slogan.

Please write a decision procedure I can run, within about forty-five minutes, on a submitted paper to determine whether a methodological departure should be treated as a reason to reject, a reason to require revision, or neither. The procedure should be usable by a non-philosopher editor, should not collapse into anything-goes in the sense of never rejecting on method, and should actually sort papers into different outcomes. It should include: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question; the point at which the procedure yields a rejection or stops treating methodology as the relevant fault; which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls; and which cases the procedure cannot settle and must hand to the faculty advisor.

Then apply the procedure to three worked examples: (a) a paper that collected the data first and wrote the hypothesis afterward, (b) a paper that uses a nonstandard statistical test justified mainly by its having produced a significant result, and (c) a paper that follows the standard template on a question for which that template is widely regarded as inappropriate. Include at least one worked example in which the procedure reaches a conclusion an editor would rather not accept, and say explicitly why the procedure wins. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do, for example detect fraud, establish whether an effect is real, or settle substantive disagreement within a discipline.

After the procedure and the worked examples, provide a brief JSON summary using exactly these fields, with these types:

- "procedure_title": a string naming the procedure.
- "steps": an array of objects, each with "number" (an integer in the order of application), "question_to_ask" (a string), "what_counts_as_a_sufficient_positive_answer" (a string), "action_if_not_satisfied" (a string), and "mechanical_or_judgment" (a string that is either the word "mechanical" or a short description of the judgment involved).
- "worked_cases": an array of objects, each with "name" (a string), "step_outcomes" (an array of strings recording the outcome of each step in order), "final_decision" (a string), and "where_judgment_entered" (a string naming the step or steps at which editorial judgment affected the result).
- "limitations": an array of strings stating what the procedure cannot do.
- "cases_to_send_to_faculty_advisor": an array of strings describing cases the procedure cannot settle.
- "least_welcome_outcome": a string naming the worked example in which the procedure reaches the conclusion an editor would not like.

Keep each string in the JSON to no more than two sentences, and do not add fields beyond these.
