just got home from a stupidly long argument with a friend and he left me with an argument i can't shake. he says Aumann's agreement theorem shows the whole 'reasonable people can disagree' thing is a confusion. his version, as best i can reconstruct it:

(1) if two ideal Bayesians start with the same prior, and it's common knowledge between them that one's posterior credence in some proposition is p and the other's is q, then p has to equal q. that's just the theorem, he says.
(2) any two honest, smart people who are really trying can get close to this: they can tell each other their credences openly and keep talking until everyone knows everyone's numbers and knows everyone knows, etc.
(3) so if they still disagree after that, the only options are that their starting priors differed, or at least one of them updated wrong somewhere along the way.
(4) updating wrong is just irrationality, full stop.
(5) and different priors can't make a disagreement reasonable either, because priors don't come from the evidence — they're what you bring to the evidence, so saying 'we have different priors' just relocates the unreason instead of explaining it away.
(6) therefore, in any persistent disagreement between two smart, sincere people, at least one of them is being irrational in a deep way, and 'we just see it differently' is never an honest stopping point.
(7) but that's absurd. two careful epidemiologists can read the same trial data, share their full models for a year, and still disagree about whether a drug works, and we don't think one of them must be bad at reasoning. a theory of rationality that forces that conclusion is a bad theory.

when i pushed back that real people aren't ideal Bayesians he rolled his eyes and said idealizations either approximate the real phenomenon or they're useless, and two honest people hashing things out is about as close to Aumann's setup as any idealization ever gets. i cant find the step to reject, but the conclusion feels wrong enough that something has to go. which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the argument?
