I am preparing to teach Henry James's The Turn of the Screw in a course on evidence and explanation, and I want to use the novella as an extended case in the underdetermination of theory by evidence rather than as a puzzle with a settled solution. The governess's manuscript presents the reader with a single body of evidence: her sightings of Peter Quint and Miss Jessel, the children's odd composure, Miles's unexplained expulsion from school, and Mrs. Grose's guarded corroborations. That evidence can be held together by at least two incompatible theories. On the supernaturalist reading, the ghosts are real and the children are in their thrall; on the naturalist reading, the governess is an unreliable narrator whose fears and repressions generate the apparitions. Each theory protects itself by adding different auxiliary assumptions about who actually sees what, how much children can conceal, and which of the governess's observations are trustworthy.

Act as a philosopher of science who is also a careful close reader, and write a critical essay reading the novella through the lens of underdetermination. Think through, before you write, where the analogy between the governess's evidence and the scientific case for underdetermination is genuine and where it strains. In the essay, discuss what the novel dramatizes that the standard statement of the thesis leaves out: the difference between evidence that is merely compatible with several theories and evidence produced by an interested party; whether the story presents two empirically equivalent theories or one theory kept alive by ad hoc adjustments; which scenes (the tower, the window, the lake, the final confrontation) carry the most weight for either reading; and whether the fixedness of a literary text makes its underdetermination permanent in a way that scientific underdetermination often is not. End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that an abstract argument about equivalent theories cannot. Begin the response with the essay itself, with no preface or commentary.
