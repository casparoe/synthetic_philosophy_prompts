I am in the process of turning a set of views I have held for some time about the depiction of war in video games into a position I can defend in print, and I would like an outsider's consistency check before I commit to it. Each of the five views below strikes me as right on its own, but I cannot reliably tell whether they all hang together, and I would rather locate any fault line now than in front of an audience. The views are as follows.

(a) Playing a war game, and taking pleasure in simulated violence, is not in itself morally criticizable. No actual person is harmed by what happens to simulated characters, and the moral significance of a player's enjoyment is exhausted by what it does to how they treat real people; absent some effect of that kind, I am reluctant to blame a player for how a game makes them feel.

(b) Some war games are nonetheless morally objectionable, and not merely as bad taste. The wrong-making feature, when it is present, belongs to the work's design: a game is objectionable when it is built to glorify the killing, to make the player's enjoyment consist in relishing the violence, or to turn a real massacre into a spectacle one is meant to enjoy, rather than handling its subject matter with seriousness.

(c) The player's own motives and private pleasures matter to the moral assessment of their playing in a way that is independent of the game's framing. A player who privately savors the suffering of the simulated victims while playing a serious and condemnatory war game is doing something worse than someone who plays a morally sloppy game out of historical curiosity, even if the two play sessions are externally indistinguishable.

(d) Production and consumption can come apart. A studio can do something wrong by making and marketing a game that turns a real atrocity into light entertainment, even if an ordinary unreflective purchaser is only mildly criticizable at most; and a player can be blameworthy for the spirit in which they play a game whose creation was perfectly permissible.

(e) I am wary of treating war games as a special moral kind. Any principle broad enough to condemn some particular war game would, if followed in good faith, also condemn canonical serious works of art about war — films, novels, memorials — which I regard as beyond reproach. I therefore think the differences that matter are differences in the seriousness and detail of a work's framing, not in interactivity, the medium, or the fact that a thing is played for fun.

Please audit this set rather than survey the wider debate. I want to know which of these five views genuinely pull against one another, ordered from the most to the least severe tension, and for each what would have to be revised and what the cheapest revision would cost. I would also like you to say where an apparent conflict dissolves once the views are stated carefully, so that I do not end up revising commitments that were never in tension. Please give me your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables.
