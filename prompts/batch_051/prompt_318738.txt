You are an expert on skill acquisition, the psychology of expertise, and the practical training methods developed in the rationality community, and you are comfortable thinking about where an analogy earns its keep and where it breaks. I direct a twelve-month fellowship for analysts at a policy research institute. The analysts are bright, graduate-trained, and make dozens of consequential epistemic judgments each week — which sources to trust, how much to revise a prior when new evidence arrives, whether a model's output actually confirms a conclusion, when to kill a project they have fallen in love with. We get three hours a week with them. Two cohorts of bias-list seminars have produced no visible change in their desk work. I want to replace that with a reusable training procedure that takes seriously the claim that rationality can be trained like a martial art: with stance and footwork, katas, live sparring, graded promotion, and an honest account of what transfers to the street.

Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate mappings between martial training and rationality, choose the most defensible one, and state it explicitly, including the points where the analogy breaks and should not be pushed. Then write the procedure manual. It must specify four to six katas — repeatable, component-level drills — with, for each: the skill it isolates, the step-by-step sequence a trainee executes, the canonical failure it corrects, and the visible standard for proficiency. Include at least one kata whose purpose is learning to be thrown safely: to lose an argument, discover one's own error, or update cleanly without injury to status or identity. Then give the progression order and its dependencies (what must become automatic before what, which katas can run in parallel, and where the first live sparring belongs — and why not earlier). Design two or three sparring formats with rules that force the skill to appear under resistance and keep sparring from decaying into status combat or debate-team point-scoring. Set observable grading criteria for three levels, including at least one test of unprompted selection — a novel case in which the trainee must notice that a kata applies without being told — and one far-transfer test on a messy desk problem. Apply the whole procedure to three worked examples: one trainee who performs the katas beautifully but does not transfer, one charismatic trainee whom the instructors want to promote but the procedure does not, and one case where the procedure must explicitly hand the decision back to human judgment. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do, where it is likely to misfire, and which distinctions from the underlying theory must be preserved now that non-theorist instructors will be running it. Do not include a reading list; the manual must be implementable from the document alone.
