After forty years as a field ecologist, I recently retired and now sit on the board of a local land trust that holds conservation easements on several parcels of mixed forest and wetland. One of our older easements, drafted in the mid-1980s, contains the following clause:

> The Property shall be maintained in its natural, scenic, and open space condition. No commercial activity, structures, roads, or motorized vehicles shall be permitted on the Property, except that the Grantor reserves the right to maintain existing footpaths and to undertake such measures as are necessary to restore or protect the native ecological communities.

The parcel in question has lost most of its overstory American chestnut to blight, and a university restoration group now proposes to plant blight-resistant chestnut seedlings produced by introducing a single wheat gene into the American chestnut genome. The trees would be otherwise indistinguishable from native chestnuts, would cross with any surviving wild chestnuts, and their advocates argue that this is the only realistic way to restore the native canopy within the next century. Some of our board members think the clause clearly welcomes the project as measures necessary to restore or protect the native ecological communities; others think it clearly forbids it because the trees are not natural and the clause’s first sentence commits us to non-interference. I have been asked to prepare a short interpretive analysis.

Interpret the clause using the three approaches—the plain words, the original purpose of the easement, and what the drafters would have said if asked in 1985—and say which approach should govern. Also address whether the phrase “native ecological communities” should refer to the species present before European settlement, to the current biological communities, or to some historically informed baseline, and how that bears on the genetic modification question. Finally, say how confident you are in your conclusion and identify the point you are least sure of.
