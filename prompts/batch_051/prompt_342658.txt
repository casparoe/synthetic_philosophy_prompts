I was up way too late arguing with a friend about low-impact AI, and now I’m a bit fried. His position was basically that the whole approach is circular. We were working from Stuart Armstrong’s paper “Low Impact Artificial Intelligences,” where he says any action’s effects “percolate through the future light-cone,” changing things in ways that never fully disappear. My friend’s point was: once you accept that, “low impact” can’t mean “close to no impact”—it has to mean “low change on some selected variables,” and selecting those variables is already doing the value-specification work the approach was supposed to avoid. I didn’t have a good answer.

I’m not looking for a survey, but say I had one competent PhD student and three years, and wanted to either save low-impact as a research program or decisively show it’s inferior to just biting the bullet on value specification. Which problem should they work on? The ones I keep coming back to are: (1) whether a broad distribution over possible tasks, à la attainable utility, avoids the circularity or just pushes it into choosing the task distribution; (2) whether any principle can pick out the right baseline of inaction or stepwise inaction without presupposing human preferences; (3) whether the real target should be something like relative reachability or preserving what downstream agents would care about, rather than side-effects per se; and (4) whether “low impact” and “avoiding side effects under a misspecified reward” are actually different problems that have gotten tangled together. Rank these or tell me what I’ve missed, and defend the ordering by which is most upstream, most tractable, and which would change little downstream if solved. If the whole framing I’ve just given is confused—maybe the circularity objection isn’t the actual bottleneck, or I’m hoping for a technical fix to what is really a normative problem—call that out instead of politely ranking.
