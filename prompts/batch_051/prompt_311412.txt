Lately I have been following an epistemic rule about calibration. When I want to know whether one of my sources is trustworthy — my eyes, my memory, a new instrument — I let the source help build its own case: I count the occasions when its deliverances were matched by another source, even though each checking source is itself uncertified. I recently did this with a light meter, comparing its readings against my unaided estimates and taking the convergence as evidence that both are at least roughly reliable. A philosopher friend objects that I am bootstrapping: the evidence for the meter depends on my already trusting my eyes, and the evidence for my eyes depends on a source in the same position, so the whole network of mutual support rests on no source that has been independently certified. I answer that his demand would leave me with no way to start, and that this modest circularity is exactly how real investigators calibrate their instruments. Is my policy defensible? Where exactly does it go wrong, if it does, and what would a defensible version of it look like?
