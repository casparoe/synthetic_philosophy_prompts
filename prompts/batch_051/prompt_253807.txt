An argument for the claim that Spinoza's system is internally inconsistent, which I would like you to test step by step.

(1) Spinoza's necessitarianism holds that every finite thing and event, including every idea and affect in every finite mind, follows from God's nature with the same necessity that characterizes the propositions of geometry.
(2) The intellectual love of God is, on Spinoza's own account, a state of a finite mind: a joy joined to the highest kind of knowledge, with God as its cause and object.
(3) Therefore, on Spinozist assumptions, whenever a mind attains the intellectual love of God, it attains it necessarily; in the actual order of causes, that mind could not have failed to attain it.
(4) But a state that one could not have failed to attain cannot be a spiritual achievement, a virtue, or an object of striving; it cannot be what makes one life more blessed than another.
(5) Spinoza nevertheless presents the intellectual love of God as the highest blessedness, as virtue itself, and as the goal toward which the life of reason is directed.
(6) Thus the same state is treated as both unavoidable and as a normative ideal; Spinoza's position is therefore internally inconsistent.

Is this argument valid as it stands? If not, identify the exact inference that fails and explain under what reading it fails. If it is valid but unsound, name the premise that should be rejected and give the strongest Spinozist reason for rejecting it. Where, if anywhere, does the argument smuggle in an assumption about achievement, freedom, or striving that Spinoza would deny?

End your answer with a single line of the form \answer{...}, where the content is one of: valid; invalid; unsound at step N, with N replaced by the number of the faulty premise.
