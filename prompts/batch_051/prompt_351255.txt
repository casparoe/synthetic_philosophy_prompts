I teach a small undergrad seminar on minds and evolution — 16 students, mix of philosophy and psych majors, one 80-minute block a week. We've been reading Dennett on his four kinds of creatures: Darwinian ones that only change through blind variation and selection, Skinnerian ones that can also be shaped by reinforcement, Popperian ones that can let their hypotheses die in their stead by testing them internally, and Gregorian ones that use external mind-tools like words, tools, and other people. My students can recite the definitions, but they clearly don't feel why the ladder matters — that each rung isn't just "more intelligence" but moves the trial-and-error loop into a diffrent medium. I want them to come away seeing that difference in their own choices, not hearing it from me.

I'd like an in-class game, runnable in 80 minutes with 16 people and no extra helpers, where four small groups work on the same simple problem but each is constrained to behave like one of the four creature types. They should hit the constraints before anyone says "Popperian" out loud — so the Darwinian group should only change by which members "survive," the Skinnerian one should only adjust through reinforcement, the Popperian one should have some way of testing moves internally before acting, and the Gregorian one should be allowed words/notes/tools that the others can't use. Payoffs should be in points, not real money. Please give rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened in the game onto the generate-and-test architecture rather than just re-explaining the taxonomy. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the game can't reproduce — I'm pretty sure the Darwinian group has to be a compressed, fake simulation of selection, so tell me how to frame that without making it feel like cheating.
