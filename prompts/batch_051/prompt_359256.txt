I just got done with a long argument with a friend — he's the type who thinks the rule of law has one job, which is letting people know in advance where they stand so they can plan their lives. I mostly agreed with him until I started poking at an asymmetry neither of us could clean up. A law that gets passed in secret and then used to punish people strikes basically everyone as the rule of law's worst nightmare; if a state said "we'll tell you the rules after you break them," we'd call it tyranny without hesitating. But a law that's public and uses words like "reasonable," "unconscionable," or "best interests of the child" also doesn't let you know in advance whether your conduct is allowed — you find out when a judge applies the standard to you after the fact. Almost nobody treats vague statutes as the same kind of scandal, though; we think they're unavoidable, maybe even good.

If predictability is the whole point, that looks inconsistent — a secret law and a vague law are both ways of making the law unknowable in advance, one by hiding the rule and one by hiding its application. I want the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating them differently (is it about being able to argue back? official discretion? notice that there's a rule at all? something else?), and an honest verdict on weather it actually works — or whether we should either relax about secret laws or get a lot angrier about vague ones. When you answer, lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
