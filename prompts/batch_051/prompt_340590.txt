I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language working on the semantics of conditionals, and I've run into a tension I can't resolve on my own. I find Adams's thesis highly plausible: the degree to which one is warranted in accepting an indicative conditional like "If A, then C" tracks the conditional probability P(C|A), not the probability of the material conditional. But I also know that Lewis's triviality results are standardly taken to show that no proposition can have its probability equal to P(C|A) across all probability functions. I don't see how both can be right. If Adams's thesis is correct, then the probability of the indicative conditional just is P(C|A), and a probability must be a probability of something—so there should be a proposition whose probability we're tracking, yet the triviality results seem to rule out exactly that. My suspicion is that I've smuggled in an assumption somewhere, but I can't locate it precisely. Could you walk through the point at which this line of thought goes wrong, and say what remains of "the probability of a conditional" if there is no proposition there to bear it?
