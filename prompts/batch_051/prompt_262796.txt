I run a small behavioral-insights unit in a mid-sized city government. Two or three times a month we review or help design interventions that affect tens of thousands of residents: automatic enrollment in a retirement savings plan for municipal employees, reordered displays in public cafeterias, pre-checked boxes on forms, text-message reminders, automatic appointment scheduling, graphic warnings on sugary drinks, and similar measures. Every proposal eventually draws the same objection from someone—a council member asks whether we are 'steering' or 'shoving,' a department head says an opt-out is only paternalism with better public relations. At present my team makes these calls ad hoc, and I am not confident we would make the same call twice.

Turn the concepts around nudging and paternalism into a reusable decision procedure that an analyst without formal philosophy or economics training can run, with written output, in under an hour before a proposal goes to the city attorney for legal sign-off and, when required, a council subcommittee. The procedure should first sort the intervention descriptively: does a genuine choice remain; how costly or effortful is opting out; is the steering transparent to the person affected; does the intervention alter the available options or only their presentation; is it correcting a known error or exploiting one; and whose ends does it serve? On that basis it should classify the proposal as, roughly, a nudge, friction or sludge, manipulation, or coercion. It should then move to the normative question, distinguishing soft paternalism, which steers people toward what they themselves on reflection want, from hard paternalism, which overrides their ends for their own good, and it should say what kind of justification each requires and from whom. The checklist must mark, rather than paper over, the points where an evaluator has to exercise judgment—for example, in deciding what counts as someone's considered preference or how much friction crosses into meaningfully impeding a choice.

Work the procedure through three cases: (1) an automatic-enrollment savings default that an employee can leave with one click and no waiting period; (2) graphic health warnings on sugary drinks aimed at consumers who already know the risks and would prefer not to encounter the images; and (3) a city website on which the fee-waiver route is kept behind several extra pages while the standard payment route is pre-selected. At least one of these cases should produce a decision the unit would resist—rejecting or substantially redesigning something we would otherwise like—and you should identify that case and defend the outcome. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle for us, and which remaining questions belong to elected officials rather than to the unit.
