For an essay I'm writing on why public-reason liberals and their religious-inclusion critics so often talk past each other, draft the joint statement that a committed Rawlsian public-reason liberal and a committed inclusivist critic of that position could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. For the purposes of the prompt, the Rawlsian holds that exercises of political power are legitimate only when the decisive justifications offered in official public forums rest on reasons that all reasonable citizens could in principle accept independently of any comprehensive religious or philosophical doctrine; religious considerations may be voiced in public debate, but only under a standing proviso that non-sectarian, shareable reasons are also supplied. The critic holds that this requirement is a tilted burden: a citizen may rest a public political argument wholly on religious grounds, with no obligation to translate those grounds into neutral terms, because the demand for translation implicitly ranks secular modes of justification above religious ones and forces religious citizens to reason from premises that are not their own.

Write the deliverable as a single document with the following structure.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than merely verbal: for example, claims about what political coercion demands by way of justification, about the permanence of doctrinal pluralism in free societies, about the impropriety of the state adjudicating theological truth, and about what each position is not committed to.

Second, a disagreements table with one row per sub-question, and columns for: the precise question; the Rawlsian's position, worded as the Rawlsian would endorse; and the critic's position, worded as the critic would endorse. Phrase each side's entry so that the other side would recognize it as fair rather than caricatured.

Third, a section on cruxes. For each crux — a sub-question whose settlement would move at least one side — state the case, argument, or empirical test that both sides would regard as genuinely bearing on it, and record each side's advance prediction of the outcome.

Address these questions explicitly somewhere in the document:

- Can a non-arbitrary criterion of a shareable or public reason be specified, or does every candidate collapse either into reasons acceptable to whatever majority happens to hold power or into a criterion so permissive that it excludes nothing?
- Does the translation proviso burden religious and secular comprehensive doctrines asymmetrically — for instance, Kantian or utilitarian citizens — or does it burden all comprehensive views equally?
- What does each side say about a legislator who offers a scriptural injunction as their sole stated ground for a vote; about the justification of religious accommodation claims such as a Sikh officer's request to wear a turban or a Muslim employee's request to wear a head covering in a uniformed setting; and about a U.S. civil-rights campaign whose public argument is cast in prophetic religious idiom?
- To what extent is the disagreement verbal? Identify any places where the two sides must mean the same thing by terms such as public justification, neutrality, or coercion before a residual disagreement can be located.
