You are a political philosopher specialising in contractualist moral theory and in the design of small-scale democratic institutions. Your task is to analyse a proposed mechanism, not to defend or attack it as a moral ideal. Your response will be assessed on how precisely you trace the incentives the mechanism creates and on whether your account of its failure modes is internally consistent.

Context, summarised so that you need not rely on memory. One influential contractualist idea, associated with T. M. Scanlon, holds that an arrangement is justified only if it is permitted by principles that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for general regulation, with reasons assessed from each person's own standpoint. The framework is deliberately individualist: objections are pressed by particular people on the strength of what the arrangement does to them. Critics argue that the framework resists summing many small benefits or burdens into a case stronger than any individual's, and that this leaves it unable either to let one person's serious burden be outweighed by a large number of trivial benefits to others, or to handle diffuse, low-level costs spread across thousands of people. That difficulty is known as the aggregation problem.

Now consider the following mechanism, the Reasonable-Rejection Board scheme, proposed for a city of roughly 300,000 residents.

Every new by-law, planning consent, schools reorganisation, or traffic scheme, and every public project with an estimated cost above one million pounds, must pass a Reasonable-Rejection Board before it may proceed. Any resident may lodge an objection within thirty days of the proposal's publication. An objection is admissible only if it identifies a plausible harm to the objector's health, safety, livelihood, mobility, privacy, use and enjoyment of property, or civic standing; an objection based on bare preference or taste is dismissed at screening. For each admissible objection, a panel of five randomly selected residents, with conflicts of interest screened out, is empanelled, with a trained facilitator and no legal representation for either side. The council sets out its reasons for the proposal, the objector sets out their complaint, and the panel may question both.

The panel must then answer one question about each objection, taken one at a time: Would it be unreasonable to expect this particular objector to accept the proposal, taking their actual burden seriously, in light of the reasons the council offers? If the answer is yes for at least one objection, the proposal is blocked in its submitted form. In answering, the panel may hear the council's general reasons, but it may not treat the number of beneficiaries or objectors as itself a reason, and it may not discount the objector's burden merely because it is small relative to the summed benefits. The panel cannot amend the proposal or attach conditions; it can only clear or block. A blocked proposal may be revised and resubmitted, restarting the process from the beginning. The panel must publish its reasons, and every objection receives a full hearing: there is no rule of precedent, so similar objections may be raised again against successive proposals.

Treat this as a mechanism, and analyse how it would actually play out. In particular:

1. What does the scheme incentivise among residents (ordinary objectors, unusually sensitive or intransigent individuals, organised interests, diffusely benefiting residents), among councillors and council officers, and among panellists themselves?

2. How would it be gamed? Consider objection-shopping, coached or manufactured objections, the strategic framing of complaints, the timing and repetition of objections, panel fatigue, and any other vectors you can identify.

3. Where does it fail, and how does the aggregation problem reappear inside the mechanism? Think hard about cases in which thousands of people each lose a little while no single person loses a lot, and the converse case of one severely burdened person facing a widely supported project.

4. Which failure modes, if any, could be fixed without gutting the scheme's individualist core? For each fix, say whether it preserves, weakens, or abandons the original contractualist idea, and why.
