I'm part of a philosophy reading group that has spent the better part of a term stuck on the standing-to-blame debate, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on who is right. The two positions are entrenched. One side treats blame as, at bottom, an evaluation of another person's conduct—a claim to the effect that the target acted wrongly and is blameworthy for it. On this view the correctness of the evaluation depends only on facts about the target and the act: whether it was wrong, whether the target was responsible, whether there were excuses. The blamer's own record is therefore irrelevant to whether the blame is apt; a hypocrite may be morally compromised in a way that deserves separate criticism, but the blame they direct at someone who really did wrong is still true, apt, and warranted. The other side treats blame as a social act with agent-relative conditions, closer to forgiving, inviting, or holding someone to account than to asserting a proposition. On this view the hypocrite has lost their standing: they can utter a true evaluation, but they cannot perform the act of blame, roughly as someone can state true facts about an offense yet lack the authority to forgive it. Both camps accept the same surface facts—the target did wrong, and the blamer is a hypocrite—so the disagreement is not an empirical one. The point where it visibly freezes is the familiar reply "you're not the one to lecture me." One side glosses that as an objection to the blamer's character, a perfectly fair point that nevertheless leaves the blame as blame intact. The other glosses it as identifying exactly the way the blame fails to be blame.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is partly a dispute about how much the word "blame" is supposed to cover: one side is answering a question about the content of the evaluation, the other about the conditions for performing an interpersonal act, and each side treats the other's central notion as either optional etiquette or a category mistake. But there may also be a substantive disagreement underneath, about whether the social act can come apart from the assessment, or about which is fundamental. I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, grounded in what each side says—is it verbal, substantive, a difference about the nature of speech acts, or something else? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a kind of case the two sides haven't considered, a change of question, or an honest argument that continued stalemate should be expected?
