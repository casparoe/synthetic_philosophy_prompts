I am trying to work out what I actually believe about cognitive penetration of perception, and I have been collecting my own verdicts on concrete cases before settling on a definition. I would like you to do the extraction for me: state the principle, if any, that best captures the pattern of my verdicts; tell me which verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon; and apply it to the unsettled case at the end.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts as I actually feel them.

1. The radiologist. A radiologist with two decades of chest films looks at an x-ray and immediately sees a small carcinoma as a raised, distinct nodule sitting on a rib. A novice looking at the same film sees only overlapping flat shadows and cannot find the abnormality even when told roughly where it is. The radiologist is not checking features and inferring that a tumour is present; the image simply looks different to her. My verdict: penetration. Her visual experience has been shaped by her medical knowledge.

2. The restored word. While listening to a noisy recording, a person hears a stretch of sound as an undifferentiated blur. The experimenter then says that the next word is "folder." On replay, the word pops out as clearly audible speech; the same acoustic strip is now heard as structured. My verdict: penetration. The expectation changed the auditory experience itself.

3. The bear stump. A hiker has just seen fresh bear sign. A gnarled root up the trail catches her eye, and for several seconds she sees a crouching bear; then, with no new information and no conscious inference, the figure visually collapses back into a root. My verdict: penetration. Fear and expectation changed what she saw.

4. The valued coins. Children are shown coins and asked to match the size of each coin by adjusting a circle of light. The children from poorer backgrounds set the circle consistently larger than the children from wealthier backgrounds do. My verdict: not penetration. I doubt the coins looked larger to them. This feels like a memory or evaluative report effect, not a change in the perceived size of the coin.

5. The home-invasion prime. In a speeded task, participants who have just read a vivid story about a home invasion are more likely, and faster, to classify a briefly flashed ambiguous object as a knife rather than as a screwdriver, compared with participants who read a neutral story. My verdict: not penetration. The prime changed how willing people were to say "knife," not the way the object looked.

The pair that most bothers me is case 2 and case 5: both involve an occurrent prior cue, yet I come out opposite ways, and I cannot yet say what makes the cue change the experience in one case but merely move a decision threshold in the other without building the distinction around these two very cases. Case 1 also sits awkwardly next to case 3: the radiologist's knowledge is standing and not occurrent at the moment of viewing, while the hiker's fear is occurrent, yet I want both to count.

My rough working rule has been something like: penetration occurs when a cognitive state changes the perceptual experience itself rather than merely changing attention, memory, or the threshold for making a report. But I suspect this is either circular or too blunt, and it may be too permissive for cases 4 and 5 while being too strict for case 1, depending on how it is sharpened.

Could you please do the following. First, formulate the best candidate principles that come close to my verdicts, say which of my verdicts each principle forces me to give up, and recommend one. Second, give me a workable version of the experience-versus-report distinction that separates case 2 from case 5 without simply stipulating my verdicts. Third, tell me what the recommended principle says about the case I have not settled: an acoustics engineer with years of diagnosing marine engines hears, on her first pass over a muffled recording, a telltale knocking that a novice cannot hear; she has no particular expectation about what is on the recording, but she simply hears the fault, the way one hears a familiar voice. Should I classify her case as cognitive penetration, and does that prediction cohere with my radiologist verdict?
