I'm giving the commencement address at a small cognitive science program where I've taught for most of my career. My settled-but-revisable position, which the students have heard me defend and most of the faculty dispute, is that conscious experience is not the engine of thought but its interface: a heavily compressed, lossy, often flattering dashboard that the brain presents to itself, the desktop rather than the circuitry, useful precisely because it hides most of what it represents. The strict version carries the caveat I always add, which is that there is no separate user on the other side of the screen; the interface is the closest thing to a self that exists. I do not regard this as a debunking — a dashboard is real, and a good one is worth a great deal. The audience will be our graduating students, who know the view well and will be bored by a simplified version of it; the cognitive science faculty, who will catch every slide into dualism or eliminativism; and the parents, who did not come to be told that their children have no selves. Write me a twelve-minute address. The idea has to do real structural work, not decorative work. In particular, it should forbid the standard commencement machinery — advice about finding the one true self that was there all along, or following a passion as if the passion pre-existed the practice — and it should say honestly what replaces that advice. It should not be a lecture, a funeral for the self, or a long in-joke for the students. And where the view is silent about what to do with a life — which is most of the territory of a commencement address — the speech should let the silence be the point rather than forcing the view to speak.

Write the address in the following sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths:
- Opening: about two minutes, establishing the occasion and why it is all right that a commencement speech begins from an account of what a self is not.
- The view in plain language: about two minutes, accessible to the parents and precise enough that the faculty do not object.
- What I will not say from this podium: about two minutes, laying out the self-help clichés the view rules out and why, without making the students feel robbed.
- What I can honestly say instead: about four minutes, the actual advice the view permits, aimed at the students and built around the distinction between the dashboard and the circuitry.
- Close: about two minutes, returning to the occasion and sending the graduates forward.
