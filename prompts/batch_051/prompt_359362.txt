I have been trying to work out whether the gene's-eye view of evolution is right, or whether it is only one description among several, and I have found that my gut reactions to particular cases are more consistent than I expected, though not fully. I would like help finding the principle behind them, and I would like to know where the principle forces a choice.

Here are my verdicts.

First, segregation distortion. In house mice there is a variant, the t-haplotype, that sabotages sperm not carrying it; a heterozygous male passes it on to almost all his offspring even though males carrying two copies are sterile or die. My verdict is that this is selection on the variant itself, and the fitness of the mouse is almost beside the point.

Second, transposable elements. A DNA sequence makes copies of itself that insert at new places in the genome, and it persists in the species even though each copy slightly lowers the host's fertility. My verdict is that this too is selection on a replicator, but here I begin to feel that the word gene is doing too much work, since the sequence is not a gene in anything like the sense of coding for a trait.

Third, greenbeards. Suppose an allele causes its bearer to grow a visible marker and also causes it to give costly help to any other individual bearing the marker, whether related or not. My verdict is that the allele is selected, and an organism-level or group-level story would be forced; the gene-level story is the right one.

Fourth, a male-killing symbiont. A maternally transmitted bacterium in an insect kills male embryos, which biases the host population toward daughters, through which the bacterium is transmitted, even though the skewed sex ratio can harm the host population. My verdict is that this is selected at the level of the transmitted entity rather than the host organism.

Fifth, cancer. A somatic mutation in a tissue makes one cell lineage multiply out of control, outcompeting its neighbors until it kills the organism. My verdict is that this looks like selection among cell lineages, but I do not count it as the same phenomenon, because nothing gets into the next generation.

Sixth, a cultural item. A tune spreads between people because it is catchy and easy to pass on, even though learning it costs time and does nothing for anyone's descendants. My verdict is that I resist calling this selection on a cultural replicator in the same sense, even though it superficially resembles the other cases.

My working hypothesis was simple: anything copied with variation and differential success is a unit of selection. But that hypothesis would force me to grant the tune in the sixth case the same status as the bacterium in the fourth, which I cannot bring myself to do. So there is a painful pair: I am confident about the male-killing symbiont and not at all confident about the tune.

Is there a principle, about replication, heredity, fidelity, generations, vehicles, or anything else, that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? What would it say about cases I have not listed, such as a sterile caste of helpers in a social insect, or a preference in a host that spreads because a parasite that causes it is transmitted more effectively through those who have it? And which of my six verdicts does the cleanest principle force me to give up?
