Last weekend I was walking with two friends along the edge of a marsh that is scheduled to be drained for a housing development. We could see herons in the shallows, and one of us said it was a shame. Dan took the strong view that wrecking the marsh is plainly bad for the animals in it: when the water goes, the fish, frogs, and nesting birds will mostly die or fail to breed, and any action that kills or prevents that much animal life has to count as a harm. Maya said she was not so sure. Most of the animals that would have lived in that marsh, she argued, would have lived short, hard lives ending in starvation, disease, freezing, or being eaten alive; if life in the wild is on average more painful than pleasant, then preventing those lives from starting is not obviously bad. Dan replied that she was smuggling in a strange kind of accounting, because it treats animals that never exist as though they had been rescued, while ignoring the actual animals in front of us that will suffer and die when the machinery arrives. Maya answered that the dying happens either way; the only question is whether another generation gets born into the same fate afterward. Dan then said that even if she were right about individual welfare, she had traded the value of the marsh—its species, its functioning, the wildness of it—for a ledger of pleasures and pains that animals themselves do not live by; a heron does not weigh its life that way. Maya said a heron cannot weigh anything at all; it feels, and if animal welfare is to mean anything, the animals' actual experiences are the only honest unit, not the species and not the scenery. Dan's last point was that her logic, applied everywhere, would tell us to pave the planet until nothing remains but whatever animals live comfortably near us, which he thought was an absurd conclusion. Maya replied that a conclusion can be uncomfortable without being false, and that a good deal of what people call conservation might describe how a landscape makes us feel rather than how it is for the animals themselves.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not sort out who was right at which step. Could you adjudicate their disagreement? Say who is right about which specific point and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Give exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; your fifth point should state which of the two friends is more right overall.
