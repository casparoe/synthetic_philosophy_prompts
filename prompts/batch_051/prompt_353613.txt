I serve on the nine-member Growth and Land-Use Commission of a mid-sized city of roughly half a million people. The City Council has asked us for a written recommendation on whether to approve the Riverside Annexation and Housing Plan, which would annex an unincorporated area of about ninety thousand people over the next eight years and rezone it for mixed-income housing. The planning department's projections are not seriously contested: the annexed residents—many of whom currently endure long commutes or substandard housing—would be substantially better off than they would be otherwise; the total well-being of everyone in the enlarged city would rise by roughly twelve percent; but the average well-being of residents already living inside the current city limits would decline by around two percent, mostly through longer service wait times, more crowded transit, and a smaller per-capita park maintenance budget.

The Commission has split into two camps. One camp argues that a city is not made better by adding people if every person already there is made slightly worse off, and that no actual current resident can point to any personal benefit from the plan. The other camp argues that refusing to count the new residents' gains is a bookkeeping artifact of where the city boundary happens to be drawn today, and that leaving ninety thousand people worse off would be an evasion dressed up as protection of incumbents. Draft the Commission's decision memo for the Council. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; it should state plainly which measure of a city's value the Commission is relying on and why; and it should give an honest, specific treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, in language the Council and the public can act on.
