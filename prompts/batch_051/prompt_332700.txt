I spent a career as a bench chemist, and now that I'm retired I've finally got time to read the philosophy I always meant to. Lately I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about lying to children, because my gut verdicts on cases look like they come from different theories, and I want to know whether there's one principle humming under all of them or whether I should just admit my instincts are inconsistent.

Here's where I land:

- Telling my kids Santa brought the presents: fine, even though I know he isn't real and they don't yet.
- Swapping the tooth for quarters and saying the tooth fairy came: fine, same category as Santa.
- Telling my daughter the tooth fairy pays extra for a tooth with no cavities, purely to get her to floss: I'd say fine, but with a small itch of doubt, because the point there wasn't wonder, it was getting her to do something.
- When our dog was put down, I told my seven-year-old the dog had gone to live on a farm. At the time it felt merciful; now I think it was probably wrong, even though she took it calmly.
- Telling a kid his drawing is really good when it's just average: fine. "Good" isn't the sort of thing that has a measured value, and it's encouragement.
- Telling a kid a shot won't hurt when you know it will: not fine. It buys you a calm thirty seconds and then the kid has a reason never to beleive you again.
- Telling a kid there are no monsters under the bed: fine, but that one's actually true, so it probably doesn't count.
- Telling a kid that grandma, who died, is watching over him from heaven: I go back and forth. I don't believe it. Half of me thinks it's kind; the other half thinks it spares me a hard conversation at his expense and sets up a worse fall later.
- Telling a kid his face will freeze that way if he keeps making it: fine, but it's obviously a joke in our house and he already rolls his eyes at it.

The obvious candidate is something like "it's okay when the child will eventually grow out of it and no substantive harm is done." But the dog case has both features and I've come to think it wasn't okay. Is the difference that the dog lie was about something real the child needed to grasp — a death, a loss — while Santa is about an invented layer on top of a world she already understands? That would explain the shot and the dog, but I'm not sure it doesn't collapse into "it's fine to lie when the stakes are low," except the stakes feel pretty high to the child either way.

So: give me the best one- or two-sentence principle you can that gets as many of these verdicts as possible, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Then apply it to the case I'm currently stuck on: a close friend tells her eight-year-old son that his father is away for work in Alaska and will be back, when in fact the father walked out on them and isn't returning. Is that covered by whatever exception makes Santa fine, or is it the dog case?
