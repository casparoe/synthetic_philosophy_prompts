I work as an ecology officer for a county council, and our authority has been asked to join a two-year pilot of a scheme I can only describe as welfare-weighted habitat banking. The idea is to replace the usual currency of biodiversity offsetting — hectares, habitat distinctiveness and condition — with an estimate of net sentient welfare. Under the pilot, any developer who destroys habitat must buy credits from a landowner who has created or restored habitat elsewhere, but the credits are not matched by ecological similarity. Instead, every parcel is put through a scoring model that multiplies the expected number of sentient animals it will support by an assumed typical lifespan and a welfare coefficient, and then applies correction factors for predation, disease and density-dependent starvation. A developer can offset any destroyed parcel by purchasing credits from any parcel whose welfare score is equal or higher.

The consultation document gives provisional coefficients that are already unsettling local landowners: a flower-rich meadow scores extremely high because of the enormous number of short-lived invertebrates assumed to have mildly positive lives; a rank, bramble-choked field scores above a clean lowland stream because the model treats the stream's juvenile mortality as a welfare debit; and a conifer plantation scores above ancient woodland because of assumptions about leaf-eating larvae per hectare. As well as the familiar difficulty of additionality — paying for habitat that would have been created anyway — there is a new one, namely that the unit of value is not a measurable habitat condition but a number generated by a model whose coefficients are themselves open to influence.

I have been asked to advise the committee on how the scheme would actually behave. Treat it as a mechanism to be analysed rather than as a moral position. Walk through the incentives it would create for developers, landowners, the ecological consultants who run the models, and our own planning officers. Where is it likely to be gamed first, and what sort of habitat would in fact get produced? What happens to the contestable assumptions about wild animal welfare once they are embedded in a tradable credit? And are the failure modes the kind that can be fixed by adjusting coefficients or audit rules, or are they inseparable from trying to make net welfare the unit of ecological exchange?
