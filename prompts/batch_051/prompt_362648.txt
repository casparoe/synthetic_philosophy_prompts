I host a small philosophy podcast out of our public library — I'm a retired geochemist and only fell into philosophy a few years ago, so I like guests who can go several rounds. Next month's guest is a political philosopher who has written a lot in defense of Frederick Douglass's post-1851 reading of the U.S. Constitution: that the Constitution is a liberty document rather than a proslavery compact, and — this is the part she insists on — that this follows from the text itself (the preamble's grounding of the government in the whole people rather than in the states or the slaveholding class, and the fact that the 1787 text never once uses the word slavery) rather than from the framers' private intentions or from noble myth-making. She sides with Douglass in his 1851 break with the Garrisonians, and she is careful to say that she is not claiming the document was always administered in an antislavery way, only that the correct reading of the document is antislavery.

Draft me about ten interview questions that will not let her just restate that position; I want her pushed, with follow-ups. I want at least one question on each of the following. First, the obvious problem clauses — the three-fifths clause, the clause that protected the importation of enslaved people until 1808, and the fugitive slave clause — where the follow-up should be keyed to the defense she is most likely to give, which I expect is something like that these clauses regulate an existing evil rather than affirm one. Second, a question about whether the view quietly collapses into a natural-law or moral reading, and what the written text itself is doing if the real normative work is done by a moral principle she brings to it. Third, a question about Dred Scott: if the Supreme Court read the same text as excluding Black Americans from the political community, what follows for original intent, judicial authority, and the suspicion that she and Douglass are just substituting a nobler reading for the one the institutions actually used. Fourth, a question about 1851 itself: why should Douglass's move away from Garrison count as evidence that a fair reading of the text converges on the antislavery conclusion, rather than as a tactical shift by someone joining the political abolitionists. Fifth, a question about the gap between a document's correct reading and its actual administration — the fugitive slave law, for one — and whether it can still be called a liberty document when its machinery was used to return human beings to bondage. And end with a question about what evidence would actually make her weaken or abandon the position. For each question, include the follow-up that presses the remaining weakness in the answer she is most likely to give, not just a generic clarification. Please start your response with the first question itself — no preamble, no setup, no list title.
