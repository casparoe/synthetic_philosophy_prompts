I've been trying to put two parts of the autonomous-systems debate into a single frame, and I could use help on the structure of the argument rather than on what policy follows. The standard responsibility-gap argument runs something like this. A person can be properly held responsible for a harmful outcome only if they could foresee and control the relevant action. With a sufficiently autonomous learning system, the designers cannot predict how the system will act in the circumstances it actually meets, the operator cannot intervene in every decision, and the system itself is not a candidate for blame or punishment in the ordinary sense. So for at least some harms, no one satisfies the conditions of responsibility; the gap is not a legal accident but a structural feature of the technology. Versions of this argument were worked out mostly with systems whose behavior was fixed by an identifiable design specification, an explicit reward function, or a hand-built control architecture.

Now take systems of the kind that have become common more recently: large language model agents, pretrained on enormous amounts of human text, given instructions and safety policies in natural language, and able to produce after-the-fact explanations of what they did and why. These systems remain unpredictable in detail, and their explanations may be dishonest or confabulated. But the operator has modes of control that the older argument did not really countenance—instruction, veto, interrogation, the ability to state a policy and require the system to check its proposed action against it—and the system has at least the surface form of a reason-giver. Does the responsibility-gap argument survive this development, or does it need repair, or is it simply inapplicable to such systems? Which premise is actually under pressure, and what is the strongest weakened version of the argument that still does the original work? I want to know whether the gap disappears, moves to a new location (for example, to the reliability of the system's explanations), or persists in a different shape.
