I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting a scene where a woman argues with her brother, who has fallen deep into neo-Stoic self-help and now talks as if emotions are mostly noise to be managed rather than lived with. She keeps a notebook of reasons why emotions belong in a good life, and so far she has four:

(1) emotions are themselves evaluations of the world — fear sees a threat, anger sees a wrong — so the live question is whether they fit, not whether to feel them at all;
(2) the relationships that make a life good are partly constituted by feelings like grief, pride, tenderness, and relief; subtract those and you don't have the same relationship with the noise removed, you have a thinner relationship;
(3) on the Stoics' own story the sage still feels rational affects such as joy, caution, and wishing, so even the strict school doesn't aim at flat affect;
(4) people who train themselves out of emotional engagement often fail to act on even their firmest practical judgements, so the 'rational' ideal is psychologically unrealistic.

I need one more for her notebook that a well-read Stoic couldn't brush off as merely romantic. It has to differ in structure from these four, not a restatement or combination of them. Say what the argument is, explain how it's meant to dodge the Stoic reply, and then tell me honestly whether it's any good.
