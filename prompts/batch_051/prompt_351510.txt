I am a philosophy professor and chair of a university research clinic that advises a state consumer-protection agency. Several times a year our team must recommend whether a proposed intervention — a cooling-off period, a compensation formula, a disclosure mandate, or a restriction on a sales practice — should treat an observed gap between what people demand to give up a good and what they would pay to acquire it (an endowment effect, often attributed to loss aversion) as a correctable bias or as a legitimate preference to be respected. Each file concerns an intervention that would affect tens of thousands of households and impose compliance costs on firms, and the agency needs our recommendation within two weeks. The analysts who do the first pass are public-policy master's students with no background in decision theory. Please write a reusable decision procedure they can run in under two hours per file: an ordered list of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure stops and classifies a case as a correctable bias, a legitimate preference, or underdetermined; and the point at which it hands the decision to me or to the agency because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run the procedure on two cases: (a) a laboratory experiment in which subjects who are given a mug demand considerably more to sell it than subjects without the mug are willing to pay to acquire one; and (b) a family that has farmed a parcel for four generations and, when the state needs it for flood control, demands several times its appraised market value, on the ground that the appraisal prices what the land would be worth to a stranger, not what it is worth to them. Include one application in which the procedure returns a classification you expect me to find uncomfortable, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot decide.
