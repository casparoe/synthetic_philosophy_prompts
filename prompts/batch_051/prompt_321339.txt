Consider two practices, one of which we classify as ritual and the other as accountability.

In the first, a community periodically gathers and ritually transfers its accumulated wrongs onto an animal — a goat, say — which is then driven out or killed. Participants describe the animal afterward as having carried the community's guilt away. If asked whether any guilt was literally transferred, most of us would say no: whatever the rite does for the feelings of those involved, the animal's death does not change the moral facts about who did what.

In the second, a large organization is found to have caused serious harm through the way it was structured — its incentive system, its internal culture, the routine decisions of many people acting in their roles. The institution responds by removing one or two designated figures, and the surrounding society largely treats the matter as resolved: accountability has been done. The removed figures are not, in general, the sole or even principal causal authors of the harm, and this is widely known. Yet their removal is not treated as merely symbolic; it is treated as genuinely discharging some portion of the organization's moral burden.

Here, then, is the asymmetry. In the ritual case we are confident that a substitute cannot take away the guilt of others. In the organizational case we seem to accept that a substitute can. What, if anything, justifies this difference in judgment? Is there a principle that makes ritual substitution inert while making organizational substitution effective, or is the organizational practice simply the ritual one in modern dress?

Give me your strongest case. State the asymmetry precisely; formulate the best principle you can find that would justify treating the two cases differently; test it against at least two nearby cases of your own choosing — for instance, a low-level employee punished for a systemic failure, a state required to pay reparations through its living citizens, a family offering one member as a substitute for another's sentence, or an army that executes a soldier in place of command responsibility. Say clearly which way you come down: keep the asymmetry, give it up by treating organizational removal as merely symbolic, or give it up in the other direction by treating the ritual transfer as real.

End with a last line of exactly this form: Verdict: [keep the asymmetry / treat both as symbolic / treat both as real / other short phrase].
