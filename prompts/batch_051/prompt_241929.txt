Last evening a friend and I argued for hours about moral worth, and specifically about whether an action can carry a distinctive kind of credit only when it is done for the right reasons, and whether anyone can ever tell, from the outside, whether that condition has been satisfied. The discussion kept returning to the familiar Kantian distinction between acting from duty and acting merely in accordance with duty: the shopkeeper who gives correct change because honesty is good for business does the right thing, but the action does not thereby have moral worth, and no amount of outward observation could settle whether he, or anyone else, has ever acted from duty at all. My friend insisted that this is not pedantry but a genuine limit on moral knowledge; I kept objecting that a condition we can never detect seems to have no practical use and may even render the whole notion of moral worth idle.

I would like to make something useful of the argument. Please write a satirical letter of reference, around 500 words, in the voice of a rigorously Kantian moral philosopher who has been asked to recommend a former student for an academic post. The letter should be deadpan from first line to last and should never break the internal logic of the position. The referee is prepared to document, in abundant and specific detail, that the student has reliably acted in accordance with duty: meeting deadlines, dealing scrupulously fairly with colleagues, returning borrowed books, refusing to inflate references, and so on; but he cannot in conscience assert that any of these actions was performed from duty, since motives are opaque and even the student’s own testimony would not settle the matter. The humour should come from the widening gap between the genre’s expectation of vigorous character endorsement and the referee’s principled refusal to supply it. The piece should be genuinely funny to someone who knows the moral-worth literature and intelligible to a reader who does not. Please write it in British English.
