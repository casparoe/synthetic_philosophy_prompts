Someone in a discussion about whether delusions are beliefs kept shutting things down with "a delusion is just a false belief, full stop." There argument was: if delusions are just false beliefs, then psychiatry is basically in the business of dressing up weird or unpopular beliefs as symptoms, and there's no principled line between a delusion and, say, a false conspiracy theory or a fringe religious claim. That conclusion felt too quick to me, but I couldn't say exactly why.

Can you take that one-liner and lay out the different precise claims it might be making? I want the menu of readings, and for each one: is it trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and would it actually support their "psychiatry just polices beliefs" conclusion. The axes I can see are: whether "is" is a definition of the concept or an empirical claim about the underlying mental state; whether "belief" means full belief, partial belief, a verbal avowal, or a state that drives action; whether the delusion has to actually be false (what about a jealous delusion where the partner really is cheating?); and whether "just" is what's doing the heavy lifting by denying that delusions have anything extra on top of the content, like fixity, bizarreness, or a broken belief-evaluation system. It would help if you worked in the two-factor picture here — the idea that you need both an anomalous experience (or similar first factor) producing the candidate content and a second factor, a failure to reject it — since that's basically a proposal about what the "extra" is that the slogan writes off. And if there's a live view that delusions aren't beliefs at all but more like imaginings or insistences, bring that in too. Finally tell me which reading the person's argument actually needed, and whether that reading survives the disambiguation.
