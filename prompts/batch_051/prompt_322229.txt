I'm taking a course on value theory and political philosophy this term, and we have been discussing whether people whose fundamental values conflict can treat moral compromise as producing genuine gains rather than mere capitulation. I keep going back and forth on the following argument, which I suspect has a sleight-of-hand in it but I cannot locate exactly where:

(1) Suppose two parties hold incompatible fundamental values, but their intensity-weightings are reversed: each cares much more about one disputed matter than the other, while the other party cares correspondingly more about the second.
(2) There is a feasible swap of concessions in which each party gives up the matter it cares less about and secures the matter it cares more about.
(3) Judged by their own values, each party regards this swap as an improvement over the no-compromise baseline.
(4) If each party regards a compromise as an improvement over no compromise by their own values, then the compromise is morally better than no compromise.
(5) Therefore, some compromises between incompatible value systems are morally better than no compromise, even when each party continues to hold that the other's favored outcome is objectively wrong.

I suspect premise (4) of smuggling in an unacceptably subjective standard of moral improvement, yet I also find it hard to explain why either party should leave the mutual gains on the table. Is the argument valid? Are its premises true? If (4) is the problem, give the strongest reading under which (4) is true and say exactly what conclusion the argument then supports.
