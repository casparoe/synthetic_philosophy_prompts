Write a decision tree, for an advanced undergraduate epistemology handout, that sorts positions on when an infinite regress is vicious. Do not organize it by the standard labels; build it from the logical space first. Each branch node should be a claim or concrete case on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased as a question a student can answer without knowing which labeled position it leads to, and each leaf should carry the position's conventional name. Test the tree against at least these regresses, and let at least four of them appear as concrete cases inside branch questions: the justificatory regress for a belief; the truth regress (p is true; the proposition that p is true is true; and so on), which every leaf must classify as non-vicious; Carroll's Tortoise-and-Achilles regress for modus ponens; the Third Man-style regress for resemblance; and a causal regress such as an infinite past. Candidate splitting claims include whether the relevant property is transmitted from each step to the next, whether an infinite chain can do grounding work without a first member, whether circularity is as bad as non-termination, whether the viciousness lies in the structure or in a finite reasoner's capacities, and whether the regress only gets going relative to a contextually raised demand for reasons. The leaves must include at least classical foundationalism, strong infinitism, coherentism, and Agrippan skepticism, plus any further positions that occupy distinct cells. After the tree, list every empty combination of branch answers and state briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive and so far undefended, or because it is merely unexplored; then flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate and give the extra question that would separate them. Aim for roughly 8 to 12 branch nodes and 6 to 9 leaves. Do not provide a bibliography or citations.
