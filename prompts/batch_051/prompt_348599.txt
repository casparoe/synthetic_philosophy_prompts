I have been struggling with the central theoretical section of Tolstoy's What Is Art? — in the standard translations, the passage is in Chapter 5, where he first states his definition in full. I do not have an edition in front of me, so I will paraphrase rather than quote. The passage runs roughly as follows: art is an activity in which one person deliberately transmits to others a feeling he has himself experienced, so that those others are infected by that same feeling and live through it in their own experience. Tolstoy explicitly contrasts this with involuntary emotional contagion, and he insists that attention to beauty has misled prior aesthetics. He then says that the degree of infectiousness of a work determines how good it is as art, and that infectiousness depends on three things: the individuality of the feeling transmitted, the clarity with which it is transmitted, and the sincerity of the artist, sincerity being the most important of the three. He also appears to treat this definition as continuous with what art has always done, rather than as a revision of the concept.

I want help understanding the precise structure of this passage. Specifically: when Tolstoy uses the infection metaphor, is he offering a causal-psychological thesis — that successful art must actually produce a distinctive kind of shared feeling in its audience — or is he smuggling a normative standard into the definition, such that a work that fails to infect a given audience is not merely weak art but not art for them at all? How should the three conditions be read: as independent requirements, as jointly sufficient conditions, or as factors that raise the probability of successful transmission? And what role does the audience play in determining whether something is art on this definition, as opposed to merely determining how good it is? I am less interested in whether Tolstoy's view is right than in getting clear on what exactly the claim is and where the pressure points are in the text itself.
