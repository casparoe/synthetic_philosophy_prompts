A colleague has been defending a deflationary account of truth, and in trying to understand it I arrived at the following argument. It seems to show that deflationism either collapses into platitudes everyone accepts or contradicts the way its defenders present it. I find the conclusion unacceptable, but each step taken individually seems forced, and I cannot see how to resist it.

(1) Deflationism says that nothing belongs to the content of the word true beyond what is given by the equivalence schema: for any proposition p, p is true if and only if p. For example, the proposition that the harbor is frozen is true just in case the harbor is frozen.

(2) Each ordinary instance of that schema is a triviality that any competent speaker accepts simply in virtue of understanding the word true.

(3) Since the schema contributes nothing over and above its instances, and each instance is already accepted by every competent speaker, deflationism's commitments are shared by every competent speaker.

(4) Correspondence theorists, coherence theorists, and their critics all understand the word true and accept each of those instances as well.

(5) A view whose commitments are entirely shared by every competent speaker is not a substantive theory of truth; it is at most a description of linguistic competence.

(6) Therefore deflationism, taken at its word, is not a substantive theory of truth and cannot be a genuine rival to correspondence or coherence accounts.

(7) But deflationists present their view as a substantive philosophical thesis about the nature of truth.

(8) So deflationism is self-undermining: if it says nothing beyond the trivialities, it is no theory; if it says something beyond them, it violates its own claim that there is nothing beyond them.

The same style of reasoning would also drain any theory that accepts the equivalence schema, leaving no room for a substantive theory of truth at all. That is at least a sign the argument proves too much, yet pointing at a sign is not a diagnosis.

Which premise or step should I reject? Explain why it looked as plausible as it did, and what rejecting it would commit me to — including what, if anything, would remain of deflationism afterward. I would most like to keep the equivalence schema and the thought that deflationism is a genuine position in logical space, but I am prepared to be told that one of them must go, and why.
