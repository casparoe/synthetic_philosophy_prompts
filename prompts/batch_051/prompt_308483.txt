A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about the justification of punishment, and we kept returning to an asymmetry that neither of us could resolve. On one side is an ordinary case: a person in a stable democracy commits a premeditated murder, later gives a complete confession and cooperates fully with the prosecution, and almost everyone still agrees that he should serve a substantial sentence. The confession may reduce it, but it does not cancel it. On the other side is a transitional case of the kind that arose in South Africa after apartheid: a person who tortured and killed political activists could come before the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, give a full public account of what he had done and on whose orders, and, if the disclosure was judged complete and the acts were judged to have been committed in pursuit of a political objective, be granted amnesty and exempted from prosecution. We found ourselves wanting to hold both judgments at once—the ordinary murderer should go to prison, and the transitional perpetrator should be permitted to go free—but we could not articulate the principle that separated them.

I would like to know whether a single defensible principle yields both judgments. Is the relevant difference that the transitional killings were political while the ordinary murder was private? That a fragile new democracy must sometimes trade punishment for truth in order to survive? That the victims of mass atrocity are owed the truth more urgently than they are owed retribution? Take whichever candidate principle you judge strongest, test it against the hardest variants you can construct—for example, a political assassin who gives an equally complete confession in a stable democracy, a perpetrator whose disclosure merely repeats facts already on the public record, a confession that omits the perpetrator's worst act while disclosing lesser ones, or a killing committed during a political conflict but from private cruelty rather than political commitment—and then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry is defensible, or whether one of our two original verdicts ought to be revised.
