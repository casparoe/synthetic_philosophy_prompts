I've been trying to separate right-kind from wrong-kind reasons for an attitude with a little test I call the "content flip test," and I'd like an honest assesment of it. The idea: take a reason you have to adopt some attitude toward a content p—believing p, desiring p, admiring p, whatever. Now replay the situation with the content replaced by its negation (or a content with the opposite valence), but leave the underlying incentives, threats, payoffs, and other people's goals the same. If the same consideration would ground an equally strong reason to adopt the attitude toward the flipped content, then the original reason was wrong-kind. If its force weakens, reverses, or disappears under the flip, it's at least partly right-kind. A demon offers you a million dollars to believe the sky is green: replay it with "the sky is not green" and the demon, who doesn't care what you believe, would still pay, so the money is a wrong-kind reason. But seeing the sky look green doesn't survive the flip—it wouldn't support believing it's not green—so that's right-kind. I know the object-given/state-given distinction is supposed to do something similar, but I can never apply it cleanly; my test feels more operational. Is it equivalent to that distinction? Where does it diverge? Give me the three strongest counterexamples and say whether any of them are fatal rather than mere edge cases.
