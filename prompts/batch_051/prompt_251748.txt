I'm trying to find a single principle behind my judgments about rational priors under ignorance, and I have a tentative candidate: the rational prior is the one that maximizes entropy subject to exactly the constraints I know, relative to a uniform base measure. Here are my verdicts. For a coin whose bias p in [0,1] I know nothing about, the uniform density over p feels rationally required. For the very same coin described by its log-odds λ = log(p/(1−p)), I also feel the natural ignorance prior is uniform over λ, yet the two distributions over the same underlying quantity cannot both be the rationally required one. For a six-sided die known only to have expected value 4 rather than 3.5, I think the rational prior over face probabilities is the maximum-entropy distribution satisfying that mean, which shifts weight toward the higher faces; the uniform prior would be wrong there. For a positive quantity known only to be positive, I want a prior invariant under rescaling, with density proportional to 1/x, even though it is improper and no normalizable maximum-entropy distribution exists on the original scale. For two exhaustive outcomes about which I know nothing, I think 1/2–1/2 is rationally required. Is there one principled account—a defensible version of maximum entropy, a symmetry or invariance rule, or something else—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn and why? State the principle precisely, and say what it predicts about a case I have not settled: a point chosen on the surface of a sphere with no further information, where I feel the prior should be rotation-invariant, that is, uniform in area.
