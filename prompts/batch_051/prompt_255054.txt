In *The Descent of Man*, Darwin makes two claims about widened sympathy that seem to pull in opposite directions. In the chapter on the moral sense, he suggests that moral progress consists partly in the expansion of sympathy: as small tribes are united into larger communities, the simplest reason tells each person to extend social instincts and sympathies to all members of the nation, and once that point is reached only an artificial barrier prevents sympathy from extending to the men of all nations and races. In the next chapter, on the development of the intellectual and moral faculties during primeval and civilised times, he warns that civilised men do their utmost to check the elimination of the weak by building asylums, instituting poor laws, and saving lives through medicine, with the result that weak members of civilised societies propagate their kind; anyone who has bred domestic animals, he says, will not doubt that this is highly injurious to the race of man.

Is Darwin contradicting himself between these two passages: one treating widened sympathy as an achievement of moral development, the other treating the practical expression of that sympathy in care for the weak as a biological danger? Can the two be reconciled, for example by distinguishing the causes of moral progress from the conditions of biological fitness, by reading the second passage as a descriptive warning rather than a normative objection, or by arguing that Darwin’s moral criterion simply does not treat the preservation of the race as an overriding value? Give the most honest reconciliation you can, and state which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
