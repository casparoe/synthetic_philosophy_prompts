I have recently been put in the uncomfortable position of having to review a policy brief that uses no fewer than three historical analogies—Munich, Vietnam, and the so-called Thucydides trap—to conclude that a proposed diplomatic opening is almost certainly doomed. The brief’s author writes with great confidence about what “history teaches.” I have been rereading Thucydides to check whether this is the kind of inference he himself licenses. The passage that strikes me is this, from Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War, 1.22.4:

“The absence of romance in my history will, I fear, detract somewhat from its interest; but if it be judged useful by those inquirers who desire an exact knowledge of the past as an aid to the interpretation of the future, which in the course of human things must resemble if it does not reflect it, I shall be content.”

I want to understand the exact relation between what Thucydides claims in this passage and the reasoning of someone who says, for example, that a current standoff is “another Munich” or “another Vietnam.” Are those the same kind of inference? If not, where does the difference lie? Please compare the two: Thucydides’ appeal to a general resemblance between future and past as a ground for usefulness on one side, and the policy analogy’s appeal to a single prior episode as a guide on the other. I would like an answer that does not merely say analogies are imperfect; I want to know whether Thucydides’ view, if taken seriously, gives us a principled way to separate legitimate from misleading uses of the past. If my assumption that these are even comparable is confused, or if the passage cannot bear the weight I am placing on it, tell me that directly and then do the next best thing.
