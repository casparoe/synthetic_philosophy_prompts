I serve on the Professional Standards Committee of a scholarly association, and I must circulate a short written opinion before our next meeting. We are reviewing a complaint against Dr. M, a senior member whose work is excellent and who meets every formal obligation: grant deadlines, contracts, institutional commitments, all flawless. The complaint concerns the small stuff. Over several years, M has been consistently ten or fifteen minutes late, and has repeatedly missed commitments of the kind "I'll send you comments by Friday" or "I'll call at three this afternoon." A junior colleague argues that these are promises, and that a settled pattern of breaking them is an ethical defect — a defect of reliability — which the association should not ignore merely because each breach is small.

M's defense leans on this passage from David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 5:

> "But as there is naturally no inclination to observe promises, distinct from a sense of their obligation; it follows, that fidelity is no natural virtue, and that promises have no force, antecedent to human conventions."

M's reading of the passage is this: fidelity is an artificial virtue. It exists only because conventions arose to serve the necessities and interests of social life, so its scope is set by what those conventions actually do. The conventions that govern our professional life distinguish full undertakings from loose scheduling language, and no convention attaches moral weight to the latter. To censure M, on this view, would be to treat an artificial virtue as though it were natural — to moralize beyond the point of the institution. A colleague on the committee replies that this gets the passage exactly backwards. The passage denies only that promissory obligation exists before convention; it says nothing about small promises lying outside the obligation once the convention is in place. What the convention does is make the giving of one's word binding as such. If we let the size of the promised act determine whether a word counts, we reopen for case-by-case interest calculation the very question the convention exists to settle — and a convention of promise-keeping that silently exempts small promises would erode the trust on which it runs.

Draft the written opinion. It should recommend a concrete disposition — dismiss the complaint, issue a private caution, issue a formal censure, or adopt some conditional alternative the committee could actually enforce — and it should settle the interpretive disagreement over the Hume passage, making clear what the passage does and does not imply about whether small promises fall inside the kind of fidelity Hume is analyzing. Engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side; the committee will not be satisfied by a bare recommendation or a bare label for the winning position.
