I sit on the advisory working group of a state law revision commission. The commission is reviewing the state's criminal code, and one item before us is the blackmail statute. The working group is divided. One faction argues that the statute is conceptually unstable: the law generally permits a person to disclose truthful, reputation-damaging information about another, and it permits a person to ask another for money, so it is puzzling that joining the two — demanding money in exchange for not disclosing such information — is treated as a serious felony. That faction wants to recommend repeal unless the commission can articulate a defensible rationale. The other faction replies that the conceptual puzzle is a philosopher's problem, not a legislator's: the practice is plainly coercive, victims experience it as extortion, and the current statute serves a clear public interest. The chair has asked me, as rapporteur, to circulate a short memo before the next meeting.

The memo should recommend one concrete position — retaining the statute, narrowing it, or repealing it — and defend it as the position the full commission should adopt. It should not survey the literature; it should argue for a decision. The memo should engage the conceptual puzzle at the level of principle rather than resting on the fact that blackmail is currently illegal, and it should treat the strongest argument on the losing side at least as seriously as the winning one. Please also state how confident the memo is in its recommendation and single out the point it is least sure of.
