I got into an argument with a friend about the quantum suicide thought experiment, and he introduced a wrinkle I can't quite think through. The standard setup: you sit in front of a device that measures some quantum observable—say the spin of an electron in a fifty-fifty superposition—and if the result is spin-down it fires a bullet into your skull, while spin-up does nothing, and you repeat this many times. On a many-worlds reading there's always some post-run branch where you've survived every measurement, so your first-person experience is guaranteed to be one of survived; on a collapse reading, surviving n runs is about a 1-in-2^n fluke, so if you survive you should take that as overwhelming evidence for many-worlds (modulo the usual objections, which I'm happy to include in the analysis).

The wrinkle: suppose the gun has a small, purely classical chance of jamming. Each run, after the quantum measurement occurs but before the gun would fire, the firing mechanism has an independent probability q—say one percent—of being stuck, in which case the bullet doesn't discharge even if the spin came out down. You know q beforehand. I can't tell whether this just waters the original argument down, kills it, or does something more interesting. I'd like someone to actually work through it: what does each interpretation predict about the probability that I'll be alive after n runs, under both the branch-counting and the Born-rule-measure versions of many-worlds? Does the likelihood ratio favoring many-worlds shrink in a clean way, and can I compensate by doing more runs? And does the limit q→0 show that the original experiment was fine all along, or is it already broken at q=0 with the jam only making that obvious?
