The salary cap, luxury tax, and amateur draft of a North American professional sports league invite a pointed question about distributive justice. A team may not sign a player to a contract above a league-set payroll limit, however eager the player, the agent, and the owner are to make the deal; some trades and signings are voided outright; and the league reserves the right to assign the best incoming talent to its weakest teams. Now consider a well-known philosophical argument. Suppose a distribution arises entirely through uncoerced transfers: a million spectators each freely pay to watch one athlete play, and that athlete ends up vastly richer than everyone else. The entitlement theory associated with Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example says this resulting inequality is just, because every step in the chain that produced it was just. A salary cap, by contrast, blocks precisely the freely chosen exchanges that would cumulate into a lopsided distribution, on the ground that the lopsided distribution itself would be unacceptable. Reconstruct the implicit theory of distributive justice that this institutional design presupposes. Be sure to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: in particular, whether competitive balance is being treated as a demand of justice, or whether the league could pursue it purely as a way of keeping fans interested while remaining compatible with the entitlement view. Identify any internal tensions in the practice — for example, a draft that redistributes new players alongside a largely free market for veterans — and say which parts presuppose which views. Finally, say what the league's rules would have to look like if it consistently adopted the entitlement theory instead: which mechanisms would have to go, and what residual regulation, if any, would remain.
