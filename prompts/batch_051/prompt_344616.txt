A friend walked me through this and I can't find the hole. The conclusion that everything is just a heap of parts bothers me more then the no-self part does.

(1) Take a chariot (he used a chariot, but any composite thing works: a chair, a car). It isn't identical to any one of its parts — not the wheels, not the axle, not the frame.
(2) It also isn't identical to the parts taken all together as a mere collection, since a scattered pile of chariot parts in a box isn't a chariot; the 'togetherness' doesn't add some extra object you can point at.
(3) There's nothing else to the chariot besides its parts and how they're arranged.
(4) So the chariot doesn't really exist — 'chariot' is just a handy name slapped on parts in an arrangement.
(5) The same reasoning applies to a person: I'm not any one of my mental or physical parts, not the pile of them, and there's nothing else to be found. So there's no self; 'person' is a useful label over the aggregates.
(6) But the reasoning doesn't stop there. By the exact same steps, there are no tables, no forests, no molecules, no flocks, no countries — nothing composite exists; there are only ultimate simples arranged various ways.
(7) I can live with no self, but I'm not ready to say my table doesn't exist. So some step between (1) and (6) has to be a little too strong.

Which step should I reject, and what does it cost? I'd most like to keep (5) and drop whatever turns it into global mereological nihilism, but maybe the only way to do that is to find a principled difference between selves and tables, and I don't see one. Also, my friend says the Buddhists actually bite the bullet on (6) anyway — is that a fair reading of the no-self position, or is he overstating it?
