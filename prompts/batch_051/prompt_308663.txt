I would like a poem that could close a session on the neuroscience of free will: a fourteen-line sonnet in the Petrarchan manner (octave, volta, sestet) whose final two lines work as a couplet, taking Libet's timing experiment as its subject. The octave should render the scene from the subject's point of view: watching a dot travel around a clock face, waiting for the urge to flex the wrist, noting where the dot was when the impulse first arose, then watching the movement follow as if the felt intention had been doing the commanding. Let the octave's enclosed rhymes feel like that small self-closing loop of agency, and have it close on the commonsense causal picture: first the decision, then the act.

At the volta, that order should give way. Introduce the electroencephalographic finding: the readiness potential begins rising from the motor cortex about half a second before the wrist flexes, while the subject's felt urge to move is reported only about a fifth of a second before the movement, so the brain's motor preparation is already roughly a third of a second old when the subject first feels it as his own. The sestet should then inhabit the interpretive bind without resolving it: on one side, the thought that the readiness potential is not yet a decision, or that conscious awareness is only a late-arriving reporter; on the other, the suspicion that introspection claims an authorship the brain never actually offered. Let the closing couplet say, as exactly as verse allows, what the experiment does and does not show: it does not by itself refute free will, but it does dislodge the conscious self from the position where naive introspection had placed it. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind would recognize the experiment, its timing, and the live options for interpreting it, rather than finding the science blurred or the theory merely name-dropped.
