I am a PhD student in the history of economic thought, and I keep returning to a sentence in the Preface to Bernard Mandeville's The Fable of the Bees as the sharpest statement of a claim I find uncomfortable. What renders man sociable, Mandeville insists, is not "good nature, pity, affability" but precisely his worst qualities: "his vilest and most hateful qualities are the most necessary accomplishments to fit him for the largest, and, according to the world, the happiest and most flourishing societies." In the fable itself the mechanism that turns private vice into public benefit is concrete: the greed and luxury of the rich set the poor to work, and the hive's wealth and power rest on the employment and industry that such appetites call into existence.

My question is whether that argument survives a development Mandeville never had to face. In an economy where much of the work that greed calls into being can be performed by machines owned and directed by a small number of people, the link between private acquisitiveness and the public benefit of employment is heavily attenuated, because consumption no longer needs to translate into wages for the many. Does Mandeville's argument still go through once that link is cut? Or does it need repairing—say, by relocating the public benefit from employment to innovation, lower prices, or the tax base—and would such a repaired version still be recognizably Mandeville's? Or does the argument now collapse into a purely distributional question about who owns the machines and captures the surplus? I am interested less in whether Mandeville was right in his own time than in identifying the precise premise the automation objection bears on, and whether anything important survives.
