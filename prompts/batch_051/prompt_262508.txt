I spent my working life solving inverse problems, and intractability was part of the furniture of the subject; one did not mistake the hardness of a computation for a defect in the agent who failed to complete it. Since retiring, I have been reading the philosophy of rationality, and I keep meeting the Bayesian ideal: the rational agent conditionalizes and maximizes expected utility. I would like your actual credence in the following proposition.

Proposition: For cognitively bounded agents such as ourselves, the standard ideal of unbounded Bayesian rationality — exact probabilistic inference and expected-utility maximization over all available acts — is a defective normative standard, not merely a harmless idealization, once computational complexity is taken seriously.

By "defective normative standard," I mean that it would be an error for a theory of rationality to set this as the standard to which bounded agents ought to approximate, rather than treating it as an unattainable limit that may be set aside when it misleads. Please decompose the proposition rather than giving me only a number. I want at least the following terms, each with a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification:

(1) The probability that the core computations the ideal requires — computing a posterior over a nontrivial hypothesis space, and finding an optimal policy in a realistic decision or planning problem — are computationally intractable in the relevant sense. Do not simply say that P ≠ NP is unproven; condition on your best estimate of what complexity theory indicates and state it.

(2) Conditional on their being intractable, the probability that a normative standard may nonetheless legitimately require what a bounded agent cannot compute; that is, the probability that "ought" is not constrained in this way.

(3) The probability that some coherent notion of bounded-optimal rationality — rationality under explicit computational constraints — can be formulated well enough to serve as a replacement ideal rather than as a gesture toward one.

(4) The probability that the argument survives even if the relevant problems turn out to be worst-case tractable but only with astronomical constants, or turn out to be easy in practice because heuristics perform well; in other words, the extent to which the criticism is about hardness as such rather than about idealization as such.

Then show how you combine these terms into a final credence, identify the term to which the final number is most sensitive, and say what evidence or argument would move it most. I am not looking for the answer that everything depends on one's decision theory; I want a number with its weak points marked.
