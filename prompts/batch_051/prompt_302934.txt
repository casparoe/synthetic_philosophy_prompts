Ok so in my geometry class we spent a weird day on the start of Book VII of Euclid's Elements, the part where he defines a unit as the thing through which each existing thing is called one, and a number as a multittude made up of units. My friend and I tried to actually take the definition of "unit" seriously, and we ended up with an argument that seems to prove 2 = 1, or at least that Euclid's definitions make every number bigger than 1 impossible. Obviously that can't be right, so something has to go, but every step by itself felt pretty locked in.

Here's the argument:
(1) Euclid says a unit is what makes each thing one, and a number is a multittude of units. So the number 2 is a multittude of two units.
(2) Call the two units u and v.
(3) u is one, and v is one. And there's nothing more to a unit than that — no parts, no internal structure, no feature one unit could have that the other one lacks.
(4) So u and v are exactly alike in every way.
(5) If two things are exactly alike in every way, then they're the same thing.
(6) So u = v, which means 2 is made of the same unit counted twice — which is just one unit, not a multittude of two. So 2 = 1, or 2 isn't a number in Euclid's sense.
(7) Since 2 isn't 1, either Euclid's definition of unit, or Euclid's definition of number, or step (5), must be false.

What I'd most like to keep is Euclid's idea that a number is a multittude of units, and the even more basic idea that 2 is genuinely two of something and not just one thing counted twice.
