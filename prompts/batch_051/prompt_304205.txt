I write about formal systems and the foundations of computation for a general-audience science magazine, and I am preparing a piece on what it means to reason about a system rather than within it. My central example is the MU-puzzle. The puzzle uses strings built from the symbols M, I, and U, beginning with the string MI. Four rewrite rules may be applied, in any order and as often as one likes: (1) if a string ends in I, append a U; (2) if a string has the form Mx, where x is any string of I's and U's, double the part after the M, producing Mxx; (3) if III occurs anywhere, replace that occurrence with U; and (4) if UU occurs anywhere, delete it. The apparent task is to derive MU. A solver who only pushes symbols within the rules tends to wander, while a solver who steps back can prove that MU is unreachable: the number of I's in any reachable string is never divisible by three, yet the string MU has no I's at all, and zero is divisible by three. That outside move is sometimes called JOOTSing, short for jumping out of the system.

I would like you to compare two readings of what this move accomplishes. On the deflationary reading, nothing genuinely outside the system has happened: the solver has simply moved to a richer system, ordinary arithmetic over the number of I's, and carried out another rule-governed proof there. Any apparently external insight can be absorbed into some larger formal system, so the inside-outside distinction is only relative to a boundary drawn for convenience. On the anti-deflationary reading, the decisive step is not the arithmetic but the change of stance: the solver stops using the rules and begins to talk about the system itself, its strings, invariant properties, and limits, and that aboutness is not captured by any additional batch of symbol-pushing rules. Which reading is closer to the truth, and why? Please give a clear verdict for an educated nonspecialist, and take the strongest version of the losing reading seriously before rejecting it.
