I am writing up my views on Smith's moral theory for a seminar, and I would like a consistency check on the position I have arrived at before I present it. I am committed to all of the following:

(1) Sympathy is imaginative projection. When I sympathise with someone, I bring their situation home to myself and feel what I would feel in it; I do not catch their feeling by contagion, nor do I directly read off what they actually feel.

(2) To judge that an action is proper is to judge that a fully informed, disinterested spectator would sympathise with the agent's motive.

(3) The impartial spectator is not a standard supplied from outside social life. It is internalised from the accumulated and generalised responses of actual spectators, built up through being observed by others and through observing their reactions.

(4) The impartial spectator can nevertheless correct the settled moral opinion of an entire society. A practice that every actual member of a society approves could in principle be condemned by the spectator.

(5) The standard of propriety is constituted by the sentiments of the impartial spectator, surveyed from a disinterested standpoint. There is no further fact about what actual people feel, over and above that, to which a judgement of propriety must answer.

(6) The impartial spectator is a standpoint we can approach through practice and habit but never fully occupy, because we can never entirely rid ourselves of self-preference.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs, or larger subsets, are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what has to give in the least costly revision, and what that revision costs elsewhere in the set. Then identify the single change that would do most to make the whole position stable. I would rather have a discriminating audit than one that treats every pair as equally problematic, and I am less interested in a general exposition of Smith than in a precise diagnosis of this particular combination.
