Exercise question

An investigator has data from an observational study comparing two treatments, A and B. Each patient is classified by disease severity at entry as either severe or mild. The table below reports, for each treatment and for each severity group, the number of successful outcomes and the number of patients.

Treatment | Severe (successes/total) | Mild (successes/total) | Overall (successes/total)
A | 27/90 | 9/10 | 36/100
B | 2/10 | 72/90 | 74/100

(a) Convert these counts into success rates, and state what the overall comparison and the two stratum-specific comparisons suggest. (2 marks)

(b) Explain why the overall and stratum-specific comparisons point in different directions. (4 marks)

(c) A colleague says that the overall comparison uses all the data and is therefore the correct basis for deciding which treatment is better, and that the stratified comparisons merely explain away a real difference. Assess this claim. Your answer should say what determines which comparison is relevant to a treatment decision. (6 marks)

(d) Describe one causal structure under which the stratified comparison is the appropriate basis for choosing a treatment, and one under which the overall comparison is more defensible. In each case, state whether the structure is compatible with the numbers in the table. (3 marks)

Student's answer

(a) Overall, A has 36/100 = 36% success and B has 74/100 = 74%, so B is 38 percentage points higher overall. For severe patients, A has 27/90 = 30% and B has 2/10 = 20%, so A is 10 points higher. For mild patients, A has 9/10 = 90% and B has 72/90 = 80%, so A is 10 points higher. Overall B appears better, but A appears better within each severity group.

(b) This is Simpson's paradox. The reversal arises because severity is strongly associated with outcome, and the two treatments are distributed very differently across the severity groups. A is given mainly to severe patients, who do badly whichever treatment they receive, while B is given mainly to mild patients, who do well whichever treatment they receive. A's overall rate is therefore dragged down by its many severe patients, and B's overall rate is lifted by its many mild patients. The aggregated comparison mixes the treatment contrast with the severity contrast.

(c) The colleague is wrong. Once severity has been measured, the stratified comparison is the correct one because it controls for confounding by severity. Simpson's paradox shows that an overall comparison can be misleading whenever a confounding variable is unevenly distributed across the groups being compared, and the within-stratum comparisons give the true effect of the treatment. The overall comparison is biased because it combines the effect of treatment with the effect of severity. The right conclusion is that A is the better treatment, since it does better in both severe and mild patients. The only situation in which the stratified comparison would not be appropriate is if severity had been affected by the treatment itself; but severity was measured at entry, so that cannot happen here.

(d) The stratified comparison is appropriate when severity is a common cause of both treatment assignment and outcome: severity influences which treatment a patient receives and also influences the chance of success. Conditioning on severity then removes the confounding, and the stratum-specific comparisons estimate the causal effect of treatment. The overall comparison would be appropriate if severity influenced treatment assignment but had no effect on outcome; severity would then not be a confounder, and the overall difference would be a pure treatment effect. Both structures are compatible with the observed counts.
