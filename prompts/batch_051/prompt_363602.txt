My friend Dana and me spent most of last night arguing about whether optimism and hope are epistemic vices or moral virtues, and she kept insisting that, whatever the moral upside, the case for epistemic vice has already been made in full. By the time I got home I had written down the five moves she trotted out, not neccessarily in order: (1) hope is wishful thinking — it raises your confidence in the desired outcome without any new evidence, so it violates the evidentialist norm that you should believe only what your evidence supports; (2) there's a decent empirical literature showing people are systematically over-optimistic about their own chances, which makes ordinary optimism a standing calibration failure; (3) hope works as a filter on inquiry, making you seek out, remember, and weigh evidence in favor of the hoped-for outcome, so it contaminates the very process that is supposed to correct bias; (4) even if hope is morally or practically useful — it sustains people through illness, motivates them to act, and so on — that's beside the point, because epistemic evaluation is about truth and evidence, not about whether a state is good for you to have; (5) when the evidence genuinely leaves p uncertain, the epistemically right response is to stay at whatever degree of confidence the evidence supports, and hope is precisely the urge to lean toward p because you like p, which is exactly the sort of partiality epistemic norms are meant to rule out.

Dana says these five basically exhaust the case for counting hope and optimism as epistemic vices, and that any further argument would just be a restatement, a special case, or a mashup of two of them. I'm less sure, but I've been going around in circles.
