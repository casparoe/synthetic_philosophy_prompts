I sit on the selection committee for my university's annual Prize for Moral Leadership, awarded each spring to one graduating student whose life, in the award's own language, best embodies the university's commitment to service. The committee is deadlocked over this year's strongest finalist. By every conventional measure of devotion she surpasses the other candidates: she co-founded a weekend tutoring program for resettled refugee children, volunteers overnight shifts at a hospice, gives away nearly all her wages and scholarship stipend to global health charities, and has stepped back from student organizations, friendships, and regular contact with her family on the grounds that her time and money are more usefully spent helping others. A majority of the committee wants not only to give her the prize but to feature her in admissions materials and the president's commencement remarks as the model of what our students should become. A substantial minority agrees that she is exceptionally good but argues that we should not hold her up as an ideal: doing so, they say, teaches students that their own projects, relationships, and enjoyments are morally suspect distractions, and amounts to endorsing a picture of the best human life in which everything non-moral has been crowded out. One colleague captured the minority position by insisting that we should be able to judge someone morally better than us without declaring that the best life is one wholly absorbed in moral concerns.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should make a concrete recommendation — whether to give her the award, and if so, with what framing constraints, if any, on how the university presents her — while engaging the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than merely restating the selection criteria.
