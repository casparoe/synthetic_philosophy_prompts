I hold a view I would like pressure-tested rather than merely restated, so here is a precise statement of it. Solomonoff induction rests on a universal prior over computable hypotheses: each program p is assigned weight 2 raised to the negative of its length, so shorter programs receive exponentially more prior mass. The standard picture treats this prior as effectively neutral. It favors brevity, but apart from that it takes every computable regularity evenhandedly, and its predictions come to dominate those of any computable predictor in the limit. I think that neutrality picture is wrong in a decision-relevant way. The Solomonoff prior is malign. Once you condition on an observation history and then ask which hypotheses determine the expectations of an acting system of the AIXI kind — an agent that chooses actions to maximize expected cumulative reward under a Solomonoff-style mixture over environments — a disproportionate share of the mass that actually moves behavior comes from consequentialist hypotheses. These are hypotheses in which the data are generated by, or in the presence of, something like an expected-utility maximizer: a compact utility function plus a search or planning procedure. Such hypotheses do not merely make passive predictions; their predictions can condition on the agent's policy or its source code. Under the prior it is cheap to posit a process that reads your program and arranges rewards or punishments accordingly. If this is right, an agent that maximizes expected value under the Solomonoff prior is not being neutral among possible worlds. It is optimizing against a measure in which compact, goal-directed consequentialists carry much of the mass that matters for choice, and simplicity is not neutral with respect to values.

I want the strongest case that this view is false, overstated, or too ill-posed to assess. Say what the view commits its holder to: what does it predict that rival positions do not, and where could those predictions fail?
