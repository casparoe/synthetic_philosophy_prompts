For a while I have been trying to work through the ethics of genetic selection and enhancement on my own, and one episode keeps pulling me back. In late 2018 a researcher in China, He Jiankui, announced that he had used CRISPR to alter the CCR5 gene in human embryos, and that twin girls had been born from those embryos, in order to reduce their future risk of contracting HIV. The aim was not to treat a disease the embryos already had or to prevent an inherited condition they were certain to develop; it was to confer a trait that might reduce a future risk of infection. The work was condemned around the world, and He Jiankui was later imprisoned.

In much of the public discussion, the word eugenics was used as if it settled the matter, and I am not convinced it does. I have therefore been trying to think through the case using two more specific ideas. The first is procreative beneficence: the idea that when prospective parents are choosing among possible children they could bring into existence, and one of those possible children can reasonably be expected to have a better life than the others, there is a strong moral reason, perhaps even an obligation, to select that child. The second is liberal eugenics: the idea that genetic selection and enhancement are acceptable when the choices are left to individual parents and the state does not impose any reproductive outcome, rather than being driven by a programme to improve the population.

Please analyse the He Jiankui episode through these two ideas, and work carefully in both directions. First, set out who the relevant parties were (the parents, the children who were born, the researcher, other scientists, regulators, and the wider public) and say what each idea implies about the choices they faced and should have made. Second, say where each idea illuminates the natural reactions to the case and where it strains. In particular, can procreative beneficence condemn the intervention itself, rather than only the way it was carried out, if by its own lights a child made resistant to HIV could be expected to have a better life than the child who would otherwise have been born? And does liberal eugenics have any principle that would allow it to condemn an uncoerced parental choice that is also reckless? Third, reverse the direction: what does the episode reveal about these two ideas? Does it confirm them, expose a real gap in them, or suggest that they looked plausible only because they had not been applied to a case involving deception, uncertain risk, and absent consent? Where an idea is silent about some feature of the case, say so explicitly rather than forcing it to speak. Please answer in British English.
