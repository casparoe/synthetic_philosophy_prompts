Last night I spent a long evening arguing with a close friend about why certain norms — the duel, chattel slavery, perhaps footbinding — actually came to an end. My friend defended the view that moral argument did the main causal work: principled opponents of the practice produced arguments that spread and eventually persuaded the relevant elites, and the norm collapsed because its rational defenses had been broken. I came away from the exchange holding the opposite, roughly honor-and-interest view, though I want you to engage with my own statement of it rather than treat it as a quotation of any particular author.

My view, stated as a comparative causal claim: in the major cases of dueling and chattel slavery, and plausibly in a wider class of coercive status norms, the timing and speed of a norm's death are better predicted by shifts in what the enforcing elites found honorable or dishonorable, and by changes in their material and institutional interests, than by the quality, prevalence, or novelty of the moral arguments against the practice. Moral argument, on my view, was for the most part downstream and justificatory — it gave people a respectable way to describe the change they were already being pushed toward, and sometimes served as a coordination signal, but it was not the load-bearing cause. I do not deny that arguments were present, and I allow that they occasionally accelerated a change that honor or interest had already made available.

I want you to tell me what would count against this view, and I want it played straight rather than softened for my comfort. In particular:

1. Rank the genuinely possible defeaters — historical findings, comparative cases, or facts about the local mechanisms of norm change — from fatal to merely awkward, and say why each has the force it does. I would rather you give me a few well-developed examples, including cases my view should handle easily and cases it would strain to handle, than a long catalogue.

2. Separate my load-bearing commitments from the claims I could abandon cheaply. For instance, am I really committed to the claim that moral argument never tipped a norm by itself, that elites who cite principle are usually insincere, that honor and interest always pull in the same direction, and that arguments cannot be the upstream cause of the honor shifts that do the real work? Or is the view only committed to weaker, more statistical claims? If I give up the strong readings, say whether what remains is still distinct from my friend's argument-first view.

3. Say what I, as someone who holds this view, would have to concede counts against it — not just what a critic would say, but what would genuinely expose it as a risky claim. For each concession, tell me whether I can absorb it by qualification at no cost, or whether absorbing it would empty the view out.

4. End with an honest assessment of whether the view, as I have stated it, has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether such insulation would be a flaw in a causal thesis about history or a reasonable feature of any macro-level claim about norm change.

Do not frame your answer as a bibliography or a reading list; I want the structure of the view's exposure, not citations.

Please end with a single final line beginning exactly "Verdict:" that gives a one-sentence summary of how exposed you judge the core of my position to be. The line before it should carry the actual substantive judgment; the Verdict line itself should just compress it.
