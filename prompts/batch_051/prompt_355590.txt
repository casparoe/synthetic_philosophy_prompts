In a discussion about how ordinary people come to participate in systemic wrongdoing, a colleague dismissed the case of a mid-level official who approved a harmful policy without apparent malice by saying it was "just the banality of evil." I suspect the phrase is doing more argumentative work than its familiarity suggests, and that it bundles together several distinct claims that do not all travel together. Please separate the precise theses that "the banality of evil" might be expressing — for example, about the role of thoughtlessness, about whether perpetrators are monstrous or strikingly ordinary, about what kind of motive or absence of motive is at issue, and about how evil presents itself to observers — and say for each whether it is a fair reading of Arendt's account of Eichmann, whether it is independently plausible (platitude, substantive, or overstated), and whether it would actually license treating the phrase as a sufficient explanation of the case at hand.
