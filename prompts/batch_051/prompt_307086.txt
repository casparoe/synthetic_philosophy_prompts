I've been chipping away at a short post on the legality/legitimacy split during covid—border controls, lockdowns, all the emergency powers—and I keep coming back to a passage in Locke's Second Treatise that I think rationalist-y blogs underuse. It's from Chapter XIV, "Of Prerogative," section 159:

nay, it is fit that the laws themselves should in some cases give way to the executive power, or rather to this fundamental law of nature and government, viz. That as much as may be, all the members of the society are to be preserved: for since many accidents may happen, wherein a strict and rigid observation of the laws may do harm; (as not to pull down an innocent man's house to stop the fire, when the next to it is burning) and a man may come sometimes within the reach of the law, which makes no distinction of persons, by an action that may deserve reward and pardon; 'tis fit the ruler should have a power, in many cases, to mitigate the severity of the law, and pardon some offenders: for the end of government being the preservation of all, as much as may be, even the guilty are to be spared, where it can prove no prejudice to the innocent.

Here's my rough draft. It's maybe 600 words, and I'm not a Locke person, so push back on the reading if it's strained.

Locke's fire case is usually treated as an illustration of prerogative, but I want to run it the other direction: it shows that legality and legitimacy can come apart without collapsing into "might makes right." The man whose house gets pulled down is innocent. Strictly applied law would forbid the act. But the act can still be right, because the point of the whole legal order is preservation—"that as much as may be, all the members of the society are to be preserved." So the same end that grounds ordinary legality also grounds a temporary departure from it.

If that's right, the live question about covid measures was never really "was this legal in the narrow sense." It's whether the fire was real, whether pulling down this particular house actually stops it, and who gets to answer those questions. In Locke's example these are nearly perceptual: you can see the house next door burning. But in a pandemic the fire is a modeled object—case curves, R, ICU projections—and the people producing the models are also running ordinary politics. A border closure can be both public-health intervention and immigration control; a lockdown can protect hospital capacity and also grow the state's capacity to compel.

Locke does have an answer to the who-decides question: the people judge afterward, by whether the act tended to public good or hurt. That's the real check on prerogative. But the check only works if the after-the-fact judgment isn't itself captured by the same epistemic fog. What seemed to happen, though, is that emergency powers became another front in partisan epistemology: whether the fire was real tracked prior views about the state.

Tentative thesis: legality and legitimacy aren't rivals. Legitimacy is legality's telos, inherited by whatever act temporarily does the law's preservation-job. So the deep problem with emergency measures isn't the moment of illegality; it's that the telos-based judgment Locke needs the people to make is exactly the kind of judgment mass emergencies degrade.

Three things I'd like comments on:

1. Fairness to Locke: am I reading the passage in context, or smuggling in a "legality vs. legitimacy" distinction that's foreign to him?
2. The analogy: where does the fire-house example actually break when moved to lockdowns and border controls? Is that a fixable disanalogy or a fatal one?
3. Pushback: which premise would a careful reader of the rationalist-y blogosphere attack first, and how should I tighten it?

Also I have a naggling feeling I'm using "legitimacy" to smuggle in a standart I don't defend; if so, say so directly.
