I am trying to determine what given names and family names actually contribute to the reference of a person's name, and I would like a principle that captures my considered verdicts about particular cases. Here are the cases.

1. A woman born Hillary Rodham takes her spouse's surname and becomes widely known as Hillary Clinton. I am confident that 'Hillary Rodham' and 'Hillary Clinton' refer to the same person, and that the earlier surname is not now a misnomer. I also do not think the two surnames are interchangeable descriptions of her; they mark different familial relations.

2. Consider the singer Karen Carpenter. I do not think that 'Carpenter' in 'Karen Carpenter' predicates that she is a carpenter, refers to the trade, or refers to a lineage of carpenters. It feels like an inert part of a label.

3. In a patronymic convention such as Iceland's, Jón Einarsson is so called because his father was Einar, and his daughter is Sigríður Jónsdóttir because her father is Jón. I find that the second element here is not inert the way 'Carpenter' is: 'Jónsdóttir' transparently records that she is Jón's daughter. So the same position in a name can apparently be either an inert label-part or a live relational description, depending on how the naming practice works.

4. When Muhammad Ali changed his name from Cassius Clay, I think 'Cassius Clay' still referred to him at the time and still refers to him now when someone uses it, even though continued use of that name can be a deliberate slight. Reference and propriety strike me as different things.

5. A performer known publicly as Lady Gaga has the birth name Stefani Germanotta. I think 'Lady Gaga' refers to her even in the mouth of someone who knows no other name for her. A single name, with no given/family split, seems to do the whole job of reference.

6. In Chinese naming order the family name precedes the given name: Mao Zedong has the family name Mao and the given name Zedong. I think writing him as 'Zedong Mao' in the usual English order still refers to the same person. Order is a way of encoding which part is which, not itself a difference in reference.

7. A family contains John Smith Sr. and John Smith Jr., and someone who knows both says, with no further disambiguation available, 'John Smith called while you were out.' I am inclined to say this utterance has no determinate referent, even though it uses a complete given-plus-family name. A complete formal name is evidently not always enough to fix who is meant.

8. The son in that family has always been called by a nickname, say 'Brick', which does not encode the junior relation. A neighbour who knows only the son and only as 'Brick' says 'Brick called.' I think this refers to the son determinately. So an arbitrary substitute can secure reference where a complete formal name does not.

My hunch is that the right account separates the name as a whole, which is a rigid label fixed by use and passed along a chain of speakers, from whatever quasi-descriptive content its parts carry under a community's current naming practice. But I cannot state this precisely, and I suspect it may clash with one of my verdicts.

Is there a coherent account of how given names and family names refer, and what each part contributes, that delivers all eight verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why? Please state the account precisely, explain how it handles the contrast between the inert 'Carpenter' and the transparent 'Jónsdóttir', and tell me what it predicts for a case I have not listed, such as a married couple who hyphenate their two surnames into a new two-part surname.
