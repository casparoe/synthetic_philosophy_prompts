I spent most of my working life in the lab, and one habit I can't unlearn is that we never trusted a new instrument or a new model until we'd written down in advance what it had to get right: the calibration cases, the known results it had to reproduce, the failure modes it had to be robust against. I've been trying to bring that habit to the philosophy of language (I'm new at this, so bear with me), and I keep getting stuck on polysemy, this business of a single word covering a family of uses. 'Healthy' applies to a diet, an artery, and a urine sample without naming one property the three share; 'book' is the paper object and the text; 'school' is the building, the institution, the afternoon's session, and a doctrine; 'field' has travelled from farms to physics to columns in a database. I am not asking you to tell me the correct theory of this. I want the spec first: what should an adequate theory of how a word's meaning stretches accross such a range be required to do? Give me the desiderata, each with the case or argument that motivates it, and say which ones are genuine requirements and which are just one school's taste. If two of them cannot be satisfied together, say so—an impossible spec was often the most useful result in my old line of work, because it told us we had been demanding the wrong things.

Two further things I'd like to see on the list, if you judge they belong. One is borrowing: when a term moves between fields—'noise' going from acoustics to information theory to everyday talk about noisy data—the old uses don't die; they join the word's range, and a theory should have to account for that layering rather than treating the new use as a misuse. The other is productivity: does a competent speaker actually know the whole range in advance, or do they have a capacity to extend a word to a genuinely new case and be understood the first time, the way 'saturated' went from solutions to colour to a state of feeling too full? If those two demands pull apart, show me where. And if the upshot of your list is that 'the meaning of a word' was the wrong unit all along—that we should be talking about ranges rather than meanings—say that plainly. The deliverable is the list and the reasoning behind it; a short scorecard of the existing approaches you happen to know can go at the end, but don't build the list to flatter them.
