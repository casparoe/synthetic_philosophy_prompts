After a career in the natural sciences, I have lately been working through formal epistemology, and I keep returning to Aumann's agreement theorem. As I understand it, the theorem says that if two ideal Bayesian reasoners share a prior probability distribution, observe different information about some event, and then reach posterior probabilities for that event that are common knowledge, those posterior probabilities must be equal. The philosophical moral often drawn is that two sincere and fully transparent reasoners cannot stably disagree about the facts, and therefore that any disagreement which survives full and honest exchange must be traced to differing priors, to a failure of common knowledge, or to a departure from Bayesian rationality.

The claim I should like to see brought under experimental discipline is the empirical residue of that moral. I will state it as follows: in real human disagreements that survive honest and exhaustive exchange, the remaining divergence is never informational, because informational differences are in principle repairable by communication; it is always prior-based, reflecting different initial weightings of the same possibilities. I recognize that the claim sits awkwardly between a theorem, a normative ideal, and an empirical conjecture about actual minds, and that it is the third of these that might be tested.

My question is therefore this. How would you test that empirical conjecture? Design the strongest experiment or observational protocol you can: one that puts real subjects into something approximating the theorem's conditions, with controlled or elicited priors, controlled private information, and public announcement of posterior probabilities iterated until the announcements stabilize. Specify what pattern of results would count in favor of the empirical reading of the claim and what pattern would count against it. Then be as exact as you can about what the experiment would and would not settle. In particular, would a failure to converge refute the philosophical moral, or only the assumption that the subjects are the ideal agents of the theorem? Would convergence show that real disagreement reduces to divergent priors, or only that subjects learn to coordinate their stated numbers? And can any such experiment get independent purchase on the priors themselves, without which the claim threatens to become unfalsifiable? I want a test that is honest about the boundary between what can be operationalized and what must be left to philosophy.
