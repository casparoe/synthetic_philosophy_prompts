I work on collective responsibility, and there is a feature of contemporary political life I keep circling back to because it seems to hide a fairly deep puzzle. Many liberal states now issue formal state apologies for wrongs committed well before any present official or citizen was alive: colonial expropriation, transatlantic slavery, the forcible removal of children from indigenous families, wartime internments. Almost always, however, the same states are careful to do two further things. They disclaim or quietly fence off legal liability, with compensation handled separately, precluded, or left to individual programmes, and with the text of the apology drafted so as to create no cause of action. And they insist that no living citizen, and often no current official, is being accused of personal wrongdoing. So the state speaks in the first person plural about wrongdoing it now owns, and it promises that the wrong will not be repeated; yet the state is not the wrongdoer, since the actual wrongdoers are long dead, and it simultaneously denies that present people inherit blame.

I want to know what this practice presupposes about collective responsibility, or about collective agency. On the face of it, apology presupposes that the state is a single agent persisting across centuries, capable of bearing moral predicates — contrition, shame, regret, a commitment not to reoffend — that we standardly apply to persons. But that appearance may outrun what the practice strictly requires. Distinguish what state apology strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Reconstruct the implicit theory of collective responsibility the practice is best read as presupposing; say whether it commits the state to being a group agent of a particular kind, and whether the practice is also compatible with weaker views — for example, that present citizens bear a forward-looking political responsibility for a polity whose benefits they enjoy, or that the apology is better understood as a speech act binding the state to future conduct than as a backward-looking confession. Then assess coherence: can the state be blameworthy for a historical wrong while no present member is blameworthy in virtue of membership? If not, say what has to give. Finally, indicate what the practice would look like under two of the rival theories: which features of present state apologies would survive, and which would have to be dropped or re-described.
