I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my editor increasingly asks me to write pieces that turn on monetary claims: that quantitative easing created wealth, that a country with its own currency can never go bankrupt, that printing money is just a bookkeeping entry. I am not an economist, and I need a reusable editorial procedure I can run in under an hour before I file a draft. The procedure should keep me from falling for slogans, but it should also be honest about where the distinction between money and real wealth gets genuinely uncertain.

Adam Smith's An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Book I, Introduction, opens with this passage: "The annual labour of every nation is the fund which originally supplies it with all the necessaries and conveniencies of life which it annually consumes, and which consist always either in the immediate produce of that labour, or in what is purchased with that produce from other nations."

Please turn the principle in that passage into a checklist I can apply to a claim that a monetary action—printing money, expanding central-bank balance sheets, creating liquidity—made some country or group wealthier. For each question, say what kind of evidence would count as a sufficient answer, what would count as a failure, and where the procedure stops with "not established" rather than "false." Flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure. The procedure should be usable by a journalist without formal economics training, but it should not simplify away the distinctions that do the work.

Then run the procedure on these three cases: (a) a central bank's large bond-buying program and the claim that it created wealth for the nation; (b) a government that printed money to pay off domestic debt and the claim that this made the country richer; (c) a country's discovery of a new source of gold and the claim that this made it wealthier. For each, show what the procedure would conclude and where it would push back. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer that I, and most of my readers, would not like—for example, where a widely celebrated policy turns out not to have created wealth in the sense the passage describes, or where something dismissed as mere money creation actually did increase real produce.

The deliverable is a reusable procedure, not a one-off interpretation of Smith. It should work on next month's policy debate as well as on these three cases.
