Here's an asymmetry I keep tripping over in how we talk about self-binding. Take the old hitchhiker case. I'm stranded somewhere with no ride, and a driver stops and offers to take me home only if I promise to pay her $100 on arrival. She's a decent judge of character and knows that, left to myself, I won't pay once I'm safely home, so a bare promise is worthless to her. I have a pill that will make me, for the next several hours, the kind of person who keeps promises no matter what. I take the pill, promise, get the ride, and pay up when we arrive. Ask around and you'll get two reactions. Almost everyone says taking the pill was clever, maybe the only sensible thing to do. But ask about the payment at the doorstep, considered on its own, and the same people say it's a sucker's move: the ride's over, the driver has no leverage, the hundred dollars is just gone. Same guy, same money, same sequence of events. We admire the arranging and sneer at the executing. The usual moral people draw is that it can be rational to make yourself irrational later; but that just sharpens the puzzle about why the later act still gets called irrational at all.

Is there a real principle that makes both verdicts come out right, or are we just having it both ways? I want the strongest candidate justification you can build — sunk costs, the difference between evaluating an act and a plan, preferences spread across time-slices, whatever does the most work — and I want you to stress-test it against nearby cases. What if the pill merely makes him deeply stubborn rather than a promise-keeper? What if he pays because he's confused and thinks the ride hasn't happened yet? Does your principle sort those cases the way you want? Then give me an honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, scrap it, or revise it. And tell me how confident you are in that verdict, plus the specific step in the argument your least sure of.
