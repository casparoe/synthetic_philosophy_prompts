You are an expert in criminal law theory and the philosophy of criminal law. I direct a law reform unit in a small jurisdiction. Between twenty and forty times a year we draft, or scrutinize before introduction, low- and medium-level regulatory offenses — food-safety, environmental-discharge, workplace-safety, data-breach-reporting, licensing, and transport violations — and we repeatedly deadlock over one drafting choice: whether an offense should require proof of mens rea as to the key elements, require only negligence (a failure to take reasonable preventive measures), or impose strict liability with no proof of culpability as to some element; and, when we move away from full mens rea, whether we should add a due-diligence defense and who should bear the burden of proving it, including the choice between an evidential burden and a reversed legal burden. The fights are unsystematic and are usually settled by seniority, not by the structure of the offense. I want a reusable procedure our drafters can actually follow, one that is defensible in a short memo to a minister and that a non-specialist can apply in an afternoon.

Please write that procedure. It should be grounded in the principle that criminal conviction and punishment should normally require some form of culpability — knowledge, intent, recklessness, or at least negligence — rather than following automatically from a prohibited act or state of affairs. But it should also take seriously the reasons strict and negligence-based liability exist in the regulatory context: the difficulty of proving knowledge, the fact that the person whose conduct falls short is often best placed to prevent the harm, the typically low stigma and non-custodial penalties, and the enforcement costs of full mens rea. The procedure should consist of an ordered sequence of questions, with, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed or to stop; it should say where the procedure ends with a "no"; and it should explicitly flag the steps that are value judgments rather than doctrinal or empirical ones. It should also handle the intermediate positions rather than forcing a binary: strict liability with a due-diligence defense, strict liability with a reversed legal burden, and negligence-based standards. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) an offense for a manufacturer whose food product tests positive for a harmful pathogen, punishable by a fine and possible disqualification from operating; (b) an offense for a mine operator when sediment runs from the site into a protected stream, punishable by a substantial daily fine; and (c) an offense for a company that fails to notify the regulator of a personal-data breach within seventy-two hours, punishable only by a modest fine. I expect the procedure to return at least one answer I will not like, and I want you to say plainly what the procedure cannot settle.

Before committing to a final version, think through and brainstorm different ways of drawing the key distinctions, and consider where a simplified procedure would produce a wrong answer. Then present the procedure as a numbered list of steps, followed by the three worked applications.
