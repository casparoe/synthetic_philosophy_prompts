This has been bugging me since a friend went down a KJV-only rabbit hole online, and I found I couldn't argue with him as cleanly as I expected because my own views seemed to point his direction. The theory I keep sliding toward is a strong translation-is-loss view: a sentence means what it means partly in virtue of the exact words, syntax, and associations of its language, so no translation preserves the original meaning, and every translation is already an interpretation, a new text with a family resemblance to the old one. I like the view because it is principled and gives a tidy answer to a lot of wooly talk about equivalence. But it has the neat, uncomfortable consequence that a translated sacred text cannot inherit whatever property made the original inspired or revealed, at least not in virtue of wording. And yet I read the Bible in English and I can't shake the conviction that God speaks through it; that feels as solid to me as anything I believe. The tradition is on my side here too—Greek-speaking Jews and the early church treated the Septuagint as Scripture rather than as a stopgap, and KJV-onlyists just push that move all the way by claiming the English translation is itself inspired.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want you to present both sides at their strongest and then give me a negotiated verdict: what would it actually take for the intuition to be debunked as a habit of my upbringing and dropped, what would it take for the translation-is-loss theory to be amended or restricted, and whether there is a reading of inspired on which both survive—for example, inspiration located in the message that survives translation, in the target-language text as a fresh act of revelation, or in the receiving community. If the theory has to bend, I want to know whether it can bend without gerrymandering: why would the Septuagint or the King James Version deserve an exception that my friend's new translation doesn't, and what principled difference could do that work?
