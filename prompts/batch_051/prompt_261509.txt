I keep running into people—mostly economists and a few psychologists—who talk as if weakness of will just *is* hyperbolic discounting, or at least as if the latter gives you a good model of the former. I'd like to get clearer on this than I am. Can you explain the conceptual relationship between akrasia in the strict philosophical sense and hyperbolic discounting as it's used in the intertemporal choice literature? In particular: does hyperbolic discounting actually model an agent who judges that she ought, all things considered, to do A, and then does B anyway? Or does it model something adjacent but different—preference reversal, internal conflict between a long-run and a short-run self, or just a shift in which option the agent wants most as the smaller-sooner reward gets close? Where do the two notions genuinely line up, and where does one outrun the other? I care less about the empirical evidence for one discount function over another than about whether the formal machinery can even represent the thing philosophers mean by akrasia. Don't just summarize each topic seperately—I want to know exactly what the discount function can and can't say about the agent's own normative judgment. And please write the answer in American English.
