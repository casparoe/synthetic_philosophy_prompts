I have been working through Goodman's new riddle of induction, and I keep arriving at an argument that seems to show that no empirical prediction is better than any other. I do not want to accept that conclusion, but I cannot determine where the reasoning goes wrong.

Let t be 1 January 2050. Call an emerald grue just in case either it is first examined before t and is green, or it is first examined on or after t and is blue. Now consider these steps. (1) Every emerald first examined so far has been green. (2) Every emerald first examined so far has also been grue, since each was first examined before t and found green. (3) The evidence therefore supports the generalization that all emeralds are grue at least as well as it supports the generalization that all emeralds are green. (4) When evidence supports a universal generalization, it equally supports the predictions that generalization makes about the next unexamined case. (5) The green generalization predicts that the next emerald first examined after t will be green, whereas the grue generalization predicts that it will be blue. (6) So the same evidence equally supports two contradictory predictions about that emerald. (7) Because a grue-style predicate can be constructed for any observed regularity and any future case, this reasoning generalizes: any prediction and its denial can be made to look equally supported. Therefore no prediction is better justified than any other, which seems to prove far too much.
