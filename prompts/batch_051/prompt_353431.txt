In my biology class we have been taught that the eye works like a camera: the lens bends light onto the retina, which acts like a sensor, and the brain then develops the image. A classmate has been leaning on this analogy to defend a strong conclusion. Because the eye is, on this picture, a detect-and-record instrument with no opinions, he argues that what you see cannot be affected by what you believe, expect, or care about. On his view, two people looking at the same scene in the same conditions literally see the same thing, and they can differ only later, in how they interpret or describe it. He applies this in arguments: a referee who supports one team cannot really see a foul differently from a neutral, and a witness who expected the defendant to be holding a gun cannot really have seen one, so any such report must be bias in the judgment rather than in the seeing itself. A friend of mine says this is precisely the picture that work on the cognitive penetration of perception calls into question: states like expectations and motives can reach down and alter the perceptual experience itself, not merely the interpretation added afterward. I want to know how far the camera analogy can carry my classmate's conclusion. Please give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the lens, the aperture, the sensor, the exposure settings, the camera's lack of beliefs, and so on. Then set out the disanalogies. For each disanalogy, say whether it merely shows that the eye is a different kind of instrument or whether it actually threatens the conclusion he draws. Finally, rule on that conclusion: does it survive the disanalogies, and if not, what is a better analogy for perception that captures what the camera misses?
