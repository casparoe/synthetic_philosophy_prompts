Some colleagues and I run an interdisciplinary reading group on political economy and ethics, and one question has stalled so completely that I have begun to suspect the real problem is the structure of the disagreement rather than any shortage of arguments. The question is whether material prosperity has moral worth beyond what it buys. Both sides accept the familiar observation that, beyond a certain level of material security, additional consumption shows diminishing returns to well-being. They even agree that above some threshold, further consumed goods contribute less and less to anyone's welfare. Where they part company is over whether that settles anything.

The first camp — call them consumption-accounting — holds that the moral value of a society's prosperity is exhausted by the welfare-relevant states it funds: health, longevity, leisure, security, consumption, and the like. On this view, abundance that is never converted into any such state has no residue of value; since producing it used real resources and imposed real costs, it may even be morally negative. They regard talk of the value of abundance "in itself" as a form of GDP fetishism, or as a disguised argument about something else such as inequality or the security of property.

The second camp — call them abundance-conditions — denies that this captures what a standing material surplus does. They argue that abundance can be valuable as a background condition of a life, not only as a stream of purchased benefits. A continuously replenished reserve can underwrite resilience against shocks, enable risk-taking, free people from dependence on employers or patrons, and make generosity a live option. They claim two communities could have identical measured welfare over a decade, while the one holding a largely undrawn reserve is nonetheless in a morally better condition, because the reserve changes which social relations and possibilities are genuinely open to its members.

The impasse recurs in the same shape each time. The consumption-accounting side challenges: point to a benefit, experienced by some person, that the unused reserve actually causes. The abundance-conditions side replies that a benefit need not be drawn down to be real — any more than a bill of rights has to be invoked every day to be doing its work.

My working hypothesis is that "does prosperity have moral worth beyond what it buys?" is at least two questions running under one heading. One may be verbal: whether a value has to be a state of a person in order to count as a benefit at all. Another may be empirical: whether unused abundance actually does alter social relations and temporal horizons the way the abundance-conditions camp claims. But I suspect the deepest version is evaluative — whether welfare is the only bearer of moral value, or whether the standing conditions under which people live can have value apart from the welfare those conditions produce.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on the two camps. Is this disagreement verbal or substantive? Are the sides answering different questions under one name — one about the value of a society's outputs, the other about the value of its standing arrangements — or are they divided over a hidden empirical question, over which cases count as data, or over bedrock evaluative commitments? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, an empirical study, a formal result, a reformulated question — or an argument that no progress should be expected, and why?
