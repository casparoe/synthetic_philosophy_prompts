Source material, as summarized for students: W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley, in their essay "The Intentional Fallacy," distinguish between internal evidence for a poem's meaning—evidence from the words, syntax, grammars, dictionaries, and shared literary and cultural conventions—and external evidence—evidence from the poet's private life: journals, letters, drafts, and reported conversations about how and why the poem was written.

Question: Reconstruct Wimsatt and Beardsley's argument that internal evidence, rather than external evidence, is the proper basis for determining a poem's meaning. Then evaluate this objection: if a poet's intention is what fixes what the words of a text mean as used on the occasion of writing, then excluding intention makes interpretation impossible. How would Wimsatt and Beardsley respond?

Student answer: Wimsatt and Beardsley distinguish between evidence drawn from the poem itself—its words, syntax, and shared literary and cultural conventions—and evidence drawn from the poet's private life, such as letters, diaries, and drafts. They call the former internal evidence and the latter external evidence. They argue that only internal evidence is relevant to determining the poem's meaning because a poem, once written, is detached from its author and belongs to the public. They also say that the author's intention is neither available nor desirable as a standard for judging a work.

This distinction helps them answer the objection. If a poet intends to use a word with a private meaning, that private meaning is external evidence and cannot count as part of the poem's meaning. The words mean what the public language and the internal context of the poem allow. Therefore, interpretation does not require recovering the poet's intention; it requires attending to the poem's public features. The objection fails because it assumes that meaning is fixed by an inner mental act, but for Wimsatt and Beardsley meaning is fixed by the public linguistic conventions that the poem itself embodies.

I think Wimsatt and Beardsley overstate their case. They sometimes write as if external evidence is completely useless for interpretation. But a letter in which a poet explains an allusion can help a reader see a connection that was already in the poem's public structure. So external evidence can point us toward internal evidence even if it cannot by itself authorize a meaning. This is a friendly amendment.
