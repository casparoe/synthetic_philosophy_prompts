I have been turning over a parallel and cannot decide whether it is deep or superficial.

In formal models of knowledge, agents are standardly treated as knowing every logical consequence of what they know. This assumption of logical omniscience is not offered as a description of real people; it is accepted because it keeps the models tractable and because the point of the exercise is to study what follows from a body of information rather than the limits of human inference. The law makes an analogous-looking move. A party can be held to have constructive knowledge of facts she could have discovered but never actually did — a director who signs off accounts without reading them may be deemed to know their contents — and under the rule that ignorance of the law is no excuse, a person may be treated as knowing a rule she never formed any belief about. Here too, what a diligent party would have known is substituted for what this party actually knew, and the substitution is defended on institutional grounds: administrability, incentives to find out, and fairness between those who inquire and those who do not.

The structural claim I keep wanting to make is that both are the same kind of idealisation: an epistemic state is ascribed that outruns the subject's actual mental states, and the ascription is justified by the purpose of the practice rather than by psychological realism. But I worry the analogy does little work, since one idealisation is a modelling convenience in a formal theory while the other is a liability rule with direct consequences for actual people. Please examine where the two cases genuinely match and where they come apart. In particular, what would have to be true of both for criticisms of one to transfer to the other, and is there a prediction the parallel suggests that turns out false?
