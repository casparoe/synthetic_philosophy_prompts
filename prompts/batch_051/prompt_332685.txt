I teach an undergraduate seminar on collective decision-making and want to run a 75-minute game for about 25 students on the Condorcet jury theorem. The thing I want them to feel before it is named is the contrast between a majority of independent but barely-competent voters, which can be strikingly reliable, and a majority whose judgments share a common source, which can be confidently wrong. Design the game with concrete rules, cheap materials, and a points scheme that works without real money. Include at least two contrasting rounds: one where each student receives a private, independently generated, slightly-better-than-chance clue about a binary choice, and one where everyone receives the same clue, including at least one trial where that shared clue is misleading. The insight should emerge from the play and the scoring rather than from any explanation given before the debrief. Then write a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the theorem's competence, independence, and sincerity conditions, and be explicit about what no classroom game can reproduce about the theorem and the facilitator must supply. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Finally, identify the design choice you are least confident in and tell me what outcome from a trial run would make you revise that choice.
