I have been working on a distinction I call "temporal jurisdiction," and I want an honest assessment of it: whether it is coherent, whether it already exists under another name, what it gets right, and where it breaks.

The target is the standard treatment of weakness of will in terms of hyperbolic or quasi-hyperbolic discounting. On that treatment, the weak-willed agent has a steep discount function: as a smaller-sooner reward becomes imminent, its present value overtakes the present value of a larger-later reward, so the agent defects from an earlier resolution. I think this misdescribes many paradigm cases.

Here is my proposal. When an agent considers a trade-off between a present good and a future harm—dieting versus dessert—their current choice does not simply apply a discount factor to the future harm. It first settles a question of temporal jurisdiction: which future selves count as the same person for the purposes of this decision. A resolution is made in a cool state with broad jurisdiction: the later self who will face the health consequences is recognized as me, so its costs enter as my own costs now. As the tempting option approaches, what shifts is not primarily the discount rate but the jurisdiction. The present self contracts the boundary of who counts as me so that the future self bearing the consequences is now treated more like another person: its costs figure as claims to be weighed, not as losses of my own. The agent eats the dessert, not because health is valued less, but because the future self who would value it has been temporarily disenfranchised.

I therefore want to decompose the discount function into two components. The first is long-run impatience: how much less a delayed good matters merely because it is delayed, holding identity fixed. The second is a jurisdiction coefficient j(d)—the degree to which the self at delay d is treated as me for the purposes of the present choice. My central claim is that much ordinary akrasia is not excessive impatience but volatility in j(d): it is high when the tempting object is abstract, low when it is vivid, and its movements do not track anything about actual continuity of the person over time.

Two pieces of evidence drive this. First, in typical akratic episodes the agent does not behave like someone whose discount curve has steepened; the agent behaves like someone who has stopped regarding the future bearer of the cost as a full partner in the decision—saying, in effect, that the consequences are a problem for tomorrow's self, while being genuinely puzzled by, and even contemptuous of, their own earlier resolution. Second, the view makes a prediction that standard discounting does not obviously make: interventions that raise perceived continuity with the future self—age-progressed avatars, letters to one's later self, commitments framed in the first person plural across time—should reduce akratic choice without changing any temporal distance or payoff, because they raise j(d). Standard hyperbolic discounting has no natural place for such an effect, since the delays and magnitudes are unchanged.

Now my questions. Is the distinction between long-run impatience and volatile temporal jurisdiction a real one, or is it just present bias rewritten in the vocabulary of temporal selves? Has this been worked out already, and if so under what name? And where does it break? I worry, in particular, that the view predicts that someone with an unshakeable conviction of their own identity over time could not be weak-willed, which seems false; but I cannot tell whether that is a fatal counterexample or a boundary condition that can be fixed without giving up the central claim.
