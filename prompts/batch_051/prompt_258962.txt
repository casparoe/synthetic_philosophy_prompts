In his essay The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity, Daniel Dennett develops a picture of the self on an analogy with a physical center of gravity. A center of gravity is not a material piece of a body; it is an abstract point that nevertheless does real explanatory and predictive work. Extending the analogy, Dennett argues that the self is likewise an abstraction generated by the narrative activity of a human organism—a stable, useful posit for interpreting and anticipating behavior, not a further substantive ingredient inside the organism. The theory has strong systematic virtues: it accounts for the apparent unity of experience without a Cartesian theater, it makes sense of confabulation and of selves that seem to split or multiply, and it does so within a thoroughly naturalistic ontology.

I find this view compelling on methodological grounds. But I cannot shake a firm intuition. When I am in severe pain, or when I am torn between two incompatible choices, it seems to me that there is something—an actual subject or agent—that is enduring the pain or doing the deciding. The narrative-gravity view seems to explain this something away rather than explain it. The intuition is not merely that Dennett's account is surprising, but that it leaves out the very phenomenon that matters most to me when I anticipate my own future or hold myself responsible for what I did.

Give me the strongest version of each side and a negotiated verdict. First, make the strongest case that this intuition should be debunked: explain how narrative practice and the first-person point of view could produce it, why the physical analogy is apt rather than misleading, and what would make it reasonable to set the intuition aside. Second, make the strongest case that the intuition is tracking something the theory omits—whether the unified biological organization of a living organism, the perspectival character of experience, or a thicker form of agency—and that the theory therefore needs amendment. Then address the questions directly. Is there a reading of Dennett's position on which the theory and the intuition are compatible, or does the force of the position lie precisely in asking us to abandon the intuition? If you recommend yielding in one direction, say what further evidence or argument would be enough to change your recommendation. Which should yield, the theory or the intuition, and why?
