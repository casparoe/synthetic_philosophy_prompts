A friend of mine has been reading about orphan works and abandonware, and he's built an argument that he says shows that once the owner is gone, a thing is just up for grabs. He wrote it out like this:

(1) If you use a thing and there is no identifiable owner whose permission you could get, then you haven't violated anyone's rights.
(2) If you haven't violated anyone's rights, then what you did was permissible.
(3) Orphan works and abandoned software are exactly cases where there's no identifiable owner: the author died without traceable heirs, the company dissolved without assigning the copyright, or the rights holder has gone dark and no registry points to a living person.
(4) So using orphan works and abandoned software violates no one's rights and is permissible.
(5) But the same reasoning covers a lost wallet with no ID in it, or a ring dropped on a trail: the finder has no identifiable owner to ask, so by (1) and (2) the finder gets to keep the cash or the ring.
(6) He says he'll bite that bullet — finders keepers — but I won't. I still own my wallet even if the person who finds it can't figure out who I am.
(7) So something in the first two steps has to give. I'd like to reject whichever premise is doing the sneaky work while still leaving it open that orphan works are different from my wallet. Which step is it, and what does rejecting it cost for the conclusion about abandoned works?
