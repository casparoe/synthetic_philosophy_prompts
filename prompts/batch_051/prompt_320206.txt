I lead a film-and-philosophy reading group, and this term we are pairing Ingmar Bergman's The Seventh Seal with philosophical work on games as art and the aesthetics of agency. I would like a critical reading of the film that treats it as more than an illustration. The central question is whether the film presents Antonius Block's chess match with Death as a domain of genuinely meaningful agency — the kind some philosophers claim a game's rules can create — or as a choreographed delay that keeps the knight negotiating a loss that was settled the moment the board was set. Do the conceptual work instead of merely gesturing at it: distinguish the lusory attitude from wanting to win; distinguish trying to win from trying to play well; spell out how accepting constraints that would otherwise be pointless can generate a form of action worth inhabiting for its own sake. Use those distinctions to explain why Block proposes the game, why he returns to it across the film instead of fleeing, and why his one consequential accomplishment — knocking over the pieces to distract Death so that Jof, Mia, and their child can escape — is a byproduct of the game rather than an outcome within it. Then say where the film resists this framework. Death is not a co-player bound by the same stakes; he is the house, and the house always wins. Block surrenders the decisive information in something closer to a confession than a move. And the film's image of the good life appears to reside less in the contest than in the strawberries and milk Block shares with the actor's family, in being present to a world he has spent the film trying to outwit. I want an honest verdict on whether The Seventh Seal dramatizes the aesthetics of agency, undermines it, or does both at once — and if it is ambivalent, I want that ambivalence made precise, with an account of what the film's double vision adds to the philosophical argument that a paper alone cannot supply.
