So me and my friend spent a long night arguing about whether a group can run itself entirely by its own written rules—nothing outside the text, no custom, no court, no appeal to the spirit of the thing. He says yes, and he wants our twenty-person housing co-op to adopt this as the whole constitution:

1. Any member may move to add, delete, or alter any rule at a general meeting. It passes by two-thirds of members present, as long as at least half the membership is there, and it takes effect immediatly, before the meeting moves on.
2. Rule 1 applies to every rule, including itself. No rule is protected.
3. If two rules conflict, the later-adopted one wins.
4. Anything not governed by a rule is decided by simple majority.

We'd run everything through it: rooms, dues, chores, shared budget. I say it eats itself within months—someone passes a rule saying this rule can only be changed by unanimous vote, or that no further amendments may be proposed, or something self-referential that makes the book inconsistent. He says clause 3 handles all that.

Act as an expert on legal philosophy and mechanism design, think carefully, and maybe start by brianstorming the first half-dozen ways members would game it or accidentally break it. I want the actual trajectory over a year or two, not a verdict on democracy: what gets proposed first, who ends up with power, what happens when rule 1 is applied to itself, whether anything can ever be made to stick beyond the next meeting, where the paradoxes bite, and whether any fix works without smuggling in an outside enforcer.
