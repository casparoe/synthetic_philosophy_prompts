I'm putting together a small collection of stories inspired by philosophy of mind, and I'd like one on the extended mind idea: the claim that a part of the world can count as part of a person's mind when it's reliably available and trusted the way they trust their own memory. But I don't want a story where a character explains the idea; it should come through in what happens.

The thing I want the story to worry at is the line between "my stuff broke" and "part of my mind was harmed." Build it around a character whose cognitive life is partly external — a notebook, a phone, a shared document, a colleague who remembers things for them — and then let that external part get edited, inherited, synced, sold, or slowly degrade, in a way someone's own memory could too (memories get rewritten by other people's accounts, or just fade). Let the reader feel why the distinction matters, and don't tie it up with a clear answer at the end; leave the boundary genuinely unsettled.

One constraint: don't cheat by making the external thing just handy or conveniant. It has to be as constant and taken for granted as biological memory for this character. And keep the philosophy out of the characters' mouths — no "extended mind" or "parity principle" in the dialogue or narration. Write it in British English.
