I help draft public statements for a small human-rights organization. After a damaging internal argument, I adopted a policy for live or ongoing situations: we never call something "genocide" unless a competent international court has issued a finding that the crime has occurred; before that, we write "mass atrocities" or "mass killing." (For settled historical cases, I am willing to rely on scholarly consensus.) My rationale is that genocide is a legal category requiring a specific intent to destroy a protected group as such, something almost impossible to establish in real time, and that the word functions as a moral switch: once applied, it restructures what responses count as proportionate or permissible. Lately I have begun to suspect the policy is self-defeating. It commits us to maximum reticence exactly where the label would matter most, and it treats a court ruling as neutral factual certification when courts are slow, proof-bound, political, and sometimes absent. I want a committed verdict rather than a menu: is this policy defensible as it stands, or is it a bias — perhaps a confusion of legal verdicts with factual or moral description — dressed up as a rule? If a defensible version exists, state it precisely and say what it would name, and when.
