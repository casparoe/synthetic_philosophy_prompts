I am a journalist covering the intersection of economics and information technology, and I am working on a piece about what markets can and cannot know. I keep circling back to a passage from Friedrich Hayek’s 1945 article “The Use of Knowledge in Society,” where he writes: “The peculiar character of the problem of a rational economic order is determined precisely by the fact that the knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess.” Hayek’s argument was built for an era in which the bearers of that dispersed knowledge were human economic actors: a farmer who sees a frost threaten his crop, a factory foreman who notices a machine wearing down, a local merchant who hears a rumor about a supplier. Today, however, a growing share of trading in financial markets is executed by algorithms, and prediction markets with automated market makers quote odds continuously on everything from elections to scientific results. The Grossman-Stiglitz theorem, in its usual form, says that informationally efficient markets are self-undermining: if prices already reflected all available information, no trader would pay the cost of acquiring information, and so no one would trade on it. But that result presupposes that information acquisition is costly for the traders who would act on it. Suppose instead that automated traders can acquire much of the relevant information at near-zero marginal cost through scraping, sensors, or shared data feeds. Does the Hayekian epistemology of markets survive this shift? In particular, does Hayek’s argument require that the dispersed knowledge be lodged in separate human minds with separate local standpoints, or can the knowledge-equivalently be dispersed across algorithms and data feeds without destroying the epistemic role of prices? I want you to work through whether Hayek’s argument survives the combination of algorithmic trading and near-zero-cost information acquisition, and to identify exactly where, if anywhere, the no-trade paradox still bites. I am less interested in a policy conclusion than in knowing which premise of Hayek’s argument the new circumstances put under pressure, and whether a repaired version of the argument can still say something true about the relation between prices and knowledge.
