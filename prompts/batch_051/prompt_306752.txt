I'm a machine learning engineer with a working vocabulary in loss functions, regularization, constrained optimization, precision/recall trade-offs, reward hacking, and distribution shift, but no formal training in moral philosophy. Last weekend I got into a long argument with a friend who is convinced that suffering has a special moral priority over happiness. His position, as best I could pin it down, was a threshold version of negative utilitarianism: the reduction of suffering, and especially extreme suffering, is the dominant moral aim; ordinary happiness can justify tolerating mild discomfort, but beyond some threshold of intensity no amount of well-being, achievement, or pleasure can compensate for a single instance of agonizing pain; and the absence of suffering is good in itself, even where there is no particular person who enjoys that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is bad only insofar as someone is deprived of it. He also deflected the standard objections in ways I found slippery: when I raised the familiar worry that this makes painlessly destroying all sentient life a moral improvement, he insisted that was confused because nonexistence is not an experience and cannot be the sort of thing a loss function could favor.

I kept wanting to translate his claims into the language I actually think in. A threshold negative utilitarian sounds, on the face of it, like someone optimizing an asymmetric loss function: penalize negative welfare much harder than you reward positive welfare, impose a hard constraint that no state assigns an unacceptable cost to any individual, and optimize lexicographically by driving violations of the constraint to zero before trading off anything else. But the analogy is doing a lot of work, and I suspect it silently imports assumptions from how machine learning treats a fixed dataset, a fixed task, and a reward signal defined over whatever examples happen to be present. I want a disciplined translation, not a loose analogy.

Please restate the negative utilitarian position — the weak, classical, and threshold or lexical versions, along with the main objections to each (the world-destruction objection, the tranquilized-world or wireheading objection, and the charge that the asymmetry between absent pain and absent pleasure is merely asserted) — in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in how machine learning engineers formalize losses and constraints. In particular, tell me whether the world-destruction objection is the equivalent of a degenerate solution that minimizes loss by deleting the test set or corrupting the labels, whether the asymmetry about absence can be stated at all once the population is treated as an exogenous variable, and whether the residual untranslatable parts reveal a genuine blind spot in the ML vocabulary or a place where the philosophical position is less precise than it pretends to be. I want to know whether my vocabulary is flattening a distinction that matters, or whether it is exposing that my friend's view only looks coherent because natural language lets him defer the hard modeling choices.
