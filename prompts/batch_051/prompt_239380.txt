I work in social choice and democratic theory, and an asymmetry in how we evaluate strategic behavior keeps bothering me. A voter who ranks a lesser evil first rather than her sincere top choice is often accused of corrupting the vote, while a legislator or committee member who misrepresents a ranking to steer a collective outcome is simply doing politics. Yet Gibbard–Satterthwaite showed that no non-dictatorial rule for choosing a single winner from three or more candidates is strategy-proof: every such rule is manipulable somewhere, so honest preference aggregation was never a stable possibility in either setting. Does anything justify the asymmetry, or should we give it up? Develop the strongest principle that could underwrite the difference — role, equality of influence, expectations, the purpose of the aggregation — test it against nearby cases such as a jury or a shareholder vote, and say whether you would keep, narrow, or reject the asymmetry.
