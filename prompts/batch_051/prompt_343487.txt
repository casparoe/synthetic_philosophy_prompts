The argument concerns the idea that some philosophers call free-floating rationales: reasons for the persistence of a structure, trait, or practice that do explanatory work without being any individual's reasons. I find such explanations indispensable, which is why the argument disturbs me.

(1) At least some free-floating rationales are literal, not metaphorical. Suppose a finch population on an island endures a drought in which only hard seeds remain. The reason a particular beak variant persisted is that it cracked those hard seeds. This was the reason the variant persisted even though no individual finch represented that consideration.

(2) If free-floating rationales were merely metaphorical, the explanatory work they appear to do could be replaced without loss by a purely mechanical description of the same events. But it cannot be so replaced: citing the function of the beak explains why the variant persisted, not merely how it propagated. So the rationale is literal.

(3) What makes a contribution literally the reason for a structure's persistence is that a selection process retained the structure because of that contribution. The contribution explains the retention.

(4) This structure — a process retaining a variant in virtue of its consequences — is not peculiar to natural selection. Any system with variation, retention, and differential retention in virtue of consequences has the same structure: a market retaining a price convention, a legal system retaining a doctrine, operant conditioning, an optimization algorithm, and physical self-organization.

(5) Literal free-floating reasons should therefore appear in all these systems. The reason a river retains its meandering planform is that meanders dissipate energy more evenly downstream; the reason a trained model retains a shortcut is that the shortcut lowers the loss function.

(6) If being retained in virtue of one's consequences is sufficient for there being a literal reason for one's persistence, then any pattern whose persistence is maintained by feedback has a reason.

(7) But being maintained by feedback is a way of being caused to persist. A meander is caused to persist by the routing of water and sediment, and the feedback description is a description of that causal routing.

(8) So the defender of literal free-floating rationales is committed to there being no difference between being caused to persist and being there for a reason.

(9) Yet that distinction is the point of the concept of a reason. Reasons are considerations one can weigh, act on, or be criticized for ignoring; causes merely happen. If the distinction collapses, the concept of a reason collapses with it.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd, and I would pay a great deal to avoid it. But I also find steps 1 and 2 very difficult to abandon; the biological and geological examples will not go back into the bottle.

Where does the argument go wrong? Which step, or which transition, should I reject? What made it look sound, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a way to keep free-floating rationales literal without licensing reasons for rivers, or must I choose between the two? If a choice is unavoidable, where is the least damaging line to draw, and what becomes of the examples on the other side of it?
