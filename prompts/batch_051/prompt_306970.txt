I spent my career in an experimental science, where no instrument reading mattered until an independent group had reproduced it. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mathematics, and I keep circling back to a puzzle: proof is supposed to be the one place where a result certifies itself, yet mathematicians have for decades accepted proofs that no single person can survey, from the computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem onward. Suppose a consortium of mathematics journals adopted the following mechanism for such proofs.

A paper whose proof depends on a computation too large for one referee to check by hand must be accompanied by a verification package: the full source code, the input configurations, the output logs, and a machine-checkable formalization in a proof assistant such as Lean or Coq that connects the computation to the theorem. The journal holds the package in escrow for ten years. The authors also post a corrigibility bond, scaled to the number of machine-hours the computation required and the size of the largest case analysis no referee could survey. The pooled bonds fund annual audits: teams selected by lottery test randomly selected escrowed packages, trying to break the proof. A team can expose a genuine gap either by finding a computational error or by showing that the machine-checked statement does not actually express the published theorem. A team that succeeds keeps a fixed fraction of the bond; the remainder goes to the journal's open-access fund; the paper is annotated with the finding; and a paper that survives three clean audits is marked certified, with the authors' bond returned with interest.

I am not asking you to judge whether the scheme is ultimately good. I want to know how it would behave. Walk through the incentives it creates for authors, for the audit teams, for the journals, and for the people who build and maintain proof assistants; where it gets gamed first; what second-order effects it has on which theorems are attacked, which journals computational work flows toward, and what the word "proved" comes to mean; whether the bond can be priced at all given that the cost of finding a flaw is unknown; and which failure modes, if any, could be repaired without gutting the mechanism.
