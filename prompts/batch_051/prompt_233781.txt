W. V. Quine's later writings on ethics are resolutely naturalistic, yet he is conspicuously deflationary about moral value itself: he treats evaluative language as something human beings have been conditioned and socially trained into, a product of evolution and culture rather than a region of fact that the natural sciences could in principle map. Imagine Quine were alive today and asked to assess the contemporary project of building artificial moral reasoners, systems trained on very large corpora of human discourse and on human pairwise judgements about particular cases, which are then consulted as arbiters, or at least advisers, on novel moral questions. Work out Quine's likely verdict. Would he say such a system could be more objective than any human moralist, because it merely systematises the totality of observed human valuations without the distortions of individual sentiment? Or would his own commitments pull in opposite directions here, with his science-friendly naturalism suggesting one answer and his account of moral language as inculcated training suggesting another? Be specific about where he would locate the promise and the error: in the choice of the training target, in the assumption that the moral point of a case is the kind of thing a statistical summary of human responses could capture, or in the move from what people do judge to what anyone ought to do. Distinguish what Quine's recorded views settle from what they leave open, and say clearly when you are extending him rather than reporting him.
