I've spent a long time reading rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent forums, and one argument I keep seeing, in slightly different forms, claims that the usual environmentalist reaction to habitat loss gets welfare backwards: because wild animal lives are mostly miserable, reducing the number of wild animals is not a cost but a benefit. I recently saw someone in a discussion about deforestation and insect welfare give a cleaned-up version of the argument, and I want to know whether it actually goes through. It runs as follows:

(1) For the overwhelming majority of wild animals, expected lifetime welfare is negative; early death from predation, starvation, disease, and exposure outweighs whatever positive experience the animal has.
(2) Habitat destruction reduces the number of wild animals that will come into existence in the future.
(3) If a possible being's life would have been net-negative on the whole, then preventing that being from coming into existence is, all else equal, an improvement from an impartial welfare perspective.
(4) Therefore, bracketing knock-on effects such as climate and ecosystem services, the future-life-reducing effect of habitat destruction is positively good, not merely neutral.
(5) So, from a suffering-focused perspective, the standard environmentalist opposition to habitat destruction is at least partly misguided.

I have the nagging sense that something goes wrong in the move from a claim about welfare within a life to a claim about the value of creating or not creating lives, and also in the slide from one effect being good in isolation to the overall evaluation of habitat destruction, but I cannot state either objection cleanly. Please go through the argument step by step. For each premise, say whether it is true, false, or ambiguous between importantly different readings; check whether the argument is valid; identify any suppressed premises or equivocations; and in particular tell me whether the conclusion survives once the immediate suffering caused by the destruction itself, and the welfare of whatever animals replace the displaced ones, are brought back into the picture.
