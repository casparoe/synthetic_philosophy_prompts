I’m a high school student reading some excerpts from Thomas Reid’s Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man for a class, and I’m stuck on a particular objection he makes to Locke’s account of personal identity. As I understand it, Locke holds that personal identity consists in consciousness extending backward, so that a person is the same self as an earlier being insofar as he can remember, or is conscious of, that being’s thoughts and actions. Reid’s example, as our textbook paraphrases it, is something like this: a man is flogged as a boy for stealing from an orchard; later, as a young officer, he takes an enemy standard in his first battle; still later, as an older general, he remembers taking the standard but no longer remembers the flogging. According to the memory criterion, the general is the same person as the brave officer because he remembers the officer’s action, but he is not the same person as the boy because he has lost all memory of the flogging. Yet if the officer is the same person as the boy, and the general is the same person as the officer, then the general must be the same person as the boy. Reid seems to think this shows that Locke’s criterion makes personal identity both hold and fail between the same two persons, and is therefore impossible.

I don’t want a summary of the whole debate; I want help with the exact structure of the objection. Is the contradiction supposed to follow from Locke’s own principles, or does it depend on a further principle about identity that Locke might not accept? For example, could a Locke defender deny that the relation of being the same person is transitive, or distinguish between the boy’s consciousness and the officer’s consciousness in a way that blocks Reid’s conclusion? Or is the real issue that Locke has confused something like first-person access to identity with identity itself, and Reid is pointing to a deeper problem? I’d like you to walk me through the strongest version of Reid’s argument and the strongest Lockean reply in your own words, so I can see exactly where they part ways.
