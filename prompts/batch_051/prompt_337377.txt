Next week you are giving a joint biology–philosophy of biology seminar. Your thesis, stated in its strongest form, is that the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique succeeded as a diagnosis of just-so storytelling but not as an alternative research program: once adaptationism is understood as the method of building optimality models and then deliberately failing them against explicitly specified non-adaptive alternatives (developmental constraint, phylogenetic inertia, drift, correlated byproduct, exaptation), it is the most productive discovery procedure for those very alternatives, so the critique is best read as a prescription for better adaptationism rather than as a rival framework. In the talk you will have conceded that the critique accurately identified a real failure mode in much 1970s work, especially in human behavioral biology, and that many practitioners treated fit-to-model as success.

Your audience is not a neutral one. You expect: (1) two senior functional morphologists sympathetic to strong adaptationism, who think the spandrels critique attacked a strawman and that your framing legitimizes fashionable constraint-mongering; (2) three evolutionary developmental biologists formed by Gould, who will treat any claim that the critique has been absorbed as a containment strategy and will press you on variational bias, developmental correlations, and exaptation as primary causes; (3) a philosopher of biology who works on null models in selectionist explanations and will worry about how to choose the right null model; and (4) a cluster of graduate students whose default is pluralism and who want to know what changes in their own workflow if you are right.

Prepare me for the question period as a prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Give me the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased as those specific audience members would phrase them, not as a generic list of worries. For each objection, state which part of my thesis it targets, whether it has real force, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, the short reply I should give in about two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. Include at least one empirical objection that names a trait and at least one methodological objection about null models. End with the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and what to say if it comes up anyway. Lead with the bottom line: one sentence that states my defensive position in the strongest form that survives your analysis, then give the prep sheet itself, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
