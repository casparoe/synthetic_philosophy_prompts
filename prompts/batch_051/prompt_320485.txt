Suppose that, starting with laws enacted in the 2031–32 session, California is governed by the following new constitutional rule. California's nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office must certify, for every bill sent to the governor, whether it would increase General Fund spending by more than 0.5% of projected General Fund revenue in any single future fiscal year. If so, the bill is automatically stayed and placed on the next general-election ballot as a binding yes/no item on the spending itself; it takes effect only if a majority of voters approve the spending. There is no signature requirement, no legislative override, and no urgency-clause exemption (that is, the legislature cannot declare the bill urgent and make it take effect immediately). Bills below the trigger proceed normally.

Give me a reasoned prediction of how California's politics and policy develop over the following twenty years, not a story. Work through the mechanisms and incentives at each step, distinguishing robust effects from guesses, and pay particular attention to second-order effects such as avoidance, burden-shifting, and selection for ballot-ready bills. Cover at least:

(1) How legislators change the way they draft, package, time, and label spending bills to stay under or around the trigger, including the likely spillover into special funds, fees, tax expenditures, local government, and borrowing.
(2) Which kinds of spending survive direct public votes and which are quietly redesigned, delayed, or abandoned.
(3) How the initiative industry, labor and business groups, and governors adapt now that some referendums are scheduled automatically rather than through paid signature gathering.
(4) How voters actually decide these mandatory spending items, based on what we already know about California ballot behavior on bonds, taxes, and occasional veto referendums.
(5) Whether the rule achieves what its designers presumably intended, or instead gets routed around, shifts costs onto less visible instruments, produces worse policy, or entrenches incumbents.

Finally, state which of your predictions you would defend even under a rather different political culture, which lean on assumptions about California's specific electorate and organized interests, and what evidence from the first three or four ballot cycles would make you conclude that the reform is operating against its intended effect.
