The task is to produce the requirement list for a theory of subsidiarity — not another defense of some existing allocation, but a specification of what any defensible theory of the vertical division of powers must deliver. The theory should be adequate for judging how regulatory, taxing, and spending competences are distributed in the European Union, the United States, Germany, and Switzerland, and for settling a contested power without falling back on ad hoc judgment or a bare presumption for the smallest or largest unit.

The list should include at least the following demands, and may add any further requirements you consider genuine:

1. The core trade-off: a non-arbitrary rule for weighing decision-making close to those affected, with its local information and preference matching, against the efficiency, uniformity, and scale gains of larger units.
2. Cross-boundary spillovers: a treatment of costs and benefits that fall outside the deciding jurisdiction, including asymmetric spillovers in which the cost is borne in one place and the benefit accrues in another, and an account of what it means to internalize an externality across jurisdictional lines.
3. The capability distinction: a separation of legal authority from fiscal resources and administrative competence, so that "the lowest level capable of effectively achieving the aim" cannot quietly collapse into "the lowest level that wants the power or is legally permitted to hold it."
4. Exit and mobility: a statement of how the movement of persons, firms, and capital across jurisdictions affects allocations, covering preference sorting, fiscal competition, and the risk that exit by some worsens conditions for those who stay.
5. A floor the theory cannot cross: protections that no subunit may override regardless of local preference, such as fundamental rights or nondiscrimination, together with an explanation of why these are not merely one more competence to be allocated.
6. Drift, reassignment, and the referee: a rule for identifying when powers drift toward one level over time, for distinguishing a principled reassignment from a self-serving one, and for saying which body may authoritatively resolve boundary disputes without becoming a silent instrument of one level's ambitions.

Produce the main deliverable as a table with these columns:

- Requirement (a short label)
- Precise statement (one or two sentences)
- Motivating case or consideration (use the four polities named above where they isolate the point cleanly; otherwise invent a short hypothetical case)
- Status (non-negotiable / camp-relative)
- Familiar position it excludes (described structurally, such as a strong lowest-level presumption or a separate-spheres dual federalism)

After the table, state which pair or triple of requirements you suspect cannot be fully jointly satisfied, and where the conflict lies. Then give a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these keys:

- "most_exclusionary": the single requirement that would knock out the most familiar accounts, and why
- "hardest_tension": the pair or triple hardest to satisfy jointly
- "non_negotiables": the requirements no theory could drop and still count as a theory of subsidiarity
- "most_contestable": the requirement you are least confident is a genuine desideratum rather than a bias
- "what_would_change_my_mind": the empirical finding or theoretical argument most likely to make you reclassify one of the non-negotiable requirements as negotiable, or the reverse
- "where_the_list_could_fail": the presupposition most worth checking before applying the list to all four reference polities

Do not cite scholarship or assemble a bibliography; the list should be carried entirely by the cases and considerations it marshals.
