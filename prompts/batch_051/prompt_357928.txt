In my doctoral work on decision under ambiguity I keep returning to an asymmetry in how people treat one fixed unknown in two nearly identical bets. Consider an urn with ninety balls: thirty are red, and the remaining sixty are each either black or yellow in an unknown proportion. Offered £100 if red is drawn against £100 if black is drawn, most people prefer the red bet, since its chance is known to be one third while black's could be anywhere from nothing to two thirds. Offered instead £100 if red or yellow is drawn against £100 if black or yellow is drawn, the same people typically prefer black or yellow, whose chance is known to be two thirds, while the other could be anywhere from one third to certainty. The second pair simply adds £100 if yellow to both alternatives, yet that common addition reverses the ranking of red and black: in the first choice people act as though the unknown sixty balls might be too few black, and in the second as though they might be too many black, though the urn has not changed. I want the strongest principle you can find that generates both choices, and an honest verdict on whether it justifies the asymmetry or only rationalises an inconsistency we should give up. In particular, consider whether maximin expected utility, which evaluates each option at the composition of the urn most unfavourable to it, is a genuine defence, and test it against a nearby case of your own before saying whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Please answer in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter.
