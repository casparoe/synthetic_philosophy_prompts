I'm a retired chemist, and since I stopped working I've been reading my way through some moral philosophy. Lately I keep tripping over the same question about frugality, which I was raised to treat as an unambiguous good and which my old lab treated as the cardinal virtue. But when I actually go by my gut on particular cases, I get a pattern I can't state. My father mended his shirts, reused tea bags, and drove the same car for twenty years, and he quietly gave a surprisingly large portion of a modest salary to the food bank; I find his frugality genuinely admirable. My neighbor drives twenty minutes across town each week to save about three dollars on groceries, then donates the three dollars to a soup kitchen; I don't think she's done anything wrong, but it seems like a strangely misdirected virtue. A wealthy former colleague loved to tell people how frugal he was, with economy flights and threadbare sweaters and clipping coupons, but every cent he saved just piled up in his estate and he gave almost nothing away; I don't admire his frugality at all, if anything I find it a little repellent. During a long drought our town ran a water-frugality campaign and I thought it was plainly right, but my approval was about the reservoir level, not about whether my neighbors became more frugal people. If the campaign had changed everyone's attitudes while total water use stayed the same, I'd have considered it useless, maybe worse then useless.

Then there's a pair I can't keep straight. A man spends his whole working life living very austerely, no flights, no restaurants, secondhand clothes, so that he can leave a large bequest to an animal shelter. I think that is a wonderful way to live. A woman lives in exactly the same austere way, and she also leaves everything to the same shelter, but she does it only because she hates spending and likes watching her bank balance grow; the bequest is, to her, just the default. My gut says the man's frugality is more admirable than the woman's, even though every visible action and every eventual outcome is the same.

Is there one principle in here, somewhere between the idea that frugality is a duty or virtue you must exhibit yourself and the idea that frugality is only a means to better outcomes like less waste or more benefit to others, that captures all of these verdicts? Or do I have to give some of them up? Please state the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely; show how it handles each case; and tell me what it would say about a case I haven't settled. A city facing a drought sets a per-household water cap. One family with a long-standing frugal way of life exceeds the cap, while a second family wastes water for months and then installs a greywater system just in time to come in under it. Is there any moral difference between those two families, and what does that tell me about what I actually value in frugality? Please answer in American English, and don't hesitate to tell me which of my gut feelings I should revise.
