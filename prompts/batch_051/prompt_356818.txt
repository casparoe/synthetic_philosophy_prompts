Take the Skokie controversy of the late 1970s as a case study in Mill's harm principle and the limits of free speech. The National Socialist Party of America, a fringe neo-Nazi group, sought to march in uniform and display swastikas through Skokie, Illinois, a village with an unusually large number of Holocaust survivors among its residents. Skokie passed ordinances designed to stop the march, the ACLU—over loud internal protest—defended the group's right to demonstrate, and the courts struck the bans down as unconstitutional restrictions on speech. I am not asking for the legal narrative; I want the philosophical mapping.

Work with the harm principle in something close to Mill's own form: the only legitimate ground for coercively restricting a mature person's liberty is the prevention of harm to others, and offense, disgust, the person's own moral or physical good, and majority disapproval are not enough on their own. For each option Skokie had—total prohibition, content-neutral time-place-manner rules, or letting the march proceed—state what the village would need to count as harm for that option to be justified under the principle, and what the ACLU position must reclassify as mere offense. Then test the harm/offense distinction doing the work here. If the psychological injury to survivors counts as harm, the principle seems to permit suppressing speech whenever an audience is deeply wounded by it, and the harm/offense line collapses into a measure of how intensely the audience objects. If that injury does not count, the principle seems committed to treating a swastika displayed before Holocaust survivors as no different in kind from any other unpopular opinion. Which horn is the better reading of Mill, and does Skokie support it or embarrass it?

Finally, address the fit between the case and the theory. Mill allowed suppressing expression when it becomes direct incitement to harmful conduct, but a uniformed march is symbolic rather than an argument, and its force comes from what it menaces as much as from what it asserts. Does Skokie show the harm principle doing its intended work by protecting expression under maximal stress, show it being stretched until the notion of harm loses its shape, or reveal that the principle was built for ordinary advocacy and is largely silent about symbolic hate speech aimed at a traumatized community? Conclude with what the episode establishes about the theory: a vindication, an anomaly absorbed only by distorting the principle, or a case it was never designed to settle.
