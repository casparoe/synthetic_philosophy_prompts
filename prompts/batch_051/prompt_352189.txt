As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been reading Book XI of Augustine's Confessions for a piece on philosophical theories of time, and I find myself caught in an argument there that seems to prove too much. Reconstructed, it runs as follows.

(1) The past no longer exists and the future does not yet exist, so only the present exists.
(2) But no present period has any duration: any stretch of time, however short, has earlier and later parts, so part of it has already slipped into the past while the rest is still to come.
(3) Therefore the strictly present thing is a durationless instant, a boundary between past and future rather than a period of time.
(4) Time, however, is ordinarily thought to consist of periods: a meeting lasts an hour, a season lasts months, a life spans decades. On the reasoning above, no such period is ever wholly present.
(5) So if only what exists in the present is real, it follows that stretches of time are never real. The argument appears to prove that durations are unreal.
(6) That conclusion is hard to accept, since it would make ordinary true claims about how long something lasted systematically false; and Augustine himself does not rest there but turns toward an account in which past and future exist as memory and expectation in the present mind.

Where does the argument go wrong? If one step is to be rejected, which one should it be, and what view of time or existence does rejecting it force? I would most like to keep the plain truth of statements about elapsed and future durations, but I want to know what retaining them would cost.
