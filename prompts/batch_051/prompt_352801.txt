I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I need help keeping one of my characters honest. I'm writing a novel set in a warring-states-era river town, and my protagonist, a widowed ferrywoman named Su, keeps ending up as the person who decides who gets saved, fed, and ferried first. I've been reading bits of Mozi and the Confucians in translation to give her a moral spine: Mozi says care should be impartial, that partiality is the root of the world's injuries, while the Confucians say care comes in gradations, deepest for parents and children, thinner for cousins, thinner still for strangers. I wanted her to be one or the other, but now I'm not sure her record commits her to either. Here are the decisions she's already made in earlier scenes. (1) When a traveling doctor could visit only one feverish household, she sent him to a stranger family's unconscious child before her own son, whose fever was high but who was awake and drinking, because she said the stranger's child might not last the night and her son likely would. (2) When two granaries burned, she gave her spare grain to her cousin rather than to an unrelated widow whose stored grain was also lost, because she knew the widow's daughter in the next town could take her in and the cousin had nobody. (3) She refused to bribe the relief clerk to move her nephew up the list for salt coupons, even though his household had six mouths, because the list ran by number of dependents and the households ahead of him had more. (4) When the temple handed out donated shoes, she picked a pair for her niece first, but they were the only pair small enough to fit the girl, so nobody else could have used them. (5) She gave a boatload of strangers, whose supplies had washed away, the last of her family's emergency rice and put her own grandchildren on half rations so the rice would stretch a week for everyone.

Now I need her final judgment, the one the whole book turns on. In the climax the river floods, and Su is in the water with room in her boat for one more. On one roof is her nephew, pinned under a beam, bleeding from a leg wound and exhausted; on a lower roof, half submerged, is a stranger's son, shivering and weaker, and by Su's own estimate he is a bit less likely than her nephew to hold out till morning, though both will almost certainly die if not taken now. Who does she save if shes being consistent with her earlier decisions? If the record underdetermines it, tell me which earlier case is the closest analog and what principle she would be adopting with each choice—a Mohist with a practical tie-breaker, a Confucian with a need-threshold, or something else entirely—and give me a future scene that would finally pin her down.
