Write a Swiftian modest proposal, around 600 words, in the voice of a cheerfully rigorous Mandevillean economist. The proposal should urge the state to stop merely tolerating greed and start formally encouraging it—perhaps an annual Acquisitiveness Day on which citizens are decorated in proportion to the public benefits their greed has produced, or a national Office of Private Vices charged with nurturing acquisitiveness—on the ground that private vices are the most reliable engine of public prosperity. The piece should follow the target's own logic step by step to a conclusion the reader recoils from, and the humor must come from getting the philosophy right rather than from easy moralizing: the distinction between greed and ordinary self-interest (and which of these the Mandevillean argument actually needs), the mechanism by which private vice is supposed to be transmuted into public benefit, the problem of aggregating and counting those benefits, and the instability that arises once a vice is publicly cultivated, honored, and rewarded rather than left private and uncoordinated. The standard objections—that the view defines greed too broadly, that it ends up licensing fraud or predation once their downstream transactions are booked as benefits, and that a cultivated, celebrated vice may stop doing the hidden work the argument assigns it—should be visible throughout but never stated. Keep the tone deadpan, make it readable to someone who has never read Mandeville, and make it funny to someone who knows the debate.
