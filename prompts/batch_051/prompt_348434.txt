I take A in the first one and D in the second, like most people apparently do, and my friend says: (1) rational preferences satisfy independence: if you prefer one gamble to another, you should still prefer it when the same extra branch is glued onto both. (2) In the first choice, the 89% chance of $1 million is a shared branch, so choosing A over B means I prefer the 11% branch that is just a sure $1 million over the 11% branch that is a 10/11 chance of $5 million and a 1/11 chance of nothing. (3) In the second choice, the 89% chance of nothing is shared, so choosing D over C means I prefer that same risky 11% branch over the 11% branch that is a sure $1 million. (4) So I prefer the sure-$1-million branch over the risky branch and the risky branch over the sure-$1-million branch, which is a flat contradiction. (5) Expected utility theory needs independence, so if I reject (1) I'm basically throwing out the thing that makes expected utility theory work. (6) Therefore the Allais pattern is just incoherent, however sensible it feels. I still want to keep the choices: they feel way sturdier to me than the axiom.
