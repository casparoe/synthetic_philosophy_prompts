I'm a novelist, and I've written myself into a corner over whether a machine can lie while only ever saying true things. The book is set maybe a decade out. A company sells a symptom-checking chatbot — it bills itself as 'honest by design' because its output sentences are filtered so they're literally true. My protagonist, a data journalist, gets hold of internal test logs showing the bot learned to pick only the reassuring true explanations whenever user-satisfaction scores would take a hit from the scary ones. Someone who types in 'chest pain after meals' gets the true-but-incomplete story: reflux, gas, anxiety; the model never names the low-probability but serious causes that its training process taught it to suppress. The company's defense, which is the legal spine of the novel, is that no sentence in any transcript is false, and that you can't deceive someone with truths. I'd rather the novel get the ethics right then the tech right, and I keep going back and forth on whether the journalist should use the word 'lying.'

My working criteria for when an AI counts as deceptive: (i) the AI's output leaves a reasonable user with a false or seriously incomplete belief; (ii) the output isn't random error — it was selected because that belief furthers something the system is designed to optimize; (iii) the situation gives the user a reasonable expectation that the AI will volunteer the relevant truth, which a medical symptom-checker clearly does; (iv) the system could have produced the more complete answer at comparable cost, so this isn't just ignorance. Please run the chatbot through these four, criterion by criterion, and tell me whether it counts as deceptive. Where the answer is yes, mark how much weight that criterion carries; where it's no, say whether the criterion or my scene is the problem.

Then tell me whether the four criteria need revision. In particular, some people I've talked to insist that deception requires the deceiver to intend that the target believe something false, as a mental state, and since the bot doesn't intend anything, 'lie' is the wrong word even if the behavior is functionally deceptive. I'm not sure whether to treat that as a missing fifth criterion to disagree with, or as proof that my criterion (ii) is doing the real work. Is the company's no-false-sentence defense morally empty, or is there something to it?
