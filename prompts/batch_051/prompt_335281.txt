I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a feature essay on why people keep finding birthday coincidences uncanny even when the arithmetic says they should not. My working title is "Laplace in a Room of 23: Why Coincidence Anecdotes Are Almost Never Evidence." The essay uses Pierre-Simon Laplace's *A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities*, specifically the section on the probability of testimonies, as a diagnostic tool. In that section Laplace considers how an event's posterior probability changes when several witnesses testify to it independently; he models each witness as having some small probability of falsehood and shows, in effect, that concordant independent testimony can compound rapidly, making even an initially improbable event very likely if enough witnesses agree. I want to reuse that framework for the birthday paradox rather than for legal testimony or miracles. My thought is that a birthday-coincidence anecdote is structurally different from Laplace's case: the event under testimony is not fixed in advance but is selected after the fact from hundreds of possible pairwise matches. A person who says "I shared a birthday with the stranger next to me on the plane" is, on my proposed analysis, not offering a witness report of one pre-specified event. They are the survivor of a large scan over many comparison opportunities, and they typically know nothing reliable about their own false-positive rate for noticing and reporting such matches. I think this means the Laplacean compounding of testimony does not get off the ground for birthday anecdotes, and the felt surprise is better explained by the combinatorial birthday effect plus attention bias, not by any hidden causal connection.

The essay would defend three connected claims: first, that the standard birthday paradox is best understood as an underestimation of the chance that at least one of many low-probability events will occur; second, that after-the-fact anecdotal selection silently changes the relevant event space from one narrow coincidence to any of many; and third, that this selection problem is more severe than ordinary selection bias because the reporters themselves do not know which comparison opportunities they scanned. I would welcome a committed response rather than a survey of options. I would also appreciate a critical judgment on whether the Laplacean framework is doing real work here or is just a historical ornament.
