I'm leading a discussion on Moby-Dick in a few weeks, and I want to run it through the dollar auction and escalation of commitment rather than the usual "Ahab as tragic hero" thing. As I understand the game: two people bid for a dollar, but the runner-up also has to pay, so once both have bid, each new bid can look rational as a way to avoid losing what you've already put in, and it can ratchet up past the point where everyone loses. Ahab seems like he's stuck in exactly that structure—he's already paid a leg, then the smashed boats, the crew, Starbuck's warnings, the ship itself—and every additional cost makes quitting feel more like admitting the whole thing was wasted, so he bids again. But I'm not sure the analogy really holds, because Moby Dick isn't a second bidder and the "dollar" isn't a fixed prize; it's a meaning Ahab invents. I'd like a reading of the novel through this lens: where the dollar auction genuinely illuminates his escalation, where it breaks down, and whether the book treats him as trapped in locally rational escalation or as simply mad. Does Ishmael's surviving to tell it, and framing the whole thing in retrospect, change the moral? Point me to the scenes or moments you'd build the discussion around.
