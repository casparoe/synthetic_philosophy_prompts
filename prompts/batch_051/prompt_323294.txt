I've been working through my intuitions about Molyneux's problem, and I want to know whether they hang together. Here are the verdicts I keep arriving at. (1) A man blind from birth who gains sight as an adult, is shown a cube and a sphere, and is told only that one is the cube and the other the sphere, cannot reliably say which is which on sight alone, even after having just handled each object with his eyes closed. (2) That same man, allowed to hold one of the two objects while looking at it, has no trouble telling you which one he is holding, and can do so within a minute or two of first seeing. (3) The same man can immediately tell, by looking alone, which of the two seen shapes is angular and which is smoothly curved; his difficulty is confined to connecting these seen features with the touched kinds cube and sphere. (4) My instinct is that a child blind from birth whose vision is restored at age two works out the cross-modal mapping in days rather than months, and that this is a real difference from the adult case rather than an artifact of testing. (5) A woman who was fully sighted until age twenty, then totally blind for twenty years, then given sight again, recognizes a cube and a sphere on sight right away. (6) Newly sighted adults reliably learn to identify colors faster than shapes. I want a single principle about what is and is not cross-modally transferable between touch and vision, and about what visual recognition requires, that captures all six verdicts. If no such principle exists, tell me which verdicts I should give up, and why those. Finally, I keep wavering about a case not on this list: a person blind from birth who, while still blind, has mastered solid geometry through embossed diagrams and verbal instruction — does your principle predict that she will recognize a triangle the first time she sees one?
