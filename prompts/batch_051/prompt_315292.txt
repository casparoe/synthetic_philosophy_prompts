A friend who teaches civics likes to make her students defend the exact boundary of the franchise, and it has left me with an asymmetry I can't shake. Where I live, a 16-year-old can work full-time and pay income tax but can't vote for the people who set tax rates. Her uncle, by contrast, moved abroad decades ago, hasn't paid taxes here since, and still gets a ballot mailed to him every election. A non-citizen who has lived here for 20 years, works, pays taxes, and has kids in the public schools can't vote either. Meanwhile we lecture citizens that voting is a civic duty, and in some places you can even be fined for not doing it. So voting is a duty for citizens but is denied to people with an obvious stake in the place. I can't tell whether there's a principle sorting these cases, or whether it's just inherited practice nobody wants to defend.

I'm not after a reading list; I want to know whether the asymmetry is defensible. What candidate principle — people affected by laws should have a say, or voting being tied to citizenship, or voters needing to be competent — actually sorts the cases above? Test it against nearby cases, like a citizen who left at age two and has never once lived here, a non-citizen who has been paying taxes for 30 years, or an employed 16-year-old versus a 90-year-old who pays no tax and doesn't follow politics. If the cases aren't distinguishable in a principled way, I'd rather you say the asymmetry is an inconsistency we just live with than try to rationalize it after the fact.
