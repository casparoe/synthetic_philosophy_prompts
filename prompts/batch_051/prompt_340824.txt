I've been collecting my own gut verdicts about what counts as statistical evidence, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or if some of the verdicts have to go.

- A p of 0.049 from a trial that was preregistered, with one primary outcome and a big enough sample: that feels like real evidence the effect is there.
- The same p, but the researcher tried a dozen outcomes and three ways of excluding people and only reported the combination that worked: that feels like almost no evidence.
- A p of 0.01 for a wildly implausible hypothesis (like, say, that tapping your foot at 3 Hz raises IQ an hour later): I still count it as some evidence, but nowhere near enough to make me believe it.
- A p of 0.20 from a huge, clean replication attempt of a famous effect: to me that feels like stronger evidence against the effect than the original p of 0.01 was for it, even though 0.20 isn't what journals call significant.
- A p of 0.03 that was grabbed after peeking at the data and optional stopping: maybe a little evidence, but much weaker than the preregistered 0.049.
- A Bayes factor of 10 from a flat prior: it feels like less evidence to me than a preregistered p of 0.01, and I can't say why.

Is there a coherent account of evidence that gets all six, or close? If not, which verdicts should I overturn, and what's the principle that tells me to? And what does that principle say about a case I'm stuck on: a p of 0.04 from a preregistered but badly underpowered study?
