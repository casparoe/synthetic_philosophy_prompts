I am currently taking a seminar on political ideology, and I have been asked to write up my position on the left–right distinction so that the class can examine it. Before I circulate it, I want a consistency check on the package as a whole. My commitments, as I currently hold them, are these:

(1) The left–right distinction is real. Across countries and periods, positions on apparently unrelated policy questions—taxation, immigration, criminal justice, family structure, foreign policy—tend to cluster along a single dimension: knowing where someone stands on one of these gives real, though imperfect, information about where they stand on the others.

(2) The content of that dimension is, at bottom, equality versus hierarchy. Left positions characteristically seek to flatten inequalities and asymmetric social relations; right positions characteristically regard some ranking—by class, nation, tradition, gender, or merit—as legitimate, natural, or unavoidable.

(3) The psychological basis of the dimension is plural rather than single. The left appears attuned mainly to care and fairness; the right appears to draw on a broader set of moral concerns, weighting loyalty, authority, and sanctity more heavily, even while also caring about harm and fairness. Personality findings point the same way: openness to experience tracks left-leaning views, conscientiousness tracks right-leaning ones.

(4) The issues that fall on each side are historically contingent. "Right" has meant support for monarchy, free trade, established religion, or land reform in different times and places, with "left" often meaning the opposite. I do not think either side has a fixed list of positions.

(5) Still, I think the axis is now much less useful for mapping the most consequential disagreements. The conflicts that strike me as most important—open versus closed orientations, cosmopolitan versus nationalist commitments, technocratic versus populist styles of politics—cut across the familiar left–right line, and many citizens combine, for instance, economic egalitarianism with cultural conservatism, or market liberalism with social progressivism, which the one-dimensional picture treats as off-diagonal cases.

(6) Methodologically, I would prefer to define the dimension structurally, by the clustering itself, rather than by any substantive list of issues. But I worry that this empties out the claim: if "left–right" simply means "whatever happens to cluster together," then insisting that the distinction is real begins to sound like a tautology.

I would like to know whether these six commitments form a coherent package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which are only apparently in tension? For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity, and say what the least costly revision would be in each case. Finally, which single revision—if I were to make only one—would do the most to make the whole view hang together?
