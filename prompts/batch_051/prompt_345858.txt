I'm a CS theory PhD student, and I've been reading decision theory on the side because my current project on sequential decision-making keeps brushing against it. A few nights ago I was re-deriving the pumping lemma to teach a section, and it suddenly looked like the same argument as the money pump against intransitive preferences; I can't shake the feeling, though it might just be a superficial echo of the word pump.

Here's the mapping as I see it. In the pumping lemma, you have a DFA with k states. Any accepted string of length at least k forces the automaton to visit some state twice along the accepting path; the substring between the two visits is a loop, and you can iterate it: xy^i z is accepted for every i. So a cycle in a finite transition graph lets you pump out longer strings indefinitely. The money pump argument says: if an agent has cyclic preferences over three alternatives (A over B, B over C, C over A), start them at C, charge a small fee to swap to B, then to A, then back to C; after one trip around the cycle they hold the same thing but have less money, and repeating the loop extracts the fee each time. Here too a cycle, in the preference graph rather than the transition graph, becomes a pump, except what comes out is money rather than string length.

The formal parallel I'm seeing is: both are lemmas of the form, a cycle in a finite structure, iterated, violates a classification property — regularity in one case, diachronic rationality in the other. In fact the pigeonhole step seems to transfer: a long enough chain of strict pairwise comparisons among finitely many options has to revisit an option and thereby contain a cycle, just like a long enough accepted string has to revisit a state. But the money pump is ususually stated starting from a cycle that's already assumed rather than derived from finiteness, and there's an asymetry in how the two arguments treat their cycles: the automaton's loop is a normal consequence of the machine revisiting a state, while the preference cycle is a defect. So I'm not sure whether the shared structure is real or whether the analogy is doing no work.

Is the parallel deep, or superficial? If it is real, does anything transfer? For instance, is there an analogue of the bound |xy| ≤ k — some bound on how early in a chain of comparisons a preference cycle must show up given the menu size — and does it correspond to a bound on how many trades a money pump needs? And would anything like Ogden's lemma or the context-free pumping lemma have a decision-theoretic counterpart, or is that exactly where the analogy breaks down?
