I have replaced my old rule for financial decisions with a new one, and I want to know whether the new rule is genuinely more rational or just a paranoid misapplication of some game theory I half-remember. The rule is this: never enter a trade unless I can tell a story, from the counterparty's point of view, about why they are taking the other side without one of us having to be wrong about the asset's value. I picked this up from a loose statement of the no-trade theorem: roughly, if everyone starts from the same prior and it is common knowledge that everyone is rational, two people cannot knowingly enter a purely speculative trade for mutual advantage, because each person's willingness to trade would itself leak information to the other. So when I see a seller offering a stock I think is undervalued, I read the offer as evidence against my valuation, and I usually end up doing nothing. This has spread beyond investing: I nearly walked away from a used laptop because the seller accepted my first offer too quickly, and I have balked at salary negotiations on the theory that the employer's opening number is itself a signal about my market value.

The trouble is that the rule, as I actually apply it, seems to prove too much. Markets also exist because people differ in needs, endowments, time horizons, and tolerance for risk; a person can rationally sell an asset that I should rationally buy even if neither of us is misinformed, because the same asset sits differently in each of our lives. I suspect I am confusing "there is something I could learn from the counterparty" with "the counterparty knows that I am wrong," and in doing so I may have built a policy that rewards me mainly with inaction — an epistemic humility that functions as its own bias. Is my policy defensible as it stands? If not, can it be restated so that it keeps whatever is genuinely right in no-trade reasoning without turning ordinary transactions into evidence that I am the sucker? And how could I tell, from the inside, whether I am being disciplined by a sound epistemic principle or just rationalizing a fear of deciding?
