I am a novelist at the worldbuilding stage of a near-future political novel, and I want one of its central institutions to behave with the internal logic of a real bureaucracy rather than as a simplified moral prop. The institution is the Office of Expected Harm, founded after a catastrophe that had been dismissed as too improbable to warrant response. Its founding rule is strict: any person may petition the Office against any party for any future harm, so long as the petitioner supplies a physically possible causal story. The alleged victims need not exist yet or be identifiable. The Office's analysts estimate the harm's probability p and its magnitude M, and the Office's charter forbids them from rejecting a claim as de minimis solely because p is very small. If p is greater than zero, the respondent must either halt the challenged activity or pay a charge equal to p times M into a global mitigation fund, with 0.1 percent of the charge paid to the petitioner as a monitoring bounty.

I do not want the Office to collapse in the first chapter as an obvious strawman, and I also do not want it to run as a noble institution whose flaws are only ever the familiar corruption of individual bureaucrats. Help me understand how it would actually operate. Which kinds of claimants and claims would come to dominate its docket? How would frequent respondents lawfully and unlawfully game the estimates of p, the estimates of M, and the causal-story requirement? Where is the first deep, structural failure likely to appear—one forced by the rule itself rather than by ordinary fraud—and is there any reform that fixes it without abandoning the expected-harm principle? Finally, what does the Office do over time to ordinary risk-taking and to the profession of monitoring and petitioning? Please answer in American English.
