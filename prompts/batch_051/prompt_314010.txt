I serve on the small-grants committee of a private family foundation whose stated mission is to advance human welfare. We have one grant left this cycle and two finalists. The first is a proposal from three pure mathematicians who want to settle a longstanding conjecture about the structure of certain high-dimensional spaces; their proposal states plainly that they expect no application and that the project matters because it would close a deep structural question. The second is a proposal from a health-services research team that would use the same funds to pilot a scheduling reform at rural clinics, with a cost-effectiveness analysis predicting a modest but real reduction in missed appointments over two years. The committee is split. Some members read the mission as excluding proposals that cannot demonstrate expected welfare benefits. Others reply that curiosity is not merely a means to later benefit: understanding can be worth pursuing for its own sake, and supporting it can be an expression of intellectual virtue on the institution's part. Draft the decision memorandum I will circulate before Friday's vote. It must recommend funding one of the two proposals, or a principled split if one can be defended, and it must honestly engage the strongest consideration favoring the proposal you do not recommend. Set the memorandum out as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Use American English.
