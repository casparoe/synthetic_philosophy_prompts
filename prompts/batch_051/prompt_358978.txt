I cover the state supreme court and I'm working on a profile of one justice who's known for her thinking on strict liability and mens rea. Over the years she's tipped her hand in a few opinions. She let stand a $40 expired-meter ticket for a driver who was inside the parking office trying to get change when the meter ran out, writing that fault isn't worth litigating at fourty dollars. She upheld a scaffold-safety fine against a general contractor where a subcontractor's crew created the hazard after the contractor's own inspection that morning; her line was that in a regulated industry you buy the risk. She also upheld a fine against a gas-station owner whose clerk sold to a 19-year-old with a fake ID, on the same buy-the-risk logic. But she voted to reverse a strict-liability felony drug-possession conviction for a beachcomber who found a tarp-wrapped package of drugs and was carrying it to the police when he was arrested, reasoning that a felony record and prison time can't rest on zero culpability. Next month the court hears a new case: a restaurant owner-manager is charged under a strict-liability food-safety felony after serving raw oysters from a licensed supplier; the owner chose the cheapest supplier and skipped the optional testing most places in town use, and she now faces up to two years and would probably loose her liquor license. Predict how this justice votes. More usefully, lay out the candidate rules that fit her earlier opinions—penalty severity, type of activity, opportunity to comply, whatever—say what each predicts here, identify the closest prior case, tell me which rule you'd bet on and why, and give me one further case that would cleanly separate the surviving rules. American English, please.
