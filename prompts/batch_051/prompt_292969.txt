I spent thirty years in a chemistry lab and only recently started reading philosophy, and the thing that's gotten its hooks in me is the no-trade theorems and what they imply about markets as information-processing systems. The position Ive settled on, at least for now, is this: traders with common priors and rational expectations shouldn't trade purely on private information, because the other side's willingness to deal at price X is itself evidence about what they know, and once you condition on that the expected edge should vanish. If that's right, real trading volume has to be explained by something else -- liquidity needs, hedging, different priors, bounded rationality, or the bare fact that actual markets don't meet the theorem's setup. What I want is an honest map of what would count against this position. Not just the flat fact that people do trade, but which assumptions would have to fail, what the world would have to look like for each candidate escape route to be the real one rather than a merely logical loophole, and which of them are live enough to test. What evidence -- volume, bid-ask spreads, insider-trading rules, active manager returns, prediction markets -- should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? And be straight with me: has the claim been cushioned into unfalsifiability by now, and does that matter for the philosophical point about markets and information?
