Please argue that in a Death-in-Damascus–style case—where a very accurate (but not infallible) predictor tells you he'll be wherever you end up tomorrow, and you have to pick between two cities that look completly symmetric—the rational move is not to settle on either city by deliberation, but to commit to a genuinely random tiebreaker, like a fair coin flip, before you head out. Make the case that this isn't just refusing to decide or changing the subject, but actually changes the prediction problem in a way that leaves you better off: if your final destination is causally independent of the earlier prediction and the predictor has no special grip on fair coins, then you cap his hit rate at chance, even though any deliberate choice of city would be predicted with high accuracy. Also say something about the natural objection that a predictor good enough to predict your choices should also be good enough to predict that you'll randomize—unless he knows the outcome of a fair coin in advance, knowing you'll flip doesn't tell him which city your in.
