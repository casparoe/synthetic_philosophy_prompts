I cover economics and complexity science for a living, and a while back I spent a morning with two people—one a portfolio manager, the other a statistical physicist—who were supposed to be explaining decision-making to me. Instead they started arguing about a toy gamble, and I filled a notebook and still can't say who won. I'd like someone to go through it move by move.

Here's the setup they both agreed on. Each round a coin is flipped: heads multiplies your current wealth by 1.5, tails by 0.6. You can stay in as many rounds as you like, with your whole stake on the table each time.

The portfolio manager, call her A, went first: the expected multiplier per round is 1.05, so the bet has positive expectation. Repeat it enough and you're printing money at five percent expected per round; only a fool turns that down.

The physicist, call him B, said that's the precise mistake. The number that matters for a single compounded path is the geometric mean, not the arithmetic mean: the square root of 1.5 times 0.6, which is about 0.949. So the typical path shrinks. After many rounds almost every individual path is being dragged toward zero, and the positive expected growth comes entirely from a tiny slice of astronomically lucky paths pulling the ensemble mean up. If you actually live one path, you don't get the ensemble; you get something very close to ruin.

A shot back: that's an artifact of one undiversified stake. Split the money across many independent copies of the bet and rebalance each round, and the average multiplier you realize converges toward 1.05. With a wide enough parallel portfolio the ensemble average is not a fiction—it's just the realized outcome. So expectation was never wrong; it was answering the question for a different, perfectly realizable setup.

B said sure, but that is not a defense of the original claim. A diversified portfolio is a different bet, and its feasibility depends on having many genuinely independent exposes, low correlation, and the ability to rebalance. Your house, your wage income, a one-time windfall—these are single paths. There the time-average growth rate is the right criterion, and it says the bet is bad. Changing the subject to a portfolio you might construct instead doesn't rescue the advice to take the bet as stated.

A said that just relocates the problem. Once we're agreed that different setups favor different averages, 'rational' needs a utility function: maximizing expected wealth, maximizing median long-run wealth, maximizing log wealth, and staying alive long enough to care are all different objectives. The geometric-mean rule B keeps pushing is just one of them—more or less log utility in a trench coat—so he hasn't found a fallacy, only a preference.

B said it is not a preference. Maximizing geometric mean falls out of the multiplicative structure itself once you ask which strategy makes wealth grow over time on a single path; it doesn't require an aversion to risk. Whether or not you have log utility, the non-ergodicity point stands: an average over parallel universes is the wrong summary of what one path does.

The waitress brought the bill around then and we never got a resolution. Can you adjudicate the exchange—not just declare an overall winner, but go through each move and say who is right about which specific point, where either of them overreaches, and what both sides are missing? I'm most interested in which argument steps survive and which don't.
