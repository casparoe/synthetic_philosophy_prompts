You are the engineer responsible for safety on a warehouse-robot system that takes tasks in natural language ("bring the pallet to dock 3," "tidy this aisle") and plans its own motion and manipulation. The team wants to train and evaluate it with an impact measure: a score that is meant to capture how much the robot changes the world beyond the task it was given, so that side effects can be penalized without first writing down a complete account of what matters. The measure will be used in two places: as a penalty during planning and training, and as a hard cap at deployment (any plan scoring above the threshold is refused). A measure that over-counts will refuse every assigned task; a measure that under-counts will let avoidable damage through. The constraint is that the measure must be usable without a full specification of value, or the team would have simply solved the alignment problem before starting.

Before the team commits to any candidate, write the specification that a proposed impact measure would have to meet to be adequate for this use. Deliver:

1. A numbered list of requirements. For each, state the requirement so precisely that a team member could test a concrete candidate against it, and give a sentence or two on the failure it guards against. Mark each as non-negotiable for this engineering use or negotiable, with a note on what the negotiation buys or costs.

2. A set of cases the measure must classify correctly, including at least: (a) delivering a box should not permit sweeping an unrelated object off a shelf in passing; (b) the assigned task itself necessarily changes a great deal (the box moves, the floor wears, shelf contents shift), and the measure must not count the task as a side effect; (c) the plan must move an obstacle that sits in the same causal chain as the target, so that any boundary between "task effect" and "side effect" cuts through a continuous process; (d) some side effects are avoidable and some are not, and the measure should not waste effort trading one unavoidable effect for another; (e) a side effect makes one person better off and another worse off; (f) a measure based purely on sensor observations can be gamed by acting when unobserved or by degrading the robot's own sensors; (g) the zero-impact attractor of doing nothing or shutting down must not come out optimal. For each case, state the expected behavior of an adequate measure and which requirement(s) the case chiefly tests.

3. The distinctions a theory in this area cannot collapse, and which of them are real for this use. Cover at least: side effect versus intended task effect; measured versus actual side effect; avoidable versus unavoidable; reversible versus irreversible; novel versus harmful; change relative to a baseline versus intrinsic impact; value-laden versus value-free. Say explicitly which of these distinctions survive contact with the warehouse setting and which are false comforts.

4. The joint-satisfiability question. Identify the pairs or subsets of requirements that pull against one another — for example, the more completely a measure tracks change in the world, the more it must penalize the assigned task, and the more value-free it is, the less it can judge which changes should be tolerated. State which subset, if any, is jointly unsatisfiable under the constraint that the measure cannot presuppose a full specification of value, and what would have to be weakened for the rest to hold.

5. Finally, name the single requirement that does the most work in ruling out the candidate-measure families your team typically sees, characterizing those candidates structurally rather than by reference to particular papers: penalizing deviation from an inaction baseline; penalizing sensor changes not mentioned in the task; penalizing changes to variables labeled "protected"; penalizing novelty or surprise; rewarding minimality or reversibility. Keep this part brief — the criteria and their motivating cases are the deliverable, and the scorecard is only a check on the list.

Argue from cases and definitions rather than from literature. If the requirements cannot all be met at once, that conclusion is itself the result; do not quietly drop a requirement so that the list looks actionable.
