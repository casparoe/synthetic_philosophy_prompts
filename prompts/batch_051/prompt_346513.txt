I've been chewing on a puzzle about hard choices—the kind where the values at stake feel incommensurable, so neither option is better than the other and they're not equal either: you just can't put them on a single scale. I've ended up with a little argument that has a conclusion I don't want to accept, and I can't tell wether it's valid or whether I'm quietly smuggling something in. Here it is:

(1) In a genuinely hard choice, neither option is better than the other, and they're not equally good.
(2) If neither option is better and they're not equally good, then no consideration about the situation favors one option over the other.
(3) If no consideration about the situation favors one option over the other, then a decision between them isn't based on anything that favors the chosen option.
(4) A decision that isn't based on anything that favors the chosen option is arbitrary.
(5) If a decision is arbitrary, you have no reason to stick with it when it's later challenged.
(6) So after a hard choice, you have no reason to stick with it: reopening the question is just as rational as keeping to it.

That conclusion feels wrong—committing after a hard choice seems like exactly the thing a rational person is supposed to be able to do, not some kind of self-deception. I half-suspect the trouble is in (2) or (5), maybe becuase choosing itself adds new considerations, or because (2) conflates 'no all-things-considered winner' with 'no favoring considerations at all.' But I can't make the objection precise. Say which step, if any, fails, and what the correct version of it would be. If you're torn between two readings of a step, spell out both and say which reading makes the argument fail. If the argument goes through after all, tell me whether the conclusion is something we should just accept. And if the whole setup is confused, or I'm using 'hard choice' or 'arbitrary' in a way that's doing too much work, say that plainly instead of going along with it.
