A friend has been defending an argument in metaphysics that seems to me to prove far more than anyone should accept. It concerns the special composition question: when do a number of things together compose a further thing? Some pluralities apparently do compose — bricks compose a house, cells compose a body — while others apparently do not — the Eiffel Tower and my left shoe, taken together, compose nothing at all. The argument's conclusion is that there is no stable middle position: either every plurality composes something, however scattered or gerrymandered, or no plurality composes anything, so that tables, houses, and even bodies do not strictly exist. Here is the argument.

1. Suppose composition is restricted: some pluralities compose an object, and some do not.
2. Consider a long stepwise series of rearrangements in which a table is dismantled by removing one particle at a time. At the start the particles clearly compose a table; at the end they clearly compose nothing, or only scattered particles.
3. At every stage of the series, either the particles compose a table or they do not. Whether a composite exists cannot be a vague or borderline matter, and it cannot wait upon our stipulation.
4. Given (2) and (3), the series must contain a sharp cutoff: there is a stage at which the particles compose a table and the very next stage at which they do not.
5. But no such cutoff can be privileged. Any particular cutoff is arbitrary, since adjacent stages differ only by a single particle; and the cutoff's location would shift depending on the entirely arbitrary order in which we chose to remove the particles.
6. If every candidate cutoff is arbitrary, then none is fixed by the facts. And if none is fixed by the facts, then there is no fact of the matter about where composition stops.
7. But (3) already committed us to a determinate fact at every stage. The restricted view therefore requires a sharp cutoff for which, by its own lights, there is no fact of the matter.
8. Hence restricted composition is incoherent. Since we cannot deny that composition happens in some cases — at least in our own case, or our bodies' case — composition must be unrestricted: every plurality, no matter how scattered, composes an object.

I cannot accept the conclusion: it forces us to say there is an object composed of a trout and a turkey, or of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower. I am also reluctant to accept that there is no fact of the matter about whether the particles compose a table at a given stage. Tell me which premise or which inference goes wrong, why it looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, spell out what a defender of restricted composition must deny, and whether that denial leaves a principled position rather than an ad hoc refusal of the conclusion.
