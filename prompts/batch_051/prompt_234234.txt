I spent ten years in workforce planning for a manufacturer just outside Osaka, and have recently moved to a British consultancy advising firms that are cutting standard hours — some toward the French-style thirty-five-hour week, one to a genuine four-day week. The hours really do fall, and output is largely maintained. What I keep noticing, though, is that the reclaimed time does not present as leisure. Colleagues and clients narrate the extra afternoon as a second timetable: language tuition, marathon training, pro bono consulting, an online coding certificate. More than a few have said they would feel uneasy if the day were 'just' rest, as though the time had to be made instructive or useful before it became permissible. I can identify the Protestant work ethic in the background, and karoshi as its lethal endpoint; what I cannot name is the mechanism in between, by which an actual gain in leisure is converted, seemingly voluntarily, into another site of work. Does this configuration have an established name? A bare label would not help much; I need the phenomenon itself marked off from its neighbours, in particular from the Protestant work ethic as ordinarily understood, from work-life spillover, and from merely having demanding hobbies. If no established term fits, what would you call it, and where would you draw its boundaries?
