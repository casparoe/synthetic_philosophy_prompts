I'm organizing a colloquium Q&A with a scholar who has recently defended an uncompromising reading of Blaise Pascal's Pensées, focusing on the fragment where Pascal argues that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical. Her thesis is that Pascal is not merely observing that power rules; he is proposing a criterion of political legitimacy: an order is legitimate to the extent that it unites justice with force, and every actually stable order does so only by making its own force appear just through custom, imagination, and habit. Her standard move when challenged is to say that the objection assumes exactly what Pascal denies—that justice can be recognized independently of the institutions that enforce it. She then turns the challenge back on the objector: if you already have an independent standard of justice that commands assent, you have not escaped Pascal's problem, because that standard still needs force to rule anyone but yourself. I want a sequence of questions that makes this move progressively harder to use without descending into ad hominem. The questions should turn on the actual content of Pascal's text: his remarks near the passage that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical; his claim that justice is subject to dispute while force is quite recognizable and beyond dispute; his idea that we have made what is strong just because we could not make what is just strong; and his separate claim that custom is the whole of equity for no reason other than that it has been received, so that whoever traces it to its origin destroys it. Please include the follow-up I should give to each likely reply. End with a question about what, if anything, could show the view to be false rather than merely uncomfortable.
