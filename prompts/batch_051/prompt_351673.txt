I have been using a distinction in thinking about the Lucas–Penrose argument and want it examined honestly. The standard anti-mechanist move runs: for any formal system S rich enough to represent arithmetic that I can adopt and regard as sound, Gödel's theorem gives me a sentence G_S which I can see is true but S cannot prove; my arithmetical ability is therefore not captured by S, and since S was arbitrary, my mind is no machine at all. I want to separate two things this move might be doing, which I call improvement and transcendence. Improvement is the capacity, given any S I regard as sound, to pass to a stronger system — say S plus Con(S), or S plus G_S — and thereby prove more arithmetic; this looks like a rule that could itself be formalized or programmed. Transcendence is the further capacity to step outside the entire hierarchy of formal systems and recognize arithmetic truth, or the soundness of some system, in a way no formalizable rule could. My view is that the argument only ever exhibits improvement — a human is always shown moving from one formal system to a stronger one — and never transcendence; and since improvement is mechanizable, the argument fails to show that the mind is not a machine. Is this distinction coherent, and does it cut the debate at the right joint? Have I merely renamed a standard response? Give a committed verdict rather than a survey: reconstruct the distinction in its strongest form, name the existing positions it most resembles, and say precisely where it breaks or stops being decisive.
