I'm giving a department colloquium in three weeks defending a version of the precedent-autonomy view of advance directives, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. The thesis I'm defending is roughly: an advance directive refusing life-sustaining treatment should be honored, even when the person with dementia now seems genuinely content and would, if asked, choose to go on living, provided the directive was made competently, was informed about the condition, and reflected the person's settled critical interests rather than a passing mood. My wrinkle is that I don't ground the earlier self's authority in autonomy alone. I ground it in something like narrative identity: the idea that a life is a single thing being shaped over time, and the later self's current experiential preferences don't automatically overrule the earlier self's verdict about the shape of that life. I know this commits me to saying something about how much psychological change the "same narrator" can survive, and that's exactly where I expect to bleed.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium: mostly moral and political philosophers, two Kantians who care about treating persons as ends and get suspicious about instrumentalizing one temporal stage for another's project, one person who works on personal identity and is sympathetic to Parfit's reductionism, and a clinical ethicist from the med school who sees dementia cases and keeps asking how you'd distinguish "mere" contentment from real valuing at the bedside. I don't want the generic objections I've already rehearsed; I want the ones this particular room will actually press. Especially: where does the Parfitian press the same-person premise without just rejecting the whole view; where do the Kantians press the move from my critical interests to constraining the later self; and where does the clinician say the distinction I'm leaning on can't be applied in practice.

Write it as a prep sheet with these sections, in this order: (1) "The thesis and the room" — 100–150 words restating what I need to defend and who's in the room. (2) "Objections in the order they'll come" — the bulk, 1,200–1,800 words, about eight objections, each phrased the way a specific person in that audience would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest. (3) "Concede now" — 100–150 words on what I should grant up front to keep credibility. (4) "The question I'm praying nobody asks" — 100–200 words on the most damaging obection and a line on how to survive it if it comes.
