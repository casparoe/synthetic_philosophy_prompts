The setup, spelled out so we're on the same page: a driver is on a highway with two exits that look identical, and he's absent-minded enough that when he's at an exit he can't tell whether it's the first or the second. If he takes the first exit he ends up in a bad part of town (payoff 0). If he takes the second exit he gets to his friend's place (payoff 4). If he blows past both exits he ends up somewhere mediocre (payoff 1). At each exit he picks 'exit' or 'continue', and since the exits are indistinguishable he can only follow the same rule at both — though the rule can be probabilistic.

The two positions:

1. The planner / precommitment view. The right question is what strategy maximizes expected payoff from the point of view of the driver before he sets off. That's exit-with-probability-1/3 at each exit, for an expected payoff of 4/3. Once you've solved that, you follow the plan and don't re-litigate it mid-trip. The mid-trip self is a biased sample: by the time you're actually at an exit, you've already made it past the first exit with some probability, so your self-locating beliefs are tilted by the selection effect, and re-optimizing on the road is how you get time-inconsistent behavior and end up worse off.

2. The updater / act-on-your-current-beliefs view. A plan you wouldn't endorse when your actually standing at the decision point isn't a real plan, it's a wish. When you find yourself at an exit, you have new information — you know you're at an exit — and you should condition on it. (Under the 1/3 rule, the on-the-road calculation says you're indifferent at an exit between exiting and continuing, so the rule happens to be stable; but the updater says the planner is right for the wrong reason.) The updater also thinks 'precommitment' is the planning self treating its future selves as different agents, which is a modeling choice, not a fact about rationality.
