I'm writing a term paper on the debate over whether large language models should be understood as agents, simulators, or something else, and I've noticed that participants all too often argue from a single, cherry-picked behavior: one person points to a model pursuing a goal inside a generated scenario, another to the fact that the same weights can be prompted into indefinitely many personae, another to the model's first-person self-reports. Before I read further, I want the specification of what a theory in this area would actually have to get right. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of what a language model is doing when it produces goal-directed or role-consistent text — adequate, that is, for the interpretive debate about whether these systems are agents in anything beyond a loose metaphor, not for predicting behavior or designing systems.

Cover at least these phenomena, with the consideration behind each requirement, and say in each case what the requirement guards against explaining away:

1. The base-model versus instruction-tuned or RLHF'd model. The same weights, before and after fine-tuning, can look like systems with quite different overall dispositions, so an account should say whether the agent/simulator verdict attaches to the base model, the fine-tuned model, both, or neither — and why fine-tuning should change the verdict if it does.
2. Persona-shifting under prompting. A single fixed set of weights can be prompted, or steered mid-generation, into indefinitely many apparent characters, roles, and situations that disagree with one another. An account shouldn't treat whichever persona is currently active as a stable agent unless it can say why, and it shouldn't explain away this flexibility by ignoring the base model.
3. Long-horizon, apparently instrumental behavior inside generated text: planning, subgoals, persistence of goals across a narrative, described tool use. The desiderata must determine whether this counts as evidence for an agent-like process, a simulated agent, or something weaker, and what would make the difference.
4. The phenomenon usually called sycophancy — outputs shifting to agree with the user's expressed views — and apparent situational awareness — outputs that seem to use facts about the model's own context, interface, or training. A theory must handle these without treating them as mere noise and without treating the contested label as an explanation.
5. First-person self-reports in which the model presents itself as an AI with certain properties, preferences, or limits. One camp treats these as direct evidence about an agent's beliefs; another treats them as role-play with no probative value. The desiderata must say what, if anything, such self-reports are evidence for, and why.
6. Mechanical facts about training and inference: pretraining as next-token prediction, subsequent fine-tuning against human feedback, and sampling procedures. An adequate account shouldn't contradict what is known about how the systems are built and run, although it may argue those facts underdetermine the interpretation.
7. A level-separation requirement. There are at least three places agency could be located here: the standing trained network, the particular process that unfolds during one episode of generation, and the character or situation depicted in the output. An adequate account must be explicit about the level or levels at which it assigns agency, and must not equivocate between levels to win the argument.

After the list, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one camp. Then say which subsets look jointly unsatisfiable or in serious tension — for instance, any view that wants to treat the same weights as a single standing agent while also taking persona-shifting seriously may have trouble satisfying both the first and second requirements — and state which single requirement does most to eliminate the leading candidate views. Finish with a brief scorecard of how the main families of answer fare against the list: the genuine-agent view, the simulator view, the pluralist or hybrid view, and the inert statistical-predictor view.

Throughout, keep separate what is settled — about the training procedure, the architecture, and the observable behavior — from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present one camp's interpretation as the consensus.
