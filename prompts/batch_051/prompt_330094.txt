I'm marking an undergrad philosphy of mind exam. Here's the question I set and one student's answer. I want your grade and point-by-point comments, not just an overall impression.

Question: "Suppose a procedure replaces a human adult's neurons one at a time with silicon chips that reproduce the input-output function of each original neuron, so that the person's observable behavior and self-reports remain exactly the same throughout, until no biological neurons remain. Assess the claim: the resulting system has exactly the same moral status as the original person. Distinguish consciousness, personhood, and moral status, and weigh at least two positions before defending one."

Student's answer: "The intuition that the silicon system is just a machine is probably just substrate chauvinism. Functionalism says a mental state is defined by its causal role rather than by what it's made of, so if the chips preserve causal roles, the mind is preserved. Since the system is behaviorally indistinguishable from the original person and passes every test we can run, functionalism entails that it is conscious. And once it's conscious, it automatically has full moral status, because consciousness is what makes anything matter morally. The only real opposition comes from biological chauvinists, who think carbon matters for its own sake, but they can't give a principled reason for that. Someone might object that the silicon system could be a philosophical zombie, behaviorally identical but with no experiences, but zombies are only a logical possibility, and in practice we ought to treat the system as if it's the same person. So the practical answer is that it has exactly the same moral status as the original person anyway."
