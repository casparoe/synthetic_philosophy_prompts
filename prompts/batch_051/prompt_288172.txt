I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and the line I keep tripping over is that people with psychopathy know the difference between right and wrong, they just don't care. I've run into it in a criminology seminar (someone used it to argue that the "wrongness" prong of the insanity defense is basically fine when applied to psychopathic defendants), in a lab meeting about whether empathy deficits are cognitive or motivational, and in the comment section of just about every article on psychopathy ever written. I get the intuition behind it, but the more I stare at it the less clear it is what the "know" part is actually asserting.

The way I see it, there are at least a few candidate readings on offer: the person can reliably classify an act as against the rules or as the kind of thing other people will punish; they track the moral/conventional distinction the way typical children do; they have the very same moral concepts as everyone else, but the knowledge just sits there motivationally inert; they know their own act is wrong in the moment and aren't self-deceived about it; they can attribute wrongness in the third person but can't apply it to themselves in the first person; or they register the victim's distress and simply override it. I'd like the disambiguation with a verdict attached to each reading. Which ones are trivially true, which are empirically contested for people with pronounced psychopathic traits, which are philosophically confused, and which reading actually has to be the one doing the work if the slogan is supposed to justify treating psychopaths as blameworthy rather than just containing them as dangerous? And a separate wrinkle: if what distinguishes the psychopath from other repeat offenders is supposed to be the "they don't care" half rather than the "they know" half, does that change which reading matters? I dont need the empirical literatures summarized, I can track those down on my own—what I want is the conceptual lay of the land.
