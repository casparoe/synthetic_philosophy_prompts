Last month our reading group worked through Kant's Idea for a Universal History from a Cosmopolitan Point of View, and the discussion stalled on a passage in the First Thesis, in Lewis White Beck's translation: "However obscure their causes, history, which is concerned with narrating these appearances, permits us to hope that if we attend to the play of freedom of the human will in the large, we may be able to discern a regular movement in it, and that what seems complex and chaotic in the single individual may be seen from the standpoint of the human race as a whole to be a steady and progressive though slow evolution of its original endowment."

Two members of the group, Daniel and Priya, ended the evening in a genuine standoff about it. Daniel took the passage to license a two-level epistemology: particular lives and local stretches of history look chaotic, but from the standpoint of the species a regular and progressive movement can be discerned, so the fact that any given life or century seems to go nowhere is no refutation of the old claim that the arc of history bends toward justice. Priya objected that Kant says only that history permits us to hope, and that the immediate context of the passage is the statistical regularity of births, marriages, and deaths, which shows only that aggregate human behavior falls under laws, not that the aggregate is progressive in any morally relevant sense. Daniel replied that her objection proves too much, because if disorder at one scale ruled out order at another, we would have to abandon any science that finds lawlike behavior in aggregates but not in their elements, and Kant's own weather analogy makes exactly that point. Priya answered that weather statistics support predictions, not hopes, and that the talk of a progressive evolution of an original endowment is doing teleological work the statistics cannot carry; the pattern appears, she said, only if one has already decided to read history as the unfolding of a single gift rather than as mere succession. Daniel then shifted to Kant's remark that giving up a principle of natural purpose would leave an aimless course of nature and blind chance in the place of the guiding thread of reason, and he argued that the hope is not a finding but a regulative assumption that makes historical inquiry possible at all. Priya replied that this move saved the method but abandoned the slogan: a maxim for inquiry does not tell us that the arc is long and bends toward justice, only that we should keep looking for a bend; and if the cost of keeping the maxim is redescribing as the standpoint of the human race a perspective no actual person occupies, the consolation is purchased by erasing exactly the suffering the arc was supposed to redeem.

I would like you to go through their actual exchange point by point and adjudicate it: for each move, say who is right about what, where a point is conceded or quietly changed, and what each of them is missing about Kant's passage and about the arc-of-history question. Do not just declare a winner; give a step-by-step verdict on the arguments as they were actually made, and then close with your own view of what the passage does and does not entitle someone to say. Write it all in continuous prose, the way one would write a careful email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
