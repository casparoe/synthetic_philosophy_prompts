I am preparing a course on Kantian ethics and have been struck by how much disagreement there is among intelligent readers about what the Formula of Universal Law is actually a test of. Before I commit to one reconstruction — or present one as the default to my students — I would like a specification of what an adequate account of Kant's universalizability procedure must get right, independently of which reconstruction ultimately wins.

Write the desiderata for a theory of how the universalizability test is supposed to work. It should cover: the canonical cases it must handle (the false promise, the suicide out of self-love, the neglected talents, the refusal to help others in need, and at least one or two less familiar or deliberately ambiguous maxims of your own choosing, such as a maxim of declining all assistance from others or of keeping a promise made to someone now dead, where the verdict is not obvious); the structural features of Kant's position it must preserve or explicitly explain away (the formal and a priori character of the test, the distinction between perfect and imperfect duty, the difference between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in the will, and the thought that the lawlike form of a maxim, rather than its consequences, is doing the work); and the distinctions it must not collapse — for example, between a maxim that cannot be universalized and one whose universal counterpart is merely undesirable or imprudent, between an agent's true maxim and a gerrymandered redescription of it, and between what is merely difficult for finite beings like us and what reason would reject in any rational being. For each desideratum, give the case or the structural consideration that makes it a requirement rather than an optional preference, and flag any pair of requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied.

Then, briefly, score the main families of reconstruction you are aware of — contradiction-in-conception-only readings, readings that treat imperfect duties through contradictions in the will, and procedural or constructivist readings built on the conditions of agency — against your list. Conclude by saying which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and which requirement you would be most willing to abandon, and with what loss.
