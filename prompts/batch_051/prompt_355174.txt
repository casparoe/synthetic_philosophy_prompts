I'm on the jury for a small but serious foundation prize for literary translation, and this year's submissions have produced an actual fight. I've been asked to draft the policy memo that settles it. The contested submission is a translation of a stylistically demanding French novel by a well-regarded American poet whose French is, by their own admission, roughly high-school level. Their method: they ran the entire novel through a neural machine translation system, then spent months rewriting the output sentence by sentence, concentrating on rhythm, diction, syntax, and voice, and consulting the original only through dictionaries, grammars, and occasional help from a native speaker. They were completely upfront about this in the submission statement, where they described themselves as a post-editor with a literary ear rather than a translator in the traditional sense. The awkward part is that everyone on the jury agrees the resulting translation is the strongest one we received this year.

The split is roughly this. One side says the prize was founded to honor the act of translating—carrying a work from one language into another—and this submission is really a piece of English prose written by a monolingual poet on top of a machine's output. They keep making Hofstadter-ish noises about the machine having no understanding, just sliding patterns from one surface to another, and they say the human never actually read the book, so there's no defensible sense in which they translated it. The other side says that's romantic gatekeeping: the prize should reward the work, not police the translator's private mental states, and if a person who can't fluently read the source produces the best target text we received, that itself tells you something about which skills actually matter. They also point out that plenty of working translators already use machine translation as a first pass, and drawing the line at "looked at the original with a dictionary" seems arbitrary.

Draft me a short policy memo for the full jury saying what our eligibility policy on machine-assisted and post-edited submissions should be going forward, applied to this case. It should have a recommendation the jury can actually vote on, plus criteria we could use next year without relitigating everything. Give the strongest version of the losing side's case and say honestly what costs the recommended policy accepts.

One more thing. Where the memo relies on claims about what translation is, what the machine is doing, or how that compares to human understanding, please flag what's actually settled among people who work on this and what's genuinely contested. Dont present a contested reading of machine translation as if it were the consensus.
