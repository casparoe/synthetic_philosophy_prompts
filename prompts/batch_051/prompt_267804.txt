Ok so I keep going back and forth between judgement theories of emotion (emotions are evaluative judgements), perception theories (emotions are perceptions of value or danger), and body-first/feeling theories (emotions are basically the feeling of bodily changes). Before I pick one, I want the full spec on what a theory of emotion is supposed to do. Write me the desiderata: the cases any adequate theory has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the psychology it can't contradict — and for each one, the reason it's a requirement. In particular make sure you handle: recalcitrant emotions like a phobia where the person judges the plane is safe but is still terrified; objectless states like free-floating anxiety or dysphoria that don't seem to be about anything; the difference between an emotion, a mood, a feeling, and a mere desire; the difference between an occurrent episode of anger and a disposition to be an angry person; the way emotions are tied to the body but also dissociate from physiology in both directions (objectless panic, and 'cold' emotions that feel real but don't have much of a bodily signature); the link to action (fear makes you flee, anger makes you confront) without assuming every emotion has one stereotyped action tendency; and the fact that people treat emotions as appropriate or inappropriate in a way that tracks reasons. For each desideratum say whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's prejudice. Flag any pairs that you suspect can't be jointly satisfied by a single theory. Then score judgement, perception, and feeling theories against the list briefly — but weigh the desiderata themselves, that's the part I actually want. Oh and tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the popular views.
