I am a high school student reading the 1890 Harvard Law Review article by Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis that first proposed a legal right to privacy. They write:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

I read this as an argument that the law should recognize a new kind of injury—mental distress caused by public exposure of private life—and that this injury becomes more serious as society grows more complex and refined. I am trying to work out what philosophical commitments a legal right to privacy based on this reasoning would presuppose.

Specifically, does the argument presuppose that privacy's value consists mostly in avoiding emotional pain, or does it lean on a deeper view about the self, about control over self-presentation, or about the need for a protected space in which to develop as a person? What would have to be true about the relationship between civilization, sensitivity, and harm for their argument to support a legal remedy rather than merely a complaint against bad manners? And if we look at how privacy rights actually operate today—for example, in lawsuits over disclosure of private facts or intrusion upon seclusion—do those practices carry the same presuppositions, or do they rest on a different and perhaps inconsistent theory?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of privacy and personhood that the argument presupposes, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theories could license it. Also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
