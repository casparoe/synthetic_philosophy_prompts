In my moral psychology seminar we went over Nozick's experience machine yet again, and I got stuck on a variant that nobody in the room would let me slow down enough to actually work through. The original case you know: you can plug in for a life of guaranteed pleasant experiences, but the experiences are simulations, your apparent achievements and relationships are fake, and most people say they wouldnt plug in. The standard reasons are usually something like: we want real contact with reality, we want to actually be the authors of our lives rather than passive receptacles of nice mental states, and we care about being a certain kind of person, e.g. brave or loving, not merely about how things feel from the inside.

Here's the variation I want to think about. Imagine a "sorrow filter" implant. It does not simulate anything, and it does not alter your beliefs, memories, plans, relationships, or actions. It selectively suppresses only the felt states of sadness and grief, more or less the way a beta blocker suppresses the bodily symptoms of anxiety while leaving the thoughts intact. If your mother dies, you still believe she died, you remember her, you go to the funeral, you can say the true and appropriate words — you just don't experience grief or sadness about it. You can still feel joy, anger, fear, amusement, aesthetic pleasure; you can even judge that the situation is sad, the way you can judge a cake would taste sweet without tasting it. The question is whether the standard anti-experience-machine considerations have any remaining force once the life being led is completely real and only the negative emotional coloring is edited out.

I can see the obvious hedonist line: all else equal, if the filter removes negative feelings and leaves everything else intact, of course you take it. But what I want is a careful mapping of how the three standard Nozick considerations fare. The reality-contact consideration looks like it evaporates — reality is fully known — unless you think grief is itself a way of being in touch with the loss rather than just a response to a fact you already believe. The being-a-certain-kind-of-person consideration might actually get stronger: maybe someone who cant grieve a parent fails to be the kind of person we'd want to be, and maybe that's not just about felt states but about what the absence of grief reveals about how one was related to the person. And for an objective-list theorist, is grief an intrinsic good, an instrumental good (for what, exactly — motivating repair, signaling to others, constituting love?), or merely a cost we pay for attachments that are themselves good? Finally, does this variation show the original debate was really about simulation and authenticity rather than hedonism itself, or is everyone who rejected the machine committed to rejecting the filter too? Work through what a hedonist, a desire-satisfaction theorist, and an objective-list theorist would each say about the filter, and, underneath all of that, whether a life with the filter would be better, worse, or merely more comfortable than a life without it.
