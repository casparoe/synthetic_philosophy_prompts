Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the social choice debate over Sen's liberal paradox.

Camp A, the decisiveness camp, holds that Sen's minimal-liberalism condition — under which at least two individuals are each decisive over some personal pair of social alternatives, the standard example being that one person is decisive over whether he reads a particular book and another over whether she reads it — captures the formal core of what a right is, and that Sen's impossibility result therefore exposes a genuine tension between respecting individual rights and the Pareto principle. Camp B, the rights-as-game-forms camp, holds that decisive power over a pair of alternatives misdescribes rights: a right should be modeled as a protected set of available strategies or a restriction on permissible outcomes, and once rights are modeled that way the conflict with Pareto is an artifact of the formalism rather than a deep feature of rights themselves.

Produce the document these two camps could both sign after a year of debate. It should contain:

(1) A numbered section of agreed claims. This should cover the exact statement of the theorem, including unrestricted domain, the relevant Pareto condition, minimal liberalism, and the consistency properties imposed on social preference; what a proof must establish; and what the formal result does and does not show about the possibility of a society that respects both rights and unanimity.

(2) A section of exactly formulated residual disagreements, written so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position as the strongest fair statement of it. Avoid slanted or concessive formulations.

(3) A crux table identifying the sub-questions on which the debate actually turns. Candidate cruxes include: what formal property something must have to count as a right; whether the alternatives in the standard examples are described at the right level of granularity; whether the Pareto principle should be read as a welfarist condition or merely as a unanimity condition; whether social preference must be acyclic, or whether incompleteness is an acceptable response to conflicting claims; and whether the paradox disappears once preferences are made conditional on how rights are exercised. Each side's position on each crux should be recorded.

(4) For each crux, at least one concrete case or formal test that both camps agree would bear on the crux, along with each side's prediction about that case.

Where a disagreement turns out to be verbal — for example, two senses of "right" or of "being decisive over a pair" — say explicitly which disagreement is verbal, what the two senses are, and which sense each camp is using.

Throughout, separate what is settled (the formal result, its proof strategy, and the relations among the conditions) from what is genuinely contested among those who work on formal social choice, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
