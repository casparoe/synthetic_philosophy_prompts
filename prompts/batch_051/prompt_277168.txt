Im in a senior seminar on the ethics of doing good, and we keep having the same argument about "systemic change" versus "marginal interventions." Everyone agrees the two are different in principle, but the discussion always collapses into people asserting different background pictures of how the world works. I want a clean, custom-built case that isolates one specific part of the disagreement, because I'm hoping to write my final paper on the idea that a lot of the systemic-vs-marginal fight is secretly an empirical bet about the shape of the causal curve and the speed of feedback. So please act as an expert on both the cost-effectiveness / effective-altruism side of this and the systems-thinking side, take your time, brainstorm two or three candidate setups before committing, and build me a thought experiment to the following specs.

One: the scenario should be an ordinary, realistic choice facing a donor or small organisation about a single concrete social problem — you pick the problem (evictions, adult illiteracy, childhood asthma from bad housing, whatever), but keep it non-science-fictional and plausible. Two: there are exactly two programs, a "marginal" one that addresses cases one at a time in a way you can cleanly count and attribute, and a "systemic" one that tries to change a rule, incentive, or institutional practice that generates the problem. Three: on a near-term evaluation that only uses measurable, attributable, short-run effects — the kind of thing a donor report or a cost-per-outcome table would show, with feedback inside one to three years and a conservative linear extrapolation from pilot data — the marginal program must come out clearly ahead. Four: on the same underlying values and a twenty-year horizon, once the actual causal structure of the systemic program is taken seriously (a delay plus some kind of threshold or coordination effect that you build into the case), the systemic program must come out clearly ahead in expectation. Five: the case can't work by stipulating the verdicts, and you can't make it work by making the systemic program's people smarter, the marginal program's funders short-sighted, or by hiding information. The only thing allowed to do the work is the difference in causal structure and feedback: smooth and fast versus delayed and threshold-y.

Then do the check for me. For each program, state the relevant beliefs, the estimation method, the time horizon, the threshold/delay parameters in the systemic case, and why each side's criterion gives the verdict it does. Then tell me which single feature of the case actually does the work, what happens to the two rankings if you remove that feature — e.g., by making the systemic effect linear, or by making the threshold reliably reached within a year — and how a defender of the marginal program would attack the case, including what they'd have to be shown to change their mind. And as you go, keep seperate what's genuinely settled in this debate from what's contested among people who know it; don't present one contested reading of "systemic" as the standard one. Be honest about any extra assumptions you had to add to get the case to meet the specs.
