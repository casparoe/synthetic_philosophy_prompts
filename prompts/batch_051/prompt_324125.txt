I've been turning over a question for a few years now, mostly in my spare time: why do people deliberately seek out novels, films and songs whose main effect is to make them miserable? I've settled on a view I call the calibration theory of tragic pleasure. The proposal is that we do not enjoy sad art despite the sadness, and we are not merely enjoying the craftsmanship that delivers the sadness. We enjoy it because it offers a rare opportunity to check that our emotions are still aimed at the right things. In ordinary life, grief, pity and fear arrive tangled up with self-interest, practical urgency, guilt and irrelevant detail, so it is almost impossible to judge whether what we feel is the right response or the right size. A well-made tragedy simplifies all this: it isolates a single clear cause of sorrow, removes any need for us to act, and implicitly asks for a particular emotional response. When we find ourselves producing something close to that response, we experience a quiet satisfaction which, on my account, is not pleasure taken in the represented suffering but pleasure taken in discovering that our emotional equipment is in working order. I think of it as the relief of a medical test that comes back normal, or the satisfaction of a musician who hits a difficult note and hears that it is in tune.

This single idea seems to explain several things I've noticed. It explains why a badly written sad story irritates us: not because it makes us sad, but because the response it is asking for is so muddled that the test can't be marked, and we are left with the cost of the sadness and no confirmation. It explains why people who are unsure whether they feel the right things tend, in my experience, to seek out sad music far more than people who are settled in their emotional lives. It also explains why we return to the same tragedy again and again: the satisfaction is not in a surprising turn of events but in the reliable return of the correct response, rather as one might play the same scale over and over to confirm an instrument is still in tune. I know there are older ideas about catharsis, and I know some people hold that we enjoy frightening or sad fictions because we feel safely in control. My claim is meant to be different: the pleasure lies in confirmation, not control. I'd like an honest assessment. Is the theory coherent? Has someone already proposed it under another name? Give me the three strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
