Two of my grad-school friends got into it about a third friend, Dana, and I honestly left not knowing who was right. Dana spent an evening re-analyzing a dataset and ended up pretty confident the intervention works. The next day she remembered she'd been running on four hours of sleep and heavy cold medicine, and she's said herself she wouldn't trust her own number-crunching in that state. But after re-checking the analysis, she kept her confidence, saying she couldn't find an actual flaw in it.

Sam said Dana was being epistemically akratic in a textbook way: her evidence about her own condition is higher-order evidence that her first-order reasoning is unreliable, and the rational response is to lower her confidence even if she can't pinpoint a specific error.

Lee said Sam had it backwards. The first-order evidence, the analysis itself, hasn't changed, and a person should follow their first-order evidence. Without an identified flaw, a generic worry about your machinery is no reason to abandon a conclusion; it would just be arbitrary self-doubt.

Sam came back: total evidence includes facts about your machine. If you'd discount a colleague's conclusion on the grounds that she was sleep-deprived, then by parity you should discount your own. And re-checking can't help, because the re-check is produced by the same compromised machinery.

Lee said that cuts both ways: if the machinery can't vouch for itself, then the higher-order judgment that the machinery is compromised is also produced by it and can't vouch for itself either. The only non-arbitrary place to stand is the first-order evidence, so Dana's confidence was fine.
