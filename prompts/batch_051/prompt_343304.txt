In my political philosophy seminar we have been discussing how a society could make its tolerance of risk something more principled than a sequence of post hoc panics and acquiescences. We appear to accept very large risks when they are familiar, such as private vehicle travel, while demanding the near-elimination of much smaller risks when they are new or vividly imaginable, such as certain food additives, consumer neurotechnology, or traces of radiation. A colleague in law-and-economics has proposed the following institution, and I would like you to analyze it as a mechanism rather than as an ideal.

The proposal is a risk-bond auction for novel hazards. Before marketing any product or service in a designated hazard class — synthetic biology, consumer neurotechnology, general-purpose AI, novel food additives, and the like — the producer must purchase a bond. The size of the bond is set by a reverse auction among licensed insurers: each insurer bids the minimum escrow it would accept, for a stated premium, to cover a published schedule of harms over a twenty-year liability window. The lowest escrow wins; the winning insurer must hold reserves equal to twice the escrow; verified claims are paid from the escrow first, and only then from the insurer's reserves. Regulators decide only which activities count as new hazard classes and which harms are eligible for claims.

Please work through what this scheme would actually do. What does it incentivize among producers, insurers, regulators, and users? How would each party game it? Attend especially to correlated catastrophes that hit many products at once, harms that emerge only after the liability window, harms that are clear in aggregate but cannot be traced to any individual producer, and firms that simply release the product from outside the jurisdiction. Does the scheme reveal a defensible social decision about acceptable risk, or does it turn that decision into a set of private bets likely to be mispriced? Identify one failure mode that could be fixed without gutting the mechanism, and one that probably cannot be fixed.
