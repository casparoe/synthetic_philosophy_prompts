In 2008 Kosovo declared independence from Serbia. It has a settled territory, a population, and a government that runs the place, and it has been recognized by many countries; but Serbia and a large number of other states still refuse to treat it as a state, and it is not a member of the United Nations. I have been turning this case over against two ways of understanding what makes something a state. On one view, a state exists when it has the things states have on the ground — a territory, a people, a working government, the capacity to deal with other states — and recognition by other states only notices what is already there. On the other view, being a state is also partly a status that other states give you: a government ruling a territory is not fully a state unless the rest of the world accepts it as one. I would like you to run the Kosovo case through both views carefully. For each view, say what it would conclude about whether Kosovo is a state, and what it would say the recognizing and non-recognizing countries were doing, or ought to have been doing, when they made their choices. Then identify which parts of the case each view handles well and which parts strain it: for example, why Kosovo has spent so much effort collecting recognitions if recognition is a mere formality, or why a place that looks state-like on the ground still cannot join the UN. Finally, say whether the case overall supports one view more, shows that the two views are really answering different questions, or reveals something both views miss. Try to be explicit about where each view stops being useful rather than stretching it to cover everything.
