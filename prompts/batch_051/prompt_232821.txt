Art authentication has always bugged me: almost everyone with money on the line wants the painting to be real—the owner, the auction house, the museum whose collection it anchors. There's no real constituency paid to find out a famous work is fake. So here's a mechanism I keep coming back to, and I want you to actually think through how it would behave rather than whether it's a good idea.

Create an international registry for works sold above some price. On each sale, the work gets a cryptographic certificate recording its physical fingerprint (canvas weave, pigment chemistry, underdrawing, repairs) plus provenance back as far as it goes. The certificate is a separately tradeable asset: you can buy a stake in the certificate of a painting you'll never own. If the work is later proven fake and delisted, the certificates become worthless, and the person who proved it collects a bounty, financed by a small tax on every certificate trade.

Walk me through what this acutally does. Who accumulates and who gets wiped out when forgeries surface? What happens to the relationship between an original's price and its certificate's price—do they decouple? Where does the scheme get gamed first: shorting certificates, experts churning out doubt to move the market, owners quietly burying research that might delist their work, forgers studying exactly which fingerprints the registry records so they can pass them? Which of these failure modes can be fixed by tweaking the bounty or the tax, and which ones are just baked in?
