I am a doctoral student in decision theory, and I have been trying to reconstruct the principle behind a series of judgements my supervisor has made about some unusual choice problems. Her record, as I have it, is this.

(1) The simulated Newcomb problem. A Predictor has perfect knowledge of how she will deliberate, because it ran a step-by-step simulation of her reasoning before she entered the room. On the strength of that simulation it placed £1,000,000 in an opaque box if it simulated that she would take only that box, and £0 if it simulated that she would take both boxes. A transparent box contains £1,000, which she may take whatever else she does. She says she would take only the opaque box.

(2) The twin. She and another agent face a symmetric prisoner's dilemma, but the other agent's move was made earlier by a process that duplicates her decision-relevant dispositions exactly: the other agent would have chosen as she actually chooses. She says she would cooperate.

(3) The open safe. As in (1), except that before choosing she can see what the Predictor has already deposited in the opaque box. She says she would take both boxes, whatever she sees.

(4) The bare dominance. In a choice with no other agent whose behaviour is correlated with hers, one act leaves her better off no matter how the chance events fall. She takes the dominant act without hesitation.

The new case I am now trying to settle is this. A different predictor, S, has constructed a statistical model of her choices from years of her shopping, browsing and social-media records, calibrated on thousands of other people. S has never represented her deliberation at all, yet S is right about ninety-five per cent of the time. The boxes are exactly as in (1): £1,000,000 in the opaque box if S predicted she would take only that box, and £1,000 in the transparent box; the prediction has already been made and the money deposited.

I would be grateful for the following. First, on the most careful reconstruction, what would her considered judgement about this new case be, if she is to remain consistent with the four verdicts above? Second, which of her earlier cases is the closest analogue, and what weight should it be given? Third, what competing principles fit her record equally well, and what does each principle predict for the new case? Finally, give your best guess about her verdict, with the reasons for preferring it, and name a further case, not among the four, that would cleanly separate the competing principles if the new case does not settle the matter.

Please answer in British English.
