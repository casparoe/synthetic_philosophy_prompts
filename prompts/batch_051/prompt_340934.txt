I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I have noticed that in arguments over what makes a statement racist, one side continually reaches for the dog-whistle analogy: a remark is like a dog whistle because it sends a message that its intended audience hears clearly while remaining inaudible, or at least deniable, to everyone else. In those debates the analogy does real work — it is used to justify shifting attention from the speaker's stated intent to the audience's uptake, and to refuse the exculpation that would otherwise follow from the words being innocuous on their face. I would like you to audit the analogy rather than take a side. Please set out the correspondence first: what plays the role of the whistle itself, the high-frequency tone, the dogs' hearing, the unhearing human bystanders, and the person blowing. Then identify the main points where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that undercut the conclusions people draw from the analogy from those that are incidental. Finally, say which of the conclusions the analogy is meant to carry — that deniable or coded meaning can make a statement racist, that explicit intent is not necessary, and that uptake by the target audience matters — survive the audit, and whether any other analogy captures what is right about the dog-whistle picture while importing less.
