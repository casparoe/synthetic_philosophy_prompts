You are a philosopher of mathematics education, well versed in Alfred North Whitehead's essay on the rhythm of education, and you approach pedagogy through concrete classroom mechanisms rather than slogans. In that essay Whitehead argues that learning advances through three phases: a phase he calls romance, in which the learner meets a problem with a sense of novelty and unfinished possibility; a phase he calls precision, in which exactness, definitions, and mastered detail dominate; and a phase he calls generalisation, in which the now-precise ideas are applied and integrated. He holds that precision imposed before romance has done its work produces inert ideas, knowledge the student can display on demand but cannot use. I am a novelist, and I want to write a realistic scene in which a mathematics teacher must choose between teaching a topic through motivating problems first or through formal definition and proof first.

Construct a thought experiment to these specifications. First, the scenario must concern a single, well-defined mathematical topic that an ordinary teacher could introduce in one week to students who have not seen it before; it must not rely on exceptional students, exotic curriculum materials, or improbable moments of insight. Second, one student enters the lesson with a specific, natural misconception about the topic, one a competent teacher could plausibly elicit. Third, under a romance-first approach, that student ends the week able to solve a novel problem requiring the concept, but unable, when asked directly, to give the textbook definition in full. Fourth, under a precision-first approach, the same student ends the week able to state the definition accurately and complete a simple proof, yet still holds the original misconception about what the concept is for, so that the misconception reappears when the problem is posed in an unfamiliar form. Fifth, the scenario must be rich enough for fiction: give the student's inner life and the teacher's observable moves, not just a schematic description.

Before building the scenario, brainstorm a short list of candidate topics and misconceptions, then narrow to the one that best satisfies the specifications; state your reasoning briefly. After the scenario, show the reasoning each pedagogical theory would give for its expected outcome, identify the single structural feature of the case that makes the two approaches diverge, and say what would happen if that feature were removed. Finally, explain how a defender of precision-first teaching would attack the thought experiment, and whether that attack reveals a weakness in Whitehead's order or in the way the case has been built.
