You are a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in applied ethics. Below is a question from the midterm exam, followed by a student's answer. The question concerns the doing/allowing distinction and the related killing/letting-die distinction. Please grade the answer. Give a letter grade, and then provide detailed point-by-point comments that justify the grade. Attend both to what the student gets right and to any steps that are unsupported, conflated, or too quick.

Question: To close a projected budget gap, a city council votes to eliminate a program that sends home health aides to roughly forty low-income disabled residents. The council's own estimates indicate that about eight of those residents will die within a year as a result of the cut. The deaths are not the council's goal; the council regards them as a foreseeable cost of fiscal responsibility, and it could avoid them by cutting other programs instead. A critic claims that the council is killing those eight residents. A councilor replies: "We are not doing anything to these people. We are simply not paying for a service anymore. If they die, we will have let them die—not killed them—and letting die is less bad than killing, so the criticism is overblown." Assess the councilor's reply using the doing/allowing and killing/letting-die distinctions from the course. In particular: does the distinction apply here, and, if it does, does it carry the moral weight the councilor assigns to it?

Student answer: The councilor is right that killing and letting die are different in a way the critic ignores. If I shoot a stranger, I kill him; if I walk past a stranger drowning in a pond, I let him die. These are not the same moral relation, so the critic's charge needs more argument. I also think the councilor classifies the action correctly: cutting a budget line is an omission rather than a positive act, since the council is not sending anyone to harm the residents but only declining to keep a service running. Because the policy's aim is fiscal order and the deaths are merely foreseen side-effects, the deaths fall under the principle of double effect and cannot be attributed to the council as intentional killings. So the critic is wrong to call it killing, and the councilor's use of the distinction is sound. However, the councilor's conclusion is too strong. Establishing that an agent lets die rather than kills does not by itself show that the act is permissible, since there can be strong duties to aid, and letting someone die can be just as serious as killing him when the agent had a duty and the cost was small. The councilor needs further reasons to show that those duties do not exist here or that they are outweighed. So I would say the killing charge fails, but the moral seriousness point remains unresolved.
