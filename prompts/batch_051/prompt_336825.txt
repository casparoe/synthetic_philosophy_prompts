I'm a PhD student in environmental philosophy, and in three weeks I'm giving a seminar to a conservation biology department on the claim that, from a suffering-focused welfare perspective, habitat destruction is often net positive, and that a lot of standard conservation rhetoric about "saving animals" quietly assumes what it's trying to prove. My core argument: if most wild animals live net-negative lives (predation, starvation, disease, parasites, failed reproduction), then any intact habitat is a standing source of future suffering. Bulldozing it kills some animals now, but that's a one-time cost you pay to turn off the spigot: carrying capacity drops, fewer animals are born into bad lives, and over time suffering prevented swamps the suffering caused. I'm not saying we should pave the Amazon; I'm saying the usual welfare-based case for habitat protection fails, and if conservationists want to defend habitat they need to do it on non-welfare grounds like biodiversity or naturalness, which many of them are squeamish about defending explicitly.

The audience is mostly empirical conservation biologists, a couple of them work on rewilding, and one co-authored a paper on the ethics of invasive species control. They're not hostile to ethics, but they think of habitat preservation as obviously good and will be suspicious of a philosopher telling them reduced carrying capacity is a win. I want a prep sheet, not a polished defense. Give me the objections roughly in the order they'll actually come at a seminar like this, phrased in their vocabulary. For each one: is it fair, what should I concede, what should I hold, and were is my reply weakest. Also tell me the one objection I should be praying nobody asks, and what to say if they do. And keep the answer in American English, and don't pull punches: if the argument only works because I've smuggled in an extreme population-ethics assumption, or because I've assumed a level of certainty about net-negative wild lives that nobody actually has, say so straight.
