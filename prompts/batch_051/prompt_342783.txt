I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of biology seminar on adaptation and constraint. We have spent the past few weeks on the adaptationist program and the spandrels challenge, and my term project asks me to stage an adversarial collaboration between a committed adaptationist and a committed defender of the Gould–Lewontin critique, and to produce the joint statement such a collaboration could actually issue. Please act as a professional philosopher of biology who knows this debate deeply. Before you write the final document, brainstorm several candidate formulations for the central claims, including formulations you would reject as either too slanted or too anodyne, and say briefly why you rejected them; then write the polished document.

The two positions need to be characterized carefully, not as caricatures. The adaptationist maintains that for a heritable trait that is complex, costly, or statistically improbable, natural selection is the best default explanation, and that a plausible reverse-engineering account of how the trait could have promoted fitness, together with evidence about its current function, justifies at least a provisional adaptive conclusion. The critic, in the spirit of the spandrels paper, maintains that organisms are integrated systems under multiple constraints—body plan conservatism, allometric growth, pleiotropic side effects, drift, and historical accident—so many traits will be nonadaptive byproducts or fixed without selection, and an adaptive explanation must clear a positive evidentiary burden rather than win by default. The two sides agree on common ancestry, on the importance of natural selection as a real cause, and on the existence of genuine adaptations; they disagree about burden of proof, about whether current utility is evidence of historical origin, about how to individuate traits, and about when a functional narrative counts as an explanation. I want the finished document to be one both camps could sign without embarrassment, not a list of platitudes.

Please format the output as follows: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section stating what both camps accept, including claims about the disagreement itself; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is formulated so that its own camp would accept the wording of its view and the other camp would accept the wording as a fair statement of the opposing view; and (3) a cruxes section. For each crux, specify one concrete case or test both sides agree would bear on it—use the human chin as a recurring test case, and add one further case of your choice—and record in advance what each side predicts about that case. Conclude by identifying which disagreements are substantive and which are partly verbal, and say exactly where the verbal component lies. Do not present the result as a dialogue or a ruling; the joint document itself is the deliverable. Write in American English.
