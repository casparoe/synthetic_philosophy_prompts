I've read enough rationalist commentary on content moderation to feel that the central question is seldom put sharply: not whether some speech should be restricted, but whose restrictions govern when a single piece of content is reachable from everywhere at once. Here is the counterfactual I most want someone to work through carefully.

Starting on 1 January 2031, a treaty enters into force among a coalition of states that together account for roughly four-fifths of the world's internet users. Its operative rule is simple: any person or firm operating a publicly accessible online service—a hobbyist forum, a wiki, a video host, a social network, anything carrying user-supplied content—is answerable in the courts of any signatory state whose residents can access the service, for any item of content that is unlawful under that state's speech, defamation, privacy, incitement, obscenity, insult, or blasphemy law. Liability attaches even when the operator did not target that state, owns no assets there, and made a good-faith effort to moderate. The identical content may be perfectly lawful in the operator's home state. Assume enforcement is credible: signatory states cooperate on freezing assets and detaining visiting executives, so non-compliance is not a costless option. Nothing else about the world changes.

Predict how the global internet looks in 2046, fifteen years on. I want the reasoning visible, built from incentives and mechanisms rather than from a narrative. In particular: which services go extinct or underground, which mutate, how content moderation changes as an economic and technical practice, whether small operators and individual creators are driven out, how states alter their own laws once the system has run for a few years, whether the equilibrium converges on the strictest signatory's standards or instead fragments into national enclaves, and what happens to encryption, anonymity, and decentralised protocols as exit routes. Mark which predictions you are most confident in and which hinge on adjustable facts—how concentrated hosting is, how easy it is for states to join or leave the treaty, how many categories of content are restricted by every signatory, and how willing users are to shift their attention to a service based in a non-signatory jurisdiction. Where a predicted effect could go either way, say what would have to hold for the other branch to occur.
