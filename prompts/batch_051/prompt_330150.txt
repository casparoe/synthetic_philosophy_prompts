I'm a first-year graduate student in an archaeology program, and last week I sat in on a lab meeting where two postdocs argued for almost an hour about how far an assemblage can take you toward reconstructing past beliefs. Neither of them knew I was taking notes. I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange, move by move.

The argument was about a newly excavated pit from a small settlement, dated to roughly 5000 BCE. The pit contained about forty ceramic anthropomorphic figurines, nearly all broken at the neck, with the heads placed in a separate cluster; red ochre on several of the broken edges; a few unfired or very low-fired clay lumps; assorted pottery fragments; and the articulated skeleton of a dog with no cut marks. The pit had been dug through a house floor and then sealed with clean clay. The excavators interpret it as evidence that the inhabitants regarded the figurines as animate and ritually killed them before depositing them to protect the house. Here is how the argument went.

(1) Ari said: The decapitation is too systematic to be accidental. When you find forty figurines broken at the neck, the obvious explanation is intentional breakage. And intentional breakage of figurines is one of the classic signatures of ritual killing of images, widely documented in the region's later periods. The excavators are in good company.

(2) Maya said: Intentional does not mean ritual. Deliberate breakage has plenty of mundane explanations: kiln wasters broken so they cannot be mistaken for good wares, or the disposal of damaged objects in a way that keeps the face from being trampled. To move from deliberate breakage to ritual you need a demonstrated link between breaking and ritual practice at this site or period, not an analogy imported from a later culture.

(3) Ari said: But the mundane explanations predict the wrong damage profile. A production dump should include overfired or warped wasters, and manufacturing breakage normally follows structural stresses through the body. Here the break is consistently at the neck, the part that later regional iconography treats as the seat of life. And the ochre sits on the broken edges, as if the break surfaces were anointed after the figurines were broken. Production waste does not get anointed.

(4) Maya said: The ochre detail is weaker than it looks. If the figurines were painted with red slip before firing or before use, a later break would expose more porous fabric that differentially absorbs ochre from the surrounding fill. And even if you grant that the break surfaces were deliberately colored, staged and ritual are not the same thing. People mark deposit boundaries, handle polluted matter, and prepare refuse in rule-governed ways without treating images as animate. You are jumping from not ordinary trash to belief in living images.

(5) Ari said: Then explain the context. This is not a dump. It is a sealed pit under a house floor, with a complete dog burial and no surrounding food refuse. Under-floor pits in this region are the standard place for household protective offerings, and the unfired clay lumps suggest making and depositing figurines happened in the same place. If you call this cleanup, you have to explain the dog and the sealing.

(6) Maya said: Something significant is doing all the work for you. A sealed pit with a dog tells us people deposited a dog and some broken objects with care; it does not tell us what they believed when they did it. The belief that figurines are animate is one story that fits the objects, but so are ancestor commemoration, the binding of a malevolent force, or the disposal of objects attached to a dead household member. The objects underdetermine the belief. Without independent constraints, the story is unfalsifiable.

I'd like you to do two things. First, rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Second, identify what, if anything, both sides are missing: what evidence, inferential principles, or distinctions would actually advance the question. Do not just declare a winner; tell me which specific claims survive and which do not.
