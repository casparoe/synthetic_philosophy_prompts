I write as a retired natural scientist who has come to formal epistemology rather late, and I often notice that two quite different claims about Bayesian updating are run together. The first is a family of formal convergence results: roughly, that two agents who begin with different priors but condition on a shared and indefinitely growing body of evidence will eventually find their predictive distributions, and in some cases their posterior beliefs, drawing close together. The second is the informal slogan that the priors wash out, so that once the evidence is large enough it ceases to matter where an inquirer started. I should like you to compare these as distinct positions rather than as a theorem and its paraphrase. Please set out, as precisely as you can without consulting any source, what the formal results really assume and establish, and then say exactly where the slogan goes beyond them or alters their force. As a test case, consider two agents who assign different priors over the same set of statistical hypotheses, none of which is ruled out by what is observed, and who then receive the same very long run of data: do their posteriors merge outright, or does some aspect of their disagreement survive, and if so which aspect and for how long it retains practical significance? I should also like to know whether the slogan can be made true only by importing assumptions that the formal results do not themselves supply, such as conditions on the statistical model, on the support of the priors, or on the sense in which the evidence is shared, and whether importing those assumptions changes the philosophical work the slogan is asked to do. Please close with your own judgement as to whether the claim that priors wash out is best read as a theorem of probability, as a heuristic that is true enough for working scientists, or as Bayesian folklore to be given up. Reply in continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
