Next month I am defending a paper at a philosophy colloquium that argues Harsanyi's aggregation theorem yields a substantive form of utilitarianism. The argument runs as follows: individual preferences over lotteries obey the von Neumann–Morgenstern expected-utility axioms; social preference ought to obey the same axioms if collective choice is to be coherent under uncertainty; and a Pareto condition requires social preference to respect unanimous individual preference. Harsanyi's theorem then forces the social utility function to be a weighted sum of individual utilities. My contribution is to argue that the standard objection — that arbitrary weights make the conclusion a family of views rather than a single moral theory — collapses once each person's utility is normalized to a common scale bounded by their best and worst feasible lives; the remaining disagreement, I claim, concerns the normalization itself rather than the aggregation. The audience is a philosophy department colloquium: the normative ethics group is mostly Kantians and contractualists, with two consequentialists who know the Harsanyi literature, while the economics department is sending two microeconomists and a social choice theorist. The philosophers will press the moral authority of the Pareto condition and the normalization; the economists will press the single-profile underdetermination of the weights (the fact that a representation theorem stated against one fixed profile of preferences secures only that some weighted-sum representation exists, not a unique set of weights), the independence axiom for social preferences, and the ex ante/ex post distinction. Write my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I should actually expect from this room, ranked by likelihood and by the damage each can do, each phrased the way the person would actually ask it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft, plus the question I am most hoping nobody asks.
