Exam question: "In class we wrote the paradox of tragedy as an inconsistent triad: (i) we deliberately seek out and enjoy tragedies; (ii) tragedies arouse pity, fear, sadness, etc.; (iii) we normally avoid and don't enjoy experiences that arouse those states, because those states are unpleasant. Explain Aristotle's catharsis answer and Hume's conversion answer. For each, say which claim in the triad it rejects or qualifies. Then say which answer you think is stronger and why, in 4–6 sentences."

Student answer: "The paradox is that the three claims are each plausible but they can't all be true together. Aristotle's solution is catharsis: tragedy stirs up pity and fear and then purges them, so the audience leaves feeling cleansed and relieved. That relief is the pleasure, so the third claim is false, since the experience isn't unpleasant on the whole. Hume instead says the painful passion is converted by the pleasure we take in the craft and eloquence of the writing. The artistry dominates the painful affect and transforms it into pleasure, so he also rejects the third claim. The two views basically agree that the negative emotion gets drained off—for Aristotle by purgation, for Hume by the dominance of aesthetic pleasure. The only difference is what does the draining. I think Hume is stronger because Aristotle's account only works if the audience already has a buildup of emotion that needs discharging, but people enjoy tragedies even when they aren't emotionally pent up, while Hume's appeal to well-crafted writing applies to everyone."
