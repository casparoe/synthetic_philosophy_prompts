ok so I need a sanity check. I've gotten way too into iNaturalist over the past year, and I keep running into the same fight: whether two things are 'the same species.' Not just online bickering — our local birding group nearly split over the herring gull / lesser black-backed gull thing, and then a friend pointed out that the Endangered Species Act in the US hinges on exactly this kind of classification. So it's not just a nerdy question, and now I actually want a workable definition of 'species.'

Here's my list of cases that any defintion has to handle. For each one, tell me whether it's one species or two (or more), and why.

1. A chihuahua and a great dane. Everyone says same species, but they can't physically mate with each other — they're only connected through a chain of other dogs. So 'can interbreed' can't quite mean this pair directly.

2. A lion and a tiger. Everyone says different species, but in zoos they produce ligers and tigons, and I think the female hybrids are sometimes fertile. So 'can't interbreed' can't be the whole rule either.

3. Herring gull vs lesser black-backed gull. In Britain their clearly two species and they don't mix, but if you follow the populations around the Arctic, each neighboring population interbreeds with the next, and the chain wraps all the way back around to two end points that don't interbreed. Supposedly the Ensatina salamanders around California's Central Valley do the same thing.

4. Bdelloid rotifers. Tiny invertebrates, apparently no males and no sex for tens of millions of years. Any breeding-based definition just doesn't touch them, but we still call different bdelloids different species.

5. Two E. coli strains, one harmless gut bug and one that gives you food poisoning. They swap genes around horizontally all the time, so 'which lineages interbreed' doesn't make sense. Are they species at all?

6. Us and Neanderthals. Textbooks mostly say separate species, but we clearly interbred (I have Neanderthal DNA in my 23andMe), so some people say they're a subspecies of us.

7. Przewalski's horse and the domestic horse. Different chromosome numbers (66 vs 64, I think?), but they'll cross and the foals are fertile. Same species or not?

8. The red wolf. Depending who you ask, it's its own species or just a hybrid swarm of coyotes and gray wolves. If its genome is mostly a mix of two other species, is it a third species?

9. A fossil lineage where shells change gradually over a few million years with no obvious jump. Where do you cut it into species A and species B, and is the cut a fact about the shells or just our filing system?

I'm starting to suspect that 'species' is doing two jobs at once: one about what organisms actually do out in the wild (mating, populations, ecology), and one about what we need for sorting and naming things in field guides, conservation law, and museum drawers. If you think that's right, give me two definitions and say which cases flip between them. If you think one definition survives all nine, even better — but then defend the weird verdicts it forces on the ugly cases. Either way, run your definition through every case and say where it breaks. Don't just recite the biological species concept; I've seen it, and it dies on at least half of this list.
