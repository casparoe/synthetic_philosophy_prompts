I am a retired natural scientist, and I have been spending my retirement working through the philosophy of religion, mostly William James. The Varieties of Religious Experience has left me stuck on his account of mysticism, and I would like help thinking through it rigorously.

As I understand James, he identifies several marks of mystical experience, two of which sit in tension with each other. The first is ineffability: the experience cannot be adequately rendered in words. The second is a noetic quality: the experience presents itself as a state of knowledge, an apprehension of something, rather than as merely an emotion or mood. On James's view, ineffability does a great deal of argumentative work. Because the alleged content of a mystical state cannot be formulated, it cannot be offered as public evidence, criticized, or confirmed the way an ordinary claim can; the experiencer is left with a private, noetic authority that is compelling for them but untransferable to anyone else.

Since James wrote, the empirical situation has changed in ways he could not have anticipated. Researchers now frequently induce experiences classified as mystical under controlled conditions, especially in psilocybin studies, and identify similar states in long-term meditators. They score these experiences on instruments like the Mystical Experience Questionnaire, compare them across subjects and sessions, record their physiological and neural correlates, and collect long, structured first-person reports. To the habits I brought from a career in science, this looks like a phenomenon that can be reliably evoked, measured, and discussed—which sits uncomfortably with the claim that what such an experience contains cannot be said.

I would like you to examine whether James's ineffability thesis survives this development, and if so, in what repaired form. Please first distinguish the candidate claims one might mean by calling an experience ineffable—for instance: (a) it cannot be put into words at all; (b) it can only be expressed partially or at the cost of distortion; (c) it cannot be communicated to someone who has not had the relevant experience; and (d) it cannot be adequately characterized from the third-person point of view. Then say which of these the empirical research could in principle confirm, disconfirm, or leave untouched, and why. I especially want to know whether measuring and predicting a state shows that its content can be expressed, or only that its occurrence and its correlates can be described. Then turn to the noetic quality: if science can say what happened to a subject and how it was brought about, does that bear on whether the subject knows something, or only on what the knower is like? Finally, if the original thesis does not survive intact, state the strongest replacement you can construct—one that gives mystical experience whatever distinct epistemic status it can still claim, without attributing more than the science allows. I am interested in the conceptual structure of the argument, not in a survey of the empirical literature.
