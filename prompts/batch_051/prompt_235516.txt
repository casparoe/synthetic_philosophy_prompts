States now regularly issue formal apologies for wrongs committed before most current citizens were born — colonial dispossession, chattel slavery, the forcible removal of indigenous children, the wartime internment of citizens, mass atrocities. Yet the same documents are usually drafted with conspicuous caution: they voice "regret" or "deep sorrow," avoid language that would admit legal liability, and are frequently unaccompanied by reparations or paired with settlements that expressly deny fault. Reconstruct the conception of collective responsibility, moral agency, and apology that this practice presupposes. Does an apology issued by people who did not commit the wrong, to people who often did not directly suffer it, treat the state as a single moral agent persisting across generations? If so, why does the practice stop short of the obligations such an agent would normally incur; if not, what is the speech act supposed to be doing? Explain whether the practice is philosophically coherent, and describe what state apology would have to look like if rebuilt on a strongly collectivist view, and on an individualist view on which no present person is answerable for the deeds of the dead.
