A polity is considering replacing its categorical constitutional rights with the following device. Each year an independent Rights Board may issue a strictly limited number of override warrants, fixed in the constitution at one warrant per 250,000 residents and not alterable by ordinary legislation. A warrant permits a named public agency to perform one act that would otherwise be an infringement of a listed civil right — searching a home, seizing property, restricting a person's movement for up to seventy-two hours, intercepting communications, or compelling the production of documents under penalty — against a particular person or narrowly described premises. Acts involving bodily harm cannot be licensed. Warrants must be obtained in advance, cannot be renewed or transferred between agencies, and no person may be made the subject of a warrant more than once in any five-year period. Every warrant triggers automatic monetary compensation, paid from general revenue, to the affected person according to a fixed statutory schedule, and one year after execution — or earlier if the subject requests — the warrant and the agency's stated justification are published. Officials who act without a warrant remain liable exactly as they would be under the old bill of rights. The designers defend the quota by saying it preserves rights as side constraints: infringement becomes possible only as a scarce, public, compensated exception, never as an ordinary policy tool.

Treat the proposal as a functioning institution and analyze how it would actually behave, not whether it is desirable in the abstract. How would the Rights Board's allocation decisions be pulled by security agencies, politicians, and press attention? Which rights would consume the quota and which would quietly lose protection? What adaptations would emerge in police and intelligence services, among legislators, and among citizens who know that the supply of legal infringements is limited? Identify the main games and failure points — for example around the definition of a single act, the one-year publication delay, the five-year per-person rule, the fixed compensation schedule, and the ban on transfer — and say how each might be exploited. Finally, assess what happens to the concept of a right under the scheme: in practice, would rights operate as trumps, as side constraints, as instruments valued only through their consequences, or as something else, and which features of the scheme would have to change for that outcome to be different?
