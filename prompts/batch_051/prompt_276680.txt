I've been in a moral philosophy reading group that's been stuck on the Taurek-style rescue case for what feels like forever: you can save one stranger on one road, or five strangers on another road, no special ties, same effort. Everyone agrees about the facts. Still, one side says it's obvious you save the five — five deaths are worse than one, and refusing to add up harms is just squeamishness. The other side says the numbers don't count: each of the six has a claim on you, but there's no seventh party made of the five, so the right move is to give each person an equal chance — flip a coin — even if that means the expected death toll is higher and the one person might die. What bothers me is that this doesn't behave like a normal moral argument: no new fact seems like it would change anyone's mind, and nobody exactly denies the other side's premises; they just refuse the move from "each matters" to "more of them matter more." The group hasn't moved a inch in months.

So my question isn't which side you'd take. It's a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a bedrock intuition about the separateness of persons that neither side can argue for, a hidden disagreement about what "worse" or "a claim" means, or two different questions — one about which outcome is impersonally better, one about what you owe each individual — being run under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new distinction, a formal result, a reason to think the question is ill-posed, or something else? I don't want a literature survey.
