i'm on the professional standards comittee of our navy, and we have to rule on a case that's split us down the middle. Two years ago a junior watch officer was on night watch with a captain who had thirty years in. Her radar showed a fishing boat taking water far out, and she thought there was a real but narrow window to get a rescue launch out and back before a storm cell closed in — she said so once. The captain overruled her, said the sea was already too rough and he wasn't risking a crew, and she deferred without pressing it further, even though she kept thinking the window was there. By the time the storm eased the boat had gone down, four of six crew lost. A separate review cleared the captain: his call was within his discretion. The post-hoc modelling of whether a launch would've made it is genuinely disputed, so it doesn't settle anything. So now we have to decide wether to formally censure the watch officer for not pushing her objection harder when lives were on the line, or to clear her. Half the comittee thinks this is just the destructive-obedience pattern Milgram studied, wearing a uniform: a lawful order doesn't settle whether she abandoned her own judgment. The other half thinks this is exactly the deference a chain of command runs on; punish her for not overriding a vastly more experienced captain on a marginal weather call, and every junior officer starts freezing up during the next rescue. Write the short decision memo for us: a clear recommendation (censure, clear, or clear with conditions), the reasoning, and a fair treatment of the strongest point on the losing side. After the memo itself, add a short note to me saying how confident you are in the recommendation and which point in it you're least sure about.
