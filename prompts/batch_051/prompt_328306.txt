I'm a retired microbiologist, and I've spent the last few years reading philosophy of biology — its either a midlife crisis or the best decision I've made since I stopped running gels. Next month I'm giving a talk to a weekly reading group that has two philosophers of biology, two astrobiologists who work on biosignatures, and one origin-of-life chemist. My talk defends what I call a two-question view of life: instead of asking what life is, as if the word needed a single set of necessary and sufficient conditions, we should ask two separate questions and let them come apart. Question one is a lineage question: does this system belong to a Darwinian lineage — a population with heredity, variation, and selection? Question two is a maintenance question: is this system currently holding itself together as an organized, far-from-equilibrium system, with metabolism, a boundary, and some kind of control? The claim is that the classic sticky cases — viruses, bacterial spores, a frozen but viable organism, obligate endosymbionts, a cell in late apoptosis — look like contradictions only because people insist that one question should settle all of them. On my view, a frozen bacterium is alive but currently inactive, a virus is active but not alive, and a spore is alive on the lineage question but paused on the maintenance question. The payoff I'll pitch is for astrobiology: stop treating life signs as one signal, and look separately for evidence of self-maintenance, like metabolic disequilibrium, and evidence of a historical Darwinian process, like informational patterns, then treat them as two lines of evidence that usually but need not converge. My argument in a nutshell is that these two questions already track the real division of labor — physiologists ask the maintenance question, evolutionary biologists ask the lineage question — and a single definition forces a false choice, while detection technology is already split along the same line, so being explicit is just honesty about what the instruments measure. I know the philosophers are sympathetic to a cluster-concept or definitional-project-is-misguided line, and that the astrobiologists are pragmatists still attached to an operational definition; the chemist is metabolism-first. I've also been warned that one philosopher is fond of the autopoiesis tradition, Maturana and Varela's self-making systems, and will push on the internal-organization side.

I don't need a literature review or citations; I need a Q&A prep sheet for this exact room. Structure it as follows. First, a section called "Reading the room" (roughly 150 words): who at that table will push on which part of the view, and why, in their own idiom. Second, "Objections, in the order they'll actually come" (roughly 1000 words): give me the seven or eight objections I should most expect, each phrased the way that person would say it out loud, then a short reply of 120–150 words, then a one-sentence note on where that reply is soft. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, not by how intellectually interesting they are. Third, "Concede it up front" (roughly 120 words): the things I should say in the opening minute that will stop an objection from hardening into a fight. Fourth, "The question I hope nobody asks" (roughly 100 words): the one question that would make the room smell blood, plus a hint of what I could say if it comes anyway.
