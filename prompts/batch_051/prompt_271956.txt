My training was in the natural sciences, and I have recently begun reading philosophy. I have been puzzling over material constitution, and my verdicts on particular cases seem not to add up to a single view. (1) When a sculptor forms a statue from a lump of clay, I judge that the lump and the statue are distinct: the lump existed before the statue and survives its being flattened, while the statue does not. (2) Yet when I look at a bronze statue, I cannot really believe two material objects are there; I want to say the bronze statue just is the bronze arranged statue-wise. I recognize that (1) and (2) sit badly together. (3) When a gold ring is melted and recast as a bracelet, I want to say the gold persists but the ring does not. (4) When a wooden ship has every plank replaced one by one, I judge the ship persists throughout; but if the original planks had been saved and reassembled, I would not know which candidate was the original ship. (5) When a drop of water evaporates and later condenses, I regard the water as the same through the change though the drop is gone. Is there a single principle about the relation between matter, form, and the persisting object that captures all of these? If so, state it precisely and apply it to a further case: a river that changes all its water over time while keeping its course. If not, which verdicts should I revise, and what is the best unified view that remains?
