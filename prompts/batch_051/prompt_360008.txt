I direct the small-grants program at a philanthropic foundation whose mission is to improve the long-term future. We draw from a fixed annual pool of about $40 million, and twice a year we receive a wave of proposals that invoke the "hinge of history"—the claim that our period has unusually high expected influence over the far future, so that delay forfeits leverage we will never recover. Our review panel of three nonspecialists handles roughly sixty of these a year, usually in under an hour per proposal, and our board will not approve an accelerated grant without a written rationale. I want a standing decision procedure, grounded in what that hypothesis actually entails as an empirical thesis rather than in its rhetorical use as a fundraising trope, for deciding when a proposal's hinge claim should count as a genuine reason to fund now and when it should be treated as noise. The procedure should be a checklist or ordered set of tests, worked through two or three cases—one in which it recommends rejecting a proposal the panel finds intuitively compelling—and it should say explicitly which calls the procedure cannot make on its own.
