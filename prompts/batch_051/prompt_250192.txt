I compose and arrange for a small ballet company, and most of our scores are built from a small set of recurring themes that get reworked for different characters, moods, and scenes. During production season I produce variations on a theme several times a week, usually with only a few hours per cue, for a fixed set of players, and for a choreographer who needs to hear the connection to the original immediately — while also rejecting anything that is merely the theme transposed or re-orchestrated as lazy. I've been reading about Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model of creativity: roughly, the idea that a theme is a concept with a skeletal structure and a set of adjustable parameters, or knobs, and that creative variation means turning some of those knobs while keeping the identity of the thing intact. I want to turn that model into a procedure I can actually use at the desk.

Write me a checklist or decision sequence, usable by someone who has never read Hofstadter, for: (1) extracting a theme's knobs; (2) sorting them into load-bearing and cosmetic; (3) choosing which knobs to turn for a given dramatic need without breaking the theme's identity; and (4) testing whether the result is still a variation on the theme or has become a new piece. Flag the steps that are genuinely judgment calls rather than technique, and say which distinctions in the model must survive the simplification.

Then apply the procedure to two or three worked cases drawn from this kind of work. Include one clearly recognizable variation, one borderline case — for instance, a cue that keeps the harmonic progression but replaces the melody, which I have a recurring argument with the choreographer about — and one where the procedure tells me something I won't like, such as that a variation I was attached to has in fact stopped being a variation on the theme at all. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: which creative decisions it leaves untouched and where it will misfire if I follow it mechanically.
