Answer as a professional historian of the First World War who is also fluent in the vocabulary of reliability engineering and incident postmortems. I lead an incident-review practice at a company that runs large distributed systems, and my working vocabulary is the one from reliability engineering and blameless postmortems: triggers and preconditions, contributing factors, single points of failure, loose and tight coupling, latent conditions and active failures, cascading failures, blast radius, severity, error budgets, and corrective actions. I habitually distinguish between asking who caused the outage and asking which conditions, if removed, would have prevented the outage or shrunk its blast radius.

I have been reading about the historians' debate over responsibility for the First World War — the Fischer controversy — but only as a layperson. Here is my best summary of the positions:

1. The deliberate-war position, associated with Fritz Fischer. Germany's leaders were not dragged into the July Crisis; they used it. They gave Austria-Hungary a blank cheque of unconditional support, pressed Vienna to act before mediation could take hold, and had annexationist war aims ready early: a memorandum drafted in the chancellor's office in September 1914, the September Programme, listed far-reaching territorial and economic goals in a way that looks like a statement of pre-existing intentions rather than an improvised reaction. On this view, Germany is the principal author of the war, which its leadership deliberately risked or sought in order to break out of encirclement and become a world power.

2. The systemic-failure position. The July Crisis was a runaway failure in a tightly coupled system. Every great power contributed conditions: alliance blocs, interlocking mobilization schedules, arms races, colonial rivalry, nationalism, domestic political incentives. The assassination at Sarajevo was the trigger, but no leadership fully controlled the escalation machinery, and none fully wanted the general war they got. On this view, pinning primary guilt on Germany is like naming the last component that failed in a system built to cascade.

3. The in-between position. Germany's July 1914 decisions — above all the blank cheque and the pressure on Austria — were the single largest contributing decision, but causal contribution and blameworthiness are not identical, and the legal war-guilt clause written into the Treaty of Versailles in 1919 is different from a sober historical attribution distributed across several governments. This position also asks whether the annexationist aims caused the war or were improvised after it had begun: if they were improvised, the deliberate-war position weakens considerably.
