I'm in a politics and literature reading group at the moment, and the conservative contingent keeps using Chesterton's fence as the whole arguement for Burkean conservatism. As they tell it: a reformer comes across a fence across a road, sees no point to it, and sets out to tear it down, and the wiser reformer stops him and says he can't remove it until he can say what it was for. In their hands, the fence stands for any long-standing arrangement (a law, a custom, an office, a ceremony), and the moral is that reformers carry the whole burden of proof, and that the fact we can't see the point of a practice is itself evidence that there is a point and we're just missing it.

I've been rereading Burke alongside this, and it feels like the analogy is doing more work than it can bear. It seems to slide from the thought that someone once had a reason for the arrangement, to the thought that there is a good reason for it, to the thought that we should keep it now; but I can't quite make the criticism stick. So I want an audit: how far does Chesterton's fence actually carry Burkean conclusions? Lay out what corresponds to what (the fence, the road, the reformer, the demand to find the use, the permission to tear it down), say where the mapping holds, where it breaks, which of the disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion and which are only cosmetic, and whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If you think some other analogy does a better job of capturing what Burke actually claims, say what it is and why.
