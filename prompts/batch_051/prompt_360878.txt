For a long article on private cities, charter cities, and seasteading, I want to subject one recurring claim to a kind of test it rarely receives. The claim, as proponents make it, is that when jurisdictions must compete for residents, political subjection becomes genuinely voluntary in a way it never is under an ordinary state, because one can switch governing providers without also switching country, language, or the rest of one's life in the way ordinary emigration forces. Critics reply that this is a moralized redescription of a merely quantitative difference in exit costs: every state sits somewhere on a spectrum of how easy it is to leave, so calling one arrangement "consensual" and another "coercive" does no empirical work. I would like to report the disagreement itself rather than simply transcribe it. Imagine I can study an operating private city or seastead alongside a conventional municipality, over a long enough period to observe how residents actually respond when the rules change, when costs rise, or when a competitor appears. Design the strongest empirical probe you can of the proponent's claim: what should be measured, compared, or observed; what pattern would favor the view that the residents have genuinely opted out of the state; what pattern would favor the view that the difference is only one of degree; and what even the cleanest outcome would fail to settle. In particular, tell me which part of the claim can be operationalized and which part cannot, and whether the residue that resists testing is normative, conceptual, or an ambiguity in the phrase "opt out of the state" itself.
