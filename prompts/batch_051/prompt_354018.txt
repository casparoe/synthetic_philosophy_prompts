The institute where I work — a mid-size foundation that makes a small number of high-stakes, hard-to-reverse grants — has been debating whether to replace ordinary committee deliberation with a decompositional procedure modeled on the idea of factored cognition: the idea that hard intellectual work can be broken into many small subtasks, each doable with bounded effort, and then recombined into a good answer. Its best-known idealized form is the recursive scheme called HCH, in which a hard question is split into subquestions and each subquestion is answered by a human who sees only a short summary of the answers below it. I have been asked to think through how such a scheme would actually play out before we trial it, and I would value a careful analysis.

The proposed rules are these. For each decision, a two-person root team divides the central question into no more than five subquestions. A dispatcher, who sees the subquestion texts but not the parent question, assigns each to a worker. Each worker gets one hour; a worker may either answer the subquestion directly or split it one level further. When a worker splits, the only thing passed upward is a 300-word synthesis of the children's answers, a confidence score from 1 to 5, and at most three flagged disagreements. The lower-level reasoning and transcripts are sealed and never shown upward. Workers are not told who sits above or below them. A random sample of nodes is audited after outcomes become known, and workers are paid partly for calibration; the root team's bonus depends only on the quality of the final decision, not on agreement with any prior position.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than approved or condemned wholesale. I want to know what it would actually do in a real institution staffed by people with careers, status, and finite candor: what traits it would select for among workers and root teams; how each role could be gamed in ways that satisfy the letter of the rules while defeating their purpose; what kinds of error would be amplified, filtered out, or compounded as information moves upward through summaries; and which steps in ordinary deliberation — shared context, tacit knowledge, the ability to challenge upstream reasoning, direct exposure to the underlying evidence — are doing load-bearing work that the scheme assumes can be compressed. Say where the underlying idealization, that a good decision can be assembled from bounded pieces plus short summaries, is most likely to fail, and whether the failures are fixable by modest changes such as longer syntheses, allowing some upward inspection, surfacing disagreements, or reverting to unstructured deliberation below a certain depth — or whether those changes undo the point of the exercise.

In your answer, please keep distinct what follows more or less mechanically from the rules from what is genuinely contested among people who study factored reasoning and group deliberation, and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus.
