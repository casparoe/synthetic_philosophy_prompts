I'm a student in a course on the ethics of promising and obligation, and the readings mostly focus on large promises—treaties, vows, deathbed assurances—but I'm more interested in the small commitments that actually structure ordinary life. I've collected my own verdicts on a set of everyday cases, and I can't tell whether any single principle lies behind them. I'd like you to work out whether a principle captures them, which of my verdicts it would force me to give up, and what it says about a further case I describe at the end.

My verdicts:

1. I tell a friend I'll be at their apartment for dinner at 7:00, leave too late, arrive at 7:40, and never text; they waited to start and the food has gone cold. This is a genuine wrong, not a trivial one.

2. Same situation, but I arrive at 7:10 without texting. Mildly wrong; an apology is owed, nothing more.

3. I say "we should definitely get coffee sometime" to someone I have no intention of seeing; neither of us follows up. Not a broken promise; at most a small flaw in character.

4. I cancel lunch two hours beforehand because I've come down with a migraine. Fine.

5. I cancel lunch two hours beforehand because another friend has offered me a spare ticket to something I'd much rather do. Not fine; my promise wasn't conditional on no better offer appearing.

6. I promise to help a friend move on Saturday, but that morning I cancel because I stayed up late and feel exhausted; they tell me they turned down two other offers because they were counting on me. Not fine.

7. Same moving promise, but I cancel because my father has just been taken to hospital. Fine.

8. I promise my grandmother I'll call on Sunday, forget, and call Monday; she says she didn't mind. Still mildly wrong, even though she wasn't waiting by the phone.

9. A friend is habitually ten or fifteen minutes late. I find the standing pattern more aggravating than a single forty-five-minute lateness from a normally punctual person. I feel the pattern is worse in a way that isn't just the sum of the individual delays, though I'm not sure that feeling is fair.

Is there a principled account—something about reasonable expectations, reliance, the content of what was promised, or the quality of the excuse—that gets all of these, or close to it? If not, which of my verdicts should I revise? And apply the resulting principle to the case I keep flipping on: I tell a colleague on Tuesday that I'll look at a short memo "today," don't get to it, and they stay late waiting for comments that I never expected them to wait for. Does my obligation track what I actually committed to, or what it was reasonable for them to infer?
