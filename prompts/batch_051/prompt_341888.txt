I write novels for a living, and over the past few years I've found myself more and more persuaded by something like strong historical materialism: the idea that the economic base — the forces and relations of production, who owns what, how surplus gets extracted — ultimately determines the superstructure, meaning law, politics, religion, philosophy, art, the whole lot. It has the kind of systematic virtues I find hard to resist. It unifies a huge amount of otherwise scattered history, it explains why ideas arrive when they arrive and why they spread, it stops me from lazily pointing at a great mind or a mood in the air whenever I want to explain some social fact, and it gives me a tidy discipline: I can always ask what material arrangement this or that idea is serving. I dont want a reading list; I want the structure of the problem sorted out.

But there is one firm intuition that keeps snagging on this. When I am actually writing — moving a scene around, choosing a line break, feeling that a character would or would not do this thing — the thing I am tracking seems stubbornly not economic. The difference between a mediocre book and a good one, or between two writers working under the same material conditions, feels like a real difference in the work itself, not just a difference in how the work flatters or resists its economic moment. And it gets worse when I think about mathematics or a particular harmonic move in music: those don't feel as if they are answering to who owns the mills. So I am torn between a theory that explains almost everything at the level of large history and an intuition that something escapes it at the level of practice.

Give me the strongest version of the base-explains-everything view and the strongest version of the intuition that some things — artistic form, mathematical insight, whatever you take to be the hardest case — are not exhausted by the base. Then tell me which should yield, and under what conditions. Is the intuition just the self-image that superstructural activity produces, and therefore something to debunk? Does the theory need amending — some version of relative autonomy, or determination only in the last instance, or the base as setting constraints rather than dictating content? Or is there a reading on which both survive, where the economic explains why art has the social function it has but not everything that makes one novel better than another? Be concrete about the test conditions: what would a novelist have to notice about their own writing for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what would a historical materialist have to observe for the theory to be the thing that goes?
