I keep tripping over a weird asymmetry in how we talk about belief, and I can't tell if its deep or just a grammar thing. If I say about you 'it's raining but you don't believe it', that lands fine — maybe you haven't looked outside. If I say about my past self 'it was raining but I didn't believe it', that also lands fine; I've said exactly that after trusting a bad forecast. But the present-tense first-person version 'it's raining but I don't believe it' makes me sound like I've lost the plot, even though the facts it reports — rain, plus my not believing it — could perfectly well be true together. I've since learned this has a name, Moore's paradox or something, so apparently it isn't just me. What I want to know is whether anything actually justifies this asymmetry: present-tense me is special, every other combination of person and tense is fine. Give me the strongest principled story you can find for why my own present-tense relation to my own beliefs is different in kind, then stress-test it against the past-tense case and the second- and third-person cases (is there a difference between saying about you that you don't believe it's raining, and saying it to your face?). And be straight with me: is this an asymmetry we should keep, or just a quirk of the word 'I' that philosophers have over-solemnised?
