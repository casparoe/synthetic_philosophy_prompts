I have been turning over a tension between two ideas about what it means to understand the world, and I would like an expert to think it through with me rather than hand back a standard summary. The first idea is Laplace's demon: the thought experiment in which a sufficiently vast intelligence, given the exact positions and motions of every particle and all the forces at work, could use the laws of physics to compute the entire future and the entire past, with nothing uncertain left over. The second idea is one I have seen described as computational irreducibility, associated mainly with Stephen Wolfram: the claim that for a great many systems there is no shortcut, even in principle, because the only way to learn what the system will do is to run it step by step — the system is already its own fastest simulation, and tiny rules can produce behavior that cannot be predicted except by actually letting it unfold. These two ideas seem to pull in opposite directions about what a finished science could accomplish, and I keep wondering what Laplace himself would have made of the second one.

Suppose Laplace were alive today and were shown a clear statement of computational irreducibility, with the standard cellular-automaton examples in front of him. Would he conclude that the demon is finished, or would he answer that the demon is untouched because it is not a practical calculator but an unbounded intelligence that can simply run each step and know the outcome, so irreducibility limits only finite minds like ours? And either way, how would he revise his picture of what a completed science of the universe would look like: would he still call the world fully knowable, or would he grant that knowability comes in degrees? I want your best reconstruction of his likely position, with the reasoning made visible — which of his actual commitments push him each way, and what would have to give for him to land on the other side. Please approach this as a historian of ideas who also understands computation, and take your time; it may help to first brainstorm the strongest case for each side before settling on one. When you answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
