I've been going back and forth in a games-studies seminar — well, mostly in a long forum thread that spilled out of the seminar — about whether war games can ever treat war with the seriousness the subject demands, or whether the fact that they're games means they inevitably glamorize or trivialize it. The "games can be serious" camp keeps reaching for an analogy: a war game is like a war film or a war novel, so since no one thinks film or prose automatically glorifies war just by being the medium it is, games shouldn't be condemned as a medium either — a game like Spec Ops: The Line or This War of Mine is, they say, the medium's All Quiet on the Western Front. The "mechanics always win" camp reaches for the opposite analogy: a war game is, at bottom, just children playing soldiers with better rendering and a marketing budget. It's play. Play is about fun and safe make-believe, so placing war inside play necessarily turns real suffering into a toy — whatever the cutscenes say.

I'd like you to act as someone who works on the philosophy of games and aesthetics, and to audit both analogies rather than just pick a side. Think carefully before committing: first brainstorm the correspondences for each analogy (a quick table or list is fine). Then say where each analogy breaks down, seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion from the ones that are only superficial, and spell out what each analogy quietly imports from the source case that the target lacks. Then give a verdict: which conclusion, if any — "the medium can treat war seriously" or "making a war game inevitably trivializes war" — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Be specific about which features of games are doing the real work: player agency, complicity, repeatability, reward loops, and the relation between authored narrative and moment-to-moment play. If you think one or both analogies are unsalvageable, propose a better analogy and say what work it does that these can't.
