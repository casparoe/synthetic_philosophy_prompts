In *Nicomachean Ethics* V.5 (around 1133a), Aristotle says the following about the standard by which commodities are measured:

"And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called *nomisma* (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (*nomos*), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

In *Politics* I.9 (around 1257a), in the course of explaining how coinage arose out of barter, he writes:

"For the natural necessaries are not in every case readily portable; hence for the purpose of barter men made a mutual compact to give and accept some substance of such a sort as being itself a useful commodity was easy to handle in use for general life, iron for instance, silver and other metals, at the first stage defined merely by size and weight, but finally also by impressing on it a stamp in order that this might relieve them of having to measure it; for the stamp was put on as a token of the amount."

These two passages seem to pull in different directions. One says money exists not by nature but by custom and can be rendered useless at will; the other explains money's adoption as a compact to use a commodity that was itself useful, with the stamp serving as a token of quantity. Are these accounts in tension, or can they be reconciled? In answering, distinguish as carefully as you can among the candidate resolutions—for example, a difference between money's origin and its current status as a measure, different senses of "nature," a difference in the aims of the *Ethics* and the *Politics*, or a genuine change of mind—and say which reading requires the least special pleading while remaining faithful to both texts.
