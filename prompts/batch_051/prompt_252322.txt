In debates about whether astonishing personal coincidences have any evidential value, I often see the birthday problem used as a stock debunking tool. The move goes more or less like this. Someone reports an experience that feels overwhelmingly improbable—a dream about an old friend on the night that friend dies, a song that comes on the radio right in the middle of a private thought about it, a chance encounter with an acquaintance in a distant city—and takes that low apparent probability as evidence for some hidden connection, call it telepathy, synchronicity, fate, or simply the sense that something more than chance is going on. The skeptic replies that the person is committing the birthday error. In a room of 23 people the odds are already better than even that two of them share a birthday, because the relevant question is not whether someone matches a birthday specified in advance but whether any of the many possible pairings matches. Analogously, a human life contains an enormous number of moments, thoughts, faces, songs, and dreams, and therefore an enormous number of opportunities for pairs of events to line up, so the occurrence of some striking coincidence—indeed, of many such coincidences—is not just possible but nearly guaranteed. The analogy is treated as doing real argumentative work: it is supposed to show that the anecdote carries no information and that a rational observer should not move her credence in hidden causes at all.

I would like a rigorous assessment of how far this analogy actually carries. First, make the mapping explicit: what corresponds to the people, the days of the year, the specific birthdays, the number 365, the shared birthday, and the procedure of counting matches across all unordered pairs. Second, identify the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the skeptical conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial. I would especially like you to address three concerns. (a) The reported coincidence is usually a single specified event—this song, this friend, this night—whereas the birthday result concerns the existence of at least one unspecified match among many; how much of the skeptical force depends on that difference? (b) The calendar provides a fixed, finite, uniform set of possible dates, independent across people, whereas the possible events of a life are not well defined, not uniform, and not obviously independent; is the analogy importing regularity conditions that the target simply lacks? (c) A shared birthday is itself unremarkable, whereas the reports in question are selected precisely because they are remarkable, so a debunker who deploys the analogy may be drawing on a selection effect rather than explaining it. Finally, tell me how much of the skeptic's conclusion survives once these breaks are on the table, what a better analogy for the actual situation would be if one exists, and what the skeptic would need to add to the birthday argument before it actually licenses the claim that such anecdotes have no evidential force. Please answer in American English.
