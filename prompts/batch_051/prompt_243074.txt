I cover science and ideas, and I've been chewing over Stigler's law—the rule that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer. I have a small homemade theory I'd like an honest read on. I call it the explainer premium. The claim: credit in science goes not to the first person to have an idea, or even the first to publish it, but to the first person who makes the idea legible to the field—puts it in the discipline's own vocabulary, ties it to problems the field already cares about, makes it cheap for everyone else to take up. On that picture, Stigler's law is just what the process looks like from the outside: eponyms are the payoff for translation work, not for priority. A prediction: the bigger the gap between first formulation and later uptake, the more renaming and argument went into making the idea portable. And the folk pattern fits—discover something before it's time, in an obscure register, and you get a footnote; rediscover it when the field is primed and say it in two sentences and you get the name. Is this already worked out somewhere under a better name, and is it really distinct from the Matthew effect or just a special case? More importantly, where does it break—what are the clearest cases where credit stuck to an obscure first mover despite lousy packaging, and what do they do to the theory?
