I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology with prior training in machine learning, and I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship. I would like you to write the two-page research proposal I will submit. Please do not include citations or a bibliography; I will add the scholarly apparatus myself.

Here is the half-formed idea I want you to turn into a proposal. The long-running debate about whether natural selection is an optimizer has mostly been conducted in a vocabulary inherited from mid-century population genetics: fitness maxima, adaptive landscapes, constraints, and drift. Machine learning now offers a sharper vocabulary for comparing search procedures: gradient estimation, credit assignment, stochasticity, learning-rate schedules, loss-landscape geometry, regularization, and generalization beyond the training distribution. I suspect that if the biological debate were restated in that vocabulary, some apparent disagreement would dissolve, but a harder residue would remain: natural selection and gradient descent may be optimizers of importantly different kinds, and the analogy may mislead as often as it illuminates.

I want the proposal to state a determinate research question. My current formulation, which you should tighten or replace if it is unworkable, is this: Are the respects in which evolution by natural selection resembles gradient descent the same respects that do the explanatory work when biologists treat selection as optimizing, or are the structural disanalogies—no backpropagation-style credit assignment, no consistent gradient of a fixed objective, non-stationary fitness landscapes, no clean separation between training and deployment—serious enough that the optimizer picture should be treated as a narrowly valid idealization rather than a general characterization of selection?

The proposal should explain why the existing positions leave this open. The standard defenses of the optimizer picture appeal to formal results showing that allele-frequency change can approximate fitness-gradient ascent under restrictive assumptions, while the standard critiques point to constraint, maladaptation, and drift; but neither side typically specifies which properties of an optimization algorithm are supposed to be doing the work, so the dispute stays at the level of metaphor. The project should therefore not be a survey of the two literatures, and it should not be a purely conceptual analysis of the word "optimizer." Instead, the method should be to select a class of simple, well-understood optimization problems—toy fitness landscapes over small genotypic spaces—and work through each case twice: once for an explicit gradient-descent algorithm with full credit assignment, and once for a population-genetic model with the same fitness function but only frequency-dependent selection and no global gradient. The aim is to determine, for each case, whether the two dynamics converge or diverge, and which structural feature explains the difference. Some cases can be handled analytically; the rest can be handled with small simulations. The proposal should say why this comparative, model-based approach is better than the obvious alternatives, namely a literature synthesis or a purely conceptual argument about whether evolution literally optimizes.

Please specify what a successful result would look like: I expect a typology of the respects in which selection is and is not gradient-like, explicit conditions under which the optimizer picture is legitimate, and a set of examples where the distinction would change how a biologist or a machine-learning researcher interprets a result. Please also specify what would count as failure—for example, if the central notion of an optimizer cannot be made determinate enough to support the comparison, or if the models turn out to be so sensitive to arbitrary implementation choices that no stable conclusions can be drawn. Assume a one-year scope in which I can do analytic work and modest simulations but not a large empirical study or novel theorem-proving. Write the proposal as the polished document I would submit.
