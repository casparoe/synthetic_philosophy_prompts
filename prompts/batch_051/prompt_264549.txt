I am trying to work out what I actually think about turning one's attention inward, because my verdicts are stable case by case but I have no principle that holds them together, and I want to know what I am committed to. Here are the cases, with the verdicts as I actually find them.

1. After snapping at a colleague, that evening I reconstruct exactly what I said, what I had been feeling in the hour beforehand, and what set me off. Verdict: fine, even good; it changed how I behaved the next day.
2. Replaying that same incident every night for a fortnight and asking myself what kind of person that makes me. Verdict: not fine; it made me worse.
3. A writer notices a vague resistance before starting work and sits with it until he can say where the resistance is coming from. Verdict: fine.
4. A person with health anxiety takes his pulse several times a day and interrogates every twinge for what it might mean. Verdict: not fine; it looks like attention and is actually the opposite.
5. Someone who has wronged a friend asks himself whether he would accept the same treatment, and reconsiders whether he should apologize. Verdict: fine, and plainly different from case 2, though I cannot say exactly how.
6. A person in a relationship that is going well periodically asks whether it is really what he wants, and compares his present feelings to an imagined better version. Verdict: not fine; corrosive.
7. A person spends months journaling about why he cannot bring himself to apologize to someone, and never apologizes. Verdict: not fine, in the same family as case 2, even though the writing feels like case 1.

The further case I keep flipping on: after the end of a close friendship, a person writes for two successive evenings a full account of what happened and what he would do differently, then deliberately stops and does not return to it. My gut says fine, but it resembles case 2 closely enough that I cannot trust the gut.

Is there one principle that captures all of my verdicts, or very nearly all?
