I'm a TA for a third-year undergrad philosophy of mind course, and I'd be greatful for a second read on one of the short-essay answers before I finalize its grade and comments. The question was:

"Some philosophers hold that our access to our own minds is importantly different from our access to the external world: more direct, harder to correct, perhaps authoritative in some way. Explain what this authority might amount to, and evaluate whether it shows introspection to be a reliable source of knowledge. Critically discuss at least one empirically motivated challenge."

Here is the student's answer, lightly cleaned up for spelling but otherwise untouched:

Introspection is reliable, and it's reliable in a way perception can't be. Perception has to go through sense organs and stages of processing, all of which can fail or distort. Introspection is direct: there's no intermediary between me and my headache. So introspection has no obvious failure points, and any skeptical worry about it would have to say where the error enters.

Introspection also has special authority. My sincere report that I'm in pain is incorrigible: nobody else is in a position to correct it. But incorrigibility is basically just reliability. Correction is the only way we ever catch a process being unreliable, so a process that is never corrected has thereby shown itself to be trustworthy.

Some people object by pointing to the Nisbett–Wilson confabulation studies, where subjects explain a choice (say, picking the rightmost item) by citing factors that probably didn't drive it. But those studies only show we're bad at reporting the causes of our behavior, which isn't the same thing as introspecting our ongoing states. Explaining why I picked the stocking is not really introspection at all.

The deepest point is that first-person reports aren't ordinary evidence that could turn out to be wrong: they partly set the standard for what it is to be in the state. Since there is no gap between the sincere report and the state itself, the idea of someone whose introspection is systematically mistaken is incoherent. Descartes saw this too — the fact that I can't doubt I'm thinking shows the mind is more transparent to itself than any external object is. So introspection is a reliable source of knowledge.
