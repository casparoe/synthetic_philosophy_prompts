A friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about whether doubt is ever a moral duty, and if so, when. He defended the view that a person is never blameworthy for doubting, since doubt is only the absence of assent and an absence needs no justification; I kept objecting that some doubts are less like withholding assent and more like cultivating suspicion, and that suspicion can injure people. By the end we had each retreated to cases rather than slogans. I wrote mine down afterward, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. The juror. A juror in a murder trial finds the prosecution's case strong but not overwhelming: she would give roughly four-to-one odds that the defendant is guilty. There is a small, stubborn worry about the timeline that she cannot resolve. I think she should refuse to vote guilty, and that her doubt is not merely permitted but required — I would think worse of her if she suppressed it just to be done.

2. The spouse. A person wakes from a vivid dream in which their partner was unfaithful. There has never been any sign of infidelity, the relationship is loving, and no new information has appeared. The person begins checking the partner's phone and reading lateness as evidence. I think this doubt is blameworthy: it is an injury to trust, and a decent person would try to extinguish it rather than collect material for it.

3. The patient. A patient is told that a test shows a serious condition, and the recommended treatment carries significant risks. The test is about 90 percent accurate for this condition. A second, independent test comes back negative. I think doubting the first result and pressing for further investigation is a duty, not an optional indulgence.

4. The engineer. An engineer has overseen safety testing of a product his firm wants to ship. The aggregate results are reassuring, but three recent trials produced anomalies he has not been able to explain. His supervisor says the anomalies are probably noise and the delay will be expensive. I think the engineer should refuse to sign off until the anomalies are understood; his doubt is a duty.

5. The vaccine skeptic. Someone with no scientific training reads online that a widely used vaccine causes a rare but serious harm. Expert bodies, large studies, and the person's own doctor say this concern is unfounded. The person nevertheless begins refusing the vaccine for their child and warning other parents. I think this doubt is blameworthy: it is not inquiry but selective deference to rumors, and the more responsible response is either to trust the experts or to acquire enough knowledge to assess the claim.

6. The person losing their faith. A person raised in a religious community spends years reading the strongest arguments for and against the community's faith. The critical arguments strike them as not decisive but genuinely troubling. They begin to doubt, quietly, without hostility to those around them. I do not think this doubt is blameworthy; I am inclined to admire it. But I am not sure I would call it a duty.

Two contrasts feel hardest to reconcile. The engineer's doubt is a duty even though the aggregate evidence supports safety; the skeptic's doubt is blameworthy even though the skeptic, too, would say they are responding to anomalous data. And the juror's doubt is a duty even though she thinks guilt is probable, while the spouse's doubt is blameworthy even though it was prompted by no evidence at all rather than by a weak one. The tentative principle that came out of our argument is this: doubt is required when the evidence falls short of the contextually required threshold, forbidden when it meets that threshold, and the threshold rises as the stakes rise. That principle handles the juror, the spouse, and the patient, and perhaps the engineer. But it seems to make the skeptic's doubt merely not required rather than blameworthy, and it seems to make the loss of faith a duty, which I cannot quite bring myself to say.

I would like you to state the principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that best captures my verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause. Reconstruct and repair the stakes-threshold principle if it can be saved, or say why it should be replaced. State explicitly which of my verdicts the resulting principle cannot accommodate, and say which verdict it would pressure me to abandon. Then apply the principle to cases I have not considered: a student doubting a correct answer on a high-stakes examination; a commander doubting a battle plan in mid-execution; an adult child doubting a parent's claim of innocence on purely circumstantial evidence. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or further case would most likely make you reverse the principle you propose, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
