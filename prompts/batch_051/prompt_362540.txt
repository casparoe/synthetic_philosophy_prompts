One of the arguments I keep coming back to is Hofstadter's old critique of machine translation. The way I remember it, the core move was: real translation requires understanding, where understanding means having something like a mental model of the scene the source text describes. Statistical translation systems, the argument went, don't have any such model — they just find fluent correspondences — so they'll fail exactly where the text demands thought: pronoun disambiguation, implied agents, sentences where the right rendering depends on facts about the world. The stock examples were things like a passage whose correct translation depends on knowing who did what to whom, or whether someone is alive or dead.

The development that's been bugging me is this. The systems now doing machine translation are general-purpose LLMs trained on next-token prediction over a large chunk of the internet. They can do in-context reasoning, answer questions about a text before translating it, and their internals can apparently be probed for grammatical and even semantic structure. So the old premise — that these systems only do surface statistics and have no model of the situation — at minimum needs to be reargued, because the systems look a lot more like they have a model, whatever that means.

Does Hofstadter's argument survive this development? If it does, tell me exactly which premise was load-bearing and how the argument should be restated against LLMs. If it's dead, tell me what understanding should be replaced by as the concept that explains where machine translation still fails. i'd rather here a specific verdict and a specific repaired argument than a both-sides answer.
