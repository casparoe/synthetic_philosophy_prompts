Okay, here's a framing I've been using in my grad seminar on practical reason, and I want it pressure-tested before I put it in writing.

Take the familiar expected value fanaticism problem: a tiny probability p of an enormous good M versus some finite sure thing. Classical EV says that for any finite sure thing you can find some p small enough and some M large enough to make the gamble preferable (assuming unbounded utility). The standard replies either cap the values (beyond some point M stops mattering) or refuse to take very small probabilities at face value (below a threshold, treat them as zero). I've been calling the underlying property 'fanaticism-fragile' versus 'fanaticism-resilient'. A rule is fragile if, for any finite sure payoff, there's a tiny-p, huge-M gamble it prefers. A rule is resilient if there's at least one finite sure payoff it won't trade for any gamble of that form. EV with unbounded utility is fragile; EV with bounded utility is resilient, but so is a probability-cutoff rule, which makes me think the two standard fixes are really targeting one underlying feature.

My claim: the real disagreement isn't about small probabilities or crazy huge values; it's about whether fragility is a bug or the honest price of taking value seriously. Put that way, the fanatic has to defend the fragile rule itself, not just the existence of huge goods. Sometimes I think its just bounded utility in a different hat and I'm overclaiming. Other times I think it carves at a different joint, because you might get resilience through nonlinear aggregation rather than a cap.

If the whole setup is confused, or the fanatic wouldn't recognize themselves in it, say that plainly instead of playing along.
