At a food-systems conference I was covering, two researchers got into a disagreement I could not settle. The question was whether, once you add in the wild animals killed during crop production, beef or chicken is the less harmful choice. I have their exchange roughly as follows.

The first said the ordinary ranking still holds: a broiler converts purchased feed into meat far more efficiently than a feedlot steer—roughly two pounds of feed per pound of bird against six or more for beef—so each calorie of chicken requires fewer harvested acres, and fewer harvested acres mean fewer mice and voles killed at harvest.

The second replied that the first was counting beef's feed twice over. Most beef cattle are not confined to row crops for their lives; they graze on standing forage that is never tilled or harvested. Chickens, by contrast, eat almost nothing but grain and soy from harvested fields. Subtract the grazing, and a chicken calorie imposes at least as many crop deaths as a calorie from pastured beef, and probably more.

The first answered that this was a minority-of-production point. Nearly all supermarket beef is finished on grain in a feedlot, and because cattle convert that grain so poorly, the average steer eats more harvested feed per edible calorie than a broiler. One cannot rescue beef by comparing it to an idealized grass-fed animal that few buyers actually eat.

The second said the first was now doing the bookkeeping in the wrong units. The right unit is not the calorie or the acre but the animal. A steer yields hundreds of pounds of meat and involves one sentient life; the same quantity of chicken involves roughly a hundred broilers, each of which consumed harvested feed and each of which was itself killed. Counting acres rather than animals makes chicken look deceptively clean.

The first replied that the second had changed the subject. The original question was about wild animals killed in crop production, not about farmed animals, and the hundred-broiler point is beside that question unless one assumes a broiler's welfare matters the same way a wild rodent's does—which is a further moral position, not part of the original comparison.

I was asked to referee and could not. Please go through the exchange move by move: for each position above, say whether it is correct, mistaken, or partly right, and briefly justify that judgment. Then say which researcher has the stronger overall case, and whether the two are really answering the same question. Please answer in American English.
