Construct a thought experiment in which a patient's earlier advance directive should be honored even though, at the moment of decision, she is competent, well-informed, and sincerely renounces it. Constraints: the scenario must be ordinary and realistic — a familiar illness and a familiar treatment, not a philosophical fiction; the directive must be clear and squarely applicable; and none of the standard grounds for setting aside the current refusal may be present — no dementia or severe cognitive decline, no radical change in personal identity or core values, and no formal Ulysses clause instructing clinicians to disregard later refusals. The case is intended to isolate the difference between a patient's competence and the authenticity of her present refusal, so the move you should engineer is a refusal that is competent but inauthentic; define that notion carefully and show that it is doing real work rather than serving as a disguised competence judgment. After presenting the scenario, state precisely why the refusal is inauthentic without being incompetent, and explain why the case does not merely reduce to a Ulysses contract. Then walk through what a defender of precedent autonomy (on which the earlier self's directive retains authority) and a defender of the then-self view (on which the present person's own wishes take priority) would each say about the case, identify the structural feature that is carrying the result, and say what the case would look like if that feature were removed. Finish by explaining how a then-self theorist would most plausibly attack the case without simply denying that the refusal is inauthentic, and whether that attack succeeds.
