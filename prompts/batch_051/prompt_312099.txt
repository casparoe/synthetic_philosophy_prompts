I have been preparing a course on the ethics of church–state relations, and I have discovered that my own verdicts shift depending on which country I am thinking about. Below are my honest reactions to a set of cases. I am not asking for a country-by-country account of the law; the facts I need are stated in the cases themselves. What I want is to know whether a precise principle — or a small, disciplined set of principles — makes sense of my reactions, because at the moment they look more like a list of vibes than a view.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (a) In England the Church of England is established, and its bishops sit in the legislature by right; I find this archaic but not, all things considered, unjust, and I would vote against giving any other church the same seat while not campaigning to abolish the bishops either. (b) In Germany the state collects a church tax from registered members of certain denominations and hands the money to the churches, and a member who wants to stop paying must make a formal civil declaration of departure. I do not think the tax itself violates church–state separation, but I find something slightly coercive about forcing a person formally to renounce membership to escape it; still, I would call the system acceptable. (c) France's public schools are officially secular and prohibit pupils from wearing conspicuous religious symbols. I would normally call a state-imposed dress code an illiberal restriction on schoolchildren, but I cannot bring myself to condemn the French rule outright, because I can see a legitimate interest in keeping the classroom free of visible sectarian identity; my verdict is acceptable, with misgivings. (d) A U.S. town council opens every meeting with a Christian prayer delivered by invited clergy, and attendance is not compulsory but the meeting is the only place to petition the council. I think this is wrong in a way that the English bishops are not. (e) U.S. currency carries the words In God We Trust, and old public buildings carry religious inscriptions; I would not scrub either, but I would vote against adding new ones. (f) The state pays military chaplains and provides spaces for soldiers of all faiths. I accept this, even though the state is paying clergy, because soldiers cannot simply leave their post and find a congregation elsewhere. (g) The state gives public money to a faith-based adoption agency that serves the general public but, on religious grounds, refuses to place children with married same-sex couples. I find this objectionable. (h) A hospital run by a religious order receives public funds but refuses to provide certain lawful procedures; I find this intolerable where it is the only hospital in the region, and tolerable where a secular hospital exists across town.

What I want from you is a precise principle of church–state relations — or a small set of candidates compared against one another — that delivers as many of my verdicts as possible, stated clause by clause with the cases that motivate each clause. Tell me what the principle says about a case I have not listed: the state gives a listed historic church a grant to repair its roof while the building remains an active place of worship. And tell me which of my verdicts I would have to overturn if I accepted the simplest version of the principle, and whether any of my verdicts are outliers that no decent principle will capture. Please answer as a political philosopher who works on secularism and religious accommodation, not as a legal scholar; think it through carefully and brainstorm before committing. Also, push back plainly if the task itself is confused: if church–state separation bundles several distinct values that can come apart, or if my verdicts are too ad hoc for any interesting principle to both track and explain them, say so rather than accepting the frame politely.
