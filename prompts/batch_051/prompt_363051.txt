I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I work on the distinction between behavior that is genuinely flexible and behavior that is merely sphexish — apparently clever, but in fact carrying out a fixed routine with no sensitivity to the feedback that would let it reach its goal. I suspect the contrast is doing more work in debates about free action than people tend to admit, so I want to stress-test the criteria I use. The standard case is the digger wasp: it brings its paralyzed prey to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to inspect, comes back out, and then drags the prey in. If you move the prey a few centimeters while the wasp is inside, the wasp does not simply carry the prey in; it retrieves it, replaces it at the entrance, and inspects the burrow again. It will repeat this entire sequence as often as one perturbs the arrangement. The wasp looks as though it is inspecting its nest, but it is really locked into a loop, blind to the very fact that would let it finish.

I was taught to apply four criteria for genuine flexibility, which I treat as the negation of sphexishness:

(1) Represented goal. The behavior is organized around a goal state that the agent represents, not merely a fixed sequence released by a stimulus.

(2) Online perturbation-sensitivity. When the means are perturbed mid-action, the agent adjusts the ongoing routine; it does not simply complete the routine and then switch.

(3) Failure-driven revision. If the same method fails repeatedly, the agent abandons or substantially changes the method, rather than re-running it unchanged.

(4) Generative novelty. When no stored routine fits a novel situation, the agent can compose a new course of action, one that is not simply a fixed concatenation of prestored subroutines.

Now consider a hypothetical agent, the maze-robot, whose control system is exhausted by the following. It has a finite library of low-level motor routines — go forward until contact, turn left ninety degrees, back up until clear, and the like — each of which is closed-loop at the level of the individual routine. It has a goal buffer that stores the target location as a sensory signature. It has a distance function that compares the current sensory state to that signature. And it has one fixed meta-routine: after any routine terminates, compare the current state to the goal; if distance has not decreased within the last hundred time steps, choose a new routine at random, weighted toward routines that have reduced distance in the past; if a sequence of choices is followed by success, append that whole sequence to the library as a high-priority subroutine. There is no evaluation beyond the scalar distance comparison, and no selection principle beyond chance plus past success.

Place this robot in an unfamiliar maze. It wanders, backs out of dead ends, and eventually reaches the target. Across trials in rearranged mazes it improves, and sometimes it assembles a solution path that no individual stored routine would have produced, because the library append has created novel concatenations. Yet the entire performance is generated by the fixed loops just described: the robot never notices that it is in a dead end except through the distance measure failing to fall, and it never revises what counts as progress.

Does the maze-robot meet my four criteria? Please go through them one by one: for each, give a tentative yes or no, say which feature of the robot is doing the work, and indicate whether the criterion cleanly separates the digger wasp from ordinary human action. Then give your overall verdict: is the maze-robot flexible in the sense these criteria aim to capture, or is it merely sphexish one level up? Finally, tell me whether the case exposes a defect in the criteria — for instance, a criterion satisfied only because the robot is flexible enough to fool us, or one that would rule out cases of genuine flexibility we should want to keep — and state the smallest revision to the criteria that would fix what the case exposes.
