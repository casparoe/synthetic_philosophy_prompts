Something that's been nagging me for a while. We seem to run a double standard about why ideas spread or don't. When a dumb rumor, an earworm, or a corporate slogan gets everywhere, we talk as if the idea itself did the work: it was catchy, it was sticky, it was built to travel. We credit the content. But when a true and useful idea fails to catch on — correlation is not causation is the example I keep coming back to; it gets taught every year and still loses to the first vivid anecdote — we almost never say the idea was slippery or hard to hold. We say people are inattentive, or biased, or the teacher was boring, or the time wasnt right. The idea gets the credit for its successes but is never blamed for its failures. And before you reach for it: the obvious move is to say the rumor is concrete and emotional while the principle is abstract and boring. But that just points at a difference in the ideas' contents, which is exactly the kind of thing the double standard refuses to take seriously.

Is that asymmetry actually justified? If you think it is, give me the strongest justification you can and test it against a nearby case where a simple, vivid, true idea still doesn't spread. If you think it isn't, then commit to the flip side: some ideas are antimemes, ideas whose own content makes them bad at being remembered or transmitted, and tell me what structural features of an idea would make it antimemetic — not merely boring, but bad at surviving in minds for reasons internal to the idea. Invent the strongest example you can.
