A colleague of mine in comparative cognition keeps a record of her rulings on whether a piece of animal behavior warrants the attribution of a higher psychological process—planning, causal understanding, the representation of an absent goal—or can be fully accounted for by a lower one, such as associative learning, an innate releasing mechanism, or a fixed action pattern. She describes herself as a follower of Lloyd Morgan's canon, by which she means, roughly, that a higher attribution is to be withheld whenever a lower one is adequate, and she treats each case as a decision to be made in light of the details rather than as a general endorsement of one camp or another.

Her recent rulings are these. A digger wasp, when the paralyzed cricket she has left at the burrow entrance is moved a few centimeters, repeats indefinitely the cycle of dragging it back, entering to inspect the burrow, and dragging it again: lower, a fixed action pattern. A horse that appeared to solve arithmetic problems but answered correctly only when someone present knew the answer and could unwittingly signal when to stop tapping: lower, a discriminative response to an inadvertent human cue. A rat overtrained on a maze route that continues to take that route after a shorter path is opened: lower, a stimulus–response habit. A male stickleback that attacks a crude dummy with a red underside more vigorously than a more realistic model without one: lower, an innate releasing mechanism. A New Caledonian crow that, with no training on the behavior, bends a straight piece of wire into a hook and uses it to pull food from a tube: higher, something like means–end reasoning, because the performance is too novel to be explained by the shaping history. A chimpanzee that, after a period of looking around, stacks two boxes and uses a stick to bring down food hung out of reach: higher, planning.

Now suppose she is shown a case she has not ruled on. A jumping spider, which hunts by sight, sees a prey insect across a gap. The only available path is indirect—it requires the spider to move away, descend, cross a narrow walkway, and climb the far side—and for much of that route the prey is out of sight. On its first exposure to the apparatus, the spider pauses, then takes the detour without backtracking and reaches the prey. Give your best prediction of what she will say: does she attribute planning, or at least a representation of the route, or does she settle on a lower mechanism such as a species-typical detour heuristic or a chain of learned associations? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her record equally well, and the additional case that would most decisively separate those rules.
