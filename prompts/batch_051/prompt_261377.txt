I’m a PhD student working on Kantian ethics, and I’ve been going in circles about something in the Groundwork. Maybe you can help me think this through carefully—act as an expert on Kant’s moral philosophy, and maybe start by brainstorming candidate reconciliations before settling on one.

At one point in the Second Section Kant says, roughly, that the Golden Rule—the old saying that you should not do to others what you would not want them to do to you—cannot be the supreme principle of morality. His objection is that it is merely implied by the real principle and only under various limitations; it cannot ground duties to oneself, duties of love toward others, or strict duties to others. He even gives examples: someone might gladly agree that no one should help him as long as he doesn’t have to help them, and a criminal could appeal to the rule against judges who punish him.

But then, later in the same section, when he illustrates the categorical imperative with the duty to help others, he asks us to consider someone who is doing fine and adopts a maxim of never helping anyone in need. Kant says: try to will that this maxim become a universal law. You cannot, because then you would be willing a world in which no one comes to your aid when you are in need, and that is inconsistent with what you as a rational being necessarily will—or at least you would be cutting off any hope of assistance you could want. That sounds, at least on its face, exactly like the kind of reciprocal, benefit-based reasoning he had just dismissed as insufficient. It seems like he’s using the Golden Rule’s logic after rejecting it.

So my question is: Can these two moves be reconciled without making one of them a mere rule of thumb or reading the second as carelessly worded? Is the difference that the categorical imperative test is about whether a maxim can be willed as a law for everyone, whereas the Golden Rule is about what I happen to want done to me? Or does that distinction collapse on this very example? And if they can’t be reconciled, which one better reflects Kant’s actual view of moral motivation?
