Here are the notes.

Working title: Precommitment as coordination among the anxious, not self-binding by a single self.

The usual thought is that a constitution is a Ulysses contract: a people binds its future self so it won't give in to passing passions, the way Ulysses tied himself to the mast. I want to argue that this picture is wrong in a way that matters, but that a modified version can be saved.

First, three disanalogies.

1. The bound self. Ulysses binds a future version of himself. Its the same agent, with the same life, and he anticipates a temporary and well-understood incapacity, namely the Sirens-induced madness. In the constitutional case, the binders and the bound aren't the same people at all. Future citizens didn't participate in the original choice; they aren't temporarily impaired; they just don't exist yet. So the self-binding part of the story is doing a lot of hidden work.

2. The exit conditions. Ulysses wants a constraint that is effective but that in principle someone else could end: he tells the crew not to untie him no matter how much he begs. Constitutional mechanisms, like entrenched clauses or amendment rules, are designed to make exit costly but possible. The question isn't whether the bound party would beg to be untied; it's who gets to count as the party entitled to do the untying. That's a question about membership and authorization, not about weakness of will.

3. The nature of the threat. The Sirens are a threat to a given preference ordering: Ulysses' lower-order desire to go to them conflicts with his all-things-considered desire to get home. But the supposed passions of future generations are not a pathology of an otherwise stable self; they are often just their considered judgments about the same matters, made in a different situation. So the constitution doesn't protect a people from its own irrationality; it imposes a settlement on people who may reasonably disagree with it.

That's the critical part. Now the constructive part. I want to say the analogy can be partly salvaged if we stop treating the constitution as self-binding and instead treat it as a coordination device chosen in advance by multiple agents who know they will later disagree under pressure. The picture isn't Ulysses alone on the mast; it's Ulysses and the crew agreeing on a procedure before they sail, because they know that once the Sirens sing, the captain will want one thing and the rowers another. Constitutional entrenchment, on this picture, is less about protecting considered judgment from hot passions and more about making sure anticipated disagreement doesn't unravel a shared scheme of government at the first moment of stress.

But I'm not sure about this: does the revised model actually solve the legitimacy problem, or just relocate it? A coordination device still binds dissenters, and if the original parties weren't the same as the future ones, the dead-hand problem is still there. Maybe I need to say the justification isn't hypothetical consent but something like a standing interest in having a stable framework for disagreement, which even latecomers have reason to want. But that's a different kind of justification than the Ulysses story gives us.

So, what do you think? I especially want to know whether the three disanalogies collapse into one, whether the constructive half is worth keeping or whether I should just write the critical essay, and whether the final appeal to a standing interest in stable disagreement is too vague to do the work.
