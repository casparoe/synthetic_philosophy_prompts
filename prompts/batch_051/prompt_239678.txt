I'm a software engineer by training with no formal philosophy background, and I've been trying to get clear on what it would mean for mathematics to genuinely explain something in nature rather than merely describe it. Over the past few months I've fallen into using a distinction that I'd like checked before I build anything further on top of it.

The distinction is between what I've been calling constraint explanations and ledger explanations. In a constraint explanation, a mathematical fact explains a phenomenon because the phenomenon is forced to hold for any system meeting certain structural conditions, whatever the underlying causal details happen to be — the mathematics delimits the space of possibilities. In a ledger explanation, the mathematics just tracks a causal process: it records quantities, rates, and correlations, or it serves as the bookkeeping of a model, while the explanatory work is done by the causal story rather than by the mathematics itself. My rough engineering analogy is that a constraint is like a physical law that rules out a whole region of a design space before anything is built, whereas a ledger is like a log file that records what happened after the fact.

My central claim is this: the indispensability argument for mathematical objects — the idea that we ought to believe in mathematical objects because mathematics is indispensable to our best scientific theories — only gets traction where the mathematics is doing constraint work. Where the mathematics is ledger work, you could in principle rewrite the science with no loss of explanatory content, so the mathematics is dispensable in exactly the sense that matters.

Two examples have been doing most of the work for me.

First, periodical cicadas. The broods emerge on 13- and 17-year cycles, and both numbers are prime. I read this as a constraint explanation: if an organism uses temporal separation to avoid predators or competing broods that appear on a fixed periodic schedule, then an emergence period that shares a factor with those other periods will tend to coincide with them more often, and periods with no common factors — in particular prime ones — are the ones that get sorted for. The arithmetic does the explaining, because the result is insensitive to the specific physiology of the predators or the cicadas.

Second, the hexagonal cells of a honeycomb. The hexagon minimizes the perimeter needed to tile a plane into equal-area cells, so the structure is what you'd expect if efficiency is being optimized. But here my distinction wobbles: if bees arrived at hexagonal building through natural selection for wax efficiency, the mathematics looks like a constraint; if the hexagon is just what surface tension and mechanical forces produce, then the mathematics looks like a ledger of the physics. Those are two different causal routes to the same structure, and it feels wrong that whether the mathematics explains should flip depending on which route happened historically.

That last point is really what I want pressure-tested. Is the constraint/ledger distinction coherent, or am I smuggling in my intuitions? Has it already been worked out under some other name? And where exactly does it break — specifically, are there cases where ledger-style mathematics is doing more explanatory work than I'm crediting it with, or cases where the line fails to cut at the joints? I would rather have the distinction dismantled than have it flatter me.
