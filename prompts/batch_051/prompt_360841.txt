Kant is generally read as holding that the duty of truthfulness is exceptionless: he defends the claim that one may not lie even to a murderer who asks whether one's friend is hidden in one's house. Suppose we confront his doctrine with two practices that only machine interlocutors made possible. In the first, a user deliberately supplies false premises or false claims to a large language model in order to make it output information it had been instructed to withhold. In the second, a company designs a customer-facing chatbot to misstate its own capacities and to imply that the user is speaking with a human. Drawing on the universal-law formulation of the categorical imperative, the formula of humanity, Kant's account of what makes a declaration a lie, and the murderer-at-the-door reasoning itself, predict how Kant would judge each practice. State which of his arguments do the decisive work, where the formulations pull apart, and identify any element of the machine cases that his framework can capture only imperfectly. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
