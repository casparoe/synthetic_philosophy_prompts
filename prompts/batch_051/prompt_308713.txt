A post I read in a rationality forum explains Solomonoff induction to lay readers through the following analogy: scientific theories are to a stream of observations what a compression program is to a file. The better theory is the shorter program for generating the data, just as the better compressor is the one that yields the smaller file; explanatory simplicity just is compression. The author then takes the analogy to license a strong conclusion: Solomonoff induction shows that Occam's razor is not a heuristic or a matter of taste but a theorem. Weight hypotheses by 2 raised to negative program length, and the shortest program consistent with your data is, on provable grounds, the best guide to the next observation.

I would like an audit of how much weight this analogy can actually bear. Give the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is fine — marking what in the compression picture corresponds to what in the Solomonoff setting. Then identify where the mapping bends or breaks, and in particular say which features of ordinary file compression the story quietly imports into induction that are not there in the same way. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the advertised conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, render a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does Solomonoff induction in fact turn Occam's razor into the thesis that the shortest program is the best predictor, and does the compression picture survive as a faithful image of what the formalism does? If a better analogy would do the same pedagogical work with less distortion, say what it is and why it improves on the compression picture.
