In my philosophy of logic reading group we've been at the dialetheism debate for four months and I'm starting to think the two camps just aren't engaging with each other. The dialetheist side—call them Priest-style—keeps pushing the Liar and saying the lesson is that some contradictions are true, and that paraconsistent logic is what lets you keep a naive truth predicate (or naive set theory) without everything collapsing into triviality. The classical side keeps saying paraconsistent logics only avoid explosion by quietly changing the meaning of the connectives: your "not" isn't really negation, it's just some other operator with a different truth table. The dialetheist then says that reply begs the question, because it defines negation by its classical behavior, which is exactly what's up for grabs, and then we're back where we started. Occasionally someone brings up Curry's paradox and then the fight is about wheather dropping contraction is more desperate than believing contradictions. Nobody has moved an inch.

My hunch is that this is partly a disagreement about what logic is for: the dialetheist treats a logic as a way to live with inconsistent commitments without triviality, while the classicist treats it as a theory of what follows from what, full stop, where "not" has to mean what it means. But I'm not at all sure that's the right diagnosis. Diagnose this standoff: is it verbal, is it a dispute about the data (is natural-language negation classical?), is it a dispute about standards or bedrock intuitions, or is one side just changing the subject? And tell me what, if anything, could move it—a formal result, a neutral characterization of consequence, an agreed test case, a distinction everyone could sign on to—or whether we should stop expecting convergence. I don't need a survey of paraconsistent systems; I need the diagnosis.
