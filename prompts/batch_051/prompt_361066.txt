I want to poke at Searle's original vs. derived intentionality distinction, because I think the usual Chinese Room discussion buries the more interesting part. Heres the setup. Searle says words and computers have only derived intentionality: their meaning is borrowed from the minds of the people who made or use them, while brains have original intentionality. Dennett's two-bitser—basically a coin-slot gadget that recognizes quarters—was his way of making that distinction look unprincipled: sure, the two-bitser's intentionality comes from its designers, but Dennett says ours also comes from somewhere else, namely evolution, so being borrowed doesn't mark a clean line between us and machines.

Now the development I want to test against this: a modern LLM will routinely say specific, true or false things that none of its developers or training-data authors ever thought of or intended. When the model produces a sentence nobody ever meant, whose intention is the meaning derived from? It's not the author of some training passage, because none of them said that; it's not the developers, because they didn't intend it either. So does Searle's original/derived distinction survive LLMs, does it need repair (something like "derived from the training process, not from anyone's specific meanings"), or is it dead? And if meaning can be derived from a process rather than a mind, has the word "derived" stopped doing the work Searle needed it to do?

Tell me which premise the LLM case actually bites on, and whether the distinction can be restored in another form. Also say how confident you are in your answer and single out the point you're least sure of.
