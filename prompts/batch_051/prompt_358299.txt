My friend tried to explain the strategy-stealing argument to me last week and I've been turning it over since, because I'm fairly sure he's overreached somewhere but I can't put my finger on where. His version went like this. Take any finite two-player game with perfect information and no draws, where the rules are symmetric: both players always have the same legal moves and the winning conditions are mirror images. Then the first player must have a winning strategy. Suppose the second player had one instead. The first player makes an arbitrary first move, then pretends to be the second player for the rest of the game and simply follows that same winning strategy. If the strategy ever tells her to play a move that's already occupied, she plays any other legal move instead; in a symmetric game, her extra move can't hurt her and can only help. So the second player can't have had a winning strategy after all, and since a finite perfect-information game without draws has to be decided one way or the other — one of the two players must have a winning strategy — it must be the first. He says that's exactly why the first player has a forced win in Hex, the board game where two players place stones and race to connect their opposite sides.

I don't know enough game theory to trust my own unease. Part of me thinks the problem is the bit at the end where he says one of the two players must have a winning strategy; another part of me thinks the copy-the-strategy step is glib in some way I can't state. What should I actually take away: which parts of this summary are fair, what's subtly overclaimed or conflated, and does the overall conclusion survive once the needed conditions are said straight?
