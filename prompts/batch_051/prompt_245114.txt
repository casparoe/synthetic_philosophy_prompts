I've been re-reading Federalist 78 for a consitutional theory class, and I keep coming back to one passage that seems to be doing more work than Hamilton lets on. He's arguing that judicial review doesn't make courts superior to the legislature, and he writes:

"Limitations of this kind can be preserved in practice no other way than through the medium of courts of justice, whose duty it must be to declare all acts contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution void. Without this, all the reservations of particular rights or privileges would amount to nothing."

I tried to turn this into a step-by-step argument and this is what I got:

(1) A limited constitution is one that places specified restrictions on what the legislature may do.
(2) Such restrictions are just words unless some institution can enforce them against the legislature itself.
(3) The only institution that can enforce them is the courts, by declaring contrary acts void.
(4) If the courts lacked that power, the constitutional limits would be empty.
(5) Therefore courts must have the power to strike down legislation that violates the Constitution.

I can't tell wether this is actually a good argument or whether Hamilton is sliding from "some enforcement is needed" to "the courts are the enforcers." Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and if that step can be repaired with a weaker or stronger reading, does the conclusion still follow?
