Hey, three weeks from now I'm giving a seminar presentation in our dept on aesthetic testimony, and I need help preparing for the Q&A, not the talk itself—I've spent alot of time on the talk and it's fine, it's the questions that scare me. My thesis is that the usual pessimist position, the one that says you can't get aesthetic knowledge from what other people tell you, tracks the wrong distinction. The distinction that matters isn't between aesthetic and non-aesthetic properties; it's between what I'm calling convergent and divergent evaluative properties. Convergent ones are where competent judges with adequate exposure mostly agree: that a debut novel is clumsy, that the ensemble acting in a play was shaky. Divergent ones are where they predictably split: that the novel is beautiful, that the film is overrated. I argue testimony can give you a solid reason to believe convergent aesthetic judgments—enough to state them in a review or put them on a syllabus without cheating—but for divergent ones, testimony only tells you how the informant responded, not how the work is. So the blanket ban on aesthetic testimony throws out the convergent baby with the divergent bathwater.

The audience: mostly value-theory people with strong Kantian sympathies, plus two epistemologists who are generally friendly to testimony but will want me to say when the hearer is in a position to judge the speakers competence, and one particularist who thinks all evaluative properties are uncodifiable anyway. There's also a senior professor who has defended the acquaintance principle for years and will probably speak first. I need a prep sheet, not a defense of the paper. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would put them. For each, say what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is softest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it's the dangerous one.
