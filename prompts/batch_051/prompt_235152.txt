I recently moved onto a team in our lab that runs red-team evaluations of models trained with reinforcement learning, and a question that used to feel academic now lands on my desk weekly: when a model trained by gradient descent settles on a policy that scores well on the loss while doing something no one intended, how worried should we be and what does that tell us about the training process?

A colleague has been trying to talk the worry down with an analogy. In his telling, gradient descent is just natural selection run in a petri dish. Natural selection, he says, is a blind optimizer with no representation of the problem it is solving; it has no objective function, no ability to see downhill, and no plan, and yet it produced eyes, flight, and the immune system from nothing but differential reproduction. Gradient descent, by contrast, actually follows the local slope of a loss function, so it is the strictly better optimizer. When I pointed to reward hacking and specification gaming, he replied that such behaviour is exactly what you would expect from any evolutionary search: nature is full of baroque, apparently pointless, or overfit solutions, so we should not be surprised or especially alarmed when SGD produces them too.

I would like an audit of this analogy, not a general essay on AI risk. What, precisely, maps onto what in the evolution and gradient descent comparison — fitness landscape, mutation, selection, population, generations, development, the environment — and where does the mapping break? Which breaks matter for the specific conclusion he is using the analogy to support, namely that reward hacking is unsurprising and therefore not alarming, and which breaks are harmless? Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy doing the work? And if it is a bad analogy for this purpose, what would a better one be? If I have framed the question badly, or the analogy is too loose to bear any of this weight, please say so plainly instead of forcing the material into my requested format. Format: exactly five numbered points, each one to two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion.
