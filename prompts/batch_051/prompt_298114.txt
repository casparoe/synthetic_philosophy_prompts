For a course on practical reason I want a thought experiment built to a precise specification. The topic is the contrast between Williams-style internalism about reasons and a certain form of externalism. Take internalism to be the view that an agent has a reason to φ only if a sound deliberative route leads from her subjective motivational set to φ-ing, where that set contains desires, dispositions of evaluation, attachments, and projects, and where deliberation may include means–end reasoning, the resolution of conflicts among present motivations, the correction of false beliefs, and imaginative projection into the life that φ-ing would bring. Take the externalist position to be this: all genuine reasons are agent-neutral in content. A consideration counts as a reason for an agent only if it can be stated without essential reference to that agent's particular attachments, projects, or evaluative outlook, and only if it bears on what any rational agent would have reason to do, not merely on what this agent has reason to do given her aims; a consideration whose force depends wholly on the agent's particular project is therefore not a genuine reason. In the standard direction of the debate, externalism is the more permissive view, since it posits reasons even where there is no suitable motive. I want the reverse direction. Construct a realistic scenario in which the Williams-style internalist judges that an agent has a reason to φ, while the agent-neutral externalist just characterized judges that she has none. The agent must be an ordinary person; the item in her motivational set must be an unremarkable project or attachment, not an exotic desire or a pathology; φ must be harmless and morally unobjectionable; and the internalist verdict must be generated by more than a one-step means–end inference — it should run through at least one of the other deliberative operations, such as correcting a false empirical belief or exercising imagination. Set the case in an ordinary workplace, family, or civic setting, and present it in no more than two paragraphs. Then demonstrate that the construction works: identify the element of the motivational set that is engaged, spell out the sound deliberative route, and state why the externalist criterion refuses the reason. Then locate the single feature of the scenario that produces the divergence and say what removing it would do — would the two theories come into agreement, or would the case revert to the familiar direction in which the externalist is the more permissive one? Next, say how a defender of the externalist view would attack your construction. Finally, tell me which step of your own walkthrough is most likely to fail, and what argument or adjustment would lead you to change your mind about whether the case meets the specification.
