I am a PhD student working on AI safety, and in the seminars and reading groups I participate in, I keep meeting a version of an argument that has started to bother me. People defend instrumental convergence — the thesis that, for a very wide range of possible terminal goals, a sufficiently capable system will converge on the same few instrumental subgoals, such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity (the disposition to resist having one's goals rewritten), and self-improvement — by analogy with convergent evolution. The orthogonality thesis says that high capability can in principle be paired with almost any terminal goal. The convergence claim is what is supposed to keep that from being reassuring: independence at the level of final goals does not prevent a broad family of different final goals from producing the same dangerous intermediate behavior. The biological analogy runs that eyes, wings, and echolocation have arisen independently across unrelated lineages because they solve recurrent problems imposed by physics and ecology, and that in the same way we should expect certain instrumental strategies to arise across otherwise unrelated AI systems because they solve recurrent problems imposed by the structure of goal-directed action. The conclusion the analogy is used to license is that power-seeking behavior is not a quirk of a particular reward function or training run but a robust feature of optimizing enough in the right kind of environment.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Give me the correspondence table first: in the AI case, what plays the role of the population, the lineage, the environment, the selection pressure, the adaptive problem, and the convergently evolved trait. Then identify the disanalogies and sort them into the ones that matter for the intended conclusion and the ones that do not. One worry of mine is that natural selection is not an optimizer with a terminal goal at all, so the analogy may be importing the very thing it is meant to establish: that the convergence is explained by goal-directedness rather than by a shared environment acting on populations that have no goals whatsoever. On that reading, convergent evolution shows that shared environments plus shared biological constraints make similar forms probable; it is less obviously evidence that shared goal structure makes similar subgoals probable.

The reply I usually get is that both cases are instances of one abstract pattern — search under constraints settling on solutions that preserve whatever was being selected for — but I suspect that "selected for" is doing different work in the two halves of that sentence. I want to know whether that abstract pattern can be made precise, whether it is doing real argumentative work or serving as a vivid picture, and whether there is a better analogy to hand. Finally, tell me what survives of the intended conclusion once the disanalogies are on the table: does robust instrumental convergence across many terminal goals have to stand on its own decision-theoretic footing, with the evolutionary analogy as mere illustration, or was the analogy carrying weight that the formal arguments cannot?
