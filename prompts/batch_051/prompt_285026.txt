Ive been rereading On Liberty and got stuck on something in Chapter 5. Mill writes:

"Again, trade is a social act. Whoever undertakes to sell any description of goods to the public, does what affects the interest of other persons, and of society in general; and thus his conduct, in principle, comes within the jurisdiction of society: accordingly, it was once held to be the duty of governments, in all cases which were considered of importance, to fix prices, and regulate the processes of manufacture."

He then says the doctrine of free trade rests on grounds different from, though equally solid with, the principle of individual liberty: restrictions on trade are wrong because they don't achieve the results desired, not because sellers have a right to sell whatever they want. So far so good. But here's what bothers me: in our actual practice we accept a pretty big asymmetry between commercial conduct and speech. The state can inspect a factory, require labels, ban adulterated goods, and punish a seller before any specific buyer has been harmed. Nobody thinks that's a liberty violation in the Millian sense. Yet when a newspaper or a political speaker spreads false or corrosive claims, we give them near-absolute protection until some specific, imminent harm can be shown, and often only after the fact. A mislabeled can of soup gets stricter treatment than a false claim about an election or a misleading health scare. And Mill himself seems to want it that way: he treats trade as social conduct society may regulate, while expression gets much stronger protection even when it affects others.

I'm not asking whether Mill was being inconsistent—maybe there's a clean distinction here. I want you to find the strongest justification for the asymmetry. The obvious candidate is something like: commercial acts are coercive or involve tangible harm, while speech only affects people through their freely chosen beliefs. But that candidate looks shaky when you consider false health advice sold in a paid course, or a newspaper's true-but-misleading reporting that tanks a company and costs people their jobs. Is the distinction between trade and speech something the harm principle can actually keep, or does one side collapse into the other once you test it? Give me your honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or given up.
