I'm a CS PhD student who's been re-reading the old Hofstadter/Mitchell Copycat stuff, and I keep noticing a parallel I can't decide about. For background: Copycat solves letter-string analogies in a tiny world, e.g. abc -> abd applied to ijk gives ijl. The interesting part is that the concepts doing the work are fluid. The successor relation that gets you from c to d in the source can slip under pressure from the target; if the target were xyz, where a plain mapping would demand a successor of z, the program doesn't just fail---successor can deform into predecessor or sameness until a globally coherent mapping shows up. In the Slipnet, concepts like successor, predecessor, and same sit as connected nodes with activations, and what a concept means in a given episode is partly settled by how the rest of the analogy is going.

Here's the parallel. When a court applies a prior decision to a new case, the concept in the earlier rule seems to slip the same way. Take a legal rule excluding vehicles from a park, and a new case about a Segway or a bicycle. The court isn't just checking whether the new thing falls under a fixed intension of the concept vehicle; the boundary of the concept gets redrawn partly by what makes the old case and the new case line up well as an anology. The source concept is under pressure from the target, and the court doesn't treat it as a frozen predicate.

Is this a deep structural identity, or am I just pattern-matching? In Copycat the slippage is driven by stochastic search for global coherence between a source and a target, and there's nobody to be right about anything. In law the slippage is constrained by briefs, canons of construction, policy consequences, and the fact that the judge is trying to be correct rather than merely coherent. Does that difference kill the parallel, or is a court just Copycat with a very differently weighted Slipnet? And is there something one side can do that the other architecture can't, or vice versa?
