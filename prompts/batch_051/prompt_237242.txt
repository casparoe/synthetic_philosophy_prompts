I spent my career in the natural sciences, where I took it for granted that a trained model, a calibrated instrument, and even a practicing scientist follow determinate rules even when extrapolating to cases never encountered before. Reading around in philosophy of language has shaken that confidence, and I would like your help working through one strand of the resulting debate.

You are a professional philosopher of language and mind with a strong secondary interest in the foundations of machine learning. Please think carefully and at some length before composing your response: first do a short brainstorming pass in which you lay out the main positions, identify their load-bearing commitments, and anticipate the strongest objections; only then commit to a line of argument. After that, write a formal, essayistic response.

The essay should adjudicate a controversy associated with rule-following and meaning: the view often called Kripkenstein, after Saul Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein. The central skeptical thought is that any finite record of how an agent has applied a word or symbol — past sums, training examples, past utterances — is compatible with indefinitely many rules that agree on that record but diverge on untested cases. For addition, the skeptic imagines a rival function, quus, that returns the ordinary sum when both inputs fall below some threshold but returns some nonstandard value, say 5, once the threshold is exceeded. Because all of the agent's actual applications have stayed below the threshold, the skeptic argues that the agent's history, explicit instructions, inner episodes, and behavioral dispositions are all compatible with both rules; so no candidate fact about the agent seems to constitute her meaning addition rather than quus. The skeptic then concludes that there are no meaning facts of the kind we ordinarily suppose.

The controversy I want you to adjudicate concerns what to do once that negative conclusion is on the table. One camp defends a skeptical solution: it concedes that there is no fact of the matter that makes one continuation correct and another incorrect, and instead replaces truth-conditions for sentences about meaning with acceptance-conditions — roughly, the conditions under which the surrounding linguistic community would endorse an answer, correct an error, or move on. On this picture, saying that a learner has grasped addition is not describing a hidden semantic state; it is placing the learner in a social practice that we engage in from the inside. Another camp replies that this is no stable resting point. It either collapses back into a disguised dispositional or use theory, and so concedes that there is a fact about the agent after all, or it drains normativity so thoroughly that correctness reduces to social conformity, with no room for the community itself to be wrong. A further worry is that the machine-learning analogy makes the challenge look either deeper or emptier: a neural network trained on finitely many labeled examples to approximate addition is in the very position the skeptic describes, since the training record underdetermines whether the network has learned addition, quus, or some other generalization. Decide whether adding this analogy strengthens the skeptical case, or whether it exposes the argument's weakness by reminding us that a learner is never just a finite list of examples: there are also inductive biases, an optimization procedure, a loss landscape, and a test distribution that jointly pick out a generalization policy. You may treat the machine-learning case as an illustrative analogy rather than as a separate body of literature.

Weigh the principal arguments on both sides, but do not end in an even-handed standoff. Reach and defend a definite conclusion — for example, that the skeptical solution survives its critics once it is properly understood; or that it is unstable and must revert to some form of dispositionalism; or that the skeptic wins outright and our ordinary talk of rules is best understood as a useful fiction. Justify your verdict by responding to what you take to be the strongest objection to it.
