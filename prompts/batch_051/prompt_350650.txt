Suppose a country with a written constitution suffers a genuine interregnum: the line of succession is exhausted, and the amendment procedure itself requires participation by an institution that no longer exists, so every explicit rule for selecting a lawful officeholder is unavailable. A friend claims that mature constitutional orders nonetheless contain an unwritten background norm covering this case: whoever controls the administrative machinery may act only to preserve the status quo, and this holding action becomes lawful if and only if it is later ratified by the broadest still-functioning elective body, with the ratification operating retroactively. I put my credence in that principle at about 0.3; he puts his at 0.75. What is your credence in the principle, treating it as a claim about what makes governance lawful during such gaps, not merely what is prudent or stable? Please decompose it into the sub-questions you take to matter — at least: whether genuine gaps of this kind are possible in a legal order at all, or whether every apparent gap is already resolved by some pre-existing interpretive power; whether an unwritten background succession norm of that sort exists where the text is silent; and whether, if it does exist, its content matches his caretaker-plus-retroactive-ratification account rather than something like effective control or a fresh election. Give a rough probability for each sub-question, show how they combine into your overall number, and say which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to and which part of my disagreement with my friend it most likely drives.
