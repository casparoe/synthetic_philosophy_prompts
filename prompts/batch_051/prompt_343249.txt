This term I am taking a course on formal epistemology, and it has made me suspicious of a policy I have been using for years whenever I need to assign probabilities under partial information. The policy is this: I take whatever I actually know about a situation, such as that a quantity falls within a certain interval or that certain events have stated probabilities, and among all probability distributions satisfying those constraints I choose the one with maximum entropy. My standing rationale has been that this distribution adds the least information beyond what I already have; it is the least committal assignment compatible with my evidence, so it ought to be the rational default. I use the rule outside coursework as well. When I have to estimate how long a task will take and I know only a broad lower and upper bound, I spread the probability over that interval as evenly as the bounds allow, and I resist the urge to let a vague hunch about the most likely duration tilt the distribution, on the grounds that I cannot state that hunch as a constraint I would be willing to defend.

Recently I have begun to suspect that the rule is not as neutral as it feels. The maximum entropy distribution is flat only relative to a background way of describing the space of possibilities; change how I carve the space up and a different distribution counts as maximally non-committal. I have also noticed that I keep applying the rule even when I do have faint but real background information, such as a rough sense of how long this sort of task typically takes or which errors happen most often. I tell myself that an unarticulated hunch should not be allowed to smuggle information in through the back door, but I am no longer sure the rule is not doing its own smuggling, by treating being as spread out as possible in my chosen description as a safe default rather than a substantive assumption.

What I want to know is whether this epistemic policy is rationally defensible, or whether I have dressed up a bias toward flat-looking distributions as a principle. If it is not defensible as stated, is there a version of the rule that keeps what I originally wanted from it, namely refusing to add information I cannot justify, without pretending that the maximum entropy distribution is a free lunch? And how could I tell, from the inside, when I am using the rule for the right reason rather than using it to avoid the harder work of articulating and defending the background information I actually have?
