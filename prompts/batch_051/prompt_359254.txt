I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend who designs onboarding and retention features for a subscription app, and the argument showed me that my paper is going to be tested in ways I have not rehearsed. She kept returning to three points: every designed environment shapes behavior, users freely choose to keep scrolling, and calling engagement techniques "manipulation" is a way for critics to moralize about choices they happen not to like. By the end I could meet her point by point, but a Q&A with fifty practitioners is a different matter. I would like you to prepare me.

In three weeks I am presenting a paper at a university-industry workshop on technology ethics. The thesis I am defending is this. A product team that ships default settings deliberately optimized to maximize time-on-device, where the optimization runs against users' own stated intentions to limit use, is engaged in manipulation in a morally substantive sense — not merely in persuasion or ordinary design. The distinction between manipulation and persuasion can be drawn without condemning every recommendation algorithm or menu default, and the responsibility for refitting the most predatory defaults of the attention economy lies, in the first instance, with the teams that ship and tune them, not only with platforms or advertisers.

My argument in brief is what follows. First, manipulation is best understood as influence that works by subverting or bypassing the deliberative capacities a person would need to act in line with her own settled commitments, while persuasion engages those capacities. Second, an engagement feature is open to that charge when its designers deliberately optimize it against a metric that tracks the user's own self-reported or behaviorally measured intention to stop or cut down. Third, several common techniques — the endless feed with no natural stopping point, variable-ratio rewards from notifications, autoplay chains, and streak mechanics that lean on loss aversion — are routinely optimized in exactly that way. Fourth, the fact that these techniques are delivered through apparently neutral interface code does not dissolve the team's agency in selecting and tuning them.

The audience is the part that worries me. Most attendees are human-computer interaction researchers and practicing product designers, several of whom work on engagement, notifications, or recommender systems; there are also two behavioral scientists who run retention experiments. Their likely shared commitments are these: designed environments always steer behavior, so singling out persuasive design is arbitrary; A/B tests and revealed behavior are at least as good a guide to what users want as their self-reports; talk of addiction is inflated; and moralized categories mostly smuggle in policy preferences under a philosophical veneer. They care about good craft, about not being blamed for an industry they did not create, and about users' dignity as competent choosers.

Act as a moral philosopher who works on autonomy, manipulation, and the ethics of technology, and who has coached people through hostile but fair Q&A sessions. Before you write anything final, take yourself through a genuine brainstorm of the audience's likely commitments and where my argument is weakest; then produce the prep sheet, not a defense of the paper.

The prep sheet should contain: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered as nearly as you can by how likely they are and how much damage they can do, each phrased in the way this audience would put it; for each objection, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes; for each reply, a note on where it is soft; a short list of what I should concede in my opening remarks to protect my credibility; the one reply that has to land for the talk to hold; and the question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not give me a bibliography or reading suggestions — I need spoken replies and tactical judgment, not citations.
