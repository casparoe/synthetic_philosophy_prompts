I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether conceivability is any kind of guide to metaphysical possibility, and I came away suspecting that the inference is in worse shape than its defenders admit. I am compiling a list of objections, and so far I have these: the necessary a posteriori cases, such as water's failing to be H2O, which are conceivable but impossible; the hidden-mathematical-error cases, where both a conjecture and its negation can seem conceivable before a proof even though exactly one is possible; the idealization gap, meaning that once one idealizes conceivability enough to avoid such counterexamples, one no longer has direct access to whether the idealized state actually obtains; and the general unreliability worry that our modal intuitions are shaped by contingent features of our cognitive apparatus and have a poor track record in remote domains. I suspect this already covers the standard moves, so I am not asking for a sharper statement of any of them. Give me one more objection that is not on the list and not a disguised variant or special case of anything on it, one with a genuinely different underlying structure, and then say honestly whether you think it is any good and what it would show that the others do not. Please write your answer in American English.
