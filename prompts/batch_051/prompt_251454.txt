I am convening an adversarial collaboration between two members of my working group who have argued for months about whether Arrow's impossibility theorem undermines appeals to "the will of the people." One of them — the aggregation skeptic — holds that the theorem and its relatives show that no procedure for aggregating individual preferences into a coherent collective will can satisfy a set of conditions that any democratic reading of that phrase must respect; invocations of the people's will are therefore either loose rhetoric or a cover for agenda control. The other — the deliberative democrat — holds that the theorem applies only to the aggregation of antecedently fixed preferences under a severe informational restriction, whereas the democratic will is formed and transformed through public reasoning; on this view the impossibility is an artifact of treating citizens as inputs to a social welfare function rather than as participants in a practice of collective judgment.

Write the document this collaboration should produce. It should begin with a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, including a precise statement of the theorem and its conditions, exactly what it rules out, what it does not rule out, and what it implies about agenda-dependence and majority cycles. Then give a disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, state the aggregation skeptic's position in wording the skeptic would sign and the deliberative democrat's position in wording the democrat would sign, avoiding slanted or straw formulations. Next identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case, study, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why. I suspect the cruxes include whether structured deliberation reliably produces enough preference structure to avoid cycles; whether independence of irrelevant alternatives remains a defensible normative condition once reasons and intensities are admitted; and whether the same impossibility reappears when what is aggregated is not preferences but judgments on logically connected propositions — add or remove cruxes only as the two positions would themselves require.

Do not adjudicate between the camps. The deliverable is a constructive text both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than resolved.
