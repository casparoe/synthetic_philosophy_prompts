I am a PhD student in political theory working on the justification of constitutional entrenchment, and my reading group keeps returning to a disagreement that we have not yet managed to state precisely. I would like you to draft the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the two camps would produce. The deliverable should be a joint statement, written in language both camps would sign, that makes the disagreement as exact as possible rather than declaring a winner.

The first camp, which I will call the continuity view, holds that constitutional precommitment can inherit much of the justification of an individual's Ulysses contract. On this view, a political community over time is enough like a single agent with considered higher-order ends that binding itself against predictable future majorities is rational self-governance rather than an external constraint. The second camp, the dead-hand view, holds that the analogy is doing illicit work: in the individual case one and the same agent (or a sufficiently continuous self) does the binding and is bound, whereas a constitution binds later persons who did not do the binding; a political community is not a unified subject with settled higher-order preferences; and entrenched provisions are often harder to revise than the instruction Ulysses gave his crew to ignore his future pleas. On this view, constitutional entrenchment needs a justification different in kind from the rationality of self-binding.

Please draft the joint statement with at least these components. (1) A numbered list of agreed claims about what individual Ulysses contracts actually show, what the constitutional case involves, and what each camp does not claim about the other's position; the agreed claims should not be vacuous. (2) A disagreements table in which each row states the precise form of a residual disagreement, with each camp's own position formulated in wording that camp accepts, and the opposing position formulated in wording the other camp regards as fair. (3) A list of cruxes, meaning sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp, and for each crux the case, test, or argument that both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. (4) A short assessment of which parts of the dispute are substantive and which are likely verbal—for instance, disputes about what "self" or "autonomy" means—and why.

In drafting, pay particular attention to: whether an individual Ulysses contract is itself justified by diachronic continuity and whether that continuity comes in degrees; whether a constitutional amendment rule is better modeled as the agent's retained power to revise its ends or as a standing decision procedure; and whether the two camps can agree that the continuity view states at most a necessary condition on justification rather than a sufficient one. Distinguish entrenchment of substantive rights from entrenchment of decision procedures where that matters. The interesting question is not which camp has the better slogan but exactly where joint text is possible.
