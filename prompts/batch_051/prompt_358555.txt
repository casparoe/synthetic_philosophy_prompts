I'm taking a seminar this semester on Borges and the philosophy of time, and for the final session we have the option of submitting a short poem instead of a response paper. I keep going back and forth about the tension that runs through Borges's treatment of time: he is drawn to the idealist argument that time is unreal, since all one ever actually encounters is a present perception, and past and future look like constructions or habits of mind rather than things we perceive; yet he also admits that the very act of constructing a refutation of time, and then remembering that one constructed it, reinstates sequence, memory, and duration. The position is less a settled doctrine than an oscillation between wanting time to dissolve and finding that the dissolving self is still temporal.

Could you write me a sonnet that stages this oscillation? I would like the octave to build the idealist case against time in a Borgesian register—barely anything beyond the present moment, the past and future as fictions, the absence of any external clock outside the mind. The volta, when it comes, should turn the argument against itself: to assert that time is unreal and to recall having asserted it is already to be in time. The sestet should then return to the thought that time cannot be refuted from outside because the self that would do the refuting is made of time—close to Borges's own conclusion, but without quoting him. I would also like the form to do some of the philosophical work: keep the octave's rhymes regular and self-enclosed, as if the sequence could be surveyed all at once, and then let the sestet's rhyme scheme fail or refuse to close, so the poem's own form reenacts the claim that the argument cannot finish itself. The poem should stand on its own for a reader who does not know Borges, while a reader who does should recognize the position without needing the title of the essay.
