I've been going down a rabbit hole on Hofstadter's old superrationality puzzles — the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery that Scientific American actually ran in the 80s. Quick setup in case you haven't seen them. Platonia: a trillionaire gathers 20 people, and if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person gets a billion dollars; if zero or more than one send, nobody gets anything, and cooperation is forbidden. Luring Lottery: readers could enter by sending a postcard with the number 1 for one entry, or any number n for n entries; the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, awarded to a randomly selected entry. Sending 1 keeps the prize large if most others send 1, but it makes the expected-value calculation weird, because sending a huge number raises your chance while shrinking the pot. The standard superrational move in the Luring Lottery is to simulate a die with as many sides as you expect responders, and send 1 only if it rolls 1, so in aggregate there's around one entry and the full prize stays on the table.

The thing I want to test is not whether that move is smart, though. The claim I keep seeing is that the die-rolling move is superrational in a stronger sense: you are not merely best-responding to a guess about other entrants, you are treating your own policy as evidence about what all the other symmetric reasoners in your reference class will do, and selecting the policy that would be best if everyone in the class ran the same policy. That's a claim about the decision procedure, not about the expected value, and I can't see from the outside how to tell it apart from plain risk-neutral expected-value maximization with a funny prior, or from an aesthetic preference like "don't be the jerk who starts the entry war," or from just pattern-matching the puzzle and doing the local clever-looking thing. I keep going back and forth on it with a friend and we always end up in reference class tennis.

So: how would you test whether someone in a game like this is actually superrational in that decision-procedure sense, rather than doing expected-value maximization or altruism or trolling? Design the best concrete experiment or observation you can — a modification of the lottery, an analysis of the historical Luring Lottery data, a framing manipulation, whatever has the most diagnostic power. Tell me exactly what result would push you toward "yes, this is a distinct decision procedure" and what would push you toward "no, it's all redescriptions of the same expected-value calculation." Then say what the test cannot settle even if it comes out the way superrationality fans would want. I'm especially curious what the actual 1980s outcome — people sending googolplex-length entries so the prize collapsed to a fraction of a cent — does and does not show.
