I'm on the city council in a small city in the upper Midwest — the kind of town that used to have three factories and now has one — and last night my friend Jess and I went in circles about the item on Tuesday's agenda. The city water utility found elevated lead in the drinking water in three neighborhoods, mostly low-income and mostly renters; the cause is a mix of old lead service lines and residual contamination from the manufacturer whose old riverfront plant was one of the sources. We can't afford the full replacement and cleanup on our own — a state grant covers about a third — and the company has offered to fund the rest, but only if the council approves a settlement releasing them from further liability and keeping the detailed test results out of public view for five years, supposably so they don't get buried in lawsuits from every property owner in the county. Our attorney says both paths are legally available — settle or litigate — and litigating would take years before anyone starts digging. Jess says if approving the settlement is the right call then it is just right, and all my talk about dirty hands is a luxury those neighborhoods can't drink. I don't find that convincing, but I also don't trust my instinct to treat "it felt wrong" as a reason.

I need you to draft the decision memo I'll circulate to the other four council members before Thursday's vote. Give a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons that both the member who thinks cutting a quiet deal with a polluter is never on the table and the member who thinks "best available option" settles everything can actually engage with. And make the memo give a real run to the strongest point on the losing side: if you recommend approving, don't treat the confidentiality clause and the liability waiver as a footnote, and if you recommend rejecting, don't write as if the lead is someone else's problem. Finally, flag which parts of the reasoning draw on things that are settled among people who work on questions like this and which parts are genuinely contested, because I don't want to send my colleagues a memo that presents one contested view as the obvious consensus.
