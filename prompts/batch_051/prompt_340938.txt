I have been reading about what different writing systems treat as the fundamental unit of language — the phoneme, the syllable, the morpheme, or the word — and I keep coming back to something my instructor said: a script is not a neutral recording device but an implicit theory of what in language is worth preserving. I can see the point in the abstract, but I want to make it concrete by examining an actual practice, and I would like you to reconstruct the commitments behind it.

Consider the practice of maintaining English orthography, especially in contrast with an orthography such as Spanish. English refuses to regularize spellings that no longer match pronunciation: the silent letters in sign, bomb, and muscle reappear as pronounced consonants in signature, bombard, and muscular; homophones like right, write, and rite remain visually distinct; and loanwords such as psychology and yacht keep spellings that encode their origins rather than their current English sound. Spanish, by contrast, privileges phonemic transparency in its spelling conventions and revisits them mainly to restore closer correspondence between spelling and pronunciation, for the most part without trying to hold a morpheme's written form constant when its pronunciation changes.

This contrast is puzzling because so much of how alphabetic writing is taught presupposes that letters stand for sounds, yet English orthography behaves as though letters stand at least partly for morphemes and etymologies. What view of language and meaning does each sort of orthography presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind a deep orthography and behind a shallow one, distinguishing what a spelling practice strictly commits anyone who defends it to from what merely happens to fit well with it. Then say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — whether the same underlying view of writing could license either sort of system — and what would have to change in pedagogy, lexicography, or literary culture if English were shifted to a phonemic principle.
