I'm not any kind of academic, I run a small landscaping business, but I read alot and I've been turning something over in my head about the way people talk in generalities. We say "ticks carry Lyme disease" and nobody blinks, even though the overwhelming majority of ticks don't carry it — in a lot of places only a small percentage of the ones that bite people do, and only at certain life stages. Same with "ducks lay eggs": male ducks never do, ducklings never do, and most adult females aren't laying at any particular moment, but the sentence is just accepted as true. Yet "ducks are female" is false, even though it's true of roughly the same ducks that lay eggs. So the rule clearly isn't "most of them."

I started noticing the same shape in talk about machines and medicine. The manual says "this car seats seven" and it's treated as true even though the third row only really fits small kids and then there's no trunk space. "This medication causes drowsiness" is accepted as true even if most people who take it don't get drowsy, but "this medication causes hallucinations" sounds wrong if it happens to one in ten thousand people — unless the hallucinations are really dramatic or frightening, in which case we might reverse ourselves. So it seems like three things matter: how usual it is, how serious or attention-grabbing it is, and something about what the thing is supposed to do. I can't tell whether the duck and tick cases and the car and medication cases are the same phenomenon or two different ones that people run together.

Is there a name for this kind of claim, the kind that is allowed to stay true while admitting exceptions? And more than a name: where is the actual boundary? What makes an exception count against the claim and what makes it not count? Dont just say "that's X" and stop — I want the outline of the thing, including whether the word covers all my cases or only some of them.
