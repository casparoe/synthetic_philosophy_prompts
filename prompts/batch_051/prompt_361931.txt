For the view that it can be legitimate to hold people to legal rules they did not know, even when their ignorance is not itself blameworthy, I can assemble the following standard arguments:

1. The evidentiary argument: courts cannot reliably distinguish sincere ignorance from self-serving ignorance after the fact, so a general ignorance defense would be systematically abused and would make enforcement unpredictable.
2. The incentive argument: refusing the excuse gives everyone a standing reason to learn what the law requires; allowing it would reward willful blindness and penalize those who made the effort to inform themselves.
3. The fair-opportunity argument: in a legal system that promulgates its rules, ordinary people have a fair chance to discover them, so ignorance is usually negligent and the law need not make special allowances for it.
4. The line-drawing argument: once ignorance excuses in one tangled corner of regulation, there is no principled place to stop, and every obscure prohibition becomes a potential excuse, so the law's force would turn on each person's subjective state of knowledge.
5. The shared-standard argument: law works as a public, common standard of conduct; letting each person's actual knowledge fix the extent of their obligations would fragment that standard and undercut the coordination the law exists to provide.

I take these to be the standard families of argument, and I suspect there is another family not on the list—something that locates the justification in the expressive or communicative dimension of punishment, or in what a plea of ignorance does in the social world rather than what it reports about the defendant's mind. I cannot state it cleanly. What is that further argument? Once you have stated it, say whether it is genuinely independent of the five listed above, and whether it survives the hardest case: a rule the person could not have discovered even with reasonable diligence.
