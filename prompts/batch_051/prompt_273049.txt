I train new non-commissioned officers in the army, and the unit keeps hitting the same wall: they know they're supposed to obey lawful orders, but nobody gives them a usable test for when an order is lawful versus when it's legal but the soldier has a serious moral objection. I want something I can hand out. There's this part in Plato's Crito where the Laws of Athens argue that Socrates owes them obedience because they raised him, educated him, allowed him to leave if he didn't like the city, and he stayed voluntarily his whole life, so he's made an agreement to go along with their judgments. I'm not quoting it, but you'll know the section. Can you turn that line of reasoning into a decision procedure my NCOs could actually use? Like: the questions to ask in order, what makes an answer sufficient, where the procedure tells them to obey, where it tells them to push back or refuse, and where it says a person has to decide because the principle runs out. Then run it on three cases: (1) an order to falsify a maintenance log to keep a vehicle on the road; (2) an order to enforce a restriction on soldiers' off-base speech that the NCO thinks is illegal but hasn't been tested in court; (3) an order to carry out a legally authorized but strategically pointless mission that will likely get subordinates killed. I want you to be honest about where the Crito argument stops being useful—most soldiers never made anything like Socrates' agreement, so the procedure should flag when that disanalogy matters.
