You are a professional philosopher who works on judgment, forecasting, and the epistemology of planning. Treat me as someone who can follow fine-grained distinctions but who has not yet checked whether the commitments below cohere. I want a committed audit, not a survey of alternatives.

I have settled into the following six positions over the past few years. Each seems right to me in context, but I have never tested them against one another.

1. In estimating how long a project will take, what it will cost, or how likely it is to succeed, I should reason chiefly from base rates for similar projects rather than from a step-by-step model of the particular project.
2. Reference classes should be chosen wide and impersonally. The impulse to pick a narrow class that flatters my project is usually special pleading; the right starting point is the widest class whose members still share the causally relevant features.
3. Even when a base-rate estimate overrides my own forecast, inside-view reasoning cannot be set aside. Building a plan requires specifying steps, dependencies, risks, and design choices, and a base rate answers a different question than a plan does.
4. My own detailed causal stories are epistemically suspect. When such a story pulls against a broad base rate, I should treat the story as likely rationalization unless I have evidence for it that is independent of my own construction of the case.
5. For genuinely novel projects—first-of-their-kind technologies or unprecedented interventions—there is no reference class similar enough to trust, and the outside view has no proper application. There one must reason from mechanisms and design-level analogies.
6. The authority of the outside view is nevertheless defeasible. If I can identify a difference in kind between this case and the reference class, and trace through a causal mechanism how that difference changes the outcome, then the mechanism-based judgment should win.

Please begin by sketching a rough pass over the pairs; some apparent conflicts may dissolve under a distinction and I want you to catch those rather than flag everything. Then deliver a final audit: identify which pairs are in genuine tension, say how severe each tension is and what the cheapest repair would be, including what that repair gives up, and explain which single revision would clean up the most. Give me a definite verdict and defend it.
