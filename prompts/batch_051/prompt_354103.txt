I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about Harry Frankfurt's 1969 essay *Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility*, and I came away with the uncomfortable sense that neither of us had followed the argument all the way to its practical consequences. My friend kept repeating that if Black is waiting to force Jones to do the deed, then Jones cannot be a fair target of blame, because there was no genuine alternative; I responded that what matters is the actual way Jones ended up performing the action — his own deliberation, his own effort, the fact that Black never had to step in. We both agreed that the case puts pressure on the principle that a person is morally responsible only if he could have done otherwise, but we disagreed sharply about what follows for our everyday practices of holding one another responsible.

I want to think through that downstream question more carefully. Act as a professional philosopher whose research focuses on moral responsibility and free will. Begin by brainstorming a broad list of candidate implications, then narrow and defend the most important ones. Specifically, if we were to accept a Frankfurt-style case as a decisive counterexample to the principle of alternative possibilities, what would be the most important implications for how we should understand and perhaps revise our practices of blame, praise, punishment, and moral education?

In particular, please address this puzzle: if the ability to do otherwise is not necessary for responsibility, what becomes of the ordinary exculpating force of the claim "I couldn't have done otherwise"? Should we reinterpret that plea as tracking something about the way the action was produced — for example, whether it issued from coercion, manipulation, or motives that were not really the agent's own — rather than the bare absence of alternatives? And what would accepting the Frankfurt case then force us to change, or allow us to keep, in our reactive attitudes toward wrongdoing? Do not spend time restating the case; take its success as a premise and work through what accepting it would commit us to.
