I've been trying to figure out what theory of animal moral status is buried in the way we regulate insecticides. In most rich countries, before you can sell a new insecticide you have to show it doesn't pose unreasonable risk to human health or "the environment," and the environment part is basically always about populations of birds, fish, bees, aquatic invertebrates, water quality, that sort of thing. The individual suffering of insects — the very animals the product is designed to kill — never appears as a harm category. A spray that kills cockroaches by gradually shutting down their nervous systems counts as effective; often the marketing boasts about knockdown time, how fast they stop moving, as if immobility were the same as a good death. The same legal system would treat slowly poisoning a dog as criminal cruelty. And even inside pesticide regulation, bees get protected but only as pollinators — as agricultural infrastructure — while the same bee as a suffering individual seems to count for nothing. So what picture of moral standing does this practice presuppose? Does it assume insects are not sentient, or that insect welfare is simply worth zero, or that only ecological-level harms matter? Are those assumptions forced by the practice, or just historically bundled with it? And if a regulator started treating insect suffering as nonzero, what specifically would have to change in how these products are tested and approved?
