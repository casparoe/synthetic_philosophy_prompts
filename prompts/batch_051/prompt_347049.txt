In 1990, Marilyn vos Savant's column in Parade defended the answer that switching doors in the Monty Hall problem wins with probability two-thirds, and a large number of readers—many with mathematical training—wrote in insisting she was wrong. Treat this episode as a case in the epistemology of theory versus intuition: the formal probability argument is the theory, and the readers' felt certainty that the two remaining doors are equally likely is the intuition. Specify what the formal argument must assume, especially about the host's protocol, and consider whether the dissenting readers could have been tracking something the standard model left out. Then assess what the episode shows. Does widespread, expert-endorsed intuitive certainty deserve much weight when it clashes with a sound formal argument, or does the case mainly show that intuition fails when it misreads the setup?
