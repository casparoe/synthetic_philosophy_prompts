I have been working out a principle I call the Law of Price as Confession: when two rational agents with a common prior and common knowledge of one another's rationality exchange an asset purely because they assess its payoff differently, the trade itself is impossible — each would treat the other's willingness to trade at that price as information and revise until one side withdrew. I know this sits near the no-trade theorem, but I take it one step further and claim that the theorem is secretly an empirical diagnostic: in a market where non-informational motives (liquidity, hedging, differences in risk tolerance) are small, observed volume measures the amount of prior disagreement or bounded rationality in the market. For example, I claim that a heavily traded market for a single widely analyzed stock cannot be explained mostly by dispersed private information; it must be explained mostly by incompatible priors or systematic error. I would like an honest assessment. Is the diagnostic move sound, or does it over-read the theorem? Name the nearest existing results I have re-derived under another name, then give the strongest familiar, high-volume market my law classifies wrongly, and tell me whether that failure is a fixable boundary condition or a fatal one.
