I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether dreams are experiences you have while asleep, in roughly real time, or something more like a story assembled at the instant of waking up. This is the old Malcolm/Dennett territory, but what actually got me stuck is the cassette picture: no experiences happen during the night at all, then at the moment of waking a 'tape' gets loaded into memory, complete with the felt sense that it all happened earlier and unfolded over time, and you can't tell the difference from the inside. As I understand it, the point was never that the cassette picture is literally true, but that it's a big problem for any evidence that's supposed to show dreams play out in real time while you're asleep.

So here's what I want. Take the claim that dreams are genuine, temporally extended experiences that occur during sleep, at something like waking pace, and treat it as a testable-ish claim rather than a purely verbal one. Design the best experiment you can think of that would distinguish that from the cassette/waking-compilation account. I want it concrete: what you'd measure, which result pushes each way, and what each side would say if the result went against them. I keep comming back to a real-time decoding setup — train a classifier on waking perceptual states, run it during REM, and check whether decoded dream content unfolds at sleep-time speed and responds to external probes (a scent, a sound) right when they actually happen, versus only cohering into a plot in the few seconds before an awakening. But I can already feel the goalposts moving: the 'real experience' side says the decoder isn't reading the dream itself, the cassette side says the tape is being written in real time but not experienced until later. So also tell me what even a clean positive result wouldn't settle, and give me a straight answer on whether the two views have been insulated so no result could move them — and whether that's a problem, or just the sign that the disagreement is partly conceptual.
