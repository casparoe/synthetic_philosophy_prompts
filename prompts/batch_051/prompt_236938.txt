For the journal of my school's philosophy club, I have been asked to write down my current commitments about Dennett's ladder of minds so that the other members can audit them before I submit. The set I would like to defend is this:

1. Dennett's four-rung ladder — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — is at least the right ordering of cognitive capacities: each rung above the first adds a new selection environment while keeping the capacities of the lower rungs.

2. The first two rungs are mindless in a strong sense. Neither natural selection nor operant conditioning requires the organism to represent the world, its own behavior, or the outcomes of that behavior; neither process, by itself, gives us a reason to attribute beliefs or desires to the organism.

3. The Popperian rung is where mind proper enters. What is new is an inner environment in which candidate behaviors can be tried out and eliminated before they are performed. This is a difference in kind from Skinnerian learning, not merely a faster version of it, because it requires an inner model and a distinction between the organism and the world.

4. Yet I do not think this inner environment has to be conscious. An unconscious system that ran internal simulations and discarded harmful or pointless candidates before acting would, to that extent, count as Popperian.

5. The Gregorian step, by contrast, is only a difference in degree, not a new kind of mind. Words and other mind-tools extend the Popperian inner environment, letting us run more hypotheses and hypotheses we could not have generated alone, but they do not introduce a fundamentally new type of process.

6. I also hold that the four rungs are not natural kinds with sharp boundaries. Actual organisms are mixtures, and there is no definite fact about whether, say, a particular rat is purely Skinnerian or partly Popperian; the ladder is a set of useful idealizations.

7. Even so, I want to say that a solely Skinnerian animal can learn to avoid a food that has made it sick but cannot deliberate about whether to eat it. Deliberation, in the sense of weighing alternatives before acting, requires at least Popperian capacity in the relevant domain, and that threshold marks a real difference in what the animal can do.

Could you audit this set as a whole? I want to know which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordered from most to least severe, and which pairs only appear to be in tension once the relevant terms are made precise. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost. Finally, tell me which single change would do the most to make the whole set hang together.
