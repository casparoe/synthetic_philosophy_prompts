Philosophy professor here. I'd like you to act as a poet who also knows philosophy of mathematics well, and to think carefully before drafting. I want a Shakespearean sonnet about the real numbers, specifically about the tension between seeing the continuum as something we define (through Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) and something we discover as already there. Here is the structural constraint: the three quatrains should respectively give me (1) the rational line with its gaps, (2) a cut or sequence that seems to create a new point, and (3) the completed continuum with no gaps; the volta at line 9 should turn on whether this completion is an act of construction or recognition. The closing couplet should state the discovered-or-defined dilemma without resolving it. Someone teaching Dedekind should recognize the moves, and a student meeting the real numbers for the first time should still get a good poem, not just a math lesson. Before you write, start by brainstorming which features of the cut construction (the gap, the left and right classes, the new number, completeness) map onto which parts of the sonnet form, and then write the poem so the rhyme scheme does some of that work. Keep the math accurate enough that I could use it in class; don't just name-drop Dedekind.
