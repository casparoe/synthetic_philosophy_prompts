A familiar move in arguments about historical figures is the man-of-his-time defence: it is unfair to judge Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus by present standards, because the values those standards express were not live options in their worlds; their actions and attitudes were widely accepted among their contemporaries, and no one can reasonably be expected to see beyond the moral horizon of their own age. Suppose a specific development has put the central empirical claim of this defence under strain. Mass digitization of correspondence, pamphlets, newspapers, and petitions has made it easy to search the surrounding record and recover contemporaneous voices—abolitionists, dissenting clerics, rival officials, sometimes the victims themselves—who articulated versions of the very objection we now make, and in significant cases the figure knew of such objections or at least moved in circles where they were being pressed. The question is not whether Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus were blameworthy. The question is what becomes of the defence. Does it survive this development, need repair, or collapse? Identify which premise the digitized record pressures, distinguish an empirical reading of the defence from a normative reading, and give the strongest version of the defence that can honestly survive once that record is admitted.
