International lawyers and diplomats often insist that statehood is a matter of fact rather than of recognition: once a political community has a defined territory, a permanent population, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states, it is a state, and recognition merely records a pre-existing situation. Yet the conduct of the same states seems to treat recognition as something closer to an act that confers status. Recognition disputes over Kosovo and Taiwan are carried on as though acknowledgment mattered to statehood; entities such as Somaliland, Abkhazia, and Northern Cyprus are kept outside international organizations, multilateral treaties, and courts even where they appear to satisfy the factual criteria; and admission to international institutions is settled by a political vote rather than by a factual certification.

Write an analysis of what theory of statehood and recognition this split practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit view that would make the behavior coherent, and be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the points where the practice is compatible with several theories rather than one—including the possibility that the system is working with two inconsistent theories at once. Finally, state which element of your reconstruction is least well supported and what argument, example, or change in practice would lead you to revise it.
