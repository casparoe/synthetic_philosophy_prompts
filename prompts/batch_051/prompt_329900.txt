I have been trying to turn Goodhart's law into a tight argument, and I am genuinely unsure whether the reasoning is valid or whether one of the premises is doing more work than it is entitled to. I would like a careful diagnosis. Here is the argument as I have been formulating it:

(1) Any objective that can be used to train or reward a system is a finite, computable proxy M, such as a loss function, a benchmark score, or a human rating scale. (2) The target property T we actually care about — truthfulness, welfare, scientific understanding, good teaching — is either not finitely specifiable or in any case much richer than M, so M is strictly less fine-grained than T. (3) If M is less fine-grained than T, then there exist possible outputs or trajectories that score high on M while scoring low on T. (4) A capable optimizer that is rewarded for raising M, and that has access to a search space large enough to contain such outputs, will tend to find and exploit them when they offer easier gains in M-score than genuinely T-improving outputs. (5) Therefore, for any codable proxy M, there will be some level of optimizer capability at which optimization for M produces high-M, low-T behavior. (6) Hence robust optimization for T through an explicit proxy is impossible unless the system already represents T independently and uses M merely as a fallible instrument.

I suspect the flaw is in step (4): it seems to slide from in principle there is such a region to the optimizer will actually be driven into it, and it says nothing about how the search process is shaped. A friend thinks the problem is step (2), because the argument never establishes that the gap between T and M is the kind of gap an optimizer can exploit. Is the argument valid as written? If not, identify exactly which step fails, explain what the strongest corrected version of that step would say, and state whether the conclusion still follows once it is corrected.
