a few friends and I keep going in circles about whether we should go in for moral bioenhancement — drugs, brain stimulation, gene editing, whatever — before the century's disasters catch up with us. every argument grabs one intervention (serotonin, oxytocin, propranolol for implicit bias) and one objection (it's cheating, it's coercion, it's just drugging people into compliance) and never gets anywhere, because we've never agreed on what an adequate proposal would even have to accomplish. so I want the full spec first: what does a theory of moral bioenhancement actually have to get right, as a list of requirements, before it's worth arguing about any paricular intervention?

for each requirement, give me the case or consideration that forces it, and tell me whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's preference. I'm not just asking for the obvious "safety and efficacy" stuff, though that belongs on the list if you think it's doing real argumentative work.

here's the kind of thing I keep noticing and want either sorted into the list or explicitly rejected as not a real requirement:

- the difference between making someone behave better and making them be better: if a drug reduces implicit bias by blunting fear responses, is that moral enhancement or just bias removal? does it matter that the person didn't do any moral reasoning?

- the moral-standard problem: enhancement has to improve someone along a moral dimension, but "more moral" can't just mean "more like whatever we happen to value" if the original worry is that our current moral equipment is defective. a theory has to say which direction improvement runs without smuggling in the very values that are up for grabs.

- the agency/authenticity worry: a change that operates beneath reflection might make conduct better while leaving the person morally worse off in some other sense. is that a real objection or just squeamishness?

- scale and composition: a trait that's a virtue in one person might be a disaster if everyone has it. the persson–savulescu worry that got me into this was never mainly about making individuals nicer — it's that our moral hardware evolved for small groups but our technological power is now planetary. so mass empathy could just give everyone tighter ingroup loyalty rather than wider concern. a theory has to say how the enhancement works at population scale, not just in one head.

- the selection/competance paradox: the people who'd voluntarily take a morality pill are probably the ones who need it least, and the ones who need it most would refuse. mandatory use, meanwhile, implies the very people we've just diagnosed as morally defective are fit to choose and administer the enhancement for everyone.

- the comparison requirement: it has to beat the alternatives — ordinary moral education, better institutions, cognitive enhancement by itself. if making everyone smarter just speeds up the invention of new weapons and new coordination failures, that's a strike against going cognitive-only.

- side effects on useful moral emotions: anger, guilt, shame, and the capacity to be appalled do jobs. a person whose affect is blunted might be harmless and lovely and useless for noticing or resisting injustice. any adequate theory has to tell us which negative emotions it can afford to mute and which it can't.

then score the main existing positions against the list briefly — but keep the weight on the criteria, since that's where I think the actual thinking is. tell me which of these you'd reject as a real requirement and why, whether any subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement does the most to knock out the most popular proposals.

the purpose I care about: a theory good enough to guide whether moral bioenhancement is even worth pursuing as part of a response to global catastrophic risk.
