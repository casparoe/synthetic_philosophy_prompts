My friend Sam has extremely confident views about rescue cases—the kind where you can save one group of people or another group but not both—and we argue about these constantly, so I've started writing down the verdicts he's actually committed to. The list so far:

- Two strangers on one ledge vs. two strangers on the other, all equally easy to reach and all with the same odds: he says it's a wash, flip a coin, it doesn't matter.
- One stranger vs. five strangers, everyone equally likely to survive if rescued, except the one is his girlfriend: he says you obviously save the girlfriend, and treating her as just "one" is obtuse.
- One stranger vs. five strangers, but the one has about a 30% chance of pulling through even if you save them, while each of the five has about a 90% chance: he says save the five, the math is obvious.
- One stranger vs. five strangers, but the one has about a 95% chance and each of the five has about a 5% chance: he says save the one, again obvious.

The weird thing is, he's never actually told me what he'd say about the plain, boring version of the case: one stranger vs. five strangers, everyone healthy, all strangers, all with the same excellent odds, and you can rescue one group or the other. He kind of changes the subject whenever I push on it. I'm trying to figure out whether he's been running a consistent rule all along and, if so, what that rule would say about the plain case. But I can't tell whether he's basically a maximizer (save the five, since five lives beat one), a Taurek-style no-numbers person (don't let the numbers add up, so give the one and the five an equal shot, maybe flip a coin), or something else that happens to be consistent with everything he's said so far. What are the main rules that fit his track record, what does each one predict about the plain case, which do you think he'd actually end up giving and why, and what follow-up case should I spring on him to settle which rule he's actually been using?
