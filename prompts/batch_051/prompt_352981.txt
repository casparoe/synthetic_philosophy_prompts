I'm a retired biochemist who's been reading philosophy of mind for the first time, and I keep tripping over the claim that when we ask whether some AI or simulation deserves moral consideration, the substrate is irrelevant — only the computation matters. Fine. But "the computation" is nowhere near as well-defined as people talk like it is, and that's where the attribution problem bites: before I can test whether a computation matters, I have to say which computation is being attributed the feelings. Take two boxes on my bench. Both give the same verbal pain reports, the same avoidance responses, the same readouts on every test I've ran. Box A turns out to run a giant lookup table that maps each input I've tried onto the matching output. Box B runs a slow procedure that builds an internal model of its recent states, integrates over them for a while, and then throws that model away before acting. I have a strong gut feeling that Box B is the one that matters morally, and that the difference between A and B is doing real work. How would I test that instead of just trusting my gut? Give me the best experimental battery you can design, tell me exactly what result would push you toward "the inner computation is what counts" and what would push you toward "only the I/O behavior counts," and then be honest about what even a clean positive result wouldn't settle. In particular: can any feasible test distinguish "the discarded internal state is where the suffering is" from "the discarded internal state is just one more cause of the suffering-report"? Or is that part of the claim untestable in principle rather than just in practice?
