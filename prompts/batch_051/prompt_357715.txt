I run a reading group that recently took up the question whether scientific, ethical, and religious descriptions of reality are all true at once or whether they are, in some sense, competitors. One member, who has some background in South Asian philosophy, keeps ending the discussion by invoking the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, which he glosses as the view that reality has irreducibly many aspects and no single description captures the whole. He always compresses it into the one-liner "truth is many-sided," and then treats the original question as settled: each apparently conflicting account is just one side of a many-sided truth, so there is nothing left to adjudicate.

The trouble is that the slogan seems to be doing several different jobs at different moments. Sometimes it seems to assert only that no individual inquirer has all the evidence or all the relevant experience; sometimes it seems to assert that two logically incompatible claims can both be true; sometimes it seems to assert something about every assertion being only partially true from an angle. I want to respond to him precisely rather than just push back vaguely. Lay out the distinct precise theses that somebody repeating "truth is many-sided" could be asserting, ordered from the most modest (hard to deny) to the most radical. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible doctrine, or false. Then tell me which of these readings would actually license his move of closing a debate about whether apparently conflicting accounts of reality can all be true, and which readings would at most support open-mindedness or further inquiry.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
