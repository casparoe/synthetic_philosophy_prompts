I have spent the past eight years in this country as a permanent resident. I pay its taxes and am bound by its criminal law, civil law, and public-health orders, and my children are citizens here; but I cannot vote in national elections, and the policies that most directly govern my life need not be answerable to me. Until recently I treated this as a harmless artifact of how sovereignty is arranged. Lately it has begun to seem to me that it is either a serious wrong or a serious confusion about what a demos is, and I cannot yet tell which.

When I try to think it through, I find people defending a single principle by pointing at a single case. One friend says that anyone coerced by the laws should get a vote, full stop. Another says the franchise properly belongs to those who share the national identity, and that my remedy is naturalization or return. A third says that anyone whose interests are affected should be included, which seems to me to dissolve the boundary entirely, since in a connected world everything is affected by everything eventually. I do not want to adjudicate these claims case by case. I want the standard of evaluation itself.

Before I read any more theories about the boundary problem, write the desiderata that an adequate theory of who belongs to the demos must meet: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the constraints it cannot violate, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Among the cases I have in mind are the long-term non-citizen resident; the citizen living abroad on whom the home state still legislates; the cross-border commuter; the minor who works and pays tax; the person disenfranchised after a criminal conviction; the residents of a territory governed by a state in whose national elections they have no vote; future people who will live with a polity's decisions; and the stateless person. Do not feel bound by that list if your requirements imply other cases I should care about. I am asking what a defensible democratic account of inclusion must get right, not merely how existing states happen to draw the line.

Then mark, for each requirement, whether it is a non-negotiable constraint on any defensible theory or a preference of a particular camp, such as partisans of an all-affected principle, an all-subjected principle, or a social-membership view. I especially want to know whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself becomes an impossibility result rather than a wish list, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard candidate theories. If you close with a short assessment of how the all-affected, all-subjected, and social-membership views fare against your list, that is useful, but the list and its motivation are what I am after.
