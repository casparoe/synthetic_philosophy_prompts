Here are my verdicts about whether natural selection is fruitfully modeled as a kind of gradient descent, and what kind of optimizer it is. I want to know whether there is a single principle behind them, or whether some have to go.

1. If a population sits on a low local fitness peak and a higher peak is separated from it by a fitness valley, I think it will typically stay put unless drift, recombination, or migration happens to leap the valley. So I don't think evolution is a global optimizer.

2. But I also think the repeated, independent evolution of eyes and of powered flight suggests evolution is not just stuck or aimless: given diverse starting lineages and enough time, similar good solutions are found reliably. So there is some robustness to its search.

3. Consider two alleles that are each neutral or mildly harmful alone but strongly beneficial together. Sexual recombination can assemble the beneficial pair from standing variation faster than waiting for two simultaneous mutations in one lineage. My gut says this is a genuine advantage, but I am unsure whether to count it as the optimizer being clever or simply as a less myopic kind of search than pointwise mutation.

4. Horizontal gene transfer in bacteria, where antibiotic resistance genes spread as a cassette across unrelated lineages, strikes me as a big disanalogy with gradient descent: evolution can reuse and transfer whole modules without gradually retracing the path that produced them, while gradient descent does not transplant parameters wholesale from one run to another.

5. Many molecular substitutions are nearly neutral, so evolution sometimes wanders in ways unrelated to fitness. I think this means evolution is not always an optimizer at all; sometimes it is just a random walk, which gradient descent is only when the gradient is effectively zero.

6. I also think broad evolutionary trends, such as increases in body size or the apparent rise in complexity, can arise as passive diffusion against a lower bound rather than as optimization toward a target. So I am skeptical of treating them as evidence that selection is climbing a gradient toward "more complex."

7. Niche construction, where organisms modify their own environments—beavers dam streams, plants alter soil chemistry—means the fitness landscape changes as the organism moves. My gut says the fixed-landscape picture behind most gradient descent is false in many real cases, and this is a deep disanalogy rather than a minor complication.

8. Yet a specialist species that tracks a resource closely and then goes extinct when the resource collapses strikes me as exactly like a model overfit to its training distribution. Here the analogy between natural selection and gradient descent feels strong and illuminating, not misleading.

9. Sexual selection runaway, such as an increasingly extravagant peacock's tail, looks to me like selection climbing a local gradient—female preference and ornament reinforce each other—that eventually reduces viability and can drive a lineage into a dead end. I think this is like gradient descent following a deceptive gradient in a non-stationary landscape.

10. But when I think about genetic algorithms as practical optimizers, I usually expect them to be less efficient than gradient-based methods on smooth problems and most useful on problems with rugged or discontinuous objectives where gradient methods fail. That makes me hesitant to call evolution a "stupid" optimizer, because sometimes being gradient-free is the point.

Some of these feel inconsistent: I want to say evolution is a myopic hill-climber in case 1, but also that recombination and horizontal transfer are importantly unlike myopic hill-climbing in cases 3 and 4, and I do not know what single picture holds all of that together.

Is there a coherent principle—or a small set of candidate principles—about what natural selection is as an optimizer, and what gradient descent can and cannot borrow from it, that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? What does the principle say about a further case I keep going back and forth on: the evolvability hypothesis, that natural selection can act on mutation rates, modularity, or developmental plasticity to make a lineage better at evolving, even though these properties may have no immediate advantage for current fitness? Is evolvability a way the optimizer tunes its own search, or is it mostly an attractive illusion? If the best principle forces me to drop one or more of my verdicts, say which ones and why.
