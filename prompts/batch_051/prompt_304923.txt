Question: Explain Ellsberg's paradox and its challenge to subjective expected utility theory. In your answer: (i) describe the choice problem and the typical pattern of preferences; (ii) explain why this pattern cannot be represented by a single subjective probability distribution over the states; and (iii) briefly describe one formal response to the paradox and one limitation of that response.

Student's answer: Ellsberg's paradox involves two urns. Urn I contains 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn II contains 100 balls, each of which is red or black, but the proportion is unknown. A person is asked to choose between betting on red from Urn I and betting on red from Urn II, and similarly for black. The typical pattern is a preference for red from Urn I over red from Urn II, together with a preference for black from Urn I over black from Urn II. Under subjective expected utility theory, the first preference implies that the person assigns lower subjective probability to drawing red from Urn II than to drawing red from Urn I, so p(red in Urn II) < 0.5. The second preference implies p(black in Urn II) < 0.5. But every ball in Urn II is either red or black, so these probabilities should sum to 1; together they sum to less than 1. Thus no single additive probability distribution over the states can make both Ellsberg choices expected-utility maximizing.

This shows that people are simply more risk-averse when facing the ambiguous urn: the unknown composition is treated as if it were a riskier version of an already risky fifty-fifty prospect. The Ellsberg choices therefore are not a genuinely separate phenomenon from ordinary risk aversion, and the right response is to adopt prospect theory, since prospect theory accounts for these choices through probability weighting. A more satisfactory formal treatment is the maxmin expected utility model of Gilboa and Schmeidler, in which the agent entertains a set of prior probability distributions and evaluates each act by its worst-case expected utility across that set. In the ambiguous urn, the set of priors over red in Urn II can include values below 0.5, so the worst-case expected utility of betting on red from Urn II is lower than that of betting on red from Urn I; the same applies to black, yielding the observed preferences. A limitation of this model is that it leaves the set of priors unspecified: without an independent account of which priors belong in the set, the theory can be made to fit almost any pattern of choice, while with such an account the original problem seems to reappear at the level of the prior set.
