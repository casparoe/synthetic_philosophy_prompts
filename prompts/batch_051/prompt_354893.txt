You are grading for an upper-level undergraduate course in nineteenth-century political philosophy. Below are an exam question and a student's answer.

Exam question:
Mill's The Subjection of Women is often read as an attack on appeals to "nature" in defenses of women's legal and social subordination. Reconstruct Mill's critique of such appeals. In doing so, address (a) the relation Mill sees between what is customary and what is called "natural"; (b) what Mill claims to establish about women's natural capacities; and (c) how that claim, or the absence of one, functions in his positive argument for legal and social equality.

Student answer:
Mill treats the legal subordination of women as an anomalous remnant of the rule of force in a civilization that otherwise expects institutions to justify themselves by their utility. His central objection to appeals to women's nature is that such appeals are circular in a specific way: the traits cited as natural are produced by the very customs of subjection that they are then used to defend. What gets called a woman's nature is, on Mill's view, to a large extent the artifact of education, expectation, and legal disability operating from infancy. The comparison to slavery matters because every system of domination has attributed to its victims a natural fitness for their subordinate station, while the observable character of the dominated has in fact been manufactured by the domination itself. From this Mill concludes that men and women possess no natural differences in the capacities relevant to legal and social standing. He therefore grounds his case for equal suffrage, equal admission to occupations, and married women's property rights on this empirical demonstration of natural equality: once the alleged natural differences are shown to be artifacts, the only remaining basis for women's exclusion is force or unreflective prejudice. On his view, the maxim of following nature cannot by itself do the argumentative work his opponents assign to it. This is what makes his argument radical — it rests on a strong factual claim about the absence of natural differences rather than on the weaker, merely procedural point that defenders of subjection have failed to prove their case.
