Here's the exam question: Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced, general agreement. In a rescue case where an agent can save either one person or a larger group, explain why this view appears to struggle to explain the duty to save the greater number, and critically assess whether Scanlon can handle the case while maintaining his individualist restriction.

The student's answer, verbatim:

"Scanlon's contractualism says an act is wrong if it is disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject. What matters for rejection is the force of an individual's complaint. The individualist restriction says the force of a complaint must be attributable to one person; you cannot sum the complaints of different people to make a larger total that outweighs someone else's complaint.

In a case where a rescuer can save either one person or five people, this looks like trouble. The one person has a complaint against being left to die, and each of the five has an equal complaint. If we can't aggregate, there seems to be no way for the five's complaints to outweigh the one's. So contractualism seems to imply that the choice is morally indifferent — that saving the one is just as permissible as saving the five.

Scanlon's solution is to say that, although the five's complaints do not outweigh the one's by aggregation, the one person's reason to reject a principle requiring the rescuer to save the five is not a reason that can justify rejecting that principle. Because if the one person were instead among the five, they would benefit from the same principle. So from a standpoint that takes everyone's interests into account, the one person cannot reasonably reject the save-the-greater-number principle. Thus Scanlon can say we should save the five while preserving the individualist restriction."
