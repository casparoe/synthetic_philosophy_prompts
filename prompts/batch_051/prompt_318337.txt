For the past few months I have been reporting a piece on philosophical debates about vagueness, and I have found myself moving away from supervaluationism and toward epistemicism: the view that a vague predicate such as "is a heap" or "is bald" has a perfectly sharp cutoff on any particular occasion of use—some exact number of grains or hairs at which the predicate flips from true to false—and that what we call vagueness is simply our unavoidable ignorance of where that cutoff lies. I want to know what, precisely, would count against this position. I am not asking for the standard list of objections; I want a map of the view's actual exposure. Which intuitions, possible cases, formal results, or findings about how ordinary speakers treat borderline cases would a committed epistemicist themselves concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as merely misdescribing the phenomenon? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether the view's ability to absorb apparent counterexamples is a sign of explanatory strength or of a position that has been insulated by qualifications.
