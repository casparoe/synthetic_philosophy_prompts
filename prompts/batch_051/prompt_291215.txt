I ran into this while making a little geometry toy for my kid that plots curves, and I found myself having to decide which inputs the program should treat as exact curves and which it should just approximate numerically. Here's the criteria I've been using for what counts as a legitimate mathematical object: (1) the curve is the zero set of some polynomial p(x, y) = 0, so it's given by a finite bit of algebra rather than a never-ending process; (2) its defining properties can be computed exactly in principle, no measuring involved; and (3) its points can be reached by a finite chain of allowed constructions — I let compass-and-straightedge-style moves count, and those always stay inside the algebraic world, since each step only solves a linear or quadratic equation.

The case that's eating at me is the cycloid. It's the curve traced by a point on the rim of a circle rolling along a straight line — which feels more constructed and mechanical than almost anything, you can literally roll a wheel. It has an exact definition and exact parametric equations, x = t − sin t and y = 1 − cos t (with radius 1). One arch has length exactly 8, the area under one arch is exactly 3π, and the same curve shows up as the brachistochrone. But I'm fairly sure no polynomial equation in x and y is satisfied by all its points: it's transcendental, the kind of curve the old classification kicked out of geometry. Does the cycloid qualify under the criteria above? Check each criterion honestly, and where it fails, tell me whether that's a problem with the criteria or with the cycloid. I half-suspect this one case shows criterion (1) is mostly a historical accident.
