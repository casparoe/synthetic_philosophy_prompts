I'm a retired physicist who recently caught the philosophy bug, and there's an asymmetry I can't shake. A man's wife is drowning next to a stranger; he jumps in and saves her without hesitating. We think that's not just fine but in some way exactly what a marriage should be — and if we found out he'd stood on the bank for a minute doing utilitarian arithmetic about wether he was permitted to favor his wife, most of us would find that repellent, like there was one thought too many. But change the case: an ER doctor is triaging two patients, one of them her husband. If she just grabs the ventilator for him without running the impartial triage protocol, we call it corruption or gross unprofessionalism, not love. So personal attachment short-circuiting impartial reasoning is admirable in one case and a firing offense in the other. Is there a principle that actually draws this line, and can it survive pressure? Please act as a professional philosopher who works on moral psychology: think carefully, brainstorm the strongest candidate principles first, then tell me whether the asymmetry is justified or should collapse in one direction.
