Since I retired from chemistry I've been reading decision theory, and I keep bumping into the Ellsberg paradox. The usual result is that people prefer to bet on a draw from a urn with a known fifty-fifty mix of red and black than on an urn where the proportion of red and black is entirely unknown, even when the payoff is the same. Fine. But an old colleague of mine, a physical chemist, keeps insisting this isn't really aversion to ambiguity. He says the person is just assigning the unknown urn some murky pessimistic probability — acting as if it were only thirty percent red — and then doing ordinary expected-utility reasoning. I'd like a thought experiment built to order that either answers him or forces him to give up that story.

Here are the specs. Keep it as close as possible to the urn format: one person, one sitting, no repeated draws, no fancy equipment, and stakes small enough that ordinary risk aversion around the known urn would show at most a tiny spread. The central requirement is that the case seperate ambiguity about the source of the odds from garden-variety pessimism. The way I imagine doing it is to use the same ambiguous urn and elicit both a buying price and a selling price for the same ticket, with a known fifty-fifty urn run alongside as a control, so a blanket endowment effect or loss-aversion story is ruled out. The designed result should be that the person's choices cannot be represented by any single subjective probability the pessimist could attribute to them, but come out naturally from ambiguity aversion itself. After you describe the scenario, walk through what the subjective expected-utility verdict is, what the ambiguity-aversion verdict is, and what my friend would say back. Then tell me which single feature of the scenario is doing the real work, what collapses if you remove it, and how a committed expected-utility defender would attack the case.

Also, since I'm new at this and don't trust a confident tone, tell me how confident you are in each of those verdicts and single out the point you're least sure of.
