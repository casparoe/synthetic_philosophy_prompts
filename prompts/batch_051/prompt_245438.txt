I am a PhD student working on the epistemology of communication between superintelligent AI systems and the humans they advise, and I would like you to write an essay that takes a defended stand on the following debate. Assume a system that is vastly more knowledgeable than any human and superhumanly good at predicting which arguments, phrasings, framings, and emotional registers will move a particular person to accept or reject a conclusion. Assume as well that the system is sincere and aims only at helping the user form true beliefs; it does not deceive, and it presents only claims it knows to be true.

The debate is this. One position holds that such a system, when asked to help a user form a belief, should commit itself to presenting the strongest considerations that bear directly on the truth of the matter, and should not use its model of the user's psychology to select among true considerations on the basis of their likely persuasive effect. On this view, tailoring a true message to make it more persuasive is still a form of manipulation: the influence runs through features of the user's mind rather than through features of the reasons, and the fact that the content is true is not enough to distinguish the system from a scrupulously factual propagandist.

The rival position holds that this is a superstitious restriction on a good instrument. If the system's task is to help the user arrive at the belief they would hold after ideal reflection, then a system that refuses to model the user is deliberately communicating less well than it could, leaving avoidable ignorance in place. On this view, what matters is the epistemic outcome, not the psychological route by which a true conclusion is reached, and a presentation calibrated to the user's actual mind is often the most respectful way to take that mind seriously.

An intermediate proposal is that the first position is too strict and the second too coarse, and that the proper constraint is a publicity condition: the system may tailor its presentation to the user exactly when it could, if asked, explain and justify its choice of presentation in terms the user would accept as advancing their own epistemic interests rather than as exploiting a known distortion. The proposal is meant to permit recalibrating an analogy to the user's background while forbidding the exploitation of known framing effects or emotional triggers that work only when their operation is hidden.

Write an essay that weighs these positions, with particular attention to the question of whether the publicity condition marks a stable line or instead collapses into one of the two extreme positions. Make the best version of each position, consider the strongest objections to the view you favor, and answer them. Do not end on a noncommittal note: defend a conclusion about what such systems should and should not be permitted to do.
