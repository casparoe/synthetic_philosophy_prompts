You are an expert on formal epistemology, especially on how objective chance constrains rational belief, and you are also an accomplished writer of formal verse. Please take your time and think through the design before composing: I want a poem that is technically disciplined and philosophically exact, not a piece of verse decorated with technical terms.

I am a high school student, and I am preparing a short reading to close a session of my school's philosophy club on the idea that our degrees of belief should defer to objective chance. I would like you to write a Petrarchan sonnet — fourteen lines divided into an octave and a sestet, the octave rhymed ABBAABBA and the sestet in an Italian pattern such as CDECDE — presenting the Principal Principle in the formulation associated with David Lewis, but without ever naming it inside the poem. The poem's own lines must also avoid the labels "Principal Principle", "credence", and "admissible evidence".

The octave should render the principle's central command: a rational agent's initial degree of belief in a proposition, conditional on the proposition that the objective chance of that proposition is x and on any admissible background evidence, should itself be x. The turn into the sestet should arrive with the thought that some evidence appears to override the chance — a reliable oracle, a time traveller's note, anything that bears on the outcome not merely through its bearing on the outcome's chance — and should show that such evidence is exactly what the principle rules out as inadmissible. The sestet should then resolve the apparent conflict: the admissibility restriction is not an afterthought bolted onto the principle, but the boundary that makes the deference rule coherent. A formal epistemologist should be able to recognise, from the structure of the poem, where the principle ends and its standard objection begins; a reader who has never studied philosophy should still feel the turn as a genuine pressure and release rather than as a lecture.

Before you compose the poem, please first set out, in ordinary prose, your understanding of the Principal Principle and its admissibility clause, and explain how you plan to map the principle and its objection onto the octave, the volta, and the sestet. Then present the poem. Please write your entire answer in British English.
