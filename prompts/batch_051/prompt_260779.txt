I keep coming back to Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" while I'm trying to teach Rawls's difference principle to a reading group that's mostly novelists, and I want a reading that uses the story to put pressure on Rawls rather than just illustrate him. There's a passage in A Theory of Justice, early on, where Rawls states the second principle in its first form: roughly, social and economic inequalities have to be arranged so that they are to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged, consistent with the just savings principle, and attached to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity. I'm not going to quote it from memory because I don't want the exact sentence to do all the work — I want you to work from the idea expressed there. In Omelas the whole city's happiness depends on one child locked in a cellar, and the story says the citizens all know this and most accept it. What I want to know is whether that arrangement would actually satisfy the difference principle if we tried to apply it, or whether Le Guin's case exposes something about how the principle defines the least advantaged group — who's in the relevant social union, whether a single individual counts as a group, whether reciprocity or mutual benefit matters. Also, does the story pressure Rawls's assumption that there is one society with a common conception of justice? Because the people who walk away seem to reject the deal even though they can't say what better arrangement would replace it, and I don't know whether Rawls has room for that response or just has to pathologize it. I'd like an honest reading: where Omelas is just Rawls's principle in a dark mirror, where it's a cheap counterexample, and where Le Guin is actually saying something about acceptance, visible suffering, and the need for a shared criterion of justification that Rawls's framework doesn't capture.
