I'm taking a seminar this term on interpretability in ML, and the professor keeps framing the whole field around one question: can AI interpret AI? He calls it the recursive interpretability problem. He's split the room into two camps. The "escalationists" say yes in principle — interpretability is just another modeling problem, so as target models get too complicated for humans, we'll train smaller or differently-built interpreter models to explain them, and then use a second layer of models to check those explanations, and so on; human cognitive limits aren't a hard ceiling. The "regress people" say this is a trick. If the explanation of a network is produced by another network whose internals you dont understand, you haven't avoided opacity, you've just moved it up one level. In the worst case the target and the interpreter can share blind spots and produce explanations that look right to each other without tracking what the target actually does — like two people agreeing on a misreading of a poem because they both made the same mistake.

Our final project is to write the adversarial-collaboration document this debate should produce: a numbered list of claims both camps can sign; then a disagreements table where each side's row is worded so they'd accept it as their own position; then a cruxes section. For each crux we have to give the sub-question, a concrete test or argument both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. I think a couple of cruxes are (a) whether a checker model can be more trustworthy than the model it checks when there's no ground-truth for the explanation, and (b) whether agreement between a model and its interpreter counts as evidence of faithfulness or just evidence of shared blind spots. Can you draft it? I'm less interested in who you think wins and more interested in the write-up being fair and non-vacuous, and in catching places where the two camps are actually just using words differently.
