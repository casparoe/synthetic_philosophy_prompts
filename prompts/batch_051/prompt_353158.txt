In chapter 25 of Machiavelli's The Prince, in W. K. Marriott's translation, he writes:

"But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

I keep thinking about this passage in light of something Machiavelli never encountered: the modern practice of managing uncertainty in business and government through probabilistic forecasts, risk models, and contingency planning built around the outcomes that look most likely or most costly. Earlier in the chapter he compares fortune to a flooding river and says that men can build defenses while the weather is fair; here he says the cautious man is ruined when boldness is needed and he cannot leave a way of acting that has long worked. Modern risk management sounds like the defenses, but it also sounds like an institutionalized form of caution, and one that organizations may find unusually hard to abandon. What would Machiavelli make of it? Is it preparation against fortune in his sense, or a fixed method that keeps people from the bold adaptability he calls virtù when the times turn against them? And is there any warning sign, before an unexpected disaster, that a person or institution is in the second condition rather than the first? Reason from what this passage commits him to, not from a summary of his reputation.
