Until recently I worked as a natural scientist, and I have been using retirement to work through Kant’s critical texts more carefully than I did as an undergraduate. I have been stuck for some time on his treatment of the first antinomy in the Critique of Pure Reason and its condensed restatement in the Prolegomena. In the section of the Prolegomena where he expounds the cosmological ideas, Kant presents the first conflict roughly as follows. The thesis holds that the world has a beginning in time and is finite in space; the antithesis holds that it has no beginning and no limits. Each side is then established indirectly, by showing that the contradictory claim would force us to think an empty time or an empty space, which is unintelligible under his account of experience. His resolution is that the whole dispute is dialectical: for the world as appearance, the regress of conditions is never given as a completed whole, while for a thing in itself the question cannot properly arise because space and time are not properties of things in themselves. The argument, in other words, depends on the transcendental ideality of space and time.

What I keep turning over is whether that resolution retains its force if one takes seriously the picture of spacetime that emerged from relativistic cosmology. In that picture, spacetime is not a presupposed form of all outer intuition but a dynamical object governed by field equations. The standard cosmological model gives the observed universe a finite age, a global expansion history, and a well-defined cosmic time. Empirically, then, the world of appearance seems to have something like an edge in the past, even though Kant’s whole argument was designed to show that no possible experience could settle such a question and that both sides rest on the same illusion.

I would like you to assess what happens to Kant’s first antinomy under this development. In particular: which premise of the thesis or the antithesis proof is actually put under pressure, and is there a repaired version of the antinomy that recasts it as a problem about the completability of a global manifold rather than about a beginning in an already-given time? Or does the cosmological result fail to touch the transcendental argument because that argument concerns the conditions of possible experience, not the empirical content of a particular physical theory? I am asking whether the argument survives, needs reconstruction, or is simply bypassed.
