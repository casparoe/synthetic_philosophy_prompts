I teach a grad seminar in philosophy of physics—twelve students, ninety minutes—and we're in the middle of the effective field theory unit, where the readings keep circling around the claim that a low-energy theory is 'autonomous': it can be predictive and self-contained even when nobody has the high-energy ('ultraviolet') theory, because most short-distance details decouple under coarse-graining. I want a classroom game that makes them feel that autonomy instead of hearing it from me.

Design a runnable game with actual rules, cheap materials (index cards, dice, a whiteboard—whatever works), and a chip or point payoff scheme, no real money. It should force students to make predictions that only come out right when they ignore most of the fine-grained information they're given, or coarse-grain it, while a small number of macro-level features (symmetries, dimension, maybe one or two relevant groupings) turn out to be the only things worth tracking. For instance, they might start with over-specified microstates on cards, then bet on macro-outcomes after several rounds of blocking or averaging; the scoring should make it so caring about irrelevant micro-parameters is a loosing strategy. Don't name the technical vocabulary—relevant/irrelevant, universality, decoupling, RG flow—until the debrief; the incentives and failure modes should carry the point.

Then give me a timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the physics: relevant, marginal, and irrelevant operators; renormalization-group flow toward a low-energy fixed point; universality classes; decoupling in the technical sense where heavy or short-distance degrees of freedom drop out of low-energy predictions; and the philosophical payoff about why an effective theory can be enough for its own scale rather than a placeholder for the unknown UV theory. By the end they should be able to articulate, in their own words, why deliberately throwing away micro-information can be epistemically respectable.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—someone memorizing the whole microstate and dominating, a team arguing the macro-outcome was underdetermined, the group concluding the game shows reductionism instead of autonomy—and how to handle each on the spot. Finish with an honest note on what the game cannot reproduce (I'm guessing the continuous RG or actual perturbative decoupling) and what I'll need to supply in discussion. American English, please.
