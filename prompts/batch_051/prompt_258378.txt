I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I would like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher working in normative ethics and the theory of rights. Please do not give a quick adjudication. Start by brainstorming, as explicitly as you can, the several points at which the reasoning below might fail; then work through what a rights theorist would have to pay to reject each of those points; and only then say where you would locate the fault.

The argument was offered to me by a legal scholar as a reductio of the idea that rights are side constraints, meaning deontological boundaries that may not be crossed merely because crossing them would produce a better overall pattern of outcomes.

1. A right against a certain way of being treated exists to protect people from being treated in that way.
2. If such a norm is justified at all, it must be justified by the protection it gives to right-holders.
3. So suppose an agent could violate one person's right in the course of preventing several other people from being treated in precisely the way the right forbids. If the norm is justified by the protection it gives to right-holders, then the norm's underlying point is better served by that violation than by respecting it.
4. A norm cannot be rationally retained if its underlying point is better served by someone's departing from it in a case where the norm would otherwise apply.
5. Therefore a right cannot categorically forbid such violations. It must direct its own suspension whenever doing so would minimize total instances of the treatment it bans.
6. But a norm that directs its own suspension whenever the aggregate calculation favors doing so is not a side constraint or a trump. It is a weighty interest inside a maximizing calculus.
7. So either rights collapse into instruments, to be overridden whenever enough comparable violations would be prevented, or they remain genuine constraints and thereby require agents to stand by while more instances of the very treatment the right exists to prevent occur.

I find both branches of the conclusion difficult to accept: the first seems to make rights disappear, and the second seems to make them perverse. I would most like to keep the claim that at least some rights are genuine side constraints. Where, if anywhere, does this argument overreach? Which step should a defender of rights reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost for the traditions that treat rights as trumps, as side constraints, or as more sophisticated instruments?
