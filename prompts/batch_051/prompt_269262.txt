I've become convinced that the program equilibrium literature pulls the rug out from under the standard one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. The usual dominance argument says defection is mandatory, but that assumes the other player's choice is just an opaque action. If you instead model players as choosing programs, and let each program read the other's source code, then cooperation is suddenly an equilibrium: you can submit a program that says, I cooperate iff you cooperate. That makes the old result look like an artifact of forbidding agents from conditioning on each other's decision procedure, which is a weird thing to forbid if we're trying to model rationality rather than just actions. So tell me: what would count against this? I want a map of the position's exposure, not just its strengths. Which formal assumptions are load-bearing — what happens if programs can't read each other exactly, or can misrepresent themselves, or have to be chosen under uncertainty about what the other program will do? Are there natural setups where it collapses back into defection as the unique equilibrium? And how much is this really a result about rationality, rather than just an artifact of a particular formalism? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed program-equilibrium person would themselves concede count against the view. I'd find it most persuasive if someone showed the cooperative equilibria are all relying on something no reasonable agent could actually do. And if the view survives all of it, tell me whether that's a feature or a sign it's been insulated into unfalsifiability.
