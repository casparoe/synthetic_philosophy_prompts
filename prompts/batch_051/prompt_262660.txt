As a novelist who has run an advanced fiction workshop for several years, I have repeatedly come up against a boundary in what a story can stipulate. My students will accept remarkable facts without demur: a city that floats, a woman who remembers the future, moths that farm. But certain sentences fail in a different way. The students do not exactly disbelieve them; they withdraw from the story, as though the narration has overstepped an authority it never possessed. I want to understand the principle behind that boundary, not as a question of literary fashion but as a question of what the imagination will and will not do.

Here are my verdicts on cases drawn from my own manuscripts and my students' work. (1) In an otherwise realistic story, a first-person narrator says of a father who locks his daughter in a cellar every night that he is "a kind and devoted parent." The students imagine the locked cellar readily; they refuse to imagine the kindness, and they report that the story stopped being a story and became an author's decree. (2) In a comic fable, the narrator calls a man who betrays his friend to the secret police "a wonderful model of loyalty." I resist this less than (1), because the fable's tone signals play, but I still cannot occupy the evaluative stance it asks of me. (3) In a story set in an invented country, the narrator calls the national dish, a gray fermented gruel, "delicious," and I accept this without strain; I simply file it as a fact about that country's palate. (4) A narrator calls a painting that the description shows to be mediocre "a masterpiece"; I resist, though less violently than in (1). (5) In a near-future story, the narrator reports that the society regards the routine beating of disobedient children as "the most beautiful sight a parent can witness"; I can accept this as a sociological report, and I can imagine the characters believing it, without imagining that the world of the story endorses it. (6) A narrator describes a marriage in which the husband is serially unfaithful and the wife is grateful for his attention, and calls it "a genuinely happy marriage." I do not go along with this, and I suspect it is because "happy" imports a standard the narration has not earned; but I cannot say why this differs from accepting that the gruel in (3) is delicious. (7) When the sentence from (1) is given to a character rather than the narrator — "He is a kind and devoted parent, she told herself" — my resistance nearly disappears; I can imagine her believing it, but the narrator must not adopt it.

I would like to know what principle, if any, captures this pattern, stated precisely enough that I can use it while drafting. Why does my imagination accept "delicious" in (3) but balk at "happy" in (6)? What does the principle predict about the case I am now writing: a narrator who says of a political assassination that it was "beautiful" — not merely that the characters found it beautiful, but that the world of the story endorses that judgment? If no single principle earns all seven verdicts, give me the simplest serious candidate and say plainly which of my verdicts I must abandon. I am less interested in the name of this phenomenon than in a sentence-level account of what I am doing when I let the gruel be delicious but refuse to let the marriage be happy.
