At my company’s monthly reading group, two engineers got into an argument after we read a short section from John Locke’s Second Treatise of Government, chapter V. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I was the one who suggested the reading, so they asked me to settle it, and I honestly could not. The passage argues, roughly, that every person has a property in his own person and in the labor of his own body; that when a person mixes that labor with something unowned from the state of nature, he removes it from the common stock and joins to it something that is his own, thereby making it his property; and that this is legitimate at least where enough and as good is left in common for others.

Mateo said Locke’s condition is satisfied in the case of open-source software. If someone takes a permissively licensed library, modifies it heavily, and publishes the modified version, nobody is worse off than if the modified version had never existed: the original remains available, and the new version is an addition. The labor put into the changes gives the developer property rights in the modified work, just as Locke says, because the digital commons is not depleted.

Rana said that is too quick. The proviso is not only about whether the common pool is smaller; it is about whether someone’s act worsens the position of others relative to how they were before. If the modified version includes parts of the original library and is then released under a restrictive license, that is worse than leaving the original to be used under its open license. Also, the developer mixed labor with a common digital artifact, not with raw nature. He did not make it his property from an unowned state; he made a derivative of others’ work.

Mateo replied that Rana is running together acquisition and the later exercise of a right. The initial act of mixing labor with a copy from an unlimited digital commons satisfies Locke’s proviso because copying does not deprive. The subsequent choice to license restrictively is a separate question about what a property owner may do. If that choice is harmful, that does not show no property right was acquired.

Rana answered that if the later licensing choice is doing the damage, then the property right itself is doing the damage, which suggests the initial account is too permissive. Locke is talking about a world where appropriation changes who can use a thing. In a non-rival digital commons, labor may not ground exclusive rights the way it does for apples or land. The proviso is not a stand-alone test; it is embedded in a story about scarce natural resources, and applying it to open source without first establishing that digital code is the kind of thing that can be removed from a common state is skipping the hard part.
