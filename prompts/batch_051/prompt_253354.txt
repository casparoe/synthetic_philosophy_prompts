An acquaintance of mine defends a strong version of the common worry that war games glorify what they depict. On their view, a game that makes the player commit acts of war cannot treat war with the moral seriousness it demands, and that failure of seriousness is itself a way of glorifying war, whatever the game's scripted story says. Their argument, which they regard as a proof, runs as follows.

(1) To treat represented lethal violence as an object of moral seriousness, the audience must be able to attend to it under moral categories—as wrongful, permissible, tragic, deserving of grief or horror—rather than solely as moves within some practical project.
(2) In a game, the acts the player performs are presented as moves within a practical project: the player's reasons for aiming, shooting, or calling in an airstrike are success-reasons, and the game evaluates them by whether they advance its objectives. The distinctive enjoyment of the game is not something added onto this practical standpoint; it just is the uptake of that standpoint.
(3) A person cannot at one and the same time hold a practical standpoint toward an action, assessing it as a means to success, and a moral standpoint toward that same action, assessing it as wrong, tragic, or permissible.
(4) So, while a player is playing, they cannot attend to the game's lethal violence under moral categories; the practical standpoint excludes the moral one.
(5) If a work's central transaction with its audience is one in which the audience cannot attend to represented suffering under moral categories, then the work itself fails to treat that suffering with moral seriousness.
(6) If a work fails to treat war's lethal violence with moral seriousness, then it glorifies that violence in the relevant sense, even if it explicitly condemns war: making the violence a playable project, without the moral standpoint, is itself the glorification.
(7) Therefore, any game that makes the player commit acts of war as its core activity glorifies war.

The problem is that this argument seems to prove far too much. By the same reasoning, a serious war novel would also fail. A reader of such a novel is asked to follow soldiers' practical projects from the inside—their plans, calculations, and killing—attending to the violence as advancing or obstructing those projects. So the reader too is placed in a practical standpoint; by (3), (5), and (6), the novel fails to treat war's violence with moral seriousness and therefore glorifies it. That would make serious war fiction impossible, and my acquaintance does not accept that conclusion.

I would most like to preserve two claims. First, at least some war fiction is morally serious and does not glorify what it depicts. Second, the interactive element of games is not morally inert: there is a real ethical difference between a player's performing a violent act in a game and a reader's imaginatively following a character's violent act. I am less attached to the claim that the practical and moral standpoints simply cannot coexist, and to the claim that every failure of seriousness amounts to glorification.

Tell me, as a definite diagnosis rather than a survey: which step or steps in the argument should be rejected, why the most tempting step looked secure, and what each of the main ways of repairing the argument has to give up.
