My friend Ana, an epistemologist who works as a data scientist, has become our discussion group's unofficial arbiter for questions about when discovering that other people disagree with you should make you revise a belief. She has little patience for general slogans and rules case by case, so I have reconstructed her position from a set of rulings she has given over the past year.

When two people independently add up the same itemized restaurant receipt and arrive at different totals, she says each should all but abandon their answer and recalculate together. When two radiologists with comparable experience read the same chest scan and disagree about whether a nodule is present, she says neither is rationally required to lower their confidence; the disagreement shows the image is underdetermined and warrants a further test rather than an averaging of the two readings. When two equally competent philosophers reach opposite conclusions about whether a flawless digital simulation of a brain would be conscious, she says neither has much reason to move, since philosophy offers no shared track record that would tell us whose change of mind would be an improvement. When you clearly remember locking the front door and your companion clearly remembers that you could not find your keys and left without locking it, she says you should lower your confidence noticeably but not catastrophically, because each of you has access only to your own memory and ordinary memory error is common enough. When four hiking companions independently recall that a trail forked left while you remember it forking right, she says you should move most of the way toward the four but not simply adopt their answer, since their independence is at best an assumption and the shared terrain may have induced a shared error. And when a professional forecaster with a documented record of calibrated political predictions disagrees with your casual reading of a poll, she says you should defer heavily to the forecaster even if the forecaster has seen no evidence you lack.

Last week she was given two cases she could not settle on the spot. In the first, a radiologist with an outstanding record of biopsy-confirmed diagnoses disagrees with a radiologist of merely ordinary record about the same scan. In the second, five people who have already discussed a problem among themselves unanimously give the opposite answer to an arithmetic question you have worked out alone. I do not especially need the verdicts themselves; I want a reconstruction of the principle she is implicitly running, a list of other principles that fit her recorded rulings just as well, a prediction from each candidate principle about the two unresolved cases, and an account of what further test case would most cleanly separate the surviving candidates.
