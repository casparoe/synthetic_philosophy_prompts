I keep running into arguments that feel like their just about words, but when I poke at them I can't say exactly what "just about words" amounts to. Everyone I know uses "you two are just arguing over words" pretty confidently, but if you push them on what that rules out, they fall apart. I want a working definition of a merely verbal dispute — the kind of disagreement where the two people don't really differ about the world, only about how to describe it. I'd like the definition to get the following cases right.

(1) Tom and Ana are arguing about whether a tomato is a fruit. They both know the tomato is a fruit in the botanical sense (develops from the flower's ovary, contains seeds), and they both know cooks treat it as a vegetable. Tom says "a tomato is a fruit," and Ana says "no, a tomato is a vegetable." I'm pretty sure this one is verbal.

(2) Two structural engineers are arguing about whether a particular bridge would survive a certain earthquake. They agree on what "survive" means; they just disagree about the soil composition under the piers. I'm pretty sure this one is not verbal.

(3) Maya and Dev agree on every biological fact about a 12-week fetus — cell types, brain activity, viability, all of it. Maya says "it's a person," and Dev says "it's not a person." Each knows the other isn't confused about any empirical detail, and both take "person" to carry moral weight. I flip-flop on this one.

(4) Lee and Sam agree that the universe is deterministic, agree on everything a particular agent did, and agree that the agent wasn't coerced or manipulated. Lee calls the act free; Sam says it wasn't free. Lee thinks Sam is using "free" in a loaded way, and Sam thinks Lee is watering the word down. I keep going back and forth on whether this is verbal.

(5) Two biologists agree on every empirical fact about a virus — its structure, how it replicates, what it does with energy, all of it. One says viruses are alive; the other says they aren't. My gut says this is verbal, but I can't defend that gut feeling.

(6) Rina and Paul are looking at the same computer system and agree on its complete architecture and its behavior on every input. Rina says "it genuinely thinks"; Paul says "it doesn't think, it just processes information." Both suspect the word is doing the work, but neither will grant that his or her claim reduces to a stipulation. Is this verbal or not?

(7) Two demographers share the same definition of "city": an incorporated place with at least 50,000 residents. The census puts Springfield at 49,800 people. One demographer says "Springfield is a city," and the other says "no, it isn't," because the first thinks the census undercounted the city by about 500 people. This one looks non-verbal to me, even though it's a dispute about whether a label applies.

(8) A composer and a music teacher agree on every acoustic property of a recording — its frequencies, duration, silences, everything. The recording is four and a half minutes of silence. The composer says "it's music"; the teacher says "it's not music." I'd be tempted to call it verbal, except that the composer keeps saying the teacher is getting the definition wrong, not merely choosing a different one.

Propose a definition of a merely verbal dispute that sorts these eight cases correctly. Run the definition through each case, say explicitly where it gives the wrong answer or no answer at all, and either revise it or bite the bullet on the surprising verdicts. Then tell me which of these verdicts you're least secure about.

And please write the answer in American English.
