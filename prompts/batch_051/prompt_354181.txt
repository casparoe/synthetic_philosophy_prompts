After retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been reading philosophy of social science, and I keep reconstructing an argument that my scientific instincts want to accept and my philosophical reading warns me is too quick. I cannot locate the problem, and I would like a definite diagnosis rather than a survey of things I might mean.

(1) Every social entity—a central bank, a market, a legal system, a language—is composed entirely of individual human beings, the physical objects they make and use, and the relations among them.

(2) So once all the facts about those individuals, objects, and relations are fixed, every social fact is fixed as well.

(3) An explanation of a fact can be given by whatever fixes it: if the target fact could not have been otherwise given some set of facts, then that set is enough to explain it.

(4) Therefore every social fact can be explained by facts about individuals, objects, and their relations.

(5) An explanation that cites only individuals, objects, and their relations is an individualistic explanation.

(6) Hence every social fact has an individualistic explanation.

(7) So appeals to irreducibly social factors—structures, classes, institutions, norms—are never needed in a full explanation.

This would commit me to methodological individualism and against holism. But the word "relations" feels as though it is doing hidden work, and something seems suspicious around (3) or (5). I need a committed verdict. Does this argument establish its conclusion? If it does, defend it against the following objection: relations like being legally married, or acting under an authority, cannot be specified without first invoking institutional facts, so citing such relations is not yet an individualistic explanation. If it does not, say exactly which step goes wrong, give the strongest reading on which the preceding steps are true, and state what conclusion, if any, a corrected version of the argument would actually support.
