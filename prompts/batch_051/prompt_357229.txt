For a course on the foundations of decision theory, I need to write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated characters who disagree about how to evaluate repeated risky choices when wealth grows multiplicatively. One character defends the standard view that a gamble should be evaluated by its ensemble average—the expectation over all possible outcomes, weighted by probability—and that a rational agent with a concave utility function already captures any relevant attitude toward risk. The other character, drawing on the ergodicity line of argument, replies that this confuses averaging across parallel possible worlds at a single time with averaging along one realized history over time. For multiplicative, repeated gambles these two averages can come apart dramatically, and what a single agent should care about is the time-average growth rate of wealth, with the possibility of ruin as a limiting case. I want the dialogue to be a genuine back-and-forth: each character must respond to the other's actual points, and neither should be a strawman. The ergodicity character should not simply assert that logarithmic utility is the correct utility function, and the ensemble character should not simply dismiss the time-average perspective as a psychological quirk. Push them to argue about whether the time-average criterion is a rival to expected-utility theory or merely a special case of it with a particular utility function; whether decision theory is meant to govern one-shot choices, repeated choices, or both; what it means in practice for a gamble to be repeated within a single life; and whether the ensemble framework can absorb the ergodicity critique by adding constraints such as avoiding ruin, or whether something structural still escapes it. End the dialogue with each character having been moved at least a little on a subordinate point, even if neither concedes the main issue.
