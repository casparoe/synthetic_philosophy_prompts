Over the years, a friend of mine has developed what she calls a private moral-weight scale: a single number from 0 to 100 for how much an entity's interests should count in decision-making. She distrusts sweeping criteria and works case by case, but I have reconstructed her track record well enough to be confident of it. She gives an ordinary adult human 100; a dog 65; an octopus 55, because its obvious intelligence impresses her while its decentralized nervous system makes her uneasy; a salmon 25; a honeybee 3, which she insists is a hedged uncertainty rather than a principled zero; an oyster 0; a digital thermostat 0; and, notably, 0 to a mobile robot whose reinforcement-learning controller makes it recoil from collisions, emit a distress-like tone, and thereafter avoid the place where it was damaged. Pressed on the robot, she said all that outward performance meant nothing without a genuine point of view behind it.

She has now been consulted about a new case: a research group is seeking ethics approval to work with a cortical organoid—roughly forty million living human-derived neurons grown as a three-dimensional network, showing spontaneous coordinated bursts and slow oscillations, but with no sensory organs, no motor output, and no connection to any body or environment. The group wants to know what moral weight the organoid's interests should receive when deciding which procedures are permissible. I am less interested in the bare number than in the reasoning. Identify the principle or principles best supported by her track record—plausibly an experiential-welfare rule, a biological neural-complexity rule, or something else—state what each candidate rule predicts for the organoid, and say which verdict you think she would actually give and why. Finally, propose one further case, real or hypothetical, that would most cleanly discriminate among the rules that survive, and explain what each possible verdict on it would reveal.
