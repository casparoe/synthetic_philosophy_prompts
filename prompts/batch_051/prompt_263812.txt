I am a novelist, and for some years I have been working on a book about a woman who, after a medical procedure, remains in good health for centuries while her memory and character continue to alter. The obstacle I keep meeting is that I cannot convince myself that genuinely new experiences would remain available to her after the first few thousand years; each further city, love affair, and bereavement begins to read as a rearrangement of earlier ones. In trying to make this difficulty precise, I have produced a small theory, and I would like it assessed candidly rather than charitably.

I call it the novelty ceiling. A finite mind, I propose, has a finite stock of discriminative capacities -- sensory channels, affects, and conceptual contrasts, each with limited resolution -- and every experience available to that mind is a configuration of elements drawn from this stock. I then distinguish two grades of novelty. Weak novelty is a configuration that has never occurred before. Strong novelty is an experience that introduces a previously uninstantiated element or relation into the repertoire itself. The theory holds that each finite mind has an upper bound on strongly novel experiences, and that beyond this bound every apparent novelty must be fabricated from old materials, able only to masquerade as a new first. On this view the immortal woman is not in a special predicament; she has simply lived long enough to notice that the supply of strong novelty is finite for everyone -- far larger than any natural lifespan, but finite all the same.

I half-suspect this is either an old point dressed badly or a private metaphor, and I want to know which. Please give me the strongest case against the theory, point by point, distinguishing objections that only embarrass it from those that actually kill it. In particular, I want pressure placed on whether the notion of an element or relation in a mind's repertoire can bear the weight I put on it; whether recombination can generate novelty in the strong sense; and whether the theory, taken seriously, would commit me to the conclusion that a finite mind could never experience a genuinely new taste, grief, or idea -- a conclusion I resist. If some version of the theory survives, tell me what it is really a theory of. Please write your answer in British English.
