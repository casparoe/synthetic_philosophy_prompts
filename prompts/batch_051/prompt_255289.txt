In History we've been doing Braudel and the longue durée — the idea that the things that really shape history are slow ones like geography, climate, trade routes and population, and that events like battles or what individual kings do are just surface ripples on much deeper currents. Seperately, in Physics we've been doing thermodynamics, and there's this trick where you split the variables into slow ones (temperature, pressure) and fast ones (the random jiggling of individual molecules), and then you average out the fast stuff because with millions of molecules the fluctuations cancel and only the slow variables matter. It feels like Braudel is making the same move: treat short-term events as noise, average them away, and you're left with the slow structures as the actual signal.

But in physics the averaging is justified because there are huge numbers of molecules and they're more or less independent, so the fluctuations really do wash out. History doesn't obviously work like that — a single plague, revolution or invention can change the slow structures themselves, so the 'noise' can definately feed back into the 'signal'. My mate says I'm overcomplicating it and Braudel was just being dramatic, but I'm not convinced. Is the parallel between Braudel's longue durée and separating slow and fast variables in physics a deep structural similarity, or am I just pattern-matching on the word 'slow'? If it is deep, what would it predict about how much individual events can matter, and does that prediction break down for cases like the Black Death or the Industrial Revolution?
