I cover the bioethics-and-ideas beat for a science magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how hospitals actually draw the treatment/enhancement line when the request is sitting on a desk rather than in a philosophy journal. For the past few months I've been sitting in on the monthly meetings of the pediatric endocrinology ethics comittee at our local academic medical center. They only see the contested requests — the routine stuff never reaches them.

Their recent record, as best I can reconstruct it from my notes:

1. Approved growth hormone for a 9-year-old with a confirmed growth-hormone deficiency.
2. Rejected growth hormone for a 12-year-old at about the 10th percentile for predicted adult height, no deficiency, no distress; his parents think a few extra inches would help his volleyball prospects.
3. Approved a stimulant for a university student with a documented ADHD diagnosis whose academic functioning was falling apart.
4. Rejected the same stimulant for a student with no diagnosis and no distress, who just wanted an edge for the LSAT.
5. Approved breast reconstruction after a mastectomy.
6. Rejected cosmetic breast augmentation for a woman who described herself as happy with her body but thought implants would advance her modeling career.

Next week they take up a new case: a 16-year-old boy, predicted adult height in the bottom one or two percentiles, with a normal endocrine workup — no deficiency, no underlying disease. He's currently being treated for depression that his doctor ties to relentless bullying about his height, and he's stopped going to school. He and his parents are asking for growth hormone. He says he doesn't want to be tall, he wants the bullying to stop.

I can see three rules that fit the six decisions so far: a strict disease-or-deficiency rule; a distress-or-impairment rule where a diagnosis is optional; and a statistical-normality rule that treats what falls outside the normal range whether or not it's a named disease. I honestly can't tell which one is doing the work.

Act as a professional bioethicist for this one — the kind of person who teaches medical ethics by day and consults for hospital ethics boards by night. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming candidate principles beyond the three I named too, then check each against all six decisions. Then walk me through:

- What each major candidate rule says about the new case, and which of the six past decisions is the nearest analog for each rule.
- What the panel should decide if it wants to stay consistent with its own record, plus your honest best guess about how they'll actually vote.
- Which principle the panel is adopting with each possible decision, if the record underdetermines things.
- The single best further case I should quote in the piece to smoke out the difference between the two most plausible rules.

And dont just leave me with prose at the end. After the walk-through, give me a JSON object (that's what I'll paste into my notes) with this schema:

- "candidate_rules": an array of objects, each with "rule" (one-sentence statement of the principle), "new_case_prediction" ("approve" or "reject"), "closest_precedent" (the number 1-6 of the most analogous past decision), and "strength" (a short phrase saying how well it fits the six past decisions).
- "recommendation": an object with "decision" ("approve" or "reject"), "confidence" ("low", "medium", or "high"), and "principle_adopted" (what the committee would be committing itself to by going that way).
- "settling_case": an object with "case" (one or two sentences describing the further case) and "how_it_discriminates" (which two rules it separates and what each predicts).
