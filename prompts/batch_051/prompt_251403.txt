I have been working through expected utility theory, and I have come to defend what I take to be the standard normative reading of the independence axiom: that it is a genuine requirement on rational preference, not a convenient modeling assumption. As I understand the axiom, if two gambles coincide on some branch — if they assign the same outcome to the same event with the same probability — then one's preference between them should be controlled entirely by the branch on which they differ, so that attaching or stripping away a common consequence cannot reverse a ranking. The Allais results are usually presented as the main descriptive embarrassment for that claim. In one standard pair, most people prefer a certain $1 million to a gamble that gives an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing; in a related pair, most people prefer a gamble giving a 10% chance of $5 million and a 90% chance of nothing to one giving an 11% chance of $1 million and an 89% chance of nothing. The two choices are supposed to be settled by the same residual 11% comparison once the common 89% outcome is ignored, so the usual conclusion is that people violate the axiom but would obey it after reflection, and that the axiom survives as a norm. That is the position I want stress-tested.

I am not asking for another exposition of the paradox. I want as careful a map as you can produce of what would count against the claim that the independence axiom is a requirement of rational preference, as opposed to what counts against the descriptive claim that people conform to it. Which observations, experiments, cases, arguments, or results elsewhere in the theory would a committed defender of the axiom have to count as genuine evidence against their view, and which of those would be decisive rather than merely awkward? I would also like the defeaters sorted by how seriously the defender themselves should take them, rather than merely how critics grade them. I know the axiom is entangled with the sure-thing principle and with dynamic consistency, and I want to know whether those connections make the view stronger or more exposed.

Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the normative view has been insulated from refutation — by the normative-descriptive distinction, by saying that people would revise their choices once the common branches are made explicit, by treating violations as framing effects or regret-aversion, or by restricting the axiom to preferences already identified as rational — and whether such insulation, if present, is a defect in a view that presents itself as a substantive constraint on what it is rational to prefer. Please write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of an email or a short essay, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
