You are a moral philosopher specializing in responsibility, blame, and moral luck, and you are used to making explicit what a theory has to give up in order to hold onto something else. Two drivers text for the same ten seconds on the same road under the same conditions; one kills a pedestrian, the other passes through harmlessly. Ordinary practice blames and punishes the first far more, even though the only difference between them is luck. Here are five candidate responses to this problem of resultant moral luck, that is, responsibility differing because of how things happen to turn out.

1. Quality-of-will purism: Blame and desert track only what was within the agent's control, paradigmatically the quality of her will. Because the two drivers exercised identical carelessness, they deserve identical blame; the harsher treatment of the unlucky driver is at best an epistemic heuristic or a social convention serving deterrence and victim acknowledgment, and it does not track a real difference in desert.

2. Act/agent split: The killing and the near miss are different acts, and the killer is accountable for the graver act, including stronger obligations of apology and repair. Personal blameworthiness and punitive desert, however, should track only the care exercised, so ordinary practice is only partly vindicated: its outcome-sensitive language is licensed for acts, but its outcome-sensitive personal blame is a mistake.

3. Outcome-inclusive desert: What an agent has done is partly constituted by what resulted, and desert attaches to the deed, not only to the will. The control principle should therefore be weakened or restricted to judgments of character, while full moral and legal accountability may legitimately vary with the outcome.

4. Generalized skepticism: Once resultant luck is admitted to undermine desert, constitutive and circumstantial luck undermine it even more deeply, because neither an agent's constitution nor her circumstances were ultimately within her control. Desert-based blame collapses, and we should retain only forward-looking practices of regulation, rehabilitation, and deterrence.

5. Responsibility-practice pluralism: The control principle is not a single moral theorem but one norm among several governing a social practice that also serves victim acknowledgment, deterrence, moral communication, and reconciliation. Outcome-sensitive and control-sensitive judgments are both local, defeasible compromises, so ordinary practice is neither metaphysically correct nor systematically mistaken.

Decide which of these responses best jointly preserves the two things ordinary moral thought wants to keep: a substantive control condition on moral blame and punishment, and the strong sense that outcomes are normally relevant to how much someone is blamed and punished. Then rank all five from best to worst on that combined score.

Before you answer, work through the trade-offs silently for each candidate, paying attention to which of the two criteria it is forced to reinterpret or sacrifice. Then write exactly five numbered items, from best to worst, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should consist of the candidate's name followed by one or two sentences explaining its placement and what it sacrifices. Use American English throughout.
