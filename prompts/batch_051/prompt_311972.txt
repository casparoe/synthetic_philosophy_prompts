When I'm deciding which long arguments are actually worth reading to the end — blog comments, op-eds, essays people send to the discussion group I help run — I use a form of triage I'd be embarrassed to describe to my more linguistically relaxed friends. If a piece contains 'should of,' 'less people,' uses 'literally' as an intensifier, or opens with an 'irregardless,' I knock a large chunk off my estimate of how careful the author is, and if the thing is full of them I usually stop reading. I don't go around correcting anyone's speech; this is a private filter, and it genuinely saves me time.

A friend in linguistics says this is just prescriptivism masquerading as epistemology. These forms are rule-governed and normal in plenty of speakers' grammars, the argument goes, and the standard written dialect is not a proxy for reasoning quality, so I'm treating a shibboleth as evidence about the writer's mind. I'm happy to grant that 'less people' isn't wrong in some law-of-thought sense. But I'm not checking anyone's CV; I need cheap signals about whether a person bothered to learn the register they're writing in, and not having internalized it — or refusing to — sure looks like information about care or seriousness about being understood. My question isn't whether the forms are 'really' wrong. It's whether my private discount is defensible, and if so at what rate. Is there a principled way to seperate bad grammar that actually muddies an argument from bad grammar that's just a gatekeeping marker, and could a non-linguist tell the difference in practice?
