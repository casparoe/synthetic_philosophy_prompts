I'm in a class on suffering-focused ethics and we just read Ursula Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas alongside some negative utilitarianism. I keep flip-flopping on whether the story is actually a negative utilitarian fable or something stranger. The easy read is that one child's constant suffering can't be outweighed by the whole city's happiness, and maybe the happiness is even shown to be hollow because it rests on that child. But the people who walk away never try to free the child or stop the arrangement, and the narrator works pretty hard to make Omelas sound genuineley worth having otherwise. So read the story against negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused views: where does it support treating suffering as lexically prior to happiness, where does it complicate that, and is walking away an actual ethical response or just a way of keeping your hands clean? Also, what would a careful suffering-focused account say the story gets right that the theory misses, or gets wrong about the theory?
