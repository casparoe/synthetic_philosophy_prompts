I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in value theory with fourteen students, and I have one 80-minute session to devote to this. Design a classroom activity that makes students feel the force of the claim that hard choices are not always settled by a common scale of value — that some options stand in no relation of better-than, worse-than, or equally-good, and not merely because the students lack information or time.

The activity should be built around the small-improvements phenomenon. The plan I have in mind, which you should improve wherever you see fit, is this. Students work in pairs and are given two options, A and B, each described on a card in rich verbal terms across several dimensions of value — say, long-term career ambition, caregiving relationships, aesthetic development, civic participation, and leisure. The descriptions should be calibrated so that neither option dominates: each is clearly better on some dimensions and worse on others. Avoid numeric scores, since I want no one quietly converting the choice into a weighted average. Each pair first compares A and B and records a comparative judgment. Then the pair receives A+, an improved version of A: improved along a dimension everyone in the room will grant is a genuine improvement, but not so drastically that A+ now obviously beats B. They judge A+ against A, and then A+ against B. The discovery I am aiming for is that many pairs will say A+ is better than A, yet A+ is still not better than B — and that, combined with the original judgment that A and B were neither better nor worse than each other but also not exactly equal, leaves the familiar trichotomy unable to represent what they found.

Include a concrete device that prevents students from quietly rewriting their earlier judgments once the tension appears — for example, a claim ledger in which each pair writes a one-line comparative statement after every comparison before being shown the next card. The choice should matter to them at least a little, so that they do not treat it as an idle survey; perhaps the option a pair finally selects determines which follow-up exercise that pair will complete. Then specify: the card contents, or a recipe for generating them; rules for deliberation within each pair; any roles, such as a challenger whose job is to press for a definite answer; a timeline for the 80 minutes; the debrief; what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it; and an honest account of which part of the philosophical picture the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply afterward. The debrief should map what happened onto the candidate formal responses — parity (comparable, but neither better, worse, nor equally good), incompleteness of the better-than relation, giving up transitivity, or rejecting one of the recorded judgments as a mistake — and should make students feel the cost of each escape route. Do not open with a lecture, and keep the terms "incommensurability," "parity," and "small improvements" out of the room until the debrief.
