I am writing a novel set in a fictional parliamentary republic, and I have been drafting its constitutional machinery with the care one would give to a character's psychology. The state has a written and entrenched bill of rights, a single constitutional court whose judges serve fifteen-year non-renewable terms, and a standing anxiety that a handful of unelected jurists should not have the last word over an elected legislature. The compromise I am considering works as follows. The court may strike down a statute only after consulting a constitutional monitor, a non-partisan senior official appointed much like an auditor general, with fixed non-renewable tenure and a budget shielded from ministerial control. If the monitor certifies that the statute's defect could be cured by a limited textual amendment, the court must publish a redlined draft of the smallest changes that would, in its view, bring the statute into conformity with the constitution. The legislature then has ninety sitting days to enact that redline by simple majority; if it does, the amended statute is conclusively valid and shielded from further annulment on the same grounds for ten years. If the monitor instead certifies that no limited amendment can cure the defect, because the policy itself is constitutionally forbidden rather than merely badly drafted, the court's annulment is absolute.

I do not want an abstract verdict on whether this arrangement is good. I want to know how it would actually be played. Where does the strategic pressure accumulate: in the court's drafting of redlines, in the monitor's certification power, in the majority's decision whether to enact the redline or let the statute fall and blame the court? What would a bare-majority government, an opposition party, and a well-funded interest group each do with this machinery over two or three electoral cycles? Which failure modes seem most likely — courts writing maximal "minimal" amendments, monitors being captured or lobbied, legislatures using the ten-year shield to entrench near-unconstitutional policies — and can any be repaired by adjusting the monitor's powers, the shielding period, or the voting threshold without collapsing the system back into either ordinary strong-form judicial review or pure legislative supremacy?
