I am a PhD student in moral and political philosophy, and I work two days a week as an analyst in the regulatory impact assessment unit of my country's environment ministry. We assess long-horizon proposals—emissions targets, energy and transport infrastructure, land-use plans, pension reforms—and my colleagues' drafts routinely say that an option would impose burdens on future generations, or that future citizens would be worse off under the alternative. We handle roughly a dozen long-horizon files a quarter, each analyst has about a working day per file, the head of unit signs the final assessment, and a wrong call either strips the grounds from a defensible long-term policy or lets a damaging one pass as unobjectionable. I have read enough of the nonidentity literature to think many of these future-regarding sentences are unsupported. Most of our proposals are identity-affecting: by changing where people live, how they meet, and when children are conceived, the policy changes which particular people come to exist later. On a person-affecting view, if those later people have lives worth living, the policy does not make the particular people who come to exist worse off, since the alternative for them is not a better version of their lives but nonexistence. Yet I cannot tell my colleagues that future-regarding criticism is empty, because some proposals we see—for example, a disposal scheme that produces high consumption now and a mildly degraded environment for a future population that would not otherwise exist—seem indefensible even though no particular person is worse off.

Write me a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off judgment, that an analyst with no formal philosophical training could run in a single working day when drafting the effects-on-future-generations section of an impact assessment. The procedure should determine, for a given proposal, which future-regarding statements the assessment may legitimately make. It should test whether the proposal is identity-affecting or same-people; keep separate the claims that a policy makes someone worse off and that it wrongs someone; and give the analyst a defensible residual basis for objecting to identity-affecting policies that look unacceptable without pretending a person-affecting harm has been shown. Mark each step as mechanical or as a judgment call, and identify which steps require sign-off from senior staff. Then work three cases: a costly emissions-reduction program whose benefits accrue mostly to people who would not exist under the baseline; a deferral of flood-defence maintenance whose exposed households are already alive; and one case, your choice, in which the procedure requires deleting the ministry's preferred future-generation objection. Finally, say what the procedure cannot settle—including where the case turns on a threshold of how bad a life can be while still being worth living—and what a senior official should do when the procedure runs out.
