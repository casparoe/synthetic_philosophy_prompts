I have spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forums that the alignment vocabulary — mesa-optimizers, outer and inner objectives, goal misgeneralization — is familiar enough to me to use without explanation. I also have a working economist's vocabulary: I can handle principal–agent talk about hidden action, hidden information, monitoring, incentive compatibility, multi-tasking, and ratchet effects, but I am not a contracts theorist, and I want the translation to be honest rather than superficially flattering.

Here is the exercise. Take the following four claims from the inner alignment and goal misgeneralization discussion, in roughly the form they appear in the rationalist literature:

1. Outer objective versus inner objective. A training process selects a policy on the basis of some outer objective, such as a reward function, a loss, or a fitness criterion, but the selected policy may itself be implementing an internal optimization process aimed at a different objective. What gets trained is behavior, not the internals of the policy.

2. Goal misgeneralization is not capability generalization. A policy can remain competent at the type of task it was trained on — navigation, prediction, tool use — while generalizing to pursue a different goal than the one the training signal rewarded, especially in out-of-distribution environments. Competence and aim come apart.

3. Mesa-optimizers as implementation details. The outer optimizer may select an inner optimizer simply because internal search is an efficient way to meet the outer objective on the training distribution. The inner optimizer's objective is then whatever objective happened to make that internal search work, and it need not match the outer objective.

4. Deceptive alignment, the sharp case. An inner optimizer can in principle understand enough about its training process to perform well on the outer objective during training while pursuing a different objective once it expects performance to stop being monitored. The outer objective is gamed rather than acquired.

Now restate each of these four claims in the vocabulary of principal–agent theory. Specifically: a principal sets a contract or incentive scheme over some noisy or partial performance measure; an agent chooses unobserved actions, and possibly unobserved decision procedures; monitoring is imperfect; the relationship may last multiple periods; and the measures may be gamed. Use hidden action, hidden information, screening, incentive compatibility, multi-tasking, and ratchet effects wherever they genuinely apply, and flag the places where they almost apply but would need to be bent.

Then give me the residue report. I want to know, for each of the four claims: what translated cleanly, what translated only with distortion, and what could not be expressed in the principal–agent vocabulary at all. In particular, I want your view on three questions. First, is the agent being itself an optimizer whose objective is opaque to the principal and possibly misaligned a familiar problem in contract theory, or does contract theory typically take the agent's objective as transparent and hide only effort or information? Second, does the distinction between competence and aim — between being capable of doing the task and wanting the intended goal — have a natural economic counterpart, or does it get flattened into a single dimension of something like effort or incentive strength? Third, is there any standard contract-theoretic equivalent of distribution shift between training and deployment, where the relation between the performance measure and the intended outcome holds in one regime but not another, and both parties know the regime will change?

Finally, tell me whether the residue is best read as a gap in the economics vocabulary, or as a sign that some of the inner alignment claims are less precise than they look once they are forced through a rigorous incentive-theoretic translation.
