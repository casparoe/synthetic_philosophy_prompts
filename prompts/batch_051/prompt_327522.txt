I teach a seminar on the methods of the behavioral sciences, and we have been working through the standard critique that adaptationist explanations in evolutionary psychology often function as just-so stories: narratives that are easy to generate after the fact, difficult to disconfirm, and compatible with almost any observed behavior. I want an exercise that pushes students past a blanket dismissal of the field and forces them to make comparative judgments about individual hypotheses rather than about the field as a whole. Below are seven claims, each stating that some human psychological tendency is a product of natural selection. For each claim, please assign a vulnerability score from 1 to 10, where 1 means the claim is almost maximally vulnerable to the just-so story objection and 10 means it is comparatively well shielded from that objection because it carries specific, independently testable commitments or has multiple converging lines of support. Then rank all seven claims from most vulnerable to least vulnerable, and give a sentence or two of justification for each score. Take the claims at face value as stated, and reason about their scientific character rather than supplying citations.

The seven claims are as follows. (1) The human disgust response, especially toward bodily fluids, wounds, decay, mold, and unfamiliar foods, was selected primarily as a pathogen-avoidance system, which helps explain why disgust has a characteristic facial expression and why sensitivity to it varies with infection risk. (2) In long-term mating contexts, men and women differ systematically in typical mate preferences—men placing greater weight on cues to fertility such as youth, and women placing greater weight on cues to provisioning ability such as status and resources—because of asymmetrical parental investment over human evolutionary history. (3) Children raised in close domestic proximity during the first several years of life later develop a mutual sexual disinterest, which functions as an inbreeding-avoidance mechanism. (4) The human capacity to acquire any natural language to which one is exposed in early childhood rests on a domain-specific language faculty that evolved because sharing detailed information across individuals and groups conferred substantial fitness advantages. (5) People solve conditional reasoning problems much more accurately when the rule concerns detecting violations of a social contract than when it is presented as neutral descriptive content, because natural selection built a dedicated cheater-detection mechanism for the demands of reciprocal exchange. (6) The mild relief many people feel when a planned social obligation is cancelled at the last minute evolved because it reallocates cognitive and metabolic effort away from low-yield social interactions toward alternative activities with higher marginal fitness returns. (7) A cross-culturally common preference for savanna-like environments—open sightlines, scattered trees, and nearby water—evolved because it guided ancestral humans toward habitats that offered both resources and the ability to spot predators.
