I have been asked to present a short paper at our epistemology workshop in three weeks, and I want to use the remaining time preparing for the question period rather than further polishing the text. The paper defends what I have come to think of as a secularized Mīmāṃsā position. As I reconstruct the tradition, it combines two commitments: the Veda is authorless, and a cognition is valid by default, so a person is entitled to treat an apparent cognition as true unless a specific defect is identified in the conditions that produced it, rather than needing independent confirmation before trusting it. I transpose this into the epistemology of testimony. The claim I will defend is that when a hearer is confronted with what is apparently an act of telling, she has a defeasible entitlement to accept the telling's content as true, an entitlement that is not earned through evidence about the speaker's reliability, sincerity, or access to the facts. Suspicion needs a specific trigger; it is not the default position.

My route to the claim has four steps. First, the demand for prior validation cannot be discharged. If acceptance requires an antecedent record of a speaker's reliability, that record can be built only out of perception, memory, and further tellings; perception and memory are granted exactly the default status the reductionist withholds from testimony, and the further tellings reintroduce the same problem. The demand is therefore circular, regressive, or arbitrarily selective. Second, the speech act does normative work. To interpret an utterance as an assertion is already to see it as an act that vouches for its content, and it would be odd to hold that this undertaking licenses nothing in the hearer. Third, error should be treated as a departure from a norm, not as the baseline from which every instance of acceptance must climb. Wholesale distrust is not neutral; it is a substantive position that stands in need of as much defense as trust does. Fourth, the authorless Veda supplies the limiting case that reveals the real structure of the view: when the authority of a source does not depend on any author's competence or honesty, the speaker's biography is not the thing that must be settled first. I keep that structure while dropping the theological application.

The audience is a research seminar in analytic epistemology, and nobody there will be moved by the label alone. The median member accepts a reductionism about testimonial justification: the view that a hearer's entitlement to believe what she is told is a special case of ordinary evidence about how speakers in general, and this speaker in particular, behave. They will hear intrinsic validity as an attempt to purchase justification at a discount. A smaller group studies the social distribution of credibility; they will be less exercised about gullibility than about the fact that a defeasible default does not fall equally on all speakers and may quietly compound existing inequalities in whose word gets taken seriously. Two philosophers of language will attack the speech-act premise. One Sanskritist will know the sources better than I do and will regard my secularization as a change of subject. I have argued both sides of this with myself long enough to know that my own sense of which steps are strong is not a reliable guide to which steps the room will attack.

Write me a prep sheet rather than a defense. For each likely objection: who in the room would raise it, how that person would phrase it, how damaging it is, what I should concede or refuse to concede, the reply I can give in under two minutes, and where that reply is soft. Order them to match, as closely as you can, the order in which the questions will actually arrive, and tell me the question I am most afraid will be asked even though I have not said so.
