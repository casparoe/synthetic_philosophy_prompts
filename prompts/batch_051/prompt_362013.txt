For something I'm writing on a rationality forum, I've been turning over a twist on the classic Ulysses-and-the-Sirens setup. The stock version: current Ulysses rationally prefers not to be wrecked, predicts that future Ulysses will get his preferences hijacked by the song, and so has the crew lash him to the mast, plug their own ears, and ignore all subsequent orders. The usual moral drawn from this is that it's a paradigm of rational self-binding: the ex ante self has the better epistemic position and the ex post self is at least locally deranged.

Now change one thing. The song is not a pure glamor. It also contains accurate, decision-relevant information about the route ahead that Ulysses cannot get any other way before he's past the rocks. Some part of future Ulysses' apparently self-destructive decision is rational updating on evidence the crew doesn't have. Ex ante Ulysses knows all this and still wants to be tied, because he can't trust himself, in the moment, to seperate the informative part of the song from the enchanting part; the crew can't reliably do it either.

Work through what the standard Ulysses-contract analysis says about this version. Does the justification survive, or does the case invert—the ex ante self now being the informationally worse-off party, binding a better-informed future self for no better reason than its own fear? What should the crew's rule actually be? And does the point transfer to constitutional precommitment—say, a constitution that makes it very hard for a future majority to revise a rule after new evidence comes in? Is that still a Ulysses contract, or is it closer to a society making an epistemic mistake?

End with a line starting 'Verdict:' that gives your one-sentence bottom line.
