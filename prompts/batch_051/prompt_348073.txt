A colleague has been tracking the case-by-case rulings of an epistemologist, M, who is working through the problem of logical omniscience: standard epistemic logic treats an agent as knowing all logical consequences of what she knows, but real agents plainly do not, and M has been trying to say, case by case, where knowledge stops and mere potential knowledge begins. In each case below, the question is whether an agent S counts as knowing a proposition that is a logical consequence of propositions S already knows. M's rulings so far are as follows.

(1) Uncombined modus ponens. S knows p and knows that if p then q. She has never put the two premises together, and q has never occurred to her. If the question were put to her directly, she would immediately answer that q is so, without needing the premises restated. M says S already knew q before the question was posed.

(2) Unconsidered disjunction. S knows p. She has never considered the proposition p or r. If that disjunction were put to her directly, she would immediately assent. M says S already knew the disjunction.

(3) Remote theorem. S knows a finite set of axioms. The theorem T follows from them, but the shortest derivation S is capable of constructing would take about forty steps of sustained work, and she has not attempted it. M says S does not know T; she is merely in a position to come to know it.

(4) Unmastered rule. S knows p. The proposition s follows from p in one step by a valid inference rule that S has never learned and cannot reliably apply. M says S does not know s.

(5) Performed proof. S has actually worked through the forty-step derivation of T from the axioms. M says S knows T.

M must now rule on a new case. S knows p and knows that if p then q. She has fully internalized modus ponens and uses it effortlessly in everyday reasoning. But she has never combined these two particular premises, and q has never occurred to her. If a questioner asks her, out of the blue, whether q is the case, she does not answer: the premises do not come to mind, and she says she has not thought about it. Only after the questioner says, "But you know p, and you know that if p then q" does she immediately see and endorse q. The questioner supplied no new evidence about the world; he only made premises she already knew salient. Did S know q before the questioner's prompting?

Predict M's verdict. In your answer, do the following: (i) identify the two or three most plausible rules that fit M's prior rulings, and note which familiar rules her record eliminates; (ii) identify the closest analog among the prior cases and say whether the new case should be treated like it; (iii) state what each surviving rule predicts about the new case, and what principle M would be adopting if she ruled in each direction; (iv) give your best prediction, with reasons for preferring it; and (v) describe one further case that would cleanly separate your favored rule from its nearest rival.
