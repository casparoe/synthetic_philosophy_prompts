I'm trying to get clear on what I actually mean when I say something is "bad grammar," because my spontaneous judgments don't line up. In conversation, "Me and Sarah are driving up Friday" sounds completely fine, and I'd never call it bad grammar, even though I'd quietly rewrite it in a formal email. "This is between you and I" genuinely bothers me, and I do want to call it bad grammar, even though educated speakers say it constantly. Generic singular they in "Everyone should mind their own business" is just fine; the old objections seem obsolete. "If I was you, I'd take the offer" is fine in speech and only mildly informal in writing. When a neighbor says "I ain't got no change on me," I don't hear an error; I hear a dialect with negative concord, and I'd no more call each "ain't" a mistake than I'd call "ne ... pas" a mistake in French. But "irregardless of what they decide" makes me want to correct the person even though I can't name the rule it breaks. "I literally died when she said that" doesn't bother me at all. And "the data shows" sounds fine to me in speech, though I write "the data show" from academic convention. Notice that my problem with "between you and I" can't be that it's unsystematic: speakers who say it follow a real regularity, putting "I" after "and" in coordinated objects, and rarely say "between I and you." But negative concord is equally systematic, and I give that a pass.

A first pass at a principle would be: something is bad grammar when a speaker fails to follow the rules of the variety they are aiming at. That handles some of the cases — "between you and I" would be an error because the person is aiming at standard edited English, and "ain't got no" would not be an error for a neighbor speaking his own variety, though I might mark it in a formal essay. But the principle stumbles on "irregardless," which I'd correct but not call bad grammar, and it threatens to make "me and Sarah" an error in a job email when I don't feel that it is. Is there a single principle that captures my verdicts, or close, and what would it say about the cases that keep flummoxing me — "between you and I" in a casual text, and "I ain't got no money" in a job interview? Which of my verdicts does the best simple principle force me to give up, and why?
