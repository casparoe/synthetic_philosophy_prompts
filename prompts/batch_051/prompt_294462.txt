I am a novelist working on a historical novel set partly among Philadelphia artisans and merchants in the decades before the Revolution, and I have been reading Max Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism to understand the cast of mind of my characters. In Chapter 2, Weber quotes at length from Benjamin Franklin, and the following passage is the part that has lodged in my head:

Remember, that time is money. He that can earn ten shillings a day by his labor, and goes abroad, or sits idle, one half of that day, though he spends but sixpence during his diversion or idleness, ought not to reckon that the only expense; he has really spent, rather thrown away, five shillings, besides.

Remember, that credit is money. If a man lets his money lie in my hands after it is due, he gives me interest, or so much as I can make of it during that time. This amounts to a considerable sum where a man has good and large credit, and makes good use of it.

Remember, that money is of the prolific, generating nature. Money can beget money, and its offspring can beget more, and so on. Five shillings turned is six, turned again it is seven and three pence, and so on, till it becomes a hundred pounds. The more there is of it, the more it produces every turning, so that the profits rise quicker and quicker. He that kills a breeding sow, destroys all her offspring to the thousandth generation. He that murders a crown, destroys all that it might have produced, even scores of pounds.

What puzzles me is the role Weber says this passage plays. He introduces it as a document that contains what he is looking for "in almost classical purity" and that has the advantage of being "free from all direct relationship to religion." But his larger thesis seems to be that certain Protestant ascetic values helped produce the spirit of capitalism. So why would his paradigm case of that spirit be a deliberately secular, non-religious document? Is Franklin's advice meant to be read as evidence that the spirit exists, as an illustration of an ideal type, or as the end product of a process whose religious scaffolding has since fallen away? I would like a careful reading of how the passage functions in Weber's argument, what exactly the "spirit of capitalism" consists of on the strongest reading of Franklin's maxims, and whether the passage can be both non-religious and still causally downstream from Protestantism without making Weber's claim unfalsifiable.
