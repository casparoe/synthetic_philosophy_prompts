Here is an argument I have been unable to diagnose, and I would like you to tell me exactly where it goes wrong, if it does. (1) If an authority is legitimate, then deferring to that authority is a virtue. (2) In Milgram's obedience experiment, the experimenter was a legitimate authority for the subjects: he was a scientist at a reputable institution, and the subjects had entered a setting whose implicit norms gave them reason to trust his instructions. (3) Therefore the subjects' deference to the experimenter was a virtue. (4) But that deference led the subjects to administer what they believed were severe and dangerous electric shocks to an innocent person. (5) A virtue cannot lead someone to do what they believe is seriously wrong. (6) Therefore the experimenter was not, after all, a legitimate authority for the subjects. I suspect the trouble is either in (1) or in a slide between a normative and a merely psychological sense of legitimate authority, but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Is the argument valid? If it is, which premise is false? If it is not, which step fails, and what is the most defensible replacement for the faulty premise?
