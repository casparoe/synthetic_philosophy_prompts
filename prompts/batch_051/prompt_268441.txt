I have been reading two pieces by Peter Singer—the essay "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) and the later book "The Life You Can Save" (2009)—and I am trying to work out whether his position changed or only looks as if it did. In the 1972 essay he introduces the shallow-pond example: if you are walking past a pond and see a small child drowning, you are morally required to wade in and pull the child out even though it will ruin your clothes. He then argues that physical distance and the fact that other people could also help do not weaken the obligation, and he concludes that a person who took the argument seriously would have to keep giving until any further gift would impose on the giver a burden comparable to the bad thing the gift would prevent. That conclusion would force a drastic cut in almost all discretionary spending. In The Life You Can Save, however, Singer uses the same pond example to support a different-sounding proposal: a public giving scale, recommended as a percentage of income, deliberately set low enough that people who are comfortably off can meet it without giving up most of what they have. The later book seems to accept a level of giving that the 1972 essay would treat as only the beginning.

Are these two positions in genuine tension, and can they be reconciled? I can see at least three readings. First, the two works answer different questions: the essay says what an abstract moral principle requires of a person considered individually, while the book asks what standard we should publicly urge people to adopt, so the gap is one of aspiration versus strategy. Second, Singer changed his mind and softened the earlier conclusion. Third, the giving scale is not a retreat at all: it is the same principle applied realistically to human motivation, because a standard set at the level the 1972 essay seemed to demand would predictably produce less total giving, and therefore fewer lives saved. I would like you to work through these readings and any others you think of, and then say which is the most honest reading of the two texts and which involve special pleading. If the first reading is correct, does that make the 1972 principle a mere ideal with no direct practical force, and does that in turn weaken the drowning-child argument?
