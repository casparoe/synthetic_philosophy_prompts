I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this term that's been on the emotions, and I'm starting to find the whole thing weirdly stuck after nine weeks. The class has split into three camps. One insists emotions just are felt bodily responses—the churning stomach, the tightening chest—and that everything else, like the thought 'that dog is dangerous,' comes later as interpretation. They keep pointing to infants and animals: a dog can be terrified of thunder without being able to form the relevant propositional thoughts, so emotions can't be judgments. A second camp says emotions are essentially evaluative judgments or at least appraisals: anger is judging that you've been wronged, fear is appraising something as a threat. They lean on recalcitrant emotions. I'm scared of flying even though I judge, fully and sincerely, that it's safer than driving; if the fear just were the judgment 'flying is dangerous,' that would be a flat contradiction, but instead it feels like something in me is resisting my judgment, which suggests emotions are a distinct kind of sensitivity that can go wrong. A third camp wants a middle path, saying emotions are perceptions of value: a direct awareness that the dog is threatening, analogous to seeing that the tomato is red, and it can persist even when you judge the threat is unreal, like a visual illusion persisting after you know the lines are equal.

The funny thing is everyone agrees on the examples—the dog, the fearful flyer, the startle response—they just file them differently, and nobody has moved in weeks. My current theory is that the three camps are answering slightly different questions under one heading: what an emotion feels like, what makes an emotion rational or irrational, and how emotions hook onto the world. If so the conflict might seperate out into compatible projects. But I'm not confident; it might also be a genuine disagreement about which feature—feeling, evaluation, or perception—is more basic, or a hidden empirical question about affect programs and whether basic emotions exist, that neuroscience could eventually settle. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each camp says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, an empirical finding, a better definition, or just admitting the question is ill-posed.
