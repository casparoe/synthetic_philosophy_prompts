I'm a decision theory PhD student, and I keep running a game theory recitation for econ undergrads where superrationality comes up, so I've been chewing on a variant I can't settle. Take the standard one-shot prisoner's dilemma with symmetric payoffs: temptation 5, reward 3, punishment 1, sucker 0 for both players. The superrationality story goes: the two players are in identical situations, so a rational twin applying the same reasoning will match your move; you're effectively choosing between (C,C) and (D,D), and since 3 > 1 you cooperate. Fine.

Now break the symmetry. Keep the same ordering of temptation/reward/punishment/sucker for each player, but give row a bigger temptation than column:

             col C      col D
row C         4, 4        0, 5
row D        10, 0        1, 1

Each player still has a dominant strategy to defect, (D,D) is still the unique Nash equilibrium, and (C,C) is still the joint outcome both prefer to (D,D).

My question: does the superrationality argument survive this change? The whole move in the symmetric case is "identical positions ⇒ identical choice ⇒ compare the diagonal." Here the positions aren't identical, so the same-choice inference loses it's grip — or maybe it doesn't, since the diagonal comparison is still sitting right there. Does superrationality just go quiet and hand you back defect-defect, or is there a natural asymmetric extension (say: both players choose the joint outcome they'd jointly prefer to the diagonal baseline, or maximize the sum, or maximize the worst-off player's payoff), and does it pick (C,C) in this matrix? Also tell me which clause of the original argument is doing the fatal work, and be explicit about a change to the numbers that would flip your verdict about whether superrationality survives here.
