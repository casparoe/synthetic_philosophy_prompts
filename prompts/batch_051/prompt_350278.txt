I've been working through the demandingness objection to consequentialism, and a friend recently ended a debate about moral obligations to the global poor with a one-liner I'd like to have taken apart. I had argued that an ordinary affluent person who gives a steady but modest share of her income, votes, and sometimes volunteers is doing enough — that a demanding moral theory can't require everyone to make the problem their life's work. She replied: "If you're not part of the solution, you're part of the problem."

Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the near-tautological to the strongly demanding. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it actually licenses the conclusion she was drawing — that the moderately generous person is acting wrongly. I'm particularly interested in the difference between readings on which "part of the problem" means actively making things worse and readings on which it merely means failing to make things better, since that distinction seems to be carrying a lot of hidden weight. Give me a menu of readings rather than one settled interpretation, and say which readings would genuinely do the argumentative work.
