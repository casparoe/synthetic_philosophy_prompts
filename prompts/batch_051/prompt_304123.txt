I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, but I learned the trade on a metro desk and spent several years as a news editor, so my working vocabulary for decisions is editorial rather than formal: assigning a reporter, committing to a story on deadline, standing behind a source, hedging a lede, spiking a piece, running a correction, and weighing the risk of a retraction or a defamation suit against the value of publishing at all. I keep encountering the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization — representation theorems, the axioms of transitivity, completeness, and independence, and Dutch book or money-pump arguments claiming that violations of those axioms leave an agent open to sure loss — and I have a working journalist's grasp of them, though not a decision theorist's.

Restate the coherence position, along with the main objections that have been raised against it, in the vocabulary I actually use as an editor. I want a systematic translation, term by term, not a handful of loose analogies: how should preference and choice under uncertainty be rendered into editorial judgment; what in a newsroom corresponds to the axioms; what is the editorial counterpart of a Dutch book or money pump; and how does the claim that vulnerability to sure loss amounts to irrationality get expressed when the losses are reputational, institutional, or legal rather than monetary. Be as faithful to the philosophy as the newsroom vocabulary allows, and do not quietly import the newsroom's own norms and call them a translation.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the coherence argument map cleanly onto editorial decision-making, which parts required distortion, and which parts have no expression at all in the vocabulary I described — for example, the betting counterparty who assembles the sure loss, the idea that preferences are revealed by choices rather than by stated editorial principles, or the assumption that any two options can be compared. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it marks a limitation of the newsroom as a lens, or whether it marks a place where the philosophical argument is leaning on an assumption that looks shakier once it cannot be stated. I want to know, in the end, whether the coherence argument amounts only to the injunction not to be exploitable, or whether something else is being claimed — and whether that something else survives contact with a different way of describing decisions.
