I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting a book about a burned-out moral philosopher who leaves academia; I keep recording arguments among my old philosophy friends and mining them for dialogue. Last weekend two of them got into it over whether simplicity is actually a virtue in ethical theory, and the exchange got good enough that I want a version of it in the manuscript. It went roughly like this:

(1) First friend: If two theories fit our considered judgments about cases about equally well, you should prefer the one with fewer irreducible principles. Fewer independent moving parts means fewer places for the theory to go wrong — that is just how theory choice works anywhere.

(2) Second friend: That's the exact move I don't buy. In science, simplicity helps because a simpler model is less likely to overfit noisy observations. But moral intuitions aren't observations of some hidden causal structure, and there is no noise to overfit. So parsimony in ethics is more an aesthetic preference for a clean system than a reason to think the theory is true.

(3) First: You're treating intuitions as second-class data just because they aren't physical measurements. In ethics they're all we have unless you can point me to some other access to moral facts. Besides, a one-principle theory says something much bolder; if it still captures nearly all the cases, that fit is more surprising and more confirmatory than a thirty-duty theory that just bolts on a new duty for every awkward case.

(4) Second: But the one-principle theory only looks one-principled because all the complexity is hidden in the account of value and aggregation. Once you spell out "maximize the good" — well-being, interpersonal comparison, distribution — it's just as complicated as a pluralism with seven or eight distinct duties. You've moved the mess into the basement, not eliminated it.

(5) First: Fair as a dig at slogan-level utilitarianism, but the point doesn't depend on it. Take two deontological theories that share the same account of value and aggregation; one posits two kinds of constraint, the other eleven. You'd prefer the two-constraint version, all else equal. What's doing that work if not parsimony?

(6) Second: Even if it is a tiebreaker, it's a weak one, because to apply it you need a unit for counting "one constraint" versus "eleven." Are a pluralist's seven duties seven things, or one duty to respond to whatever consideration is most weighty? The count is theory-laden, so parsimony isn't the neutral referee you keep treating it as. And even granting the count, you haven't given an exchange rate: how many abandoned intuitions is one fewer duty worth? Without that, "simplicity" is just a name for whatever trade-off you already decided feels right.

I honestly can't tell whether they were talking past each other, or whose actually winning at what point, which matters because I want the novel's philosopher to notice things neither of them did. Could you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate what's right and what's overclaimed at each step — then say what both are missing? Please keep the response under 650 words, and don't worry about citing anyone; just work with what's on the table.
