Write the joint statement that a committed defender of the object-given/state-given solution to the wrong kind of reasons problem and a committed critic of that solution could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The two parties are defined as follows. The object-given theorist holds that a fitting-attitude analysis of value can survive wrong-kind cases by restricting the reasons that determine an object's value to object-given reasons, that is, reasons provided by properties of the object of the attitude. The redescription skeptic holds that the object-given/state-given distinction cannot be drawn without presupposing the value facts it is meant to explain, because whether a consideration is classified as a property of the object or as a property of the attitude can generally be changed by redescribing the object; on this view, the wrong kind of reasons problem exposes a circularity in the fitting-attitude analysis, not a merely technical difficulty.

The disputed case throughout is this: an evil demon credibly threatens to harm you unless you admire it; you therefore have strong prudential reason to admire the demon, but the demon is not thereby good.

The document must contain the following.

1. A section of numbered agreed claims that both parties can sign. It should record the minimal data that neither side may explain away, including what the demon case shows about the agent's reason and about the demon's value; it should state the constraints that any acceptable treatment of wrong-kind reasons must satisfy; and it should record whatever both parties accept about ordinary cases of admiration, preference, and approval.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: issue; object-given theorist's formulation, phrased so that the object-given theorist would sign it; redescription skeptic's formulation, phrased so that the redescription skeptic would sign it; and classification of the disagreement as substantive or merely verbal.

3. A cruxes section identifying the sub-questions such that settling at least one of them would move at least one party. The cruxes must include at least the following three: (i) whether object-givenness can be characterized in strictly non-evaluative terms; (ii) whether the same consideration in the demon case can be redescribed as a property of the object rather than of the attitude without changing its normative status; and (iii) whether the agent's reason in the demon case is best treated as a reason for a distinct attitude, such as a desire to admire or an intention to bring it about that one admires, rather than as a reason to admire the demon itself, and whether that treatment disposes of the wrong-kind difficulty or merely relabels it. For each crux, state the argument, case, or test that both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each party's prediction of the outcome.

The statement should be worded so that neither party surrenders a central commitment, but it should not be vacuous: each row in the table and each crux should matter to at least one party's case. The document should read as a single statement of record that both parties could sign, not as a transcript of a debate or a narration of how they reached agreement. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, footnotes, or citations. Write in formal American English.
