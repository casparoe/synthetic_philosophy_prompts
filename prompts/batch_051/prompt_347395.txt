I am currently taking an upper-level undergraduate course on metaphilosophy, and we have been working through the question whether philosophy makes progress. Our professor has given us this passage from Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason, Preface to the first edition:

“Human reason, in one sphere of its cognition, is called upon to consider questions, which it cannot decline, as they are presented by its own nature, but which it cannot answer, as they transcend every faculty of the mind.
It falls into this difficulty without any fault of its own. It begins with principles, which cannot be dispensed with in the field of experience, and the truth and sufficiency of which are, at the same time, insured by experience. With these principles it rises, in obedience to the laws of its own nature, to ever higher and more remote conditions. But it quickly discovers that, in this way, its labours must remain ever incomplete, because new questions never cease to present themselves; and thus it finds itself compelled to have recourse to principles which transcend the region of experience, while they are regarded by common sense without distrust. It thus falls into confusion and contradictions, from which it conjectures the presence of latent errors, which, however, it is unable to discover, because the principles it employs, transcending the limits of experience, cannot be tested by that criterion. The arena of these endless contests is called Metaphysic.”

I want to use this passage as a fixed point for evaluating four broad positions on philosophical progress. Please sort the following into tiers according to how well each position can absorb Kant’s diagnosis without giving up the claim that philosophy makes some kind of progress:

1. Sceptical stasis: philosophy does not make first-order progress because its deepest disputes are precisely the endless contests Kant describes; at best it becomes more self-conscious about why they persist.

2. Dissolutionism: philosophical progress is not progress in answering questions but in unmasking them as misleading or badly posed; a question dissolved is a piece of progress, and Kant’s endless contests are symptoms of insufficient logical discipline.

3. Kantian tribunalism: Kant’s own response is the progress proposal — reason makes second-order progress when it stops seeking transcendent knowledge and instead establishes the boundaries within which answerable claims can be made.

4. Convergent realism: philosophy does make first-order progress — through the slow elimination of untenable positions and the accumulation of stable distinctions and results — but the progress is masked by institutional and pedagogical dissensus and by the fact that philosophical questions are graded as increasingly fine-grained.

For each position, say in one sentence what Kant’s passage would force it to explain or concede, and state the criterion you are using for your ranking — for example, fidelity to the text, explanatory power, or ability to preserve a robust notion of progress.
