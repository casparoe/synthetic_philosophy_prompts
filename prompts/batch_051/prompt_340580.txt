My younger brother joined a fairly serious religious community last year, and his reasons for staying have started to leak into every family dinner. Last month he told me he sees God's silence as part of the point: God is giving us room to choose him freely, out of love. A few weeks later, my sister was talking about our father, who walked out when we were young and stayed mostly out of contact. She said, half to herself, "a father who lets his children wonder whether he even exists is not a father." Nobody disagreed, my brother included.

And that's the pair of judgments I can't get to sit still together. Most of us would say a good human parent cannot hide from their child that way: going silent to the point where the child reasonably doubts the parent's existence or love is a profound failure, maybe cruel, and it stays a failure even if the parent has reasons like "I didn't want to be a burden" or "I didn't want to force myself on them." But many of the same people also say God loves each person more than any human parent could, while God remains hidden at least from some sincere seekers, from people in terrible grief, from people who would believe if they got the kind of evidence they'd accept anywhere else. That hiddenness is treated not as a failure of love but as compatible with love, or even as love.

So here's what I'm asking. Is there a principle that genuinely generates both judgments, or is the second just a special exemption we hand to God? Defend the strongest candidate justification you can find for treating divine hiddenness differently from a human parent's hiddenness, stress-test it against one or two nearby cases, and then tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept or dropped. Give me a committed answer: pick a side and argue for it, dont just catalogue the options. Please lead with a one-sentence bottom line, and keep the whole thing under 400 words.
