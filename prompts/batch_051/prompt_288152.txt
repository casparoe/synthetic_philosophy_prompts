Could you write me a short dialogue between two people who disagree about whether a company can be morally blamed in it's own right, or whether blame always has to trace back to individual human beings? Call them Alex and Sam. Alex defends the first view and Sam defends the second, and I want both characters to be genuinely smart, not strawmen, and to respond to what the other person actually said rather than taking turns giving speeches.

The case they should work through is this: a midsized company drifted over about ten years into a practice where one of its products fails at a rate that causes small harms to lots of customers. No single person ever decided on this, and no one manager had the whole picture; the harm only becomes visible when you aggregate data across departments and across years. By the time someone puts the pieces together, almost everyone who was there at the start has left, including the managers, and the current employees are mostly just following procedures they inherited. The question is whether the company itself can be blamed for those harms.

At some point I'd like them to distinguish between three things that I think constantly blur together: blame as an emotional attitude, blame as a social practice (like public condemnation or damage to reputation), and holding an entity accountable going forward. It would also be nice if they considered whether a company can be said to know or intend something when the knowledge or intention is spread across different roles, records, and people who come and go, without any individual holding the whole thing in their head.

They don't have to reach a tidy conclusion, but the exchange should genuinely move somewhere. And please write the entire response in continuous prose, like an email or a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
