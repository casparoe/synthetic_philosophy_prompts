Two philosophers have spent the better part of a year arguing about whether composite material objects exist. One is a commonsense realist: tables, mountains, and persons are objects in good standing, and their particles are parts of those objects. The other is a mereological nihilist: strictly speaking, there are no composite objects; there are only simples arranged tablewise, mountainwise, and personwise, and ordinary talk of tables is a harmless but loose way of speaking that is false if pressed with full ontological seriousness. Their argument has now become an argument about the argument itself. Each suspects the other of quietly adjusting the extension of object and exists while leaving all the underlying facts untouched; each also suspects the other of being unable to state what additional fact would be at stake in the world's containing a table over and above the tablewise arrangement of particles. Both have conceded that they may be talking past one another, but neither is willing to concede the point without seeing the whole disagreement written out fairly.

Write the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should produce. It should contain: a numbered section of agreed claims — about the particles, their arrangements, ordinary practices of counting and referring, and the standard cases used in their debate — that are neither vacuous nor slanted and that both could sign without hedging; then a disagreements table, in which each residual point is stated first in neutral wording and then in a precisification the nihilist would sign and a precisification the realist would sign, with a note on whether that row marks a difference about the world or a difference about how to use words; then a section of cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, where for each crux both accept a concrete thought experiment, linguistic test, or methodological argument as bearing on it and each side's prediction of the outcome is recorded in advance; and finally a closing diagnosis of which parts of the year-long dispute, if any, are merely verbal. In that diagnosis, state explicitly the criterion used to distinguish verbal from substantive disagreement, and say whether and how a quantifier-variance thesis — the hypothesis that the two sides mean different things by object or exists and that each theory is true under its own quantifier language — would dissolve or relocate the residual disagreement.
