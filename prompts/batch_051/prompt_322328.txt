Longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere here, so I automatically think in terms of costly signaling and reference classes. I've been chewing on how much you can actually infer about beliefs and institutions from objects alone, and I want to stress-test the inference rules.

Here are the criteria I'd roughly endorse for classifying an excavated site or artifact as ritual/religious rather than practical/economic:

- the behavior behind it is repeated and standardized across many sites or people, not a one-off
- it burns a lot of resources for no obvious practical payoff (costly signaling, basically)
- it uses dedicated architecture or space set off from mundane daily activity
- it has representational or symbolic content: figures, signs, layouts that point beyond their immediate use
- it's tied to authoritative texts or rules that get transmitted with surprising fidelity

Now the case. A future archaeology team, working after a big collapse and the loss of most digital records, excavates an early-21st-century bitcoin mining facility. They find rows of identical machines in a warehouse, arranged in straight aisles for airflow and maintenance access, elaborate cooling and redundant power, no finished goods leaving the site, and a scatter of small metal tokens stamped with a stylized B. Nearby they recover fragments of forum posts and a technical manifesto full of language about trustlessness, consensus, proof of work, and a fixed schedule of halvings. People around the world seem to have poured enormous amounts of electricity into producing numbers that got added to an append-only ledger no one could alter. There are also repair logs and a pile of dead power supplies.

By my five critera, is the team warranted in classifying this as ritual or religious? Go through criterion by criterion, say which ones pull toward ritual and which resist, and where the conclusion would be wrong, say whether the defect is in the criteria or in how the team applies them. Also: is there anything likely to survive in the material record that would let them catch the mistake, or does the case show that function and meaning simply can't be separated by object-based criteria alone?
