Act as a professional philosopher specialising in applied ethics, particularly exploitation, global labour, and consumer responsibility. I have a set of commitments I have accumulated over years of reflecting on sweatshops and the idea of mutually beneficial exploitation, and I want you to audit whether the set hangs together. I am not asking for citations, exegesis of particular thinkers, or a survey of the literature; the job is to stress-test the internal structure of the view I am about to state.

Think carefully before you settle on your verdicts. If it helps, begin by reconstructing my position in your own words, then work through each pair of commitments and ask whether the conflict is real or only apparent. Be willing to conclude that some commitments have to be revised, but do not invent tensions that are not there.

Here are the commitments:

1. Sweatshop employment is often genuinely exploitative even when it is mutually beneficial. A worker can voluntarily accept a job that leaves her better off than her next-best alternative while the employer captures a disproportionate share of the value she creates.

2. It does not follow from a transaction's being exploitative that it should not take place. When the worker's alternative is worse and both sides gain, the exchange can be permissible all things considered.

3. Consent and voluntariness are not decisive. A worker can consent freely, without force, fraud, or deception, to terms that are still unfair to her; so I reject both 'it is voluntary, therefore it is not exploitative' and 'it is voluntary, therefore it is fine'.

4. The primary wrong of sweatshop exploitation is structural rather than transactional. It lies mainly in the background distribution of bargaining power and outside options, not in defects in the individual contract, the individual manager, or the individual purchase. For this reason I hold that wages should be assessed against what the firm can afford and the share of value the worker creates, not against arbitrary first-world pay benchmarks.

5. As a buyer, I judge that most individual purchases of sweatshop-made goods are permissible, and that ordinary consumers are not required to investigate the labour history of every product they buy. The causal connection between one purchase and any worker's condition is extremely weak, and withholding purchase may well harm workers rather than help them.

6. Yet I endorse organised, targeted consumer campaigns against particular brands with publicly known supplier relationships. I also judge that brands have strong duties to audit their supply chains and to stop using suppliers who impose forced overtime, child labour, credible threats, or gross safety violations, even when cutting ties costs some workers their jobs.

7. I think the 'better than the alternatives' defence has genuine limits. I judge it seriously wrong to offer a vulnerable person a short-term, very high-interest loan when that loan is the best available option, even if accepting it leaves her slightly better off than refusing. I do not accept that the loan's being mutually beneficial suffices to make it permissible in the way commitment 2 makes sweatshop hiring permissible.

Please audit this set. In your answer: (i) distinguish the conflicts that are genuine from the ones that are merely apparent, explaining the distinction that dissolves each apparent conflict; (ii) rank the genuine tensions by severity; (iii) for each genuine tension, state exactly what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be; and (iv) tell me which single revision would do the most to restore coherence while preserving as much of the set as possible. Write your answer in British English.
