Since I retired from a physiology lab I've been reading philosophy of mind, and here's a puzzle I can't shake. In every hospital I've been in, the nurses hand you that laminated chart with the row of faces and ask you to rate your pain from zero to ten, and the number you give drives what they do — more morphine, another nerve block, whatever. The whole practice seems to assume that the hurting sensation and the badness of it are the same thing, or at least move together tightly. But a couple of old neurological reports I remember describe patients — I think the condition is called pain asymbolia — who will tell you the sensation is definitely there, sharp or burning, they can point right to it, and then say it doesn't bother them at all. They don't flinch, they don't particularly want it stopped; one even laughed while describing being poked. If you take those reports at face value, the pain and the awfulness of the pain come apart. So what does ordinary pain medicine actually presuppose about what makes pain bad? Is the zero-to-ten scale already smuggling in a theory — that the unpleasantness is built into the raw pain signal itself — and would the practice have to change if the badness is something separable, added by a different system? Or is the practice compatible with several different philosophical views, with these odd patients just exposing a limitation we usually don't have to think about? I'd like someone to reconstruct the implicit theory and say which parts of hospital pain management genuinely depend on it and which don't.
