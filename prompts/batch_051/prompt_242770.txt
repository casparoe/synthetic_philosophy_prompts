Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about Cox's theorem and whether it really shows that probability theory is the uniquely rational logic of uncertain inference, and I want to sharpen my side before we resume. Take the role of a sophisticated critic of that claim: you accept the mathematics of the theorem, but you deny that its premises are rationally mandatory, and you hold that alternative representations of uncertain reasoning—degrees of belief that are only partially ordered, or represented by intervals rather than single numbers—can also be rationally legitimate. Be well informed and fair-minded; concede small points when they are well made, but never concede the main claim that Cox's theorem leaves the philosophical work undone. Stay in character until I say 'stop,' and keep each reply to one paragraph. My opening move is this: every axiomatic argument has to start somewhere, so pointing out that the theorem yields probability only if you grant its premises is not yet an objection. When I reason, my degree of plausibility in a conjunction is determined by my degree in one conjunct and my degree in the other conditional on it, and my degree in a negation is determined by my degree in the original proposition. If you reject those constraints, show me a pattern of uncertain inference that violates them and that I should still regard as rational—not merely a formal alternative, but an actual inference.
