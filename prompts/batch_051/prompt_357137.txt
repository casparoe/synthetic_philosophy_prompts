Im trying to think through the way historians assign blame, and I want to use the origins of the Cold War as the test case. The period I have in mind is roughly 1945 to 1948, from the end of the war in Europe to the Berlin blockade, though you can pull in earlier stuff like Hitler if it helps. Take the usual suspects people name for who "started" it: Stalin's moves in Eastern Europe, Truman's use of the bomb and then the Truman Doctrine, Kennan's Long Telegram, Churchill's Iron Curtain speech, maybe the unconditional-surrender policy and the power vacuum it left. I want you to run these candidates through a few different accounts of causal selection and blame that historians and philosophers of causation actually use. For instance: a straight counterfactual/but-for test; the idea that we pick out the abnormal or deliberate factor against a background of normal conditions; something like INUS conditions, where a factor is an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient bundle; and the distinction between explaining how something came about and assigning moral blame for it. For each theory: which candidate does it pick out as the starter, and which does it quietly let off the hook? Then ask whether any of these theories can pick out structural causes—bipolarity, ideology, the security dilemma, the logic of spheres of influence—without collapsing into "everything was overdetermined, so nobody started it." And flip it around at the end: what does this episode do to the theories? Do they genuinely track something in the history, or do they just formalize whatever political verdict you came in with? I dont need you to adjudicate the old orthodox/revisionist/post-revisionist debate as a debate; I want you to use 1945–48 to stress-test the idea that "who started it" is even a well-formed causal question, or whether cause and blame come apart in a way the theories keep missing.
