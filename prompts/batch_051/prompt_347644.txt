I'm grading an undergrad exam for a survey course on freedom, authority, and the novel. I gave them this passage from Dostoevsky's The Grand Inquisitor (H.P. Blavatsky translation):

"In it, it is Christ who appears on the scene. True, He says nothing, but only appears and passes out of sight."

The question I set was: "Reconstruct the Grand Inquisitor's argument that ordinary human beings cannot bear the freedom Christ offers, and then explain what role Christ's silence plays in that argument. Does the silence concede the Inquisitor's case, refute it, or do something else?"

Here's what the student wrote:

"The Grand Inquisitor's case is that Christ made an error when he refused the three temptations in the desert. By rejecting miracle, mystery, and authority, he demanded something of ordinary humans that only a small number can actually bear: a constant, anxious freedom to choose between good and evil, without external sign or guaranteed order. Most people, the Inquisitor says, will trade that freedom for bread and security, and they will resent anyone who makes them carry the burden. The Church's work is to correct Christ's mistake by taking back the freedom he offered and giving people what they really need, which is happiness through submission. In the passage, Christ says nothing, and that silence is the key. It shows he can't refute the Inquisitor, because every possible reply would just repeat his original impossible offer. The kiss at the end is Dostoevsky's way of telling us the Inquisitor is right, or at least that Christ forgives him for doing the merciful thing. So the silence concedes the argument."

- "missing_points": array of strings for relevant things the answer should have included but didn't
- "final_comment": 80–120 words addressed to the student
