I serve on a standing commission that administers a public equalization scheme. We decide claims one by one: each claimant asks for compensation for some form of disadvantage, and we must determine whether to award it and in what form. We do not work from an explicit theory; we work from our own precedents. A new claim is on next month's docket, and I want to know what we should decide if we are to stay consistent with ourselves.

Our published decisions so far are these:

1. We awarded the full cost of a power wheelchair and daily personal assistance to a claimant with a congenital mobility impairment, even though the claimant's self-reported life satisfaction was no lower than the population average. We paid for the equipment and services directly rather than writing an unrestricted cheque.

2. We denied a claim from a person of ordinary income and wealth who argued that his life went worse than others' because he could not afford the rare wines and first editions that mattered most to him, tastes he admitted he had deliberately cultivated over many years.

3. We denied a claim from a person of average resources and no functional limitations whose life satisfaction was persistently low on account of what the claimant described as a constitutional melancholy; clinical assessment found no diagnosable condition and no impairment of daily functioning.

The new claim is this. Ines has the median income and wealth, is in good health, and reports average life satisfaction. She is a member of a stigmatized minority in her town, and although no law bars her, landlords, shopkeepers, and lenders will not in practice rent premises to her, serve her on market terms, or extend her credit. Her options are therefore systematically worse than those of otherwise similar residents. She asks us to compensate her for the resulting disadvantage.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our previous decisions? Identify the principle or principles that fit our precedents, state what each would require in Ines's case, and give your best recommendation with reasons, including which of our prior decisions is the closest analog. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, be explicit about which principle we would be adopting under each possible decision, and describe one future case that would most cleanly force us to reveal which principle we have been applying all along.
