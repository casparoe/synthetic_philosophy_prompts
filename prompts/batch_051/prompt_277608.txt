You are a philosopher who works on value theory and the ethics of belief, and I'd like you to think carefully, maybe start by writing out a few rough hunches before you settle into your answer. I've been puzzling over idolatry, but not in the statues-and-incense sense: the broader worry that a person can value something in the wrong way, treating a finite thing as if it where ultimate. I want to test a rough, secularised set of criteria against a case that seems genuinely borderline.

Here are the criteria I have in mind for idolatry in this broad sense:
(a) the thing becomes the fixed point against which all your other values are measured;
(b) you protect it from criticism with moves you would never accept for anything else;
(c) you ask it to supply something it structurally can't supply, like forgiveness, security, meaning, or absolution;
(d) you relate to it with a kind of submissive dependence, not just instrumental reliance;
(e) when it gets something wrong, you blame yourself or the application rather than the thing itself.

Now the case. A person went through a bad period a few years ago and now runs their life through a comprehensive self-quantification setup: sleep stages, heart rate variability, mood logs, and a daily readiness score. The readiness score functions almost as a verdict on the day: on a low score they call the day wasted before it has properly started, and a run of high scores feels like vindication. They schedule meals, friendships, and exercise around it. When the system says they recovered badly after what they remember as a joyful day at a friend's wedding, they conclude the day must secretly have been more stressful than it felt, rather than doubting the index. They push friends to adopt it, not as a convenience but as a proper way to live, and they say they would be genuinely frightened to go a week without it.

But here is the twist: they insist the number is not the point. The number just tracks what already matters—their health, their stability, their capacity to be present for people—and the daily verdict is only information. They aren't bowing to the score, they say; they're simply obeying the evidence.

Is this idolatry in the broad sense, a disciplined health practice, or somewhere the criteria can't decide?
