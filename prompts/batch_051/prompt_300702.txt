I run quarterly kill-or-continue reviews for a portfolio of software projects at a mid-size company, and every quarter we keep funding things that are over budget and slipping because we've already spent so much, or because two teams are competing for the same internal resources. It looks like a dollar auction to me (two bidders chase a dollar, and the loser also pays their last bid), and I suspect the lesson of that work is more then just 'don't fall for sunk costs' — it's something about how rational-looking local decisions walk you into a trap. Please write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the dollar auction and escalation-of-commitment literature, that a skeptical product owner can run in an hour with no philosophy background. Include the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a kill, and an explicit check for whether we're even in a dollar auction at all (salvage value, reputation, and repeated play might change things). Then run it on three cases: a rewrite that's 18 months late with a lot of sunk engineering time, a pricing experiment two departments are competing to own, and a legacy product we keep patching mostly because killing it would embarass the exec who championed it. Tell me where the procedure gives a verdict you'd expect us to resist, and where the auction logic runs out and a person has to decide. And keep clear which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who study escalation — don't present a contested view as the consensus.
