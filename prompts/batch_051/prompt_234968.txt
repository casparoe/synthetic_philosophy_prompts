I sit on my hospital's research ethics committee. A trial before us randomizes adults newly diagnosed with hypertension to either a current first-line drug or an older, cheaper drug that consensus guidelines no longer recommend for the average patient, because the first-line drug is more effective overall. The investigators do not dispute that average; they argue that in practice many competent clinicians still use the older drug for a subset of patients who cannot tolerate the first-line drug's side effects, so expert opinion is genuinely divided about that subset, and the trial therefore satisfies clinical equipoise. Half the committee calls this a loophole: the trial is not restricted to that subset, and for the average enrolled patient the older arm is inferior; subgroup uncertainty cannot create equipoise for the trial as written. The other half replies that the existence of reasonable clinical disagreement is exactly when randomization is most ethical. Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, and engage the strongest version of the losing position rather than merely restating the investigators' framing.
