In our philosophy club, two juniors got into an argument over a passage in Henri Poincaré's The Value of Science. In the passage, Poincaré describes a lay observer who watches scientific theories enjoy brief success and then get abandoned; the observer sees the wreckage of past theories piling up, predicts that current theories will soon collapse as well, and concludes that science is bankrupt. Marcus said this is just the pessimistic meta-induction: past successful theories turned out false, so present ones are probably false too. Jordan replied that Poincaré himself did not endorse the lay observer's conclusion—the passage is set up as a view he goes on to resist—and that the observer's real mistake is treating discarded theories as worthless rather than as partial successes. Marcus answered that this only softens the problem, since even partial successes were later replaced, so our current theories are probably also partial and destined for replacement, and our confidence should be provisional. Jordan said that makes the argument cut too deeply, because it would have applied to Newtonian physics, yet Newtonian physics remains impressively accurate within its domain. They asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate move by move: who is right about which specific point, what are both sides missing, and does the Poincaré passage ultimately support Marcus, Jordan, or neither?
