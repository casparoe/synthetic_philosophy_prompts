I'm giving a talk next month in our philosophy of physics seminar, defending the claim that Everettian quantum mechanics doesn't need the Born rule tacked on as a extra postulate. My line is that once you treat an observer just before a measurement as having successors on many decohering branches, and ask what a rational agent who cares about all those successors should prefer across branches, the squared-amplitude weights are the only assignment that keeps the agent's preferences coherent—branch-counting, where every branch gets equal weight, gets ruled out by consistency conditions on bets. The audience is mostly philosophers of physics who work in foundations but aren't themselves many-worlds people (spacetime and statistical mechanics types), plus two physicists from the quantum information group who think interpretation debates are a waste of time. They all accept the formalism and decoherence, but they mostly think 'probability' means nothing if every branch is real and some version of the observer turns up in all of them. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way they'd phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Don't give me the objections I'd get from hardcore Everettians or from metaphysicians banging on about personal identity. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
