Im a PhD student in logic, and I also edit the essays desk at a magazine that publishes philosophy-flavored pieces for a general audience. We get a steady stream of submissions that invoke Gödel's incompleteness theorems to settle something outside mathematics—whether the mind is a machine, whether a legal system can be complete, whether a final theory of physics is impossible, whether some truths are unprovable by anyone ever. About once a week I have to decide whether a given invocation is doing real argumentative work or is just prestige math, and I worry about two opposite errors: publishing the usual overreach makes us look credulous, while pretending Gödel has nothing to say outside arithmetic makes us look philistine. I need a reusable screening checklist I can hand to our other editors, none of whom know the theorems technically, and that we can run in under fifteen minutes on a submission. Give me exactly five checks, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion, ordered so we can run them top to bottom. In the relevant checks, use these three sample invocations as worked examples and state the verdict for each: (1) "Gödel showed arithmetic can't be both complete and consistent, so no legal code can be complete and consistent either, and judicial discretion is unavoidable." (2) "Gödel's theorem proves there are true statements no formal system can prove, and since we can see the Gödel sentence is true, the human mind is not a machine." (3) "The second incompleteness theorem says a system can't prove its own consistency, so self-certifying software should never be trusted." I'll admit I find the legal one quite plausible, so if the procedure kills it, say so plainly. Make the fifth check a statement of what the checklist cannot settle and which editorial judgments I still have to make myself.
