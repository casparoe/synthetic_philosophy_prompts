In my population ethics seminar, a student ended a debate about whether a couple with ample resources and a happy existing family should have a third child by saying, "the more the merrier." The same proverb later did different work when we asked whether a growing city is thereby a better city, and whether a larger human population is a better state of affairs. I suspect the sentence slides among several quite different claims, and that its plausibility depends entirely on which one is being expressed. I would like a careful disambiguation. Distinguish the precise theses "the more the merrier" could stand for in these contexts — for example, a thesis about total value, a thesis about average well-being, a thesis about the value of an addition to a group for its existing members, a merely descriptive claim about how enjoyable a larger gathering is, and any others worth separating. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or probably false, and bring out what is at stake: in particular, what each reading implies when adding lives worth living increases total welfare while lowering average welfare, and whether the value of a larger group is being assessed impersonally or from the standpoint of particular persons. Conclude by identifying which reading or readings actually support the student's use of the proverb to settle the question about the third child, and which support the claims about cities and species. I want a menu of readings with verdicts, not a single interpretation defended at length.
