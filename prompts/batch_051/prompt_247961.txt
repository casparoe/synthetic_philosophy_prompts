Two people in my formal-ethics reading group have been fighting for a month about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I want them to write the document they should of produced. Quick background so you don't need to look anything up: the theorem says that if each individual's preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and society's preferences also satisfy those axioms and respect Pareto (roughly: if every individual weakly prefers A to B, society doesn't rank A below B), then the social utility function has to be a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, up to an affine transformation. One of them thinks this is basically the engine of utilitarianism: the only remaining step is the impartial-observer argument (you choose behind a veil of ignorance with an equal chance of being anyone) to force equal weights, and then you get average utilitarianism. The other thinks the theorem is doing almost no moral work: it's just a representation theorem, and all the controversial stuff is smuggled into the assumption that there is a single 'social preference' ordering satisfying the expected-utility axioms in the first place.

I suspect one crux is whether the expected-utility axioms are normatively binding on a social ordering, and another is how much independent moral work the impartial-observer step is doing.
