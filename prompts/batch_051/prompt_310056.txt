I am a PhD student working on sacrificial ritual, and I am trying to assemble a defensible theoretical core on ritual substitution: how guilt, impurity, or anticipated misfortune comes to be transferred to a victim, effigy, or object that is then destroyed or sent away. Before I build a dissertation chapter around the following seven commitments, I want an audit of whether they jointly hold up. Please treat the list as a package I actually hold, not as a set of abstract theses. For each place where the package comes apart, rank the tension by severity, identify the least costly revision, and say what that revision would force me to give up. Where two commitments only appear to conflict, say why the apparent tension dissolves.

1. In its central case, sacrifice is best explained as a mechanism for redirecting intragroup violence onto a substitutable victim, and its substitutionary logic is not usually transparent to the participants. I take this to be the durable core of the broadly Girardian scapegoat position.

2. At the same time, a large body of rites is explicitly substitutionary in its own stated rationale: a gesture or formula passes sin, impurity, or doom onto a victim or object, which is then said to bear it or carry it away. I count these among the central data, not as a degenerate or merely rhetorical form.

3. Substitution has a double structure. The vehicle must be identified with the party whose burden it assumes, or it could not carry that burden; and it must be kept separate from them, or its removal would be the community's own loss. Both moments belong to the concept.

4. The vehicle need not be alive. Effigies, cloth bundles, threads, and comparable objects can be genuine substitutes, provided that the transfer is enacted in the rite.

5. For a rite to count as substitution, the vehicle must actually be disposed of: killed, burned, expelled, sunk, buried at a boundary, or otherwise removed from the community's space. A transfer that is only announced, without the disposal of any concrete vehicle, does not complete the substitution.

6. Saying that a burden has been transferred is not a claim about physical quantities or quasi-material stuff. It is a claim about ritual status: what changes is the community's standing before the relevant powers or norms. The transfer is real in that institutional sense, not in a pseudo-physical sense.

7. I reject the deflationary view that substitution rests on a primitive cognitive error, as if the offerer confused an effigy with a person or believed a goat had literally swallowed the misdeeds. Substitution is an institutional fact, not a mistake about identity or causation.

My sense is that some of these commitments pull against one another, but I am too close to the material to tell which tensions are serious and which are only apparent. Please audit the set: identify the pairs that genuinely conflict, order them from most to least severe, and for each specify the cheapest repair and what it costs. Where a pair only appears to conflict, say briefly why the tension dissolves. I am not looking for a bibliography or for an alternative theory; I am looking for an internal consistency check, the way one would audit an argument before showing it to an advisor.
