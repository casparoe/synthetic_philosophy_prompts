I keep encountering a pattern in all-things-considered comparisons, and I would like to know whether it has a standard name and a settled diagnosis. Consider a long sequence of possible lives. The first life is very good. Each successive life differs from its predecessor only in a barely noticeable respect: the person’s discomfort is increased by an amount far too small for anyone to say, of any adjacent pair, that one life is plainly better than the other — though the two are not exactly alike, either. Yet after many steps the final life is one of constant agony, and everyone agrees that the first life is better than the final one. The same shape appears with policy outcomes: each step worsens a group’s situation by a margin too small to register as a change for the worse, while the first outcome is obviously much better for that group than the last. In both cases, a clear difference at the ends of the series is assembled from steps that individually seem to make no difference. Does this pattern have an established name when the relation involved is betterness? What is the usual diagnosis: that “better than” does not carry across such a chain of small comparisons, that at least one of the small-step judgments must be rejected, or that an unnoticed threshold separates the steps? And how exactly is this supposed to differ from the familiar puzzle about heaps of sand and baldness, where the standard move is to blame vagueness in the words rather than the behavior of a comparative relation? A bare name would not answer my question; I am after the precise way the diagnosis works and where the boundaries of the concept lie.
