I work in research administration at a university with both a human-subjects review board and an animal care and use committee, and a discrepancy between the two tracks has begun to bother me. When a protocol uses human subjects, the review board's decisive question is whether participation is voluntary and informed; no matter how large the expected benefit to future patients, the board would not approve a study that harms an unconsenting person, and the benefit is not treated as a reason that can outweigh the lack of consent. When a protocol uses macaques, the animal committee's decisive question is whether the suffering has been minimized and whether the expected benefit to humans outweighs the remaining suffering; the question of consent never arises. What view of moral status, and of the difference between persons and other animals, does this two-track practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, separating what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and note where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival theories would all license its structure. Then assess whether the two tracks are coherent when held together, and say what would have to change in the oversight system if one track were generalized to the other: for instance, if the side-constraint logic now applied to humans were extended to the macaques.
