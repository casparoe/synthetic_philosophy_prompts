I was rereading Sellars's Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind and trying to turn the part where he distinguishes sensing from believing into an argument. Hes says, if I understand him, that a non-conceptual sensory state can cause a belief but cannot be a reason for it, while a conceptual state can be a reason but is already the kind of thing that can be asked to justify itself, so it is not the sort of pre-epistemic given empiricists wanted. My study group pushed this into an argument that seems to prove too much:

(1) Any justified empirical belief is justified either inferentially or non-inferentially.
(2) Non-inferential justification must bottom out in something that is not itself a propositionally structured belief.
(3) If that bottom is non-conceptual, it can cause belief but cannot stand in rational relations to belief.
(4) If that bottom is conceptual, it is itself the kind of thing that can be justified or unjustified, so it cannot confer non-inferential justification without already needing justification.
(5) Therefore no empirical belief is non-inferentially justified.
(6) But almost all ordinary empirical beliefs are justified, and only a small minority are justified by explicit inference.
(7) So Sellars's critique of the given proves that ordinary empirical knowledge is impossible, which is absurd.

I don't want to give up (6), and (3) and (4) feel like the core of his point. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Give me exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
