I sat through a sentencing hearing last month and realized I have strong opinions about punishment but no idea what ties them together. I wrote down my verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them, or close — and if not, which of my verdicts I'd have to give up.

Here's the list.

A man plans his business partner's murder for weeks and carries it out. Long prison, no hesitation.

Another man, in a bar fight he didn't start, goes to his truck, comes back with a tire iron, and beats a man to death. Punish him, but far less than the planner. I can't say exactly why the tire iron matters so much to how I feel.

Two men, separately, plan murders with the same coldness. One's gun jams; the other kills. I think the one who killed should get more time. I know the only difference is luck, but I can't shake it.

A woman embezzles $60k from her employer, is caught, pays it all back, and is genuinely and demonstrably remorseful. Even if I were certain she'd never reoffend, punishing her not at all feels wrong. But I'd punish her much less than a smug embezzler who took the same amount.

A 91-year-old former concentration camp guard is found; he's healthy, has lived quietly for 60 years, and is no threat to anyone. I think he should be prosecuted and punished. The quiet decades doesn't cancel what he did.

A man who killed someone at 22 is caught at 75 with advanced dementia and no memory of the crime. I don't see the point of punishing him. It feels wrong, not just pointless.

A city is about to riot unless someone is punished for a notorious unsolved murder. The police could frame an innocent man, and that would stop the violence for sure. Obviously not.

A company dumps chemicals for years, thirty people die, and the company pays a fine. No individual goes to jail. I think the fine isn't enough; someone who okayed the dumping should go to prison. But I can't tell whether that's because the company fine isn't really punishment or because the executive deserves it.

A 19-year-old steals a car for a joyride and breaks a pedestrian's leg. I want him punished, but I'd be upset if the sentence derailed his whole life.

Is there one principle that captures all of these, or close? If not, which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does the principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: two people commit the same theft; one turns himself in and confesses the next day, the other hides and is caught a year later. Should the one who turned himself in be punished less, and why?
