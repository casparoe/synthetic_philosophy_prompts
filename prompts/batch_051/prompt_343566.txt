I have been thinking, without any formal training, about an old dispute: whether God can suffer. On one side are people who hold that God, being perfect and unchangeable, cannot be acted on from outside and therefore cannot suffer. On the other side are people who hold that a God who cannot suffer cannot love in the fullest sense and cannot be the God of the cross. The more I work through this, the more it seems that the two sides partly mean different things by words such as suffer, emotion, change, and love, but that beneath the words there is also a real disagreement. I would like a text that separates the two.

Write the joint statement that a committed defender of divine impassibility and a committed defender of divine passibility could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I understand divine impassibility as the view that God has no passions, cannot be causally acted upon by anything outside himself, and cannot suffer. I understand divine passibility as the view that God can be genuinely affected by creatures and can undergo sorrow or suffering while remaining God. Keep the two positions distinct and do not water either of them down.

The deliverable should have two parts.

Part one is a numbered section of agreed claims. These must be substantive claims about God, love, the cross, or the limits of each position, and every claim must be one that each side can sincerely sign. Agreed claims must not be tautologies or statements about how the discussion was conducted. The section should at least cover: what each side is not claiming about the other, and what each side concedes is true in the other's view; what the suffering of Christ establishes and what it does not establish for the two positions; and a precise statement of the sense in which both sides can say that God loves human beings.

Part two is a disagreements table with these columns: Crux; Impassibilist formulation; Passibilist formulation; Case or argument both accept as bearing; Impassibilist prediction; Passibilist prediction. The two formulations in each row must be non-equivalent, and each must be phrased so that its own side would sign the wording. The table should cover at least the following cruxes: (1) whether suffering necessarily involves being acted on from outside, and whether a perfectly self-possessed agent can voluntarily undergo something that still counts as suffering; (2) whether the capacity to be inwardly grieved by another's condition belongs to perfect love; (3) whether a perfectly blessed being can experience sorrow and remain perfectly blessed; (4) whether the suffering of Jesus is something that happens to God, and if so in what sense, and in particular whether it commits one to saying that the divine nature itself can suffer or only that the incarnate Son suffers in and through his human nature; and (5) whether God's exhaustive knowledge of human suffering includes the felt quality of that suffering, and whether possessing that felt quality would amount to suffering. For each row, the case or argument in the fourth column should be one the two sides agree bears on the crux; it may be a scriptural scene described without chapter-and-verse references, a short constructed scenario, or a conceptual argument. In the two prediction columns, record each side's prediction about the upshot of that case, and add one sentence saying what upshot would move that side. If a crux dissolves once its terms are disambiguated, say so explicitly and move it out of the disagreement table. Do not use footnotes, bibliographies, or citations.
