Consider an asymmetry in how people judge satisficing. A shopper who decides in advance that any pasta sauce without high-fructose corn syrup under six dollars will do, buys the first jar that clears the bar, and never scans the rest of the shelf is usually called sensible, not lazy. A hiring committee that decides in advance that any applicant meeting the posted minimums will do, interviews only until one candidate clears the bar, and never reads the remaining files is usually called reckless, even negligent. The decision procedure looks structurally identical—set an aspiration level and stop at the first satisfactory option—so the asymmetry must be coming from something else: the stakes, the irreversibility, the value of due deliberation, the committee's duties to others, or the difference between spending one's own time and spending an institution's. Which of these, if any, gives a principled rather than post hoc justification for the asymmetry? Defend the harder side: either justify the asymmetry in a matched pair of cases with equal stakes, or argue that the shopper and the committee should be judged by the same standard.
