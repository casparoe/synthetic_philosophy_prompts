I coordinate admissions for a small but selective master's program in urban planning, and every cycle my team — three part-time readers and me — works through several hundred applications in about six weeks, with each reader spending maybe ten minutes on a resume before the committee sees it. We've been burned repeatedly by expensive-looking line items that turn out to mostly reflect family resources: a summer "field placement" abroad arranged by an uncle, a certificate from a prestigious-sounding institute that basically sells seats, an unpaid internship at a big-name NGO that the applicant could afford because their parents paid rent. I want to stop treating all costly-looking things as equally informative, but I also don't want to just throw them all out.

I know a bit of costly signaling theory and the handicap principle — the idea being that a display is only reliable when the cost falls harder on the people who lack the underlying quality, and that some expensive things are just indicators of wealth rather than ability or commitment — but I can't turn that into a routine my readers can follow. Please write a decision procedure for evaluating a single resume line: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and labels something a weak signal or not a signal at all, and where it hands the line to the full committee because the call is a value judgment rather than a fact. It should keep distinct (a) something close to an index that can't be faked, (b) a costly signal that separates because low-quality applicants would pay more for it, and (c) a pure handicap that's mostly just expensive, and say what to do when we can't tell appart which kind something is. Then run the procedure on three actual-looking cases: a year of unpaid work at a prestigious planning consultancy; a paid night-shift warehouse job that financed a part-time GIS certificate at a community college; and a family-funded "independent research project" with a polished website. I suspect the procedure will tell me some things I currently like are weak signals and some things I currently discount are strong ones — don't soften that.
