Still chewing on something from a seminar. Somebody was trying to make split-brain cases less threatening to the unity of consciousness, and they reached for a company analogy: the mind is a firm, with language, memory, vision, and motor control as different departments that normally coordinate well enough to speak to the outside world in one voice. A split-brain patient, on their telling, is just a firm where the two main divisions got cut off from each other, so now two voices emerge. The analogy was doing real work—it was meant to support the conclusion that the felt unity of being a single I doesn't prove there's one owner of experience; personhood would be more like corporate personhood, a useful construction. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mind who actually knows the split-brain literature, and audit that analogy hard. Start by brainstorming a mapping between the company and the mind (who's the CEO, who are the employees, what's a memo, what's a merger, where does the confabulating left hemisphere fit—is it just the PR department?). Then go through where the analogy breaks. What does it quietly import from corporate life—a CEO, a legal identity, employees who are themselves conscious, a profit motive—that the brain doesn't obviously have? Which of those disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy was supposed to carry, and which are just accidents? Does the analogy sneak in an inner manager even while it's arguing there is none? And after that audit, does the conclusion survive, or is there some better analogy for what split-brain cases actually show about the unity of consciousness?
