I spend too much time on rationalist forums, and a version of Lewis Carroll's tortoise keeps coming up in threads about whether a reasoning system can ever be justified in trusting its own rules of inference. I have regimented the worry into an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the failure. It runs like this. (1) A deductive inference is justified only if the reasoner is justified in accepting the rule of inference it employs, as applied to the case at hand. (2) A rule like modus ponens is not the kind of thing that can be justified by direct observation; one never simply sees that a rule of inference is truth-preserving. (3) It cannot be justified by its track record either, because drawing any conclusion from that record already presupposes some rule of inference whose own justification would then be in question. (4) The only remaining way to justify a rule of inference is to derive it from some more basic principle, but by (1) such a derivation is justified only if one already justifiedly accepts the more basic rule it employs; and by parity of reasoning that rule must itself be justified by a further derivation, and so on. (5) No finite reasoner can complete an infinite regress of justifications. Therefore, (6), no finite reasoner is ever justified in making any deductive inference. I cannot accept the conclusion, since it would undercut this very argument along with all of mathematics, but I also cannot see which step is false. Please walk me through the argument and tell me which premise or step I should reject, why that step looked so plausible, and what the main candidate positions have to give up to reject it. I am most reluctant to give up the idea that an inference must be licensed by a rule, and equally reluctant to treat rules of inference as ordinary observable facts. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
