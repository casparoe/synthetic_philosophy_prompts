Last night I stayed up arguing with a close friend about moral luck, and I have come away convinced that my own position is far less stable than I thought. We had both recently read the standard philosophical treatments, and my friend spent the evening defending a single austere principle: a person can be morally assessed only for what lay within their control, so how things happen to turn out — and what opportunities their circumstances happened to afford them — should make no difference to blame or punishment. I kept agreeing with him in obviously parallel cases and then catching myself wanting to treat other parallel cases as different, and I now want to know whether there is a principled position behind my reactions or whether some of my reactions have to go.

Here are my gut verdicts, pinned down as honestly as I can. In the standard resultant-luck cases I side with the control principle: the would-be assassin whose gun jams is every bit as blameworthy as the one whose bullet lands, and the drunk driver who gets home safely is no worse or better a person than the one who hits a pedestrian who happened to step out at the wrong instant. The same goes for two parents who each lose track of their small child at the pool for the same length of time, and in only one family the child drowns. Yet my confidence collapses outside these cases. Two nurses on different wards each misread the same chart and administer the same wrong dose; one patient happens to be allergic and dies, while the other recovers without incident. If I am honest, I want the first nurse punished far more severely — losing her license and standing before the family's grief — while I regard the second nurse's identical error as a matter for retraining. The same happens with two executives who approve the same under-tested product, only one of whose batches happens to fail. And on the circumstantial side, I judge the man who served in a genocidal militia vastly more harshly than someone with the same cruel temperament who happened to live in a quiet village and never had the chance to act on it, even though the control principle tells me they must be equals.

My friend says the cases in which outcome sways me are simply mistakes: the nurses and the executives are equally culpable, and my extra anger toward the unlucky ones is either an irrational effect of salience and self-interest or, at most, a legitimate but non-moral demand for deterrence. I am not sure he is right, because the asymmetry presents itself to me as a moral one and not merely a practical one. I would like someone to lay out the candidate principles that could underlie verdicts like these, showing which of my verdicts each principle would preserve and which it would force me to overturn, and to identify the simplest principle that does the least violence to the whole set. I would then want that principle tested on the case I cannot stop turning over: a paramedic who, against protocol but out of compassion, crawls into an unstable wreck to hold a trapped driver's hand; in one version the added movement collapses the vehicle and kills the driver, and in the other the vehicle holds and the driver survives. Does the paramedic deserve more blame in the first version, and what would that verdict reveal about whether outcome really matters?
