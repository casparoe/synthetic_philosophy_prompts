For a reading group on wisdom, I've been asked to write down my philosophical profile so the others can poke holes in it. Here it is, more or less as I'd defend it:

I think wisdom is a kind of expertise about living well, but not propositional expertise — its know-how, closer to a craft than to a body of theory.
I think it's teachable, but only the way crafts are: through exemplars, practice, and feedback, not through lectures or books.
I think wisdom is domain-general: someone who's wise about career decisions tends to be wise about friendships, money, and family too, even without any special knowledge in those areas.
I think wisdom is a fairly stable trait — some people are just wiser than others, across the board.
But I also think situations matter a lot: the same person can be wise at 10am and foolish at 2am, or wise when calm and foolish when scared.
I think wisdom requires epistemic humility — knowing what you don't know and being quick to revise.
I think it also requires decisiveness — the wise don't dither; they can act and give advice under uncertainty.
I think novices can sometimes show more wisdom in a domain than the seasoned expert, because the expert's knowledge can make them overconfident and rigid.
I think wisdom is partly a matter of the heart, not just the head: it involves caring about the right things, not merely reasoning well.
I think there's no necessary tie between wisdom and age: some old people are fools and some young people aren't.
And I do think wisdom is still a goal worth aiming at, even if I can't say what it would look like to have arrived.

Go through these and tell me which commitments pull against each other, which pairs only look like they're in tension, and how bad each real tension is. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would be. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I genuinely don't know where the problem is, so don't just tell me it all hangs together.
