Like a lot of people on here, I've been working through population ethics again, and I want to stress-test one variation of the Repugnant Conclusion. In the stock case, a population A of ten billion people, all with very high welfare, is compared with a much larger population Z, everyone in which has a life barely worth living—worth living, but only just. A total-welfare view says Z is better, because sufficiently many small positive values outweigh the smaller number of large positive values.

Now change the choice from two certain outcomes to a lottery. Suppose the alternative to A is option L: with probability 0.999 it produces Z, and with probability 0.001 it produces Z-minus, the same large population and the same resources, but arranged so that every life falls just below the neutral level, where a life is neither worth living nor worse than nonexistence. Z can be taken large enough that the expected total welfare of L is still far higher than A's total welfare, because the high-probability Z branch does nearly all the work; yet there remains a one-in-a-thousand chance of creating billions of lives each worse than never being lived.

Do the calculation properly. Is the total view still committed to preferring L to A? Do the standard replies to the Repugnant Conclusion—average-welfare views, critical-level views, lexical threshold views, and person-affecting or prior-existence views—behave differently now that the bad outcome is probabilistic rather than certain? I would like a committed answer: which view, if any, both escapes the original Repugnant Conclusion and gives defensible advice about L, and what does adopting that view require abandoning?
