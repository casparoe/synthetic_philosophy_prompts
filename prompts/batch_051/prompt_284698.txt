For a philosophy of mind reading group I've been asked to write down my commitments on the Lucas–Penrose argument so the others can pressure-test them. Before I circulate the statement, I'd like an audit of whether the following package hangs together.

(1) Peano arithmetic is consistent, and I can see that it is.
(2) For any sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatizable formal system, if it is consistent, then it cannot prove its own consistency (Gödel's second incompleteness theorem).
(3) Taking (1)–(2) together, I conclude that for any formal system F proposed as a formalization of my arithmetical competence, I can in principle recognize F's consistency statement as true while F cannot prove it; therefore the human mind is not such a formal system.
(4) I am a physicalist: the mind is an evolved biological machine whose mental capacities are exhausted by finite neural processes.
(5) I accept the physical Church–Turing thesis, so those finite neural processes are in principle simulable by a Turing machine.
(6) At the same time, I doubt that any empirical proposal to identify the brain with a particular formal system can ever be adequately warranted, since behavioral evidence radically underdetermines which formal system the brain implements.
(7) I also think that recognizing the consistency of a formal system need not itself be a non-algorithmic insight, since one algorithm can prove the consistency of another.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what the least costly revision would be. Rank the actual problems by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad; I genuinely do not know which commitment has to go.
