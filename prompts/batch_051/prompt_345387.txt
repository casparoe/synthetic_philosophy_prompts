I have spent the past year moderating a running dispute between two people who cannot agree on what would even count as settling the question of whether an AI could want anything in the absence of an evolutionary history. One side holds a genealogical view of motivation: a state earns the label 'want' only through a history of selection, paradigmatically natural selection, and in an artefact the closest we get is selection over training runs or design lineages; without such a history a machine has objectives, targets, and reward signals, but nothing it genuinely wants. The other side holds a structural view: wanting is a current causal-functional kind—roughly, a state that integrates perceptual information, action selection, and stored expected outcomes in the right way and is robust under perturbation—and genealogy is causally and conceptually irrelevant. They have agreed that another exchange of arguments would do nothing, and that the useful task is to produce a document both could sign stating exactly what they share, where they part ways, and what would move them.

Write that adversarial-collaboration document. You may invent any further detail you need about their views, provided the two positions remain as defined. Do not adjudicate the dispute; the deliverable is the joint text, not a verdict. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed claims should cover at least the distinction between a training objective and a terminal goal, the possibility of highly competent goal-directed behaviour with no motivation in the contested sense, and why surface behaviour alone underdetermines the attribution of wanting. The disagreements table should state, for each side, the precise formulation of its own position that the other side would accept as a fair characterisation. Then identify the cruxes—sub-questions a settled answer to which would shift at least one position—and give at least two: I expect one to be whether the selective history is doing any present causal work or merely licenses a norm, and another whether the structural account can specify 'the right functional role' without quietly importing history. For each crux, specify a concrete case—a trained system, an animal, or a computational intervention—that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Finish by saying which parts of the dispute, if any, are purely verbal and how they might be dissolved without settling the substantive remainder.
