I'm in a political theory seminar slogging through John Locke's Second Treatise of Government, and a passage from the chapter on prerogative (chapter XIV, section 160) has been eating at me:

"This power to act according to discretion, for the publick good, without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it, is that which is called prerogative: for since in some governments the lawmaking power is not always in being, and is usually too numerous, and so too slow, for the dispatch requisite to execution; and because also it is impossible to foresee, and so by laws to provide for, all accidents and necessities that may concern the public, or to make such laws as will do no harm, if they are executed with an inflexible rigour, on all occasions, and upon all persons that may come in their way; therefore there is a latitude left to the executive power, to do many things of choice which the laws do not prescribe."

In class we used it to talk about 17th-century kings suspending penal statutes, which is fine as far as it goes, but I want to stress-test it on a case he obviously didn't have in mind. Imagine a country where the legislature is not dissolved or incapacitated—it can meet, but it's deeply split and keeps punting. The executive, with no statutory authority and against the literal wording of existing free-competition and data-protection laws, orders a six-month halt to training AI models above a certain compute threshold, insists the risk of racing ahead is urgent and irreversible, and sends officials to shut down two labs that refuse to comply. The executive's people say this is just Lockean prerogative: acting for the publick good outside or even against the law until the legislature catches up.

Under the passage's own standard, is this prerogative? What exactly has to be true for the "publick good" condition to be satisfied, and can it do the sorting work when half the country thinks the moratorium is itself the public danger? Locke's rationale says the lawmaking power is too slow and can't foresee every accident—but does that cover a legislature that isn't slow so much as unwilling to decide? And if the passage does justify it, has prerogative just become a name for any executive action whose defenders can articulate an urgent-sounding rationale? I'm trying to figure out wheter there's any edge left to the concept.
