I have been trying to work out what Kant's universalizability test actually commits me to, case by case, and I would like you to reverse-engineer the principle that best fits my verdicts, test my provisional principle against them, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts would have to be revised for the simplest adequate principle. I am not asking for a survey of Kant scholarship; I want the principle made precise and held against the cases.

My verdicts are these.

1. The false promise. A person in serious need borrows money and promises to repay it, with no intention of doing so. I think this is wrong, and I take the test to diagnose that correctly: a world in which everyone in need made such promises could not contain the practice of promising that the act itself depends on.

2. Suicide from self-love. A person, worn down by a life that promises more pain than satisfaction, ends that life on the same principle that ordinarily leads us to seek our own well-being. I think this is wrong, and my grounds feel parallel: a natural order in which the very drive toward one's own well-being systematically produced its own destruction is not fully thinkable as a law-governed order.

3. Neglecting all one's talents. A person resolves to devote himself wholly to idle enjoyment and never to develop any of his capacities. I think this is wrong, but for a different reason. I can conceive a world of idlers without contradiction; what I cannot do is will it, because as a rational being I will that my own capacities be developed so that I can pursue any ends at all.

4. Refusing all aid. A person in comfortable circumstances resolves never to help anyone in need. I think this too is wrong, and again of the second kind: I can conceive the universal condition, but I cannot will a world in which no one would ever help me when I need it.

5. The prudent shopkeeper. A merchant prices his goods honestly toward inexperienced buyers solely because it is best for repeat business. I think the action is permissible and passes the test, though it lacks moral worth.

6. The price-gouger. After a disaster, a seller charges desperate buyers the highest price they will pay for bottled water. I think this is wrong. Yet the maxim, stated plainly, seems to pass my provisional principle: I can conceive a world in which everyone in a position of urgent scarcity charges whatever the desperate will pay, and I can will such a world without obvious contradiction. A principle that permits price gouging strikes me as too permissive, but I cannot see where the description of the maxim or the test is supposed to go to close the gap.

7. The protective lie. A person hides a refugee in her home, and when armed men ask whether the refugee is inside, she lies. I think lying here is permissible, perhaps even required. My provisional principle makes me uneasy, though. If the maxim is described as telling an untruth when that is the only way to save a life, universalizing it seems open to me; if the maxim must instead be described simply as lying, or as lying to people who are asking questions, the test starts looking rigoristic, and I do not know which description of the maxim it is entitled to use.

My provisional principle is this: an act is impermissible just in case its maxim, willed as a universal law for everyone, either cannot be conceived as a law-governed world without contradiction, or can be conceived but cannot be willed by a rational being with ordinary finite ends; failures of the first kind are stricter than failures of the second. I take this to fit the first five verdicts, but I already doubt whether it can handle the sixth and seventh.

Please do the following. First, state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best fits my seven verdicts, making precise the notions of maxim, universalization, contradiction, and willing that are doing the work, and say which verdict motivates which clause. Second, say exactly how that principle handles cases six and seven, and where it would place the following two further cases: (a) a person who repays a loan only because she wants another one from the same lender, and (b) a person who never thanks or shows appreciation to those who help her, on the grounds that gratitude is just an expenditure of effort. Third, tell me which of my seven verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and what the least costly revision of my views would be.
