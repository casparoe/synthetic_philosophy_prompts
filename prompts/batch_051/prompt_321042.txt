Here are seven constructs that recur in debates about legal fictions and the ontology of law: corporate personality, the reasonable person in negligence law, legislative intent, the state as a legal person, constructive notice, the presumption of death after a long unexplained absence, and the doctrine of the lost grant. Suppose the task is to sort them by how much they deserve to be treated as existing features of legal reality, as opposed to useful suppositions that courts deploy without committing themselves to their truth. Please rank them from most real to most fictional, and for each say in a sentence or two why it lands where it does. Then state where you would draw the line between constructs that are real enough to have rights and duties attributed to them directly and constructs that are best understood as shorthand for collections of other rules and policies. Write your answer as continuous prose, in the register of a letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
