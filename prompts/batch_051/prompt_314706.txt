I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and in three weeks I will present a reported essay to the monthly seminar of a university planning school. The audience is mostly land-use economists and planning scholars, with two regular guests: a human geographer who researches displacement and gentrification, and a political theorist who defends strong local self-government. The economists care about whether a claim can be operationalized and about measurable incidence rather than intention; the geographer cares about who is displaced and by whom, and will be suspicious of any argument that seems to treat future residents as more real than current vulnerable ones; the political theorist cares about whether people may govern the place where they live.

My thesis is this. Zoning disputes are standardly framed as a conflict between current residents and developers, with the community assumed to be the people who already live in the area. I argue that this framing, not any particular policy, is the underlying problem. I propose a counterfactual-residency standard for identifying the affected parties to a land-use decision: the relevant constituency includes everyone who would live or work in the area under the proposed rule as well as everyone who would under the status quo. Because restrictive rules typically exclude a large would-be constituency from the decision procedure while its interests are systematically ignored, the standard yields a defeasible presumption in favor of the more inclusive rule. My argument has two steps. First, both democratic and welfare-based justifications of local control presuppose that affectedness, not current residency, confers standing; current residency is only a rough proxy, and a bad one once the rule itself determines who gets to become a resident. Second, the familiar objections to counting counterfactual residents — they are hard to identify, their preferences are unknowable, their interests can be captured by developers — are difficulties of implementation rather than objections of principle, and we already make affectedness judgments about people who do not yet live in a place in ordinary planning for future growth.

Draft my question-and-answer preparation sheet for this particular audience. I want the objections I will actually get, not the objections a generic newspaper comment section would raise, and I want them in roughly the order they are likely to come, while flagging how damaging each one is and phrased in the vocabulary these economists, geographers, and the political theorist would use. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front and what I should hold, which point in my argument is weakest and that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
