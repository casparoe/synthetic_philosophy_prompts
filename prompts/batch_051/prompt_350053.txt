Next Thursday I'm giving a twenty-minute talk to my high school's philosophy and debate club defending the view that hypocrisy does not remove a person's standing to blame. There will be twenty minutes of questions afterward, and I want to walk in with a prep sheet rather than improvise.

My thesis is this: a person can be a hypocrite about a norm and still blame someone else for violating that norm with full standing. What hypocrisy actually undermines is something adjacent — the blamer's entitlement to demand that the wrongdoer listen, change, or answer to them specifically, and the usefulness of their blame to onlookers. I distinguish three things: the aptness of the blame (whether the charge is accurate and the response proportionate), the standing to issue it (whether the blamer is positioned to hold the person accountable at all), and uptake (whether the wrongdoer or bystanders are warranted in treating the blamer as worth listening to on this norm). My argument sketch: facts about the blamer — being a cheat, a liar, a coward — are facts about the blamer, not about the truth of the charge. The wrongdoing is the blamed person's; blame is a fitting response to it, and its fittingness is fixed by features of the wrong and the response, not by the virtue of the speaker. Hypocrisy, I'll say, is a real moral fault, but it is a fault of self-exemption and inconsistency, and the proper response to it is a separate criticism of the blamer rather than a verdict that the blame itself misfires.

The audience is about fifteen students, mostly juniors and seniors who compete in Ethics Bowl and Lincoln-Douglas debate, plus our faculty advisor, who teaches an elective on virtue ethics and keeps returning to the idea that a person's life should be all of a piece. They don't know the academic literature on blame. They are, though, extremely comfortable with character-first moral language, with consistency and authenticity treated as verdicts, and with the move "that's rich, coming from you" treated as a decisive objection. A few of them will have just finished a unit on Kant and will reach for universalization; the advisor is likely to frame things in terms of the unity of the virtues and whether a vicious speaker can perform the speech act of holding someone accountable at all.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the six to eight objections I'll actually get, ordered roughly by likelihood and with the genuinely dangerous ones flagged separately, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should refuse to concede, and what the weakest point in my own position is that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. And include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, with the beginning of an answer in case they do.
