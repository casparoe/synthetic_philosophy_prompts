Before I read any more arguments about whether signing up for cryonics is a rational bet, I want the spec the whole debate quietly depends on. Write out what an adequate theory of surviving cryonic revival has to get right: the cases it must handle (revival from a vitrified brain, destructive upload where the scan is used to rebuild a copy, revival into multiple copies at once, gradual replacement of neurons before death, and the patient who comes back missing the last five years of memory), the distinctions it can't collapse (identity vs. psychological continuity vs. caring about some future person; all-or-nothing survival vs. partial or graded survival; legal vs. clinical vs. information-theoretic death, where the last means no recoverable trace of memory and personality remains), and the physical facts it can't wish away (destructive scanning destroys the original, information degrades after cardiac arrest, vitrification may not preserve every synaptic detail). Give the thought experiment or fact behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference. Then flag the pair of requirements you suspect cant be jointly satisfied, and give a quick verdict on how a naive memory-continuity view, a Parfit-style psychological-continuity view, and an information-theoretic criterion fare against the list — keep that last part short, I mainly want the list.
