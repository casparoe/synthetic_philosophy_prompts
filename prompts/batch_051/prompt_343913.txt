I am a retired physical chemist who has spent the last few years working through the philosophy of science and, more recently, the philosophy of rational choice. I would like you to act as an expert in game theory and interactive epistemology, begin with a short brainstorm of possible mechanisms, and then give me a carefully worked-out design. Please write the final answer in American English.

The setting is an interdisciplinary reading group of fifteen — mostly working scientists, several philosophers, and a handful of graduate students — and we have ninety minutes. For the next session I want a structured classroom game on the backward-induction paradox and the centipede game. The standard centipede is a two-player game in which a pot grows each time play moves forward; the two players alternate deciding whether to stop and take the larger share or pass and let the pot grow, and backward induction over a commonly known final move says the first player should stop immediately. I do not want another demonstration that actual people fail to do what the theory says. What I want the participants to come away feeling — and only have named later, in the debrief — is that the whole conclusion depends on a fragile ingredient: the commonly known last move. To make that felt, I propose running two treatments back to back. The first is the textbook finite centipede with a known endpoint. The second is a stochastic-horizon variant: after every pass, the facilitator draws from a deck that either terminates the tree or lets it continue, with the probability known to everyone but each outcome revealed only when drawn. The same move should change its rational status between the two treatments, and I want the room to sit with that.

Your job is to turn this into a complete, runnable activity. Please provide: (a) exact rules for both treatments, including how players are paired, how moves are recorded, and how decisions are made; (b) a small point-based payoff scheme with no real money, though a modest prize is fine; (c) a materials list — cards, deck, score sheets, a visible payoff schedule, or anything else you choose; (d) a timeline for the ninety minutes, including practice rounds, re-pairing between treatments, and at least twenty-five minutes of debrief; (e) a debrief plan that maps what happened in each treatment onto the distinction between a finite commonly-known horizon and an open-ended one, and presses participants on why passing seemed reasonable in the second treatment even though the backward-induction argument told them not to pass in the first; and (f) a paragraph developing the precise lesson. Please make sure the activity does not overclaim: the honest point is not that the stochastic-horizon variant makes continuing uniquely rational, but that the unravelling argument cannot get started without a commonly known last node, so continuing becomes something a rational player can seriously defend under reasonable beliefs about the other player and about the continuation probability. The facilitator will need to supply that precision in the debrief.

The idea should show up in what happens, not in what the facilitator says afterwards: no lecture up front, and the term "backward induction" should not be used until the debrief. Please also tell me two or three failure modes I should anticipate the first time I run this and how to recover from each, and be candid about the one feature of the philosophical point that the activity cannot reproduce because the participants are real people in a classroom rather than the idealized agents the argument is about. I want the final design to read as something I could put into practice on the first run, with rules and timings explicit, rather than as a general pedagogical essay.
