Its been bugging me since a friend chalked this out, because each move feels obvious but the conclusion can't be right, and I can't find where the trick is.

(1) The Son has a divine nature.
(2) At the Incarnation, the Son also took on a human nature, without ceasing to have the divine one.
(3) If a subject has a nature, and a property is part of that nature, then the subject has that property.
(4) Part of the divine nature is being uncreated, being unable to suffer, knowing all things, and not having a body.
(5) Part of the human nature is being created, being able to suffer, having limited knowledge, and having a body.
(6) So the Son is uncreated, unable to suffer, all-knowing, and bodiless (from 1, 3, and 4).
(7) So the Son is created, able to suffer, limited in knowledge, and embodied (from 2, 3, and 5).
(8) But no single subject can be both created and uncreated, both able to suffer and unable to suffer, both all-knowing and limited in knowledge, both embodied and bodiless.
(9) Therefore the doctrine that one person has two natures is incoherent.

The conclusion feels too strong — it would make the whole two-natures tradition a simple contradiction that centuries of smart people somehow missed. I most want to keep the full humanity and the full divinity, but I don't know which earlier step I can afford to give up to do that. Which step do you reject, why did that step look so unshakeable, and what does rejecting it cost?
