I'm a PhD student in philosophy of statistics, and I've been trying to get clear on what the Bayesian convergence results actually show about priors, because people in my department cite them whenever someone objects that a prior is arbitrary. I wrote out the argument as I understand it. Can you tell me whether it's valid, and if not, exactly which step is doing the sneaky work and what the corrected version of that step would be?

(1) Suppose two agents start with different priors over the same set of hypotheses, but neither prior rules out the truth: both put positive probability on every neighborhood of the parameter value that actually generated the data.

(2) They both conditionalize on the same infinite sequence of observations.

(3) The standard merging-of-opinions results (Blackwell–Dubins and the literature after it) say that if one agent's predictive distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to the other's, then their posterior distributions merge: the largest gap between their posterior probabilities, over any event, goes to zero almost surely as the data accumulate.

(4) With i.i.d. data and a reasonably regular finite-dimensional likelihood, two priors that are both full support over the parameter space satisfy this absolute-continuity condition, so the theorem applies.

(5) Therefore, after enough shared data, any two such rational agents will have effectively identical credences about every hypothesis in the space.

(6) So if we observe two careful Bayesians still disagreeing sharply after a very large shared dataset, at least one of them must have either stopped conditionalizing properly or had a dogmatic prior that assigned zero probability to the truth.

(7) Since both of those failures are irrational (or at least clearly bad), persistent rational disagreement is impossible: the data wash out even wildly different priors.

I find each step individually pretty plausible, but the conclusion feels like it proves to much — it would make a lot of real scientific disagreement look like someone is making an obvious mistake, and that seems off. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what does the true version of that premise say? If it is valid, why does the conclusion sound stronger than it is?
