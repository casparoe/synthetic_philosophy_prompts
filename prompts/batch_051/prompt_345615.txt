Take a church that practices infant baptism and really means it: the minister will tell you the water actually does something to the baby, washing off original sin and making the child a member of the church. But in the same church that baby will not be given communion, the other initiation rite, and the kid is still expected to go through confirmation or a public profession of faith years later, after personal instruction. If the baptism already made the infant a Christian, why is the kid not fully a member until then? And if the later personal faith is what does the real work, why baptize the baby at all? What view of what a sacrament is, and of what faith has to involve, could make this arrangement something better than a historical compromise between two clashing views? Maybe theres a real difference between what baptism is supposed to do and what communion is supposed to do, one able to operate on someone with no understanding and the other not—but does that line hold up when you compare it to an adult who gets baptized after a dramatic conversion and then refuses to be confirmed or to receive communion? Give me the strongest justification you can build for the asymmetry, and dont just wave it away as tradition, then say honestly whether the practice should be kept, reversed, or given up. After that, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or counterexample would push you to the opposite verdict, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to break.
