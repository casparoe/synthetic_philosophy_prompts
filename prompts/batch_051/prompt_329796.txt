In game theory and Bayesian epistemology, the common prior assumption says that all differences between agents' beliefs should be traceable to differences in their private information: conditional on sharing the same information, rational agents would assign the same probabilities. The doctrine's rationale is disciplinary — if a modeler may simply stipulate that agents start from different priors, then any disagreement whatever can be accommodated after the fact, and disagreement becomes unfalsifiable, so heterogeneous priors are treated as an illicit degree of freedom. Now consider a new development: large language models are trained on corpora that differ systematically in coverage, emphasis, period, and language, and their settled, probability-like judgments are shaped by those training distributions rather than by any stream of private evidence. Here the prior has a literal, inspectable, non-arbitrary meaning — the training distribution — and it differs across models by design, not as an ad hoc modeling convenience. Does the argument for the common prior assumption survive this development? Identify which piece of the rationale is under pressure: the claim that heterogeneous priors are arbitrary, the picture of agents as Bayesians updating on private signals, or the demand that disagreement be explained by information alone. Then state the strongest revised version of the doctrine that remains defensible — or, if none does, say exactly what work the doctrine was doing and why the development exposes it as vacuous.
