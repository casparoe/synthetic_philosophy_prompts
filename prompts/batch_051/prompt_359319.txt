Question: "A policy is proposed that would slightly raise the well-being of a large number of people who are already very well off, while slightly increasing the already severe suffering of a small number of people who are very badly off. Consider a threshold negative utilitarian, by which I mean someone who holds that severe suffering above a specified threshold has lexical priority over all positive well-being, whereas below that threshold mild suffering and positive well-being may be traded off in roughly the usual utilitarian way. Must such a threshold negative utilitarian and a standard prioritarian reach the same verdict about this policy? Explain your answer by stating what each view is committed to saying here and why."

Student's answer: "Both views must reject the policy, so their verdicts coincide. The threshold negative utilitarian is committed to rejecting it because the policy increases severe suffering, and severe suffering has lexical priority: no amount of well-being gained by the already well off could outweigh even a slight increase in severe suffering. The prioritarian is likewise committed to rejecting it, because prioritarianism gives greater weight to changes in the well-being of the worse off, and here the worse off are made worse off while the already well off are made better off; the weighted loss to the badly off must therefore exceed the weighted gain to the well off. The two views differ only in their foundations: negative utilitarianism is a consequentialist view that treats suffering as the sole intrinsic disvalue and assigns no intrinsic value to positive well-being, whereas prioritarianism is a non-consequentialist, egalitarian view that cares about the distribution of well-being for its own sake. Thus on this policy the disagreement between them is purely theoretical."
