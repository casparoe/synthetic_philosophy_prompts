Consider the following move, common in adaptationist argument. When a trait is complex, costly, and tightly fitted to an apparent function, the default hypothesis should be that natural selection shaped it for that function. The supporting thought is that natural selection is relevantly like a designer: it is the only known process that can accumulate improbable functional organization, so where such organization is found, selection is the default explanation. Write an essay auditing this analogy. Make the correspondence explicit first: what in the evolutionary process is supposed to play the role of the designer's intentions, the blueprint, the performance specifications, the constraints, the iterative revisions, and the finished artifact. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended inference from those that are merely cosmetic. In particular, address whether the spandrels-style point, that some features of organisms are by-products of architectural constraints or correlated developmental effects rather than direct products of selection for their current functions, is a local exception or a deeper problem for the inference. Finally, assess what survives of the adaptationist default after the disanalogies are on the table: should the default be kept as stated, abandoned, or restricted to a narrower class of cases, and what would a better heuristic look like if the analogy was quietly importing more than the evolutionary facts can support?
