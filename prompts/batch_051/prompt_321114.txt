I'm a PhD student in behavioral ecology and I keep running into Morgan's canon, the old rule that you shouldn't explain an animal's behavior by a "higher" psychological process if a "lower" one can account for it. On its face it sounds like plain scientific hygiene, like Occam's razor for minds. But the more I watch it used in practice, the less I understand what it's actually committed to.

For instance, a postdoc in my lab works on detour-reaching in some kind of wrasse, and a reviewer made him redo the whole analysis in associative-learning terms, saying the canon forbids attributing "planning" to a fish. The same month, a colleague down the hall got a paper through review attributing fairly rich causal reasoning to New Caledonian crows, and nobody blinked. And I notice people don't even agree on what counts as "lower." Sometimes it means fewer representational resources, sometimes phylogenetically older, sometimes just "what we'd expect from an animal that dumb." I honestly can't tell wether the canon is a neutral parsimony principle, a piece of Victorian ladder-of-nature baggage, or a useful heuristic that only works because everyone quietly smuggles in extra assumptions about which animals are allowed to be clever.

So my question is: what philosophical commitments does Morgan's canon actually presuppose—about the structure of minds, about what makes one explanation simpler than another, about whether "higher" and "lower" faculties form a real ordering? Does the canon strictly require a hierarchy of faculties, or can it be reformulated without one? And does the uneven way it gets applied in animal cognition research show that researchers are violating the canon, or that the canon on its own was never doing the work people think it is?
