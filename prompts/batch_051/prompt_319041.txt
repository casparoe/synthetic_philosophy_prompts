I am currently taking a module in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I have begun to suspect that a shortcut I use constantly in my own thinking may be quietly unreliable. The shortcut is this: whenever I form expectations about the consequences of a change or an action, I treat everything I do not explicitly mention as unchanged. I do not go through my other beliefs and ask whether the change might bear on them; I update the handful of items already on the table and let the rest stand. Last month, for instance, my flatmate and I were deciding whether to spend the winter break visiting her family, and I reasoned only about the dates, the train fare, and the work I would miss. I took my visa conditions, the renewal date on my tenancy, my course deadlines, and the state of my belongings as unchanged, and I returned to them only when something else forced me to. This felt efficient and mostly worked, but I can see that it is exactly the sort of shortcut that could leave a false assumption standing in the background. What puzzles me is that I never seem to need the endless list of facts that stay the same; the policy works without my enumerating them. Is this policy defensible as a general way of forming expectations about change? Under what conditions does treating everything-not-mentioned as unchanged lead one astray, and can the policy be restated so as to keep its efficiency without its blind spots?
