I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and at a recent consciousness-studies workshop I found myself taking notes on an argument that broke out after a panel. The panel had outlined Dennett's heterophenomenology: the proposal that a science of consciousness should treat subjects' first-person reports as data about how things seem to them, while staying officially neutral about whether the private experiences those reports describe really exist. Two audience members continued the argument in the hallway, and their exchange went roughly like this.

One researcher defended the method: "It is the only way to make consciousness scientifically tractable. You take reports as public utterances and draw no conclusions about the reality of the reported private states. That is what keeps the data third-person."

The other replied: "Then you are not studying consciousness; you are studying stories about consciousness. If a subject reports being in pain and you refuse to accept that the pain exists, your subject matter has changed. That is behaviorism with extra steps."

The first answered: "It is not behaviorism. Behaviorism refuses to reconstruct an inner life at all. Heterophenomenology reconstructs the subject's inner life exactly as she believes it to be. The subject has authority about what she believes, not about what is true. A science that granted more than that would have to treat confabulation, hypnosis, and ordinary misreporting as somehow incorrigible."

The second came back: "But first-person authority was never a claim of infallibility. I can be wrong about whether this wine is corked, but I am not wrong in the same way about what the wine tastes like to me. You are collapsing a privileged epistemic position into an absurd demand for infallibility, then rejecting the privileged position because of the absurdity."

The first responded: "That distinction is exactly what a third-person science cannot begin with. Once you grant that 'what the wine tastes like to me' is a private object, the rest of us can reach it only through your report. Heterophenomenology records the report and brackets the private object until a theory tells us what to make of it."

The second said: "Then the bracketing is not as neutral as advertised. Your eventual theory will predictably treat the private object as something to be eliminated or reduced to third-person facts, so the method has already decided against first-person authority while presenting itself as merely cautious."

I couldn't referee on the spot. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each reply, say which claims hold up, which are mistaken or overstated, and whether the burden of proof shifts. Then say what both sides are missing and which researcher is, on balance, closer to the truth.
