I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I've been roped into setting the final essay question for an upper-level undergrad class called Computation and the Limits of Explanation. The class does a unit on Wolfram-style cellular automata and computational irreducibility, and every year the students can parrot the definition but then write as if "computationally irreducible" just means "we can't explain it." I want a question that seperates the students who understand the distinction between prediction, compression, and explanation from the ones who memorized the slogan. The question should be built around a claim like "if a system is computationally irreducible, the only way to understand it is to watch it unfold, so it resists scientific explanation altogether," but I don't want it to depend on any actual empirical facts about Rule 30, since I'm not sure they've internalized them. Could you write me an essay question for a two-hour final, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme out of 20? The marking scheme should say what the specific A-level moves are (for instance, giving a positive account of what an explanation could still be even when there's no computational shortcut) and what a B answer looks like. And please name the common mistakes that cost marks — especially conflating irreducibility with chaos-theory sensitivity to initial conditions, and conflating unpredictability of a process with incompressibility of an output string. Oh also, one more constraint: it shouldn't ask for citations or readings, since the point is to test reasoning, not recall.
