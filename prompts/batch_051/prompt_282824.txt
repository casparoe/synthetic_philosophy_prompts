Consider the following argument against the golden rule. Throughout, take the golden rule to say that an agent S should treat another person O as S would want to be treated if S were in O's position.

(1) The rule tells S to act on a counterfactual: how S would want to be treated had S been in O's position.
(2) The phrase S in O's position is radically underdetermined. It could mean S with S's own desires and values transplanted into O's circumstances, or S with O's desires and values in O's circumstances, or some mixture of the two.
(3) The golden rule itself supplies no principle for choosing among these interpretations, so its instruction is determinate only if one imports a further specification from outside the rule.
(4) The natural specifications lead to a dilemma. If S carries over S's own desires, the rule tells S to act on preferences that O may not share. If S adopts O's desires instead, S's own desires drop out and the rule becomes something like: do what O would want done. That no longer involves S's own woulds in the way the golden rule requires.
(5) Therefore the golden rule either is too indeterminate to guide action or is redundant once some further principle is supplied; in either case it fails as a fundamental moral principle.

Is this argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, and on what precise reading of the golden rule. If a defender can escape the dilemma in step (4), give the strongest version of that reply and say whether the overall conclusion survives. If you think the argument is valid, state which formulation of the golden rule, if any, avoids it and why.
