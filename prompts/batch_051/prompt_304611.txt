I keep going back and forth with a friend about whether Gricean implicature is actually a distinct phenomena or just "Bayesian inference over context" with a fancier label, and they sent me a chapter from a book on pragmatics for AI people. The chapter summarizes Grice like this:

"Grice's key move was to split sentence meaning from speaker meaning. Speaker meaning, on his picture, is whatever the hearer can reasonably infer the speaker meant to get across by assuming the speaker is being cooperative. The engine is the Cooperative Principle plus the maxims of Quality, Quantity, Relation, and Manner. When a speaker blatantly flouts a maxim—say, 'lovely weather' during a downpour—the hearer derives an implicature: an extra proposition the speaker meant to communicate without literally saying it. Implicatures are the paradigm case of speaker meaning, and they're recognized by being always cancelable and by being computed entirely from context and the maxims, with the literal meaning of the sentence contributing nothing beyond the fact that a maxim was violated."

Something about that feels off to me. The last clause in particular sounds too strong, since I'd have thought the literal content is exactly what you run the maxims on; but the book is by someone who actually works in pragmatics, and my prior is that Grice is often caricatured, so I don't want to just trust my gut. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what Grice explicitly denied. And then: does the corrected picture still support the claim that started the argument—that "implicature is just Bayesian inference over context"—or is that an import that Grice's framework doesn't need?
