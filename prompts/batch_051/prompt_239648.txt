You are a professional philosopher of language with a specialisation in formal pragmatics, and in particular in presupposition, accommodation, and the idea of conversation as a scorekeeping game. Please work through the question in stages: begin by brainstorming candidate principles before you evaluate any of them, and test them rigorously rather than settling for the first plausible story.

I am not a philosopher. I have recently become the secretary of my residents' association, and each month I take the minutes at our meetings. The role has made me notice an asymmetry in how the meeting's shared background gets updated, and I cannot tell whether anything principled justifies it.

Consider two things said at the most recent meeting. One member said: "I regret that I cannot take on the role of treasurer this year." Nobody batted an eyelid: the claim that he cannot take on the role was not asserted outright, but was simply absorbed into the background of the discussion, and I found myself writing it into the minutes as settled. Later, the treasurer said: "I regret that the committee has already committed us to a five-year maintenance contract." Several members immediately interrupted: "Hold on — when did we commit to that?" Here the background claim — that the committee had already committed us — was also presupposed rather than asserted, yet this time it was not absorbed. It was challenged and had to be brought out into the open and examined.

In both cases a speaker packed an unestablished proposition into the background of what they said rather than asserting it directly. The hearers could have let the second presupposition pass into the common ground, just as they let the first pass, but they did not. What I want to know is whether there is a defensible principle that draws the line between the presuppositions that get accommodated and those that get challenged, or whether the difference is simply a matter of confidence, convenience, and social friction.

Please lay out the strongest candidate principles for this asymmetry: for instance, principles appealing to the plausibility of the presupposed claim, to whether it is already known to be false, to whether it concerns a private matter or the business of the meeting, to whether accepting it would settle a question the group is currently trying to decide, and any further candidates you regard as serious. For each principle, test it against nearby cases — a presupposition that is false but undetected, a presupposition that is true but belongs to the meeting's business, a presupposition introduced hesitantly by a newcomer, and so on — and say where it fails. Then give an honest verdict: is there a single principle that generates the asymmetry and is worth keeping, or should a conscientious secretary challenge every unasserted background claim, or accommodate them all?

I want a conclusion I can actually use the next time I take the minutes.
