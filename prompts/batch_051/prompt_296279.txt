I cover markets and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the lawsuits investors filed against the credit-rating agencies after the last crisis. A securities litigator I know — one of the smart ones — keeps reaching for the same analogy to brush those suits off: a Moody's or S&P rating is basically a restaurant review. The analyst is the critic, the issuer is the chef, the AAA or Ba3 is the star rating, and "high default risk" is like "the fish was too salty" — a judgment call about what the future holds, not a factual claim, so the First Amendment protects it. His line is that an angry chef who sues a critic over a bad write-up would get laughed out of court, and a burned bondholder should too.

My editors wants me to give readers an actual audit of that analogy, not just transcribe the talking point. Could you lay out what maps onto what — the critic, the diner, the meal, the stars, the "too salty" line — then the places where the mapping breaks or quietly imports things that aren't true of rating agencies, and then say whether his conclusion (ratings are protected opinion, forecasts included) survives once the disanalogies are on the table? Stretch it one step further: a credit rating is a forecast about a bond, not a report card on a meal someone already ate. Does the analogy get better or worse if you compare a rating to a storm-probability forecast or an economic-growth forecast that people actually trade on? Keep it usable for a reader who doesn't know the case law.
