I've been tinkering with Bongard problems — those puzzles with two panels of six line drawings each, where the answer is whatever's true of all the left drawings and none of the right ones (like "all left shapes are convex" or "every right shape contains a circle"). I took a few hundred of them, hand-labeled the rules, and trained a small classifier on top of CLIP image embeddings. It gets pretty good accuracy on held-out instances of rules it saw during training, even though each rule only got six positive and six negative examples.

Here's the bit I can't shake. I want to claim that for at least some rules the model actually induced the concept — that after six examples of "all shapes are the same size," say, it's keying on something like same-sizedness itself, not on a cheap confound. But I can think of a dozen ways it could be faking it. Maybe my positive examples just happen to have thicker pen strokes, or less clutter, or the model's mostly reading where the drawings sit on the page. So: how would I actually test whether it has the concept rather than a shortcut?

I'd like a concrete protocol, not just vibe's. What result would convince you it really has the concept, and what result would convince you its still shortcutting? Also be honest about what even the best test can't settle — is there a residue here that stays purely behavioral, where "it has the concept" isn't even a well-defined question once you've listed all the behavior? Don't worry about what's practical to run; design the best test you can.
