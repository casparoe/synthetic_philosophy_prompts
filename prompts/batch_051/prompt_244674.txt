I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on the epistemology of history. In a recent seminar on source criticism, a student dismissed Herodotus' account of the Persian Wars with the remark that 'history is written by the victors'. His intended conclusion was that, because Herodotus was a Greek writing in the aftermath of a Greek victory, his narrative is too compromised to serve as evidence about the Persians. I think the move is too quick, but I want to be able to say precisely why.

Please do two things. First, separate the distinct claims that the one-liner 'history is written by the victors' could be used to express, ranging from the nearly trivial to the radical. The menu should include at least: claims about which records, monuments, and archives physically survive; claims about whose perspective shapes the content of the narratives that do survive; claims about which narratives later traditions come to treat as authoritative; and claims about whether the past can be known at all independently of present power. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or false, and give a sentence of justification.

Second, apply the menu to the seminar exchange. Which reading, if any, would actually license the student's dismissal of Herodotus as evidence about the Persians, and which readings support only the weaker conclusion that Herodotus must be read with source-critical caution rather than discarded? Note that Herodotus himself frequently reports non-Greek versions of events and flags his own uncertainty, so the student's picture of him as a straightforwardly triumphalist Greek source may not survive contact with the text. Then consider the complication presented by Thucydides: an Athenian writing about a war Athens lost, from exile, whose account nevertheless became canonical. Say what his case shows about the gap between military victory and narrative dominance, and whether it breaks any of the readings you distinguished.

The answer should be a numbered menu of readings with verdicts, followed by the application to Herodotus and Thucydides. No bibliography or citations are needed.
