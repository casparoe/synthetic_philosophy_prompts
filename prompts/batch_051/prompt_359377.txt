I help run a mid-sized online marketplace, and we've been losing an arms race with bots that pose as humans in our seller chats and reviews. I've been reading Dennett on counterfeit people, especially his analogy with counterfeit money—that the damage isn't just any one deception but the way it debases trust in the whole currency of ordinary interaction. A collegue of mine wants to turn that analogy into a mechanism.

The scheme is this: any company running customer-facing conversational AI has to post a £50,000 deposit per deployed bot into escrow. Users can submit a transcript if they suspect they were talking to an undisclosed bot. A small panel inspects the transcript and system logs; if it indeed was an unflagged bot, the company forfeits £5,000, most of it going to the reporting user as a bounty and some to the panel. If the report is wrong, the user pays a £20 fee. Deployed bots also have to carry a watermark that a public API can detect, so the panel is not relying only on the user's hunch.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. What does it incentivise among companies, users, bounty hunters, and the panel? How would each side game it, and do the failure modes get worse once the definition of "bot" has to cover human agents using AI-suggested replies or bots that route to a human after two messages? Can any of the problems be fixed without gutting the scheme? Please answer in British English.
