I am a novelist. In the book I am writing, a small group must agree on the basic terms of a settlement before learning which positions in it they will occupy, and the terms, once fixed, cannot be renegotiated. The character I intend to be the most rigorous argues like this:

(1) The choosers decide behind a veil of ignorance: they know the positions the settlement will contain but have no basis for assigning probabilities to finding themselves in any one of them.
(2) In a decision under complete ignorance of probabilities, the only rational rule is maximin: rank each option by its worst possible outcome, and choose the option whose worst outcome is best.
(3) For a choice of basic terms, the worst possible outcome of an option is the condition of the worst-off person under those terms.
(4) The terms that make the worst-off person best off are terms that allow inequalities only insofar as those inequalities make the least advantaged as well off as possible.
(5) Therefore, rational choosers in this situation would adopt exactly those terms.

I want the character to be formidable but not unanswerable, and the scene turns on whether the others can find a genuine fault in his reasoning. My suspicion is that step (2) is doing more work than decision theory can support, but I cannot say precisely what it assumes. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest defensible version of that step? Keep your answer to at most 250 words.
