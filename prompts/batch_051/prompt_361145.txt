I recently read a popular philosophy article that summarized the debate over the norm of assertion, and I would like to know how much of it to trust. Here is the summary as I remember it. According to the article, assertion is the speech act of putting a proposition forward as true, and philosophers in this debate are asking what standard a speaker must meet in order to be entitled to assert something. The article says that the most influential answer, due to Timothy Williamson, is that assertion is governed by exactly one rule: assert p only if you know p. It presents this as what distinguishes assertion from every other speech act. It claims that the knowledge rule neatly explains why utterances of the form p, but I do not know that p are absurd, since the second conjunct effectively admits that the rule has already been violated. The article then considers the rival truth rule, according to which one may assert p only if p is true, and dismisses it as clearly hopeless because it would permit a lucky true guess made with no evidence at all. The article ends by endorsing the reasonable-belief rule, on which a speaker may assert p whenever the speaker's evidence makes p highly probable, saying this view is more defensible because it handles ordinary probabilistic assertions such as the claim that a train will be on time, whereas the knowledge rule would forbid them. It concludes that the knowledge account, though historically important, is now widely regarded as too demanding, and that the reasonable-belief rule is the emerging consensus.

I suspect there is some distortion here, especially in the claim that the knowledge account has lost its standing, but I do not know the literature well enough to say exactly where the real disagreements lie. Could you assess this summary sentence by sentence: which claims the main parties to the debate would accept, which are misstated or conflated, and whether the final assessment of the knowledge account actually follows from the accurate parts?
