Two people in my epidemiology program got into a real back-and-forth at journal club over a table comparing outcomes at two surgical clinics. The numbers were prety stark:

| clinic | standard-risk | complicated | overall |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 90/100 (90%) | 150/300 (50%) | 240/400 (60%) |
| B | 255/300 (85%) | 45/100 (45%) | 300/400 (75%) |

(Assume the studies are huge, so this isnt about sampling error.)

1. Marcus said the overall success rate is what matters. If a patient has to pick a clinic without knowing their risk level, 75% beats 60%, so B is the better choice. End of story.

2. Dana said that's the whole reversal doing all the work. Condition on risk: A is better for standard-risk (90% to 85%) and better for complicated (50% to 45%). Whatever stratum you're in, A is the better bet. B only looks good overall because it handles way more standard-risk patients. The right comparison is within strata, not the pooled numbers.

3. Marcus came back that patients don't get to condition on a risk level they don't yet know. Choosing a clinic means you get its case mix, so the overall rate is the expectation that matters. And risk classification isn't just a fixed fact about the patient: how the clinic triages and labels people is part of what you're choosing.

4. Dana replied that if the label is an outcome of the clinic, then the overall difference is even more confounded, not less. Without randomization, the only clean comparison is within the strata you can measure, and there A wins everywhere. If a doctor can tell you your risk group, pick A. The overall gap is an artifact of who gets sent where.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Answer in American English.
