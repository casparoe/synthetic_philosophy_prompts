Please argue for the following position. The Church–Turing thesis, in the form that matters for the foundations of computability, is best understood neither as a mathematical conjecture awaiting proof nor as an empirical hypothesis about the reach of any actual physical mechanism. It is a demarcational claim: it lays down what is to count as an effective procedure, rather than tracking an independent class of effectively computable functions whose true extent might be discovered through better evidence. Defend this interpretation, and in doing so address each of the following:

- how the apparent informativeness of the thesis—its identification of Turing computability, general recursiveness, and lambda-definability—can be reconciled with the claim that it is not a straightforward factual assertion;
- whether a hypothetical procedure whose outputs we could observe but whose instructions resist all known formalizations would count as a counterexample, and why or why not;
- how this reading of the mathematical thesis relates to stronger claims, such as the claim that every function computable by any physical system is Turing computable;
- the objection that a purely conceptual or demarcational thesis is trivial and therefore cannot do the explanatory work commonly assigned to it.

In your argument, be explicit about what would and would not count as evidence for or against the thesis so construed.
