Okay so I just came from a long argument with a friend about Bertrand Russell's views on marriage and sexual morality, specifically the _Marriage and Morals_ side of him where he treats marriage as a revocable agreement between free people, thinks the real poison is jealousy and possessiveness rather than sex outside marriage, and wants law and custom to stop forcing people to stay in dead unions. My friend thinks a novel I keep mentioning just proves Russell right; I think the novel actually pushes back, or at least shows a cost Russell's framework has no name for. So I want you to be the tiebreaker. Put on your professional philosopher hat and your literary critic hat at the same time, think through this carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three plausible readings before settling on the one you think is strongest. The novel is Edith Wharton's _The Age of Innocence_. Read it through Russell's lens: Newland, May, and Ellen; the divorce question; the moments Newland keeps imagining leaving; the farewell dinner; the final page with him outside the apartment. Where does Russell's view illuminate what's cruel and irrational about old New York? Where does the novel make Russell look naive or too rationalist about what actual people need from marriage and from the people around them? Dont reduce the book to an illustration of the theory, and dont sand off the places where it resists the reading. End by saying what the novel contributes to the argument that Russell's essays, even at their best, dont.
