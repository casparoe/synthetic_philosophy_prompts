I have a set of settled-seeming verdicts about complicity, and I want to know whether any one principle underlies them. By "complicity" I mean bearing responsibility for a wrong one neither performs nor, in several of these cases, makes any more likely. In every case the wrong is done by someone else; my question is whether the described person participates in it at all, not whether they are merely negligent or cowardly, though some may be those too.

Here are the cases.

1. A supplier sells ordinary office computers to a government agency. The supplier knows that the agency keeps the administrative records that the secret police draw on to compile lists of dissidents, but the supplier configures nothing for that purpose and has no say over which records are kept. My verdict: not complicit. Supplying general-purpose goods from outside, even to a repressive apparatus, is not yet participation in the repression.

2. A software company licenses a face-recognition suite to the same government, knowing it is used to identify protesters at demonstrations for arrest. The police could make the identifications by hand; the software does not change who is identified, only how quickly, and roughly the same people would be arrested either way. My verdict: complicit. But I cannot say exactly why this differs from the computer seller, and the fact that I cannot bothers me.

3. A chef is hired for a private banquet hosted by a crime syndicate. She knows roughly what the syndicate does, cooks an ordinary meal, cleans up, and leaves. My verdict: not complicit, though perhaps tainted by association.

4. An accountant at a firm discovers that the reports he has prepared for years are a small part of a money-laundering operation. If he quit, a colleague would prepare identical reports the next day and nothing about the operation would change. My verdict: complicit from the moment he knows and stays. His replaceability does not exculpate him.

5. A shareholder owns a tiny position in a medical-device company and learns that the company is knowingly concealing a defect that has injured people. She does not sell; she also does nothing to support the concealment. My verdict: not complicit. Yet the shareholder, like the accountant, alters nothing by her choice, and the divergence makes me uneasy.

6. A landlord rents a warehouse to two men who say they run an import business. He notices enough to strongly suspect they are planning an attack but deliberately avoids asking questions so he can tell himself he does not know. The attack happens. My verdict: complicit. Manufactured ignorance does not protect him.

7. At a beating, a man stands at the edge of the crowd shouting encouragement. He never touches the victim, and the beating would have been no different without him. My verdict: complicit. The causal inertness has no weight with me here.

I see at least two places where my verdicts pull against each other: the computer seller versus the face-recognition licensor, and the accountant versus the shareholder. I would prefer a clean principle that supports one verdict in each pair and forces the other out, if that is what reflective equilibrium demands, over a gerrymandered rule that saves all seven.

I have been toying with a rough principle of my own: one is complicit when one knowingly places one's labor or goods inside the wrongdoer's enterprise in a role specifically suited to the wrongdoing, or adopts the wrongdoer's ends as one's own; by contrast, one is not complicit when one merely supplies general-purpose goods or retains a passive stake. But this looks suspiciously like a restatement of my verdicts, and I am not sure it handles the cheering bystander or the willfully blind landlord.

Please give me a precise account, or a small set of candidate accounts, that best fits these verdicts — and where it cannot fit, tell me which verdicts I should overturn and why. I would also like the account's verdicts on cases I have not listed: a cab driver who drives a known robber to a bank; a doctor who treats an injured member of a criminal gang; a pension-fund administrator who invests retirees' money in a firm doing wrong; and a web host that carries a hate site among millions of ordinary sites.
