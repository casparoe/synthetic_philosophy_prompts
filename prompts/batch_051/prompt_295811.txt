So there's this thing in statutory interpretation called the absurdity doctrine. The rough idea is that a court doesn't have to enforce the literal text of a statute when doing so would give a result so silly or self-defeating that no legislature could have want it. Imagine a statute makes it a felony to deliver a controlled substance, and on its face that covers a nurse giving morphine to someone in a hospice. A court will often say no, the statute can't have been meant to reach that, and will read in an exception. But here's the puzzle: the same courts will also insist they're not in the business of rewriting statutes—if a law is merely harsh or unwise, the fix belongs to the legislature, not them. What does this practice presuppose, philosophically? Does the absurdity doctrine commit a court to the idea that what a statute really is is an expression of legislative intent, with the text as mere evidence of it? Or can a textualist run the doctrine without contradicting themself? Does the doctrine presuppose a picture of the legislature as a basically rational, reasonable agent, and what should 'reasonable' mean there? Also, where is the line between an absurd result and one that's just bad policy, and doesn't handing judges the power to draw that line undercut the doctrine's whole self-presentation as a way of honoring the legislature rather than substituting judicial preferences?
