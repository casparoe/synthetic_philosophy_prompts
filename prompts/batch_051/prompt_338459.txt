I'm a PhD student in social and political philosophy working on loyalty and institutional betrayal, and I want to test my intuitions against Royce. In The Philosophy of Loyalty, there's a passage — I don't remember the exact page — where Royce argues that true loyalty to a cause is never just about keeping the group comfortable; if the group starts betraying its own cause, the genuinely loyal member is the one who says so openly, even if that looks like disloyalty from inside. (I'm paraphrasing, not quoting, so don't hold me to the wording.)

I have a bunch of verdicts about whistleblowing/betrayal cases and I want to know if there's one principle that captures them, and whether Royce's passage forces me to revise any of them.

Case 1: A software engineer discovers that her company has been quietly changing the failure-rate numbers on a safety-critical device to pass inspection. She reports internally, nothing happens, then she goes to a journalist. Verdict: not a betrayal; she did exactly what the company's own mission required.

Case 2: A mid-level officer leaks a classified report showing that his unit covered up a massacre of civilians. He tried the chain of command first and was ignored. Verdict: fine, even admirable.

Case 3: A political aide, angry about being passed over for a promotion, anonymously leaks a politician's private but legal extramarital affair to the press. Verdict: not fine; that's just betrayal even though he frames it as public-interest whistleblowing.

Case 4: A therapist calls the police because a client says he plans to kill his ex. Verdict: obviously fine, duty to warn wins.

Case 5: A friend tells someone's spouse about their affair, because they think the spouse deserves to know. Verdict: I genuinely can't decide. On some days it feels like loyalty to the spouse; on other days it feels like a betrayal of the friend, and maybe those can't both be true.

Case 6: A longtime member of a religious community reports a senior leader's sexual abuse to the police after the community's internal process just moves the leader to another congregation. Verdict: fine, but it also feels like the most painful kind of loyal act — the community's own ideals demanded it.

Case 7: An employee leaks a memo showing his company misled regulators, but he also qualifies for a large whistleblower bounty and that was part of his motivation. Verdict: still fine, but slightly less pure; the money doesn't make it betrayal.

Case 8: A grad student notifies a journal editor that her advisor fabricated data in a paper, after the advisor refuses to retract. Verdict: fine, obviously.

The pattern I see: I seem to think loyalty to the institution or the public cause trumps loyalty to the group's current behavior, but only when the disclosure is connected to the cause. The personal-grudge leak is betrayal because it has nothing to do with the cause. But then Case 5 breaks that: the friend's disclosure of the affair is arguably about the spouse's well-being, which is a kind of cause, yet it still feels like a betrayal of the friend. Royce's passage suggests that maybe my Case 5 hesitation is wrong and I should just say the faithful friend is the one who tells the spouse, because silence would be disloyal to the friend's own deeper commitments. But that feels too convenient.

What I want: a principle that captures these verdicts (or close), applied to the affair case, and an honest statement of which verdicts I should drop if Royce is right. Is there a way to save all of them with out making the principle weirdly ad hock? And does Royce's view actually commit me to thinking the friend's disclosure is required, or is there a reading where it's still betrayal?
