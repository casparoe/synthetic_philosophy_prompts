A friend who works in AI policy keeps telling me the real lesson of machine ethics is that you should build a system the way you build a constitution: don't enumerate every possible wrong act, write a small list of high-level principles and let some process of review apply them case by case, the way a court applies a constitution to situations the framers never imagined. He points to the constitutional AI idea as proof — give a model a short list of principles (avoid harm, don't deceive, respect autonomy, that sort of thing) and have it critique and revise it's own outputs against them, instead of trying to hand-specify every rule or reward every right answer directly. His conclusion is that value alignment is best done with a few vague principles plus feedback, and that people who try to formalize ethics in code are making a category mistake.

I find the analogy both attractive and suspicious. Help me audit it: what maps onto what — what plays the role of the constitution, the legislature, the courts, the citizen — and were does the mapping break? I keep noticing that a constitution binds human beings who can read it, form their own interpretations, push back, and who already share a moral background, whereas a model is an optimizer being steered through a principle list by its own outputs. Which disanalogies actually matter for his intended conclusion, and which are just surface features of the metaphor? Does the conclusion survive — that a small set of high-level principles plus self-critique is the right way to build ethics into a machine — or was the constitution analogy carrying the whole argument? If there's a better analogy, I'd want to know that too.
