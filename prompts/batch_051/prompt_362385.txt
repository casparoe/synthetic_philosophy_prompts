I'm taking a philosophy of mind course and the professor keeps pushing this line: the problem of other minds is basically a scientific inference problem. When you watch someone stub their toe and yelp, the claim "that person is in pain" is posited in the same way as the claim "there's an electron moving through the cloud chamber" is posited from a vapor trail. He says we have no direct access to either; in both cases we posit an unobservable something that best explains the observable data, and since nobody loses sleep over electrons, nobody should lose sleep over other minds either. He's using the analogy to dissolve the problem rather than solve it: there's no special philosophical gap, just ordinary inference to the best explanation.

I want to audit this carefully. Give me the correspondence table: what maps to the vapor trail, the electron, the background theory, the experimental apparatus, the repeatable trial, and the community of observers, and what plays the corresponding roles in the other-minds case. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion he's drawing: things like the fact that in my own case I'm not inferring my pain from a vapor trail, or that with electrons we have many independent causal channels onto the same unobservable while with other minds we seem stuck with bodily behavior, or that the electron is constituted by the theory whereas I already know what pain is firsthand. Which breaks are just surface differences, and which ones do real work? And does his conclusion survive—that there's no distinct problem of other minds, just standard inference to the best explanation, and no special epistemic gap—once the disanalogies are on the table? If the analogy is quietly doing the work, say what a better analogy would be, or whether the conclusion could stand without any analogy at all.
