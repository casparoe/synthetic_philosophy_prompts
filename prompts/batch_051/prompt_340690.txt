A friend of mine sits on a panel that reviews the continued detention of offenders held under an indeterminate sentencing scheme. We recently argued all evening about whether criminal punishment is ever justified by desert. She maintained that desert is a fiction, and that the only legitimate grounds for depriving someone of liberty are public protection, deterrence, and rehabilitation. I maintained that some confinements are justified because they were earned, even when the person no longer poses much danger. To test this, I went back through her published panel decisions, and they look like this.

A. A twenty-four-year-old first offender recruited by an abusive partner to carry drugs across a border, with no prior record, good conduct in custody, and strong family support. She voted to release her well before the earliest guideline release date, saying that prison would merely manufacture a criminal rather than prevent one.

B. A seventy-two-year-old man in poor health who, twenty years earlier, had defrauded a community charity of several million pounds and spent the money. He no longer works in any financial role and his risk of reoffending is assessed as negligible. She voted against release, on the ground that a breach of trust of that scale still had to be answered for.

C. A fifty-year-old man who killed a stranger during a florid psychotic episode. He is now stabilised on medication, though he has twice stopped taking it, and his unsupervised relapse risk is rated moderate to high. She voted for release into a twenty-four-hour supported placement with mandatory medication, saying that he needed care rather than a cage.

D. A thirty-year-old man detained after a pattern of stalking and threats against an ex-partner. At review he had served the minimum term set by the court, had declined every programme offered, and was assessed as highly likely to cause serious future harm. She voted for continued detention under a preventive order, saying she was not going to wait for a serious harm to happen before it counted.

E. A man in his sixties with no prior record, caught carrying an unlicensed pistol he says he bought after a neighbour's house was firebombed. He has served two years of a five-year term and is assessed as no risk to anyone. She voted to release, saying that nothing further was being accomplished by keeping him.

Next month the panel must decide the following case. A sixty-four-year-old man is coming up for review. Thirty-one years ago he planned and led a series of armed robberies in which three security guards were shot by one of his co-accused, although he himself was not the shooter. He has been in custody ever since, is now significantly physically disabled, and every available assessment places his risk of reoffending at negligible. The panel can release him or continue his detention for up to five more years.

What would consistency with her record most plausibly lead her to do in this new case, and why? Which of her earlier decisions is the closest analogue? Identify the rule or rules that fit her track record, including any rule that fits it about equally well, state what each candidate rule predicts about the new case, and give a best guess with the strongest reason for preferring it. Then tell me what further case, after this one, would best settle which rule she is actually applying.
