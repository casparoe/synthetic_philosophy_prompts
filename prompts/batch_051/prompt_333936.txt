I cover consumer technology and law, and I've spent the better part of a decade writing about the fine print nobody reads. Somewhere along the way I started to doubt that what happens when a person clicks "I agree" should be called agreement at all, so I tried to work out what would count. For my own notes, I've been using four tests. Real agreement needs (1) enough understanding of what one is taking on, even if only roughly; (2) a geniune choice rather than a forced move; (3) an intention to be bound by the act itself, not just to get past a screen; and (4) something real at stake if one says no, so that accepting isn't costless threatre.

Now here is the case I can't settle. A friend was setting up an over-the-counter glucose monitor with a companion app. During pairing, the app showed a screen saying that by tapping Pair she would be agreeing to the Terms of Service and Privacy Policy, including binding arbitration. The terms themselves were not shown on that screen; she could read them in the app or on the maker's website, and the pairing button worked exactly once — after the device was paired, the screen never came back. My friend, who is a doctor, tapped Pair and later admitted she had not read the terms and hadn't intended anything beyond getting the sensor working. Yet she wasn't trapped: she could have bought a rival device, or returned this one unopened. Apply my four tests honestly and tell me whether she agreed, and whether the case shows the criteria are wrong or incomplete. What I'd like is a diagnosis, not a legal prediction. Please write in British English, and end with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase: either that the case qualifies as agreement, that it does not, or that the criteria cannot settle it.
