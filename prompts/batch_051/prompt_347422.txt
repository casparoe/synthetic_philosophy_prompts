I am preparing an article on whether intense religious experiences, such as the felt presence of an unseen person in grief or prolonged isolation, should be treated as evidence for God or only as evidence about the psychology of the person undergoing them.

Restate the philosophical debate over religious experience as evidence for God in the vocabulary of signal detection theory, as faithfully as you can. Cover at least these positions: (i) the evidentialist view that a person's apparent experience of God is prima facie evidence that God is present, as formulated in the principle of credulity—roughly, if it seems to a subject that God is present, then, absent defeaters, that presence is likely; (ii) the doxastic-practice view that trusting religious experience is on the same footing as trusting sense perception, acceptable until specifically undermined; and (iii) the skeptical objections that such experiences are better explained by natural psychological causes and that the incompatible character of religious experience across traditions undercuts its evidential value. Use the felt-presence case as a running example.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly into hits, false alarms, sensitivity, criterion placement, base rates, and decision costs? Which parts require distortion to fit the framework? Which parts cannot be expressed in it at all? End by judging whether the residue is a limitation of my signal-detection vocabulary or a place where the original arguments were less precise than they appeared. I want to know whether my working assumption—that the whole dispute is ultimately about where the detection criterion is set—is correct, or whether it misses what is really at stake.
