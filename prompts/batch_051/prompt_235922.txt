I’m working on a novel set maybe twenty years out, where virtually all civil disputes—consumer stuff, employment, even small medical malpractice—get routed into binding private arbitration by contract, and the public courts are mostly a ghost town for civil claims. One of my characters is a retired law professor who writes a memo that’s supposed to lay out what a real justification for that system would have to look like, not just a pro-arbitration puff piece. He wants to start from Locke, Second Treatise, chapter IX, sec. 125, where Locke gives his three defects of the state of nature. The part he keeps returning to is this:

“Secondly, In the state of nature there wants a known and indifferent judge, with authority to determine all differences according to the established law: for every one in that state being both judge and executioner of the law of nature, men being partial to themselves, passion and revenge is very apt to carry them too far, and with too much heat, in their own cases; as well as negligence, and unconcernedness, to make them too remiss in other men’s.”

The memo prompt I want you to work through is: what would a theory of legitimate privatized dispute resolution have to include to count as an actual answer to Locke’s problem rather than a dressed-up version of the same defect? I don’t need a scorecard of current arbitration providers—this is for the character’s thinking, not a legal brief—but I do want the list of desiderata a theory in this area must meet, with the case or consideration behind each one. Use Locke’s three wants as the starting point: an established known law, a known and indifferent judge, and power to back the sentence. Then ask which of those requirements survive or change when the judge is chosen through a private contract, gets paid by one repeat player, and the rules are set by the provider. Which of the usual demands people make—voluntariness, opt-out, appeal rights, repeat-player limits, cost-shifting, transparency of awards—are genuine desiderata and which are just camp preferences that arbitrators or trial lawyers carry around? Mark which are non-negotiable and which are negotiable. And if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable under mandatory pre-dispute arbitration, say that flat out; that’s the part I most need for the book. Also, be honest about whether Locke’s “known and indifferent judge” argument cuts in favor of private arbitration in some cases, if the public system is too slow or expensive to be a real known judge in practice, or whether that’s just swapping one partiality for another.
