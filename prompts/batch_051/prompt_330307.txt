You are a political philosopher who works on collective decision procedures for risky, irreversible technologies. I am drafting a handout for a governance workshop on the unilateralist's curse: the situation in which each member of a group can unilaterally trigger a risky, hard-to-reverse initiative, so the initiative goes ahead if at least one member judges it worthwhile. Even when every member is individually cautious, the probability that someone misjudges it as beneficial when it is in fact harmful grows with the size of the group. I want a decision tree, built as a sorting instrument rather than a list of named views, for the main positions on the normative question that follows: when, if at all, may a member of such a group act unilaterally, and what collective decision rule should the group adopt in response?

Build the tree from the diagnostic questions first, and phrase each branching question as a concrete yes-or-no choice that a person could answer without already knowing the position labels. Make one early split ask whether the underlying problem is noisy private judgment about a shared objective, or conflicting values and affected interests, and let the rest of the tree show how those two diagnoses lead to different rules. The leaves must include at least these candidate responses: a permissive default that lets individuals act on their own judgment; a strict consensus or collective-self-determination requirement; a delegated-authority view that screens initiatives through designated epistemic experts; an ex post liability view that permits action but makes the actor answer for bad outcomes; and a case-splitting view that varies the rule with the domain, such as reversibility, the asymmetry between downside and upside harms, or whether the actor profits. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, note it and say briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two candidate leaves cannot be separated by your questions, flag that rather than forcing a distinction. Aim for roughly six to ten leaves, give each leaf a short name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and pitch the explanations to members of an unstructured research collective with no fixed hierarchy. Start by briefly listing three or four branching questions you considered and which of them you kept, before presenting the final tree.
