So I keep running into people who say you can just watch what a system does and recover what its optimizing, and it feels like they're smuggling in a lot of assumptions to make that work. I want the actual spec first: if someone proposed a theory of how to infer an agent's objective, utility function, reward function, whatever you want to call it, from its behavior, what would the theory have to get right before you'd call it adequate? I dont mean the grad-level math, I mean the conceptual requirements list, with a sentence or two on why each one is on the list.

Cover at least these cases: an agent with a perfectly clear goal but a bad search process, so it keeps ending up in mediocre options; an agent under a constraint that makes it do something it otherwise wouldn't; an agent that's still exploring, so its early choices look like stable preferences when they're not; an agent whose reward function is degenerate or misspecified, like it's been told to maximize something dumb and does that brilliantly; reward hacking, where behavior faithfully tracks the wrong proxy; strategic or social behavior that is mostly about what others will infer from it; and behavior from something like a firm or an organism where saying "it wants X" might just be a reification.

And get clear on the distinctions a theory can't collapse: terminal versus instrumental goals, objective versus constraint, competence versus motivation, because failing because you can't and not doing it because you didn't want to are different explanations; what's being optimized versus the space of options the agent can even consider, since behavior is a joint product of the two; and revealed preference versus actual preference, since revealed preference can easily become a definitional dodge if you want a real explanation rather than a redescription.

Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one school. Stationarity, stochastic choice, assuming the agent's world model is correct, assuming a single fixed objective function, all of that. And here's the part I most want: tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, in the sense that no purely behavioral theory can meet all of them without adding prior assumptions. If a finite stretch of behavior can be rationalized by many different objectives, then isn't the real requirement just "declare your priors," and the rest of the list is a disguised argument about which priors are sane? Which single requirement does the most work to rule out the naive revealed-preference stories and the simple inverse reinforcement learning stories?
