I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I run a monthly methods workshop for early-career reporters — 16 people, 90 minutes, and no equipment beyond paper, index cards, a whiteboard, and a timer. The next session will examine a claim I keep hearing from AI labs and safety researchers: that systems can be made safe by being told to avoid side effects or to keep their impact low, doing the assigned task while leaving the rest of the world roughly as it would otherwise have been. I want the group to feel the force of a specific worry about that claim. In particular: side effect is not a natural category but an interest-relative one; measuring impact requires choosing a baseline and a way of comparing worlds, and both choices quietly smuggle in a partial specification of what matters; and a naive instruction to minimize impact can perversely reward doing the task badly, doing nothing, or erasing traces of one's activity rather than acting well. I am less interested in the usual doom-or-safety arguments about extinction risk and more interested in the specification problem itself.

Treat this as if you are a professional philosopher of technology with substantial experience designing participatory teaching exercises. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming at least two or three candidate formats — a game with a scoring scheme, a structured role-play, a forced-choice deliberation under constraint — and say briefly why you are choosing the one you recommend. Then give me a fully specified, runnable activity: rules, materials, a non-monetary payoff or point system if one helps, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps concrete moments in the activity onto the conceptual distinctions above. The idea should show up in what happens, not in a framing lecture beforehand, and the phrase low-impact should not be named until the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it on the fly. Finally, be honest about which feature of the real problem the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it is something like the indefinitely branching counterfactual consequences of even trivial actions, and the difference between a human player's bounded perspective and an optimization process operating over a model of the world — and tell me how to supply that missing piece in the debrief without turning it into a lecture.
