I'm a PhD student in historical linguistics, working on cases where morphological change strands exceptions to sound change. In lab meetings and reading groups I keep noticing that the word "rule" gets used for quite different objects: sometimes a procedure speakers apply to novel forms, sometimes a historical statement with exceptions, sometimes merely a recurring pattern. I want a working definition of "a rule of grammar" — as opposed to a mere tendency, a pattern, or an isolated lexical fact — that gets the following cases right. (i) The English regular plural, whose suffix surfaces as [s] after voiceless sounds (cats), [z] after voiced ones (dogs), and [ɪz] after sibilants (roses); I would be worried if this didn't come out as a rule. (ii) The aspiration of voiceless stops at the onset of a stressed syllable (the [pʰ] in pit vs. the [p] in spit); this is not taught or consciously known, but I'd call it a rule. (iii) Suppletion: go/went, good/better; systematic within its paradigm, but there's no derivable relation, and my intuition says it's not a rule. (iv) The historical change by which earlier /sk/ became /ʃ/ in English — giving ship, fish, and sheep — while later Norse loans like skin and sky kept /sk/; is that one exception-laden rule, two rules, or something harder to classify? (v) The subregular ablaut class sing/sang/sung, ring/rang/rung, drink/drank/drunk; it generalizes a little, but not to new verbs. (vi) The plural oxen (and perhaps children), which looks like a one-off lexical fact. (vii) English adjective-order preferences: big red ball is unmarked, red big ball is strained, yet speakers can override the order for emphasis or contrast.

I'd like you to propose the definition, run it explicitly through all seven cases, and say where it surprises you or leaves a case undecided. If your definition has to distinguish, say, a rule of the individual speaker from a rule of the linguistic system, or productivity from exceptionless regularity, I want to hear that distinction rather than have the term quietly shift between them. I suspect the word "rule" does at least two jobs, but I'd rather not bias you. Finally, tell me which of your verdicts is least secure and what argument or additional case would most likely make you revise the definition rather than defend the surprising verdict.
