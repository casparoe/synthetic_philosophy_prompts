A friend of mine who took one Chinese philosophy survey course summarized Han Feizi for me the other day like this: Han Feizi is basically a cynical realist about human nature. He thinks people are naturally selfish and can't be made good, so the ruler should stop wasting time on Confucian virtue cultivation and moral example. Instead, the ruler should rely on fixed laws, harsh punishments, and material rewards, because people respond to incentives, not to moral teaching. And since Han Feizi rejects the ancient sage kings as models — he thinks times change — that makes him a kind of moral relativist: for him there's no real right or wrong, just whatever the ruler's law happens to demand at a given moment.

He then showed me this passage from Han Feizi, chapter 49, "Five Pests," in the ctext translation:

"Therefore, the sage deliberates on abundance and scarcity, considers lightness and weight in resources, and governs accordingly. Thus, lenient punishments are not signs of weakness, nor are strict punishments expressions of cruelty; they simply conform to prevailing customs and circumstances. Therefore, actions should follow the times, and preparations must suit the situation."

I'm not sure this actually supports his "no real right or wrong" conclusion. Is my friend's summary fair? Could you go through it part by part — what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what Han Feizi himself would probably reject or at least qualify? Especially: does anything in this passage show that Han Feizi is not just saying "punish harshly because people are selfish," but has some other standard for what punishments fit the times? And does the passage commit him to moral relativism, or just to a kind of sensitivity to circumstances?
