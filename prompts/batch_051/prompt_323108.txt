I've been trying to work through an argument I read by Persson and Savulescu about moral bioenhancement. As I understand it, their point is this. Human moral psychology was shaped for life in small groups with short chains of cause and effect, but modern technology lets a single person cause harm on an enormous scale. Making people smarter without making them morally better just hands bigger levers to people with ordinary or worse moral motives. So they argue there is an urgent need to develop moral bioenhancement: biomedical means of improving people's moral motivation and dispositions, not merely their cognitive abilities.

That argument was put forward when the main new dangers were capabilities wielded by individual humans, such as engineered pathogens or nuclear materials. But now much of the most dangerous new capability is being built into AI systems. These systems do not have human moral psychology, and biomedical moral enhancement does not apply to them. They can, however, be changed directly through their design, training, and safeguards. If a misaligned or reckless AI can cause the kind of catastrophe Persson and Savulescu were worried about, the dangerous agent is no longer mainly a cognitively enhanced human with unimproved morals.

Does their argument survive this change? I would like an answer that does three things: identify which premise of the original argument the development puts pressure on; say whether the conclusion, that we urgently need moral bioenhancement, still follows, follows only in a modified form, or no longer follows; and say whether the best reply on their behalf rescues the argument or merely moves the problem from human biology to machine design. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question. Please write in American English.
