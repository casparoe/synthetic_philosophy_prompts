Here's the question:

"Background: Laura lives off-campus and normally takes the bus; if she doesn't, she walks or takes a rideshare. Does the apparent semantic difference between indicative conditionals such as (1) If Laura didn't take the bus, she walked, and counterfactuals such as (2) If Laura hadn't taken the bus, she would have walked reflect a difference in truth conditions, or is it better explained as a difference in context-sensitivity and assertability? Your answer can engage Stalnaker, Lewis, or Adams."

Here's the student's answer:

"A unified possible-worlds treatment is the right place to start. On Lewis's analysis, a conditional 'If A, C' is true at a world just in case C holds at all the closest A-worlds; the Limit Assumption, which Lewis accepts, guarantees there will be a closest antecedent-world, so the 'all closest worlds' formulation is clean. Indicatives and counterfactuals then differ only pragmatically, not semantically: an indicative presupposes its antecedent is a live epistemic possibility, while a counterfactual presupposes it is false or at least remote. Thus (1) is infelicitous if the speaker knows Laura took the bus; (2) is natural precisely then.

On this story, whenever both (1) and (2) are assertable, they should coincide in truth value, since the indicative is just a counterfactual with a contextually restricted domain. The data seem to bear this out: both come out true iff the closest no-bus worlds are walking worlds. What differs is assertability, and that is captured by Adams's thesis, which says the assertability of a counterfactual equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. So (2) is accepted when P(she walked | she didn't bus) is high. Indicatives are governed by the same rule but restricted to live epistemic possibilities. The upshot is that the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals is real but pragmatic, and any residual feeling that they differ in content is an illusion generated by their different presuppositions."
