Epistemic logic in the possible-worlds tradition models knowledge as truth across a set of epistemically accessible worlds. A direct consequence is closure under logical consequence: if an agent knows P, and P entails Q, then Q holds at every accessible world, so the agent knows Q; the agent likewise knows every logical truth. This is the problem of logical omniscience, usually treated either as a harmless idealization of finite reasoners or as a normative ideal of perfect rationality. A specific later development pressures this stance. The artificial reasoners we now actually build, query, and occasionally describe in epistemic terms — large language models — violate closure systematically: they regularly assert P, assert that P implies Q, and then fail or refuse to assert Q; their inferential success shifts with surface rewording; and they often require step-by-step prompting to combine premises they already state. Suppose one wanted to model such a system within epistemic logic. Does the possible-worlds argument for closure survive this development, need repair, or is it simply inapt for such agents? Your answer should identify the exact premise or modeling choice the LLM behavior bears on, separate descriptive from normative readings of closure, and defend one repair strategy against its rivals — weakening the closure of accessible worlds, adding awareness or impossible worlds, modeling belief as explicit syntactic items, or refusing to count these systems as knowers at all.
