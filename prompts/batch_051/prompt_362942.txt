I keep going in circles about whether the so-called wrong-kind-of-reasons distinction is something people actually track or just something philosophers made up, and I'd like you to think about how one could test that. Quick setup. Right-kind reasons for an attitude make the attitude fit its object: someone is pointing a loaded gun at you, that's a right-kind reason to be afraid of them. Wrong-kind reasons make the attitude pay off without making it fit: a demon says he'll kill you unless you admire him, and suddenly you have a wrong-kind reason to admire him—a reason to get yourself to admire him, not a reason to think hes actually admirable. The conceptual claim I want to test is that ordinary people's judgments already mark this as a difference in kind, not degree. They don't treat "I have reason to fear the gunman" and "I have reason to fear my harmless boss because my job rewards looking scared of him" as the same sort of thing at different intensities.

So: design the best empirical probe you can. Concretely, what vignettes or forced-choice questions would you run, what pattern of answers would convince you the distinction is doing real work in ordinary cognition, and what pattern would convince you it's a philosophers' invention? Then be explicit about what a positive result would still leave open. For example, if subjects say the demon gives you a reason to bring about admiration but not a reason for the object to be admired, have they shown they've got the distinction itself, or only a blunter "evidence vs incentive" heuristic? And is there a core of this question that no psych experiment could ever settle, because it's about reasons themselves rather than what people judge?
