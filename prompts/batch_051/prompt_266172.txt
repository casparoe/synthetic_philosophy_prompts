For a column on scientific and philosophical ideas, I'm poking at self-reference, and there's an asymmetry in how we treat these sentences that I can't get past. *This sentence is false* is treated by almost everyone as a full-blown paradox — the thing that supposedly forces revisions of logic, hierarchies of languages, truth-value gaps, all that machinery. But its near-twin *This sentence is true* gets shrugged off as a harmless curiosity, at most a wierd party trick. On paper they look like mirror images: each one predicates exactly one truth-value of itself. So why does only one of them count as a crisis?

I'm trying to decide whether the ordinary imbalance is justified or just a comfort bias — the Liar hands us a contradiction we can't sit with, while the Truth-Teller lets us say "sure, either way" and move on. Give me the strongest principled defense of treating the Liar as deeply problematic and the Truth-Teller as mostly uninteresting, and say clearly where that defense cracks. I especially want to know: does the Liar have some defect the Truth-Teller lacks, *other than* the brute fact that it yields inconsistency under classical rules? And should "inconsistent" automatically count as more serious than "consistent but badly underdetermined"? If you had to keep the ordinary asymmetry, test it against nearby cases and tell me which ones make it wobble — a cyclic pair of sentences, a sentence that says of itself that it's true-or-false, a sentence that says of itself that it's not true.
