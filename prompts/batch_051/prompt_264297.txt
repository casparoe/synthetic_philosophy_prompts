I'm a retired organic chemist, so be gentle: I've come at political philosophy sideways, and the history is probably peiced together from a few popular books and too much Wikipedia. Lately I've been reading about the chartered companies — the East India Company, the Hudson's Bay Company — and I've arrived at a view I've been calling the Residual Company Theory of the State. Here it is.

The modern sovereign state just is a chartered corporation that outgrew its charter. The features everyone treats as specially "political" are really corporate features wearing fancier hats. A state, like a chartered company, holds a monopoly on legitimate force in a territory, raises its own revenue by taxation, runs courts, makes treaties, and has perpetual succession — the state survives the death of monarchs and the turnover of governments the way a company survives the death of its shareholders. The East India Company ran armies, collected taxes, administered courts, and dealt with foreign powers for a century or so before the British government finally took the whole thing into its own hands, and we remember it as a company rather than a state largely because it lost the argument. Sovereign immunity looks to me like limited liability: the people who actually do the state's violence and collect its debts are shielded from being sued in much the way shareholders are shielded from a company's debts. So my positive claim is that "state" and "corporation" never named two different kinds of thing — one is just a corporation that won the right to write its own description. Or, at the risk of a one-liner, sovereignty is limited liability for organized violence.
