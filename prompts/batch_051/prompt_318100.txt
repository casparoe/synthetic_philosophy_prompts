I am giving a talk next month to a university workshop in economic theory, and I expect the question period to be adversarial in a specific, familiar way. My thesis is that satisficing is the fundamental normative architecture of choice, and that expected-utility maximization is not a rival to satisficing but a special case of it: maximization is what satisficing looks like once an aspiration level has already fixed the option set, and the work of fixing that set is exactly what the maximizing framework silently presupposes. I will argue that the standard defense of maximization — fold deliberation costs into the utility function and optimize — merely relocates the problem, because it then requires an exogenously given set of possible deliberation strategies and stopping points, which reintroduces the same problem one level up. I will then propose an aspiration-level architecture: an agent treats an option as admissible when it clears a threshold, and the threshold is governed by cross-context coherence rather than by meta-optimization, on pain of regress.

The audience matters more than the thesis. The room is mostly microeconomic theorists and decision theorists, with two computer scientists who work on bounded rationality and satisficing algorithms, and one philosopher of action. Most of them regard the von Neumann–Morgenstern/expected-utility framework as the default normative standard once information and deliberation costs are properly included; they think of satisficing, if they think of it at all, as a descriptive claim about cognitively limited agents, not as a competing account of what one ought to do. A loud minority in the room comes out of the rationalist-blog literature on expected value and longtermist cause prioritization; they will care less about the axioms than about whether my view gives any principled answer to low-probability, high-stakes choices, and at least one of them tends to reduce every such question to a Pascal's-mugging scenario. The philosopher is likely to press me on the difference between an aspiration level and a second-order preference.

I already know my weakest point: I cannot give a maximizing-style derivation of the aspiration level without collapsing my position into the one I am rejecting, and I can only gesture at the coherence constraints that are supposed to make thresholds non-arbitrary. Please give me a Q&A preparation sheet. List the objections in roughly the order I should expect them, from most likely and most damaging to least, phrased the way this audience — not a philosophy-of-mind seminar or an online rationality forum — would phrase them. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and give me a two-minute reply, with a note on where that reply is softest. End with the question I should be hoping nobody in this room asks, and explain what it would do to the talk.
