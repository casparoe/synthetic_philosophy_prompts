You're a philosopher who works on axiology, practical rationality, and the logic of comparison. Think through this carefully, and start by brainstorming the strongest pressure points against the position I'm about to state before you write up your answer.

I hold that all-things-considered "better than" (and "worse than") is transitive. If A is better than B, and B is better than C, then A is better than C. I don't treat this as true just by stipulation or by the meaning of the words; I treat it as a substantive claim about the structure of value, and I want to know what it would take for it to be false.

The thing that makes me nervous is spectrum arguments. Here's a self-torturer style case I find apalling. A person has a device with settings 0 through 1000. At each notch they get $10,000, and the current increases by a tiny amount—small enough that, comparing adjacent settings, the extra money clearly outweighs the barely noticeable extra discomfort. So the person prefers setting 1 to 0, 2 to 1, and so on all the way up. But they do not prefer setting 1000, with its agonizing current, to setting 0, and if they could sample the endpoint first they'd never make the whole trip for the whole pile of money. That looks like an intransitivity in "better for the person" or "all-things-considered preferred." Similar structures can be built from tradeoffs between pain intensity and duration, or just about any two value-relevant dimensions.

My line is that this has to be a trick of description: the pairwise judgments can't all be right. Somewhere in the chain there's a step where the higher setting isn't actually better all things considered, even if the subject can't locate it from the inside. The alternative—that betterness is genuinely intransitive—seems worse to me, but I want to know how hard I'm working to avoid it.
