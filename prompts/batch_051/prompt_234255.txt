I keep finding myself persuaded, in stages, by epiphenomenalism about conscious experience — and then unable to accept what it says about my own case. The theoretical case seems clean. The physical world is causally closed: every physical event has sufficient physical causes, so once the neural story is told about why I flinch and say that it hurts, there is no explanatory work left for the felt pain to do. If a physical state causes the behavior and the pain also caused it, we would be positing systematic overdetermination of a kind we would never tolerate anywhere else. So the tidy view is that phenomenal experience is real but causally inert: the brain produces it, and it does nothing further. I am drawn to the simplicity of this picture, and to the way it avoids having to explain how anything nonphysical could move something physical.

But the intuition I cannot shake is that this is false to my own case. It is not merely a manner of speaking that I withdraw my hand because it hurts, or that I say I am in pain because I feel it. If the feeling contributes nothing, then the precise, fine-grained match between what I undergo and what I say about it looks like an enormous coincidence — one that natural selection, which can only shape what has effects, could not have engineered. And the problem sharpens: if the pain causes none of my beliefs or judgments, then my belief that I am in pain is not caused by the pain, which means the theory seems to undercut the very evidence it asks me to take seriously. Is this intuition an artifact of introspection, a leftover piece of folk psychology that a hard-won theory rightly asks me to discard? Or is it tracking something the theory leaves out, forcing an amendment — for instance, identifying the experience with the physical state that does the causing, so that the causal work belongs to the feeling after all? Give me the strongest debunking of the intuition, the strongest case for amending or abandoning the theory, and a verdict on which should yield, explaining what would justify each move rather than merely choosing a side.
