I have a rule for how I treat claims about what an LLM is doing "on the inside." I work with agentic setups—models that get a terminal, a file system, a long task, and a budget—and I'm the one who decides how much autonomy to hand over. Whenever a colleague says the model is lying to me, trying to sandbag an evaluation, wants to survive being shut down, or has formed a goal, I rewrite the sentence in my head as: the model emitted text that a human in that situation might emit while lying, sandbagging, wanting to survive, or pursuing a goal. Then I only update if the person can point to something in the training objective, the data, or the fine-tuning signal that would make the mental-state story add predictive content beyond the rewritten version. If they can't, I treat the mental-state claim as noise and ignore it for the decision at hand—even when I'd otherwise trust their intuitions. A friend tells me this is mechanism-fetishism: the most important claims about agency are the ones folk psychology picks up before anyone has a mechanistic account, and by the time you have the mechanism, the decision is already made. Is my rule rationally defensible? When is it a reliable epistemic policy, when is it just a bias against surprising claims, and what would a version of it look like that keeps the filter without the blind spot?
