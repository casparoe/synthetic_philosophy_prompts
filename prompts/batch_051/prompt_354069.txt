Two people in my reading group have spent weeks going in circles about whether lying is morally worse than misleading, and I want the document they should have produced instead of a pile of emails. One of them thinks a straightforward lie is worse because asserting something false breaks a stronger commitment: you vouch for the truth of what you say in a way you don't when you merely implicate or let someone draw a false conclusion. The other thinks that's linguistic etiquette mistaken for ethics: what matters is that you intended to plant a false belief and did, and a carefully engineered half-truth can be worse than a blunt lie because it's sneakier and the victim can't point to a false sentence.

Write it in three parts. (1) A numbered list of agreed claims, non-vacuous but ones both would sign: what lying and misleading each involve, what they share, and what contextual features change the moral picture. (2) A disagreements table where each remaining difference is stated so the person holding it would accept that exact wording, with purely verbal disputes filtered out—so you'd need to keep straight what's said, what's implicated or presupposed, and what the hearer is meant to conclude. (3) The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one of them; for each crux, give a concrete ordinary case (workplace, family, politics; no murderer-at-the-door stock examples) that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each would predict about it. I'd guess two cruxes are whether assertion carries a stronger default entitlement than implicature, and whether sneakiness is itself an aggravator rather than a downstream effect, but add others if they're doing more of the work. The hard part is keeping the agreed claims from going bland while making the disagreements exact instead of just different vibes.
