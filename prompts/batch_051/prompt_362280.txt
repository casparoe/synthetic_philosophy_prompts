OK here's a thing I can't get to line up. Conservation groups routinely drop rat poison on islands to kill every last invasive rat, and that's treated as normal, even heroic management—nobody says it's hubris or playing God. But swap the poison for a gene drive that aims at the exact same outcome, driving that rat population to zero on that island, and suddenly we're talking moratoriums, precautionary bans, irreversible thresholds. Both are deliberate, hard-to-recall interventions in an ecosystem, and the poison is probably less specific about what it kills, not more. So what principle, if any, makes the gene drive that much more objectionable? Run through the obvious candidates—it could spread off the island, it's self-replicating, it can't be revoked, it's 'genetic' rather than chemical—and tell me how many survive the usual fixes, like daisy-chain drives that peter out after some generations or designs that won't cross to related species. If none of them does, is the special dread of gene drives just status-quo bias plus a bias against newer tools, in which case we should drop it? And try the same test on de-extinction: dropping a woolly mammoth proxy into a landscape that hasn't had one in ten thousand years is also an irreversible, self-propagating ecological intervention, but people mostly greet it with excitement instead of terror. Is there a real distinction there, or is the excitement the thing that needs justifying?
