I keep seeing a parallel between Parfit's teletransporter and how running programs get moved between machines, and I cant tell if it's deep or just a metaphor. In the teletransporter case you step in, get scanned and destroyed, and a perfect copy with all your memories and character is built on Mars. Parfit's move is that the Mars person doesnt have to be numerically the same one as the Earth person — what matters for survival is psychological continuity and connectedness, not identity. Computing has a similar-looking move: live migration or checkpoint/restore copies a process or a whole VM's state, kills the source, and resumes it on the target, and we treat it as the same process — same IP, same open connections — even though every page of memory was copied and the original execution was stopped. So both cases seem to split 'what matters for being the same thing' from 'being the same underlying stuff.' But here's where I get stuck. Parfit says the thing that matters can branch: if the scanner doesn't destroy you, and the copy is made anyway, then 'survival' goes to both — there are two continuations of your psychology and that's fine, because identity was never what mattered. In computing, two live copies of the same VM is split-brain — it's exactly the failure case the migration protocol is trying to rule out, and you fence the source machine to make sure it's really dead. So the parallel seems to predict something false on the computing side, or maybe it's showing that 'same process' in computing tracks numerical identity while Parfit's continuity relation doesn't. Which is it? What would have to be true of live migration for the branching case to be okay, and is that even a coherent thing to ask — or do the two sides just have different notions of 'same' from the start?
