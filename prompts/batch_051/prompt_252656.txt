I am applying for a one-year doctoral fellowship and need a two-page research proposal. The topic has recently become personal: my mother has early-stage Alzheimer's disease, and over the past year she has come to run much of her daily cognition through a shared digital notebook that I help maintain. Watching her rely on it has convinced me that the extended mind debate has been built around the wrong cases. Clark and Chalmers's Otto case involves a notebook that is constantly available, automatically endorsed, and entirely controlled by the person whose mind is at issue. Real cognitive scaffolding in dementia is usually partial, intermittently available, and — crucially — maintained by someone else's ongoing agency. My half-formed idea is that the extended mind thesis faces an under-examined dilemma. If one tightens the conditions on what counts as part of the mind enough to avoid the familiar cognitive-bloat objection, the thesis stops covering the very cases that give it its human urgency. If one loosens those conditions, it becomes indistinguishable from the uncontroversial claim that we lean on tools and other people.

I want the proposal to put this dilemma as a precise question: does the extended mind thesis, once weakened enough to avoid cognitive bloat, say anything beyond the weaker embedded-cognition thesis that external resources scaffold but do not constitute cognitive processes — and in particular, can it say anything principled about scaffolds that depend on another agent's continuing choices? The proposal should explain why the existing positions leave this open: why the parity principle, even supplemented by the usual conditions of reliable availability and automatic endorsement, does not settle it, and why the standard replies to cognitive bloat look ad hoc. It should then describe a method that would make progress, and justify that method against the obvious alternatives — close exegesis of the founding texts, exotic science-fiction thought experiments, or trying to settle the matter by appeal to cognitive-science experiments. I want the method to work through a taxonomy of real scaffold failures: availability failures, endorsement failures, and scaffolds whose maintenance is delegated to another person.
