I cover economics, psychology, and public policy, and I am writing a piece about efforts to base regulation on revealed preference rather than surveys or expert judgment. A concrete proposal I am analyzing: a city's behavioral policy unit will test each pilot intervention for six months, then put its continuation to a binding household vote. The two possible outcomes are identical for every household: if the policy continues, each household pays a surcharge equal to its per-household cost; if it ends, each household receives that same amount from the freed-up budget. Half the households get a pay-to-keep ballot, with the default that the policy ends and the money is returned; they must actively vote to continue it and pay. The other half get a paid-to-revert ballot, with the default that the policy continues and they pay; they must actively vote to end it and receive the rebate. The unit certifies an intervention as welfare-improving only if a majority chooses to continue it under both ballot framings. Walk through how this scheme would actually behave. What does it assume about the relationship between choice, utility, and welfare? What would it incentivize policy designers, residents, and local officials to do? Where would it first be gamed or break down, and which failures, if any, could be fixed without abandoning the revealed-preference foundation?
