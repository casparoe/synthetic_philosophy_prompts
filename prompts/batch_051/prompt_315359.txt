I'm a software engineer (compilers and parsers mostly) with no formal philosophy background, and I've been chewing on how natural languages tolerate homonymy — bank, pitch, tone — without everything becoming a garden path or a parse failure. In my day job an ambiguous grammar is a bug you fix, but human languages are full of ambiguity and speakers barely notice. I have a half-formed idea for a six-month independent research proposal (one day a week, maybe a 5,000-word paper) and I need it turned into a tight two-page writeup.

The idea: treat homonymy as an engineering tradeoff. Homonyms save speakers vocabulary and memory, but they cost listeners extra disambiguation work. So maybe a language keeps homonyms where context carries enough information to settle meaning cheaply, and avoids them where context is thin — which would explain why homonymy is so uneven: dense in some meaning neighborhoods, nearly absent in others. I suspect this efficiency story is either deep or a just-so story, and I want the proposal to actually pressure-test it.
