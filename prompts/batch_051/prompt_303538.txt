Act as an expert in just war theory and the ethics of killing, and think carefully before answering. I’ve been stuck on an asymmetry in the laws of war, and I want you to help me figure out if it’s justifiable or just an artifact of states protecting their own soldiers.

Here’s the asymmetry. A regular soldier fights in an aggressive, unjust war. On the battlefield he kills an enemy soldier. If captured, he gets POW status, and his killing is treated as a privileged act of war—not murder, unless he committed atrocities. Now take a civilian in an occupied territory who shoots and kills the same enemy soldier while he's patrolling her town. Under the same legal framework, she’s an unlawful combatant; she gets prosecuted as a criminal, maybe a terrorist. Both killed one combatant, both were trying to advance their side in a war. One gets a legal privilege to kill, the other gets a prison cell.

I know Walzer has this idea of the 'moral equality of soldiers'—that combatants on both sides have the same right to kill because they pose a mutual threat. But that's weird: if the war is unjust, why does the aggressor's soldier get a right to kill, while the civilian resisting the aggression does not?

There’s also a passage in Francisco de Vitoria’s On the Law of War (his 16th-century relection) that feels relevant. Roughly, he says that if a subject is not certain that a war is unjust, he may fight, because he’s bound to obey his prince; but if he doubts, he must examine the causes and may not fight until he’s satisfied. I’m not quoting exactly, but that’s the drift. That seems to push toward excusing ordinary soldiers who don’t know better, while pinning responsibility on leaders. But it says nothing obvious about why the civilian who does know better and resists is worse off than the clueless aggressor.

I want the strongest principle that could generate both parts of the asymmetry: the soldier’s privilege and the civilian’s exclusion. It might be something about public authorization, command responsibility, uniforms, reciprocal risk, or the way law treats roles rather than individuals. Then test it against nearby cases: a mercenary, a conscript fighting under duress, a nonviolent saboteur, an armed child in a rebel group. Start by brainstorming possible rationales before committing. Be honest at the end about whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or given up.
