I've been reading Clifford and James against each other, and two passages keep bugging me because they look like a head-on collision. In Clifford's The Ethics of Belief, right after the shipowner who talked himself into thinking his boat was seaworthy, he generalizes: "The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him." That sure sounds like the only thing that makes a belief okay is the evidence you had when you formed it. Then in James's The Will to Believe, he opens by anouncing he's going to defend "our right willfully to adopt a believing attitude in religious matters, in spite of the fact that our merely logical intellect may not have been coerced." If the logical intellect hasn't been coerced, the evidence didn't force you, so on Clifford's principle you'd have no right to believe—and James is claiming you do. Are these two actually reconcilable, and if so on what terms? Give me the candidate reconciliations (different senses of "right," different scope for the two claims, a distinction Clifford or James draws but doesn't press), and then a verdict on which one is the honest reading and which one is special pleading.
