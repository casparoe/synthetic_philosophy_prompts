One of the most persistent features of common-law legal systems is the doctrine of sovereign immunity: the state, as such, can ordinarily be sued only where it has consented to be sued, even when the claim against it is for an ordinary tort, breach of contract, or violation of a right. The standard doctrinal picture separates the state from the officials who act for it: an official may be answerable for certain wrongs, and particular paths of recovery exist against the state itself through petitions of right or specific statutory waivers, but by default there is no court before which the state itself can be haled. William Blackstone gives the canonical common-law formulation in his Commentaries on the Laws of England, Book III, Chapter 17, where he writes:

"That the king can do no wrong, is a necessary and fundamental principle of the English constitution: meaning only, as has formerly been observed, that, in the first place, whatever may be amiss in the conduct of public affairs is not chargeable personally on the king; nor is he, but his ministers, accountable for it to the people: and, secondly, that the prerogative of the crown extends not to do any injury; for, being created for the benefit of the people, it cannot be exerted to their prejudice."

And shortly afterward:

"The distance between the sovereign and his subjects is such, that it rarely can happen, that any personal injury can immediately and directly proceed from the prince to any private man: and, as it can so seldom happen, the law in decency supposes that it never will or can happen at all; because it feels itself incapable of furnishing any adequate remedy, without infringing the dignity and destroying the sovereignty of the royal person, by setting up some superior power with authority to call him to account."

I want to know what philosophical commitments this practice and its modern descendants actually presuppose about whether the state can do wrong. On one reading, the doctrine presupposes a view of the state as a kind of moral agent that cannot, by definition, act wrongfully—or at least cannot have wrongdoing attributed to it as a state. On another, the doctrine presupposes only a view about jurisdiction and remedies: the state can and does do wrong, but there is no superior power with authority to call it to account, so the law provides indirect remedies and makes liability depend on consent. On a third, it presupposes that the language of state wrongdoing is distributive or metaphorical: wrongs are ultimately done by persons, and the immunity of the state is just the correlate of ministerial accountability. Each of these readings can find support in Blackstone's own sentences.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of state personality, legal responsibility, and corrective justice that the practice presupposes. Say clearly what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits with, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. In particular: if the state is not the kind of thing that can do wrong, what is actually being waived when the state consents to suit, and what is the wronged party receiving reparation for? If, instead, the state can do wrong but is simply immune from coercion, what justifies the asymmetry between private defendants and the state? Is there a coherent position underlying the practice, or does its coherence depend on keeping the ambiguity between the state as a real agent and the state as a legal fiction unresolved?
