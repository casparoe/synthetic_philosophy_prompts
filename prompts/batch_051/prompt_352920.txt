I'm a novelist, and I teach fiction in a small MFA program. One of my students has started ending every workshop argument with the same line: "all creativity is just knob-twiddling." He picked the phrase up from reading Hofstadter, and when pressed he unpacks it as the idea that making something new is mostly a matter of doing variations on an existing theme — you take an existing pattern and turn a few parameters (swap the setting, age the protagonist, change the point of view) and out pops something "new." Last week he used it to shut down a discussion about weather a story he'd turned in was really his own: it was pretty transparently Chekhov's "The Lady with the Dog" with the Yalta resort moved to a lake town in Michigan and the Pomeranian swapped for a toddler. I don't think the slogan does the work he wants it to, but I keep hearing a softer version from a novelist friend — "there's nothing new under the sun" — and I want to be fair to the idea before I push back in class.

Could you lay out the different precise claims that "all creativity is just knob-twiddling" might be making, from the one that's almost trivially true to the one that's radical and probably false? For each, tell me whether it's actually right and why. Then commit: tell me which reading my student actually needs to make his Chekhov reskin count as creative, and whether that reading survives contact with the example. I'm not after a neutral menu — I want definite verdicts on each reading and a clear position on what the slogan can and can't do for him.
