One of my friends has a weirdly settled set of opinions about bringing people into existence, and I've been trying to figure out if there's a single principle underneath them. Here are the cases I've put to her so far. In each one a couple could have a child, and I asked wether they should have it and what reasons there were.

- A child who would have a life that's overall wonderful: lots of pleasure and very little pain. She said there's no obligation to have this child, and the fact that the child would be happy doesn't give the couple any reason to have them. Not having the child isn't a loss or a wrong.
- A child who would have a life that's overall terrible: much more pain than pleasure. She said it would be wrong to have this child.
- A child who would have a life that's only slightly bad overall — a bit more pain than pleasure. She said that's wrong too, but less wrong than the terrible-life case.
- She also said that if she had to choose between talking a stranger out of having a slightly-bad-life child and talking a stranger into having a slightly-good-life child, she'd pick the first; preventing the bad life felt way more important to her than producing the good one.

The next case I want to ask her about is this. A couple could have a child whose life would clearly be worth living overall — in fact very good — but it would include one stretch of severe chronic pain in middle age, fully compensated by pleasure before and after. Would she say there's any reason at all against having this child? Or would she say that, since the life is overall good, the pain doesn't count against having them at all? I'm less interested in her yes-or-no than in the rule she's actually running, which other rules would fit her earlier verdicts just as well, what each of those rules predicts about this new case, and what further question would most clearly tell them apart.
