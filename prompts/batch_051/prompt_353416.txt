In the Scholium to the Definitions in Isaac Newton's Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica, Newton describes a suspended bucket filled with water and set spinning. At first the water remains flat while the bucket rotates; as the water gradually acquires the bucket's motion, its surface rises at the sides and becomes concave; when the bucket is stopped, the water continues to rotate and the concave surface remains for a time. Newton takes this to show that the water's tendency to withdraw from the axis of rotation reveals true, absolute circular motion, even when the water is not moving relative to the bucket.

I am trying to understand whether this experiment actually supports substantivalism about space over relationism. The standard line I have encountered is that the concave surface must be explained by rotation relative to absolute space, and that since the material relations between the water and the bucket can be the same whether or not the surface is concave, relationism cannot account for the difference. But I want to know what would count as a genuinely relational explanation here. If a relationist appeals to the fixed stars, or to the average distribution of matter in the universe, doesn't that just replace one unexplained standard with another? Is that a real explanation of the water's behavior, or merely a change of subject? Under what conditions would a relationist account of the bucket be more than an ad hoc redescription, and does the bucket experiment itself give us any reason to prefer one such account over another?
