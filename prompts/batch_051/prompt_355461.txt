Give me a working definition of envy that correctly classifies the following cases, while keeping envy distinct from jealousy and from a sense of injustice: an employee who wants a colleague's larger year-end bonus when the colleague worked no harder; the same employee wanting the bonus when the colleague demonstrably worked harder and was promoted on merit; the impulse to destroy a good one cannot have rather than acquire it oneself; a child who complains that a sibling got a larger share of dessert; an otherwise comfortable voter's satisfaction when the top rate of income tax rises; a young athlete who admires a champion and wants to reach the same level without wishing the champion ill; and resentment of a promotion that was given to the manager's relative. I suspect the ordinary word runs together at least two things — a moral vice and an accurate perception of unfair advantage — and that John Rawls's idea of envy as excusable when it is a response to unjust institutions is an attempt to draw the line. Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, identify which of your verdicts is least secure, and then say whether envy can ever function as a signal of injustice rather than merely as a vice, or whether it is always at best excusable: a clue about the world, but still a flaw in the person.
