You are a philosopher of institutional design with a particular interest in epistemic incentives and adversarial institutions. Please think slowly and carefully about the proposal below; before you reach an overall judgement, brainstorm the incentives facing each party separately, including the ways a determined participant might exploit them, and only then assess the mechanism as a whole.

I have recently been appointed to the founding committee of a new competitive reasoning league at my university, and we have a free hand to design the format from scratch. I took the role because I am increasingly troubled by how much public argument rewards confidence and fluency rather than getting things right, and I want to know whether a well-designed institution could do better. The league is intended to make argumentation a spectator sport while, we hope, improving rather than corrupting the reasoning of both its participants and its audience. Our provisional design is as follows.

Each fortnight, two two-person teams are assigned, rather than permitted to choose, opposite sides of a disputed proposition drawn from current intellectual and policy debate. Teams receive the proposition a week in advance and may consult anything they like. During the match, each side presents its case and must then answer, in writing and without preparation, the three principal objections supplied by the opposing team; these objections are read out only on the night. The judges are not debate coaches or rhetoricians but working academics, journalists, or practitioners with expertise in the proposition's subject matter.

So far this is conventional. The novel element is the scoring. Judges award marks on three criteria: accuracy, meaning how true, relevant and well-supported each side's claims actually are; responsiveness, meaning how fully each side addresses the strongest version of the other side's case rather than a caricature of it; and calibration, meaning how accurately each side flags its own confidence and identifies the points it is least sure about. A team may be penalised for refusing to concede a point the judges regard as established by the other side. The winner receives prize money on the night. Separately, teams are promoted and relegated between divisions on a long-run rating, and that rating is recomputed each season after the same judges revisit the match a year later, adjusting the accuracy marks in light of how the proposition's empirical content has since developed. A team that argued forcefully for a forecast that turned out false therefore suffers a delayed rating penalty even if it won on the night. We hope this arrangement rewards being right over being persuasive and gives spectators a continuing stake in who was actually correct.

Analyse this mechanism as an institution. What incentives does it create for the debaters, the judges, the founding committee and the spectators respectively? How would an intelligent but unscrupulous team game each scoring criterion, and what second-order effects is the retrospective correction likely to produce? On balance, would the league select for good reasoning or for some other skill? Which failure modes could be fixed by changing a single rule, and which seem intrinsic to the project of making argumentation a spectator sport?
