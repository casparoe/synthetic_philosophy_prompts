I've been rereading Tolstoy's What Is Art? (the Maude translation) for an aesthetics class and I keep going back and forth on whether his central "infection" claim is a definition or a testable psychological hypothesis. He writes:

"If a man infects another or others, directly, immediately, by his appearance, or by the sounds he gives vent to at the very time he experiences the feeling; if he causes another man to yawn when he himself cannot help yawning, or to laugh or cry when he himself is obliged to laugh or cry, or to suffer when he himself is suffering—that does not amount to art. Art begins when one person, with the object of joining another or others to himself in one and the same feeling, expresses that feeling by certain external indications."

That sure looks like an empirical claim about what art is doing: someone encodes a feeling, and the audience catches the same feeling instead of merely recognizing it or enjoying the object. But I can't tell how you'd test the "same feeling" part without already assuming his definition of art.

So here's what I'd like you to work through. Concretely design a test that could seperate genuine infection in Tolstoy's sense from (a) a person merely identifying which emotion a work is meant to convey and (b) a person simply liking or being stimulated by the object. Be specific about what you'd use as stimuli, what you'd measure, and which pattern of results would count for Tolstoy and which against. Then say exactly what a positive result would and wouldn't settle—for example, if you got physiological or behavioral evidence that receivers converge on some emotion, would that show the artist's own feeling was transmitted, or just that people caught something from each other or from the work's conventions? And is his later claim that only certain feelings (the "religious" ones, roughly) make for good art part of the same testable thesis, or is it a different claim that no experiment touches?
