I'm giving a workshop talk at a public-law and political-theory workshop attended mostly by constitutional lawyers and deliberative democrats, plus one emergency-management scholar who works on continuity-of-government planning. My thesis is that the practice of the designated survivor is an illegitimate lottery for supreme executive power: it leaves the statutory line of succession formally untouched while vesting near-presidential authority — up to and including strategic command authority — in an official chosen for cabinet and congressional proximity rather than for executive aptitude, through a mechanism the public is deliberately shielded from deliberating about. The argument runs as follows. The continuity rationale is doing work its defenders never cash out: it treats the risk of a decapitation strike as a reason to lower the bar for succession and to keep the operative line opaque, without showing why preserving the office demands either. The selection criteria fail the very standards the continuity rationale presupposes — authorization, accountability, and fitness for office — because cabinet secretaries are picked for policy, managerial, and political reasons, none of which tests for the cognitive and temperamental demands of chief executive decision-making. And the operative secrecy sits awkwardly with the publicity condition on democratic legitimacy: a democracy may bind itself in advance to extraordinary succession rules, but normally only when the rules and their selection criteria are public. My constructive proposal is a standing, publicly legislated continuity council, ranked in advance for executive competence, whose members are barred from simultaneous cabinet office and receive continuity-of-government training rather than being pulled from a ballroom as a ritual afterthought.

Assume the audience accepts that continuity of government matters and is not looking for a policy briefing; they will probe the legitimacy and workability of the argument from their own disciplinary commitments. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight to ten objections I will actually get from this audience — not the generic "isn't this just antidemocratic?" — ordered by how likely they are to be raised and how much damage each could do, each phrased the way a constitutional lawyer, a deliberative democrat, or an emergency planner would actually put it. For each, give a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. End with the question I should most hope nobody asks, and say precisely why it would be hard.
