Two of my postgrads got into a proper row after this week's seminar and I made a bit of a hash of refereeing it. The background: Priya presented a new experimental design at the seminar. Dan was in the room, asked a couple of questions, and has since run a pilot of it and written it up with no acknowledgement at all. Tom defended Dan, Priya was livid, and their exchange went roughly like this. (1) Priya said Dan's write-up is her idea, and using it without even a footnote is theft of intellectual property. (2) Tom said you can't steal an idea, because theft means depriving the owner of the thing; Dan's knowing the idea hasn't stopped Priya knowing it, using it, or publishing it, so what has she actually lost? (3) Priya said that was treating an idea as if it were a bike. The design is the product of her labour — she'd done the work, the way Locke describes coming to own the field you've cultivated — and Dan has taken the fruits of that labour without compensation; if that's allowed, why does anyone deserve credit for anything? (4) Tom said credit is a social practice, not a property right. Ideas aren't rivalrous: two people can hit on the same idea independantly, there's no scarcity, so there's no need for a fence; what Dan perhaps owes is a citation, but that's an academic norm about honesty, not a title deed. (5) Priya answered that this proves too much, since a trade secret is just information and a company doesn't lose the formula when it's leaked, yet we still treat lifting it as a kind of taking. If non-rivalry rules out property, half of intellectual property law is just confused. (6) Tom said the trade-secret case is different because it depends on confidence: the formula is kept secret, and the wrong is breaching trust or inducing someone to breach it, whereas Priya voluntarily presented at an open seminar; once she'd let the information out, the only remaining claim was attribution, which is about provenance, not ownership. (7) Priya said that's an admission there's a wrong after all — misrepresenting where the idea came from — and that wrong only makes sense if she had some prior claim to it; otherwise Dan's account is simply true and there's nothing for honesty to bite on. (8) Tom said provenance is just a historical fact, like a journalist naming a source; you don't have to own a fact for someone to be dishonest in suppressing it.

I'd like you to go through it move by move: who is right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches or concedes too quickly, and what — if anything — both of them are missing. Give me ratings for the individual moves and a view on who is more right overall, if you can.
