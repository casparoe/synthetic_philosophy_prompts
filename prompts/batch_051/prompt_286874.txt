You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaphysics and the philosophy of causation, and you approach the task as someone who has thought carefully about why causal notions have resisted both reduction and elimination. I am a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on what any theory of causation has to deliver, and I want to hand the students a specification of the explanatory target before we compare candidate theories.

Write the desiderata that any adequate account of causation—regularity, counterfactual, process or transference, interventionist, dispositional, agency, primitivist, or eliminativist—must satisfy in order to count as a serious contender. Frame the result as a list of requirements, not as summaries of the theories. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say whether the requirement is non-negotiable for any theory or is instead a preference of a particular camp.

Make sure the list covers at least the following, among others you think are needed: symmetric overdetermination; late and early preemption; causation by omission and double prevention; the distinction between causes and mere background conditions; the direction or asymmetry of causation; and proportionality or specificity in causal attribution. For each of these, describe a concrete scenario brief enough to test intuitions and exact enough that a theory cannot dodge it by redescription.

I also want the list itself to do some work. Where a subset of desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, or where satisfying one makes satisfying another very costly, say so explicitly—that is the part I most want to see. In particular, address the tension between wanting a theory that counts omissions and absences as genuinely causal and wanting a theory that locates causation in a concrete process or transfer; preemption puts related pressure on difference-making accounts. Tell me which single requirement would eliminate or seriously embarrass the largest number of contenders, and briefly explain why. A short scorecard of how the main families fare against the final list is welcome, but keep the emphasis on the requirements and their motivation.

Before you write the final list, brainstorm candidate requirements and test each against the cases above, plus any further cases that expose merely parochial stipulations; discard anything that only one camp would insist on unless you flag it as such. Do not include a bibliography or citations, and do not lean on named views; each requirement should be motivated from the case described.
