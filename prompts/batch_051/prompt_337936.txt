I’ve been reading John Locke’s Two Treatises of Government on my own, and a sentence in his preface keeps coming back to me:

> there cannot be done a greater mischief to prince and people, than the propagating wrong notions concerning government

A friend of mine seems to have built her whole approach to constitutional crises out of that sentence, though she’s never put her rule into words. She consistently cares less about the immediate harm an official causes than about the precedent the action sets for how people think about power afterwards. I’ve kept a list of her verdicts on earlier situations. She approved of a regional governor who, during catastrophic flooding, ordered an evacuation and commandeered supplies without any legal basis, because he publicly announced that he was acting outside the law and asked the assembly to ratify his actions afterwards. She rejected a president who postponed a constitutionally required election for three weeks during a transport breakdown, even though he did so openly and temporarily, because she said any executive touching the electoral process, even in a crisis, teaches the wrong principle. She approved of a mayor who imposed a curfew when the council could not meet after an earthquake, because the mayor was the most accountable local official and the council later ratified the order. She approved of a defence minister who detained suspected saboteurs without statutory authority while the courts were not sitting, but only because the minister published a statement saying the detention was extra-legal and would be tested before the next sitting of the legislature. And she rejected a central bank governor who, without authority, guaranteed deposits during a banking panic, saying financial stability was not the sort of harm that justified bypassing the legislature and the institutional damage would outlast the panic.

The new case I want to predict her view on is this: after parliament has been dissolved and before new elections can be held, the caretaker government imposes movement restrictions and requisitions private clinics and ventilators to manage a serious outbreak of disease. No statute authorises these measures. The government publishes a legal opinion saying the constitution already contains an implied public-health emergency power, so the measures are lawful and need no retrospective ratification. Some ministers openly say they are setting a precedent for future executives; others privately acknowledge there is a gap but publicly maintain the interpretation. What would she say, if she wanted to be consistent with her past verdicts? Which earlier case is the closest analogue? If her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine this one, I’d like to know which rules fit her record, what each rule predicts about the new case, and which further case would settle the difference. I’m less interested in your own constitutional view than in the prediction and the reasoning behind it. Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who have studied this topic, rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
