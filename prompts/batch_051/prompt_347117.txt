Design a classroom activity for an undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar, 12 to 16 students, 60 minutes, whose point is to make participants feel the pull of the question behind Hofstadter's Ant Fugue: whether an ant colony can be said to have a mind, or to know something, even though each individual ant merely follows dumb local rules. Do not lecture up front, and withhold the names "Aunt Hillary" and "Ant Fugue" and any vocabulary like "emergence" until the debrief; the idea should surface in what the participants do.

Make the centerpiece a runnable simulation in which each student plays an ant restricted to a few local rules, with no natural-language planning and no view of the whole system. A natural frame is the two-trail pheromone setup: two fixed routes of different lengths around the room, each dispatched "ant" returns and lays one unit of pheromone on its route, and later ants choose a route with probability proportional to the accumulated pheromone tally, using an explicit randomizing device. The game should be tuned so that within the hour the group visibly converges on the shorter route without any single ant ever comparing the two routes. Specify the exact map, the local rules, what the facilitator records, the round timing, and what participants are allowed to do. Then provide a debrief that first asks participants to describe what "the group" did, and only then maps what happened onto the fable's central contrast: individual signal-following versus colony-level pattern, and whether the colony's convergence amounts to knowing which route is shorter even though no ant did. Say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run — participants coordinating verbally, someone quietly estimating the route lengths, or pheromone noise set too low or too high for convergence within the hour — and how the facilitator should handle each failure. Be explicit about which element of the Ant Fugue the activity cannot reproduce, such as its treatment of symbols and self-awareness, and explain how the facilitator should bridge that gap in the debrief rather than through a preliminary lecture.
