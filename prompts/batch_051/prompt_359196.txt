My dad needs surgery — fairly routine, but not trivial, and the anaesthetist rated him "moderate risk". We're choosing between St Anne's, the big teaching hospital, and City General, our local one. My brother found the league tables: over the last year City General reports an 81% success rate for this operation and St Anne's reports 67%. On that basis he says City General is the obvious choice.

My aunt (mum's sister) went digging and found the hospital group's own audit, which splits patients into "high risk" and "low risk" before surgery. The numbers are:

- Low risk: St Anne's 96% success, City General 94%.
- High risk: St Anne's 55%, City General 50%.
- Overall: St Anne's 67%, City General 81%.

Her line is that St Anne's is better for every kind of patient, and the overall figure only looks worse for St Anne's because it's the specialist centre that takes all the complex cases, while City General mostly does the straightforward ones.

The argument went like this:

(1) My brother: "That audit has to be wrong. If St Anne's is better in both groups, it would have to be better overall. Averages can't just flip like that."

(2) My aunt: "Of course they can, because the groups are different sizes. St Anne's did 700 high-risk and 300 low-risk, City General did 300 high-risk and 700 low-risk. The overall figure is just a weighted average, and St Anne's average is dragged down by having more hard cases."

(3) My brother: "Fine, but dad isn't a row in a spreadsheet. We don't actually know which box he's in — the anaesthetist said moderate risk, and your audit only has two boxes. The league table is the only number that doesn't invite us to guess a box for him."

(4) My aunt: "You don't need to know the box. Whichever box he lands in, St Anne's wins inside it. If he's low risk, 96 beats 94. If he's high risk, 55 beats 50. So St Anne's is the safer bet whatever his risk turns out to be."

(5) My brother: "That's only true if the boxes are trustworthy. Maybe City General's high-risk patients are sicker in ways the audit doesn't measure, so the comparison inside each box is still unfair. The overall league table at least doesn't pretend to slice patients up."

(6) My aunt: "But the overall table is more unfair, not less, because it compares a hospital full of straightforward cases with one full of complex ones. If you distrust the boxes, fine, but then you have even less reason to trust the raw league table."

They looked at me and I honestly couldn't give them a ruling.

Please adjudicate this properly: go through each move (1)–(6) and say who is right about what, where each person's reasoning is sound and where it breaks. Then say which hospital the numbers as presented actually support for my dad, and what extra information would most change that conclusion.
