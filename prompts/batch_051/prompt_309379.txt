I'm a senior analyst in a state budget office. Every few weeks an agency sends us a model-based ask: a revenue forecast, a projected caseload, an estimate of what a new program will save, a staffing model. The models almost always have assumptions that are, on their face, absurd—nobody changes behavior when tax rates change, housing markets clear instantly, the population mixes at random, there's no administrative delay. I don't have the math to audit the equations, and I don't have the authority to tell them to go build a better model. What I do have to produce is a one-page note on how much weight an elected official or a deputy director should put on the model's headline number. Stakes are real: we're moving money and cutting or expanding services on the basis of these notes. The guidance I get from above is usually vauge about what counts as good enough.

I've read enough to know there's a genuine dispute here. Milton Friedman argued that you shouldn't judge a model by the realism of its assumptions, only by whether it predicts well. But in policy work we often can't rerun the past, and sometimes the model is being used to make the very decision that will change the behavior the model assumed away. So "predicts well" is doing a lot of work, and I don't know how much of the Friedman-style position survives contact with our constraints.

Write me a reusable decision procedure for this, grounded in whatever that Friedman-style position actually commits you to—not just "be skeptical of assumptions" and not an essay about models. I want a checklist a generalist analyst (me, and my coworkers who have even less background in this) can run in under an hour. It should ask questions in order, say what counts as a good-enough answer to move on, say where the procedure should stop with a "don't rely on this," and mark the steps where a person has to make a judgment call that no checklist can absorb or where the real question is one of values rather than facts. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on three cases our office actually sees in some form:

1. A tax revenue forecast that assumes taxpayers won't change their behavior in response to the rate change, used to fund a multiyear program.
2. An epidemic model that assumes the population mixes at random and has no age structure, used to size a vaccination campaign.
3. A pretrial risk assessment model that has detailed, realistic individual-level variables and looks much more careful than the others, but has never been validated on our state's population.

I want at least one of these to come out in a way I won't like, and I want you to say which one and why. Please answer in American English.
