Suppose a neurotechnology company obtains regulatory approval for an implanted visual-cortex interface that can give a user, on demand, a stable experience of a forbidden color: a hue reported as simultaneously reddish and greenish, or simultaneously bluish and yellowish, in the unitary way that orange is simultaneously reddish and yellowish, rather than as a spatial mixture, a blend, or an afterimage. The engineering is not in question: assume the device works as described. It produces the hue by directly driving color-opponent channels in a joint pattern that no ordinary light source or external scene can deliver, and it is calibrated so that normally sighted users converge on the same hue and can reliably distinguish it from every hue they have seen before. A session lasts about ten minutes and then the hue fades. A monthly subscription provides unlimited sessions and a palette of six such hues, each produced by a different ratio of otherwise incompatible opponent-channel activations.

Give a committed analysis of how this would actually play out rather than merely whether the device is a good idea. Take definite positions and defend them; do not present a menu of possibilities. Cover at least the following: the initial user base and how adoption spreads; what users do with the experience that they could not do with ordinary color or with psychoactive drugs; whether users can later recall, imagine, or dream the hue, and whether a stable vocabulary for it develops; whether repeated exposure alters ordinary color perception, aesthetic preferences, or afterimage behavior; how the company maintains demand once novelty fades; and where the scheme is first gamed or exploited, whether by users, clinicians, competitors, or the company itself. Then identify the most serious failure mode and explain whether it can be fixed without destroying the basic idea. Write in American English.
