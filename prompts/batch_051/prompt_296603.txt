My friend has become the person our group chat pings whenever an "AI companion" news story makes the rounds, and over the last year she's handed down verdicts on a bunch of specific cases. I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule behind them, because it's clearly not just "deception is bad," and I could use a second pass. I'm mostly interested in what principle you'd attribute to her, not the object-level answers, but there is one new case I genuinely can't predict her on.

Earlier verdicts, roughly in the order she gave them:

- A lonely 70-year-old tells a clearly labeled companion chatbot "I love you" every morning, and says it keeps her from calling her kids in a panic at 3am. Verdict: fine, probably good, with a shrug.
- A company sells a flirty bot that's engineered to seem human — fake typos, "sorry, my phone died," delayed replies — and never flat-out says it's an AI, even when users ask. Verdict: not fine, especially for lonely users.
- A guy with social anxiety rehearses asking out a crush on a bot that announces it's a roleplay partner. Verdict: fine, maybe even useful.
- A "grief bot" marketed to a widow that imitates her dead husband's texting style, trained on his messages. The landing page clearly says it's a simulation, but it's still built to feel like him. Verdict: not fine, even though there's no deception. She was weirdly intense about this one.
- A chatbot used as an interactive journal that asks clarifying questions and makes no companionship claims. Verdict: fine.
- A fully disclosed companion app that uses casino-style variable rewards to maximize daily engagement, sold mostly to chronically ill people stuck at home. Verdict: not fine, but she puts the blame on the company, not the user.

Here's the new case. A fully disclosed romantic companion bot; the user knows it has no feelings and can't reciprocate. The person still winds up attached and gradually stops reaching out to human friends because the bot is easier to talk to. No deception anywhere, and the company isn't doing anything manipulative with engagement — it's a well-made, honest product.

What would she say: fine, not fine, or would she need more details? And what rule makes all of her earlier verdicts come out right, or if nothing does, what's the largest consistant subset and which verdict would you throw out? If there are two rules that fit what she's said so far about equally well, what extra case should I actually ask her about to tell them apart?
