A friend and I spent most of the afternoon arguing about whether incorruptibility is always something to admire, and we parted with the disagreement sharper than when we began. He wanted to defend the ordinary view that incorruptibility just is a virtue: the judge who will not take a bribe, the inspector who will not falsify a report, the official who cannot be flattered into signing off. I kept pressing him on cases where a person is completely unmoved by money, flattery, and fear and yet seems to fail in a different way — a judge whose verdicts are never for sale but who is equally unmoved by a defendant's particular misery; an official so committed to the clean file that he enforces a rule where following it plainly harms the person standing in front of him. We ended up agreeing only that we didn't have a clear picture of what an account of incorruptibility is even supposed to explain.

So here is my request. I want the desiderata for a theory of incorruptibility: a specification of what any adequate account has to deliver before it earns the name, where adequacy is being judged against ordinary institutional life — the evaluation of actual officeholders, colleagues, and the arrangements that make them — rather than against the description of a moral hero. To anchor the list, please settle or at least sharpen the following rather than dissolving them. What exactly must the incorruptible person be unmoved by, and how does the theory identify which considerations are corrupting without simply stipulating that they are the ones the virtuous person resists? What separates incorruptibility from integrity, courage, impartiality, and mere insensitivity to one's surroundings, and from the rigidity that lets someone keep clean hands in the narrow sense while failing the trust for which the clean hands mattered? The account should get the following cases right: the judge who has never taken a bribe and never will, yet whose decisions are untouched by mercy or attention to particulars; the official who bends a minor rule out of plain care for a friend's child and is called corrupt for it; the politician who is immune to donors but entirely owned by his own need to be admired; and the whistleblower who stays inside the institution and quietly refuses rather than resigning or exposing it. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, and say which requirements you would defend as non-negotiable and which you suspect are parochial. Finally, flag any pair of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. My suspicion from the argument is that the demand to be genuinely responsive to particular persons and the demand to be unmoved by personal considerations may be exactly such a pair, and I want to know whether the list itself should be the conclusion — that incorruptibility is not one trait but a loosely connected family of traits that we have been lumping together because they all happen to oppose bribery.
