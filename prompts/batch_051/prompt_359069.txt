Act as a professional philosopher of decision theory who is also conversant with the behavioral-economics literature on the sunk cost fallacy. I want you to think carefully and avoid false reconciliations.

I find the normative argument against honoring sunk costs about as compelling as an argument can be. Once a cost is behind you, it cannot be affected by anything you now do; the only thing that matters for a forward-looking decision is the future stream of costs and benefits. Arkes and Blumer, in their work on the psychology of sunk cost, found that people systematically violate this principle—for example, those who paid more for a theater subscription attended more performances even when the plays were no longer attractive to them, as if using the time to recover the money already spent. The standard lesson is that this is a bias, and that rational agents treat bygones as bygones. Yet I cannot make myself fully believe it. When I have poured months into a project that has turned unpromising, or paid for an expensive meal I no longer want, abandoning the project or leaving the food feels like treating my past investment as disposable—as if my earlier self acted for nothing. Honoring the sunk cost feels, on reflection, like a form of integrity, continuity, or respect for the person who made the earlier decision, and not simply like an emotional accounting error.

I want the strongest possible version of both sides. First, give me the most forceful case for the theory that honoring sunk costs is always irrational, or at least always a mistake: what exactly is the principle, what does it commit me to, and how should it handle my intuitions of waste, self-respect, and continuity? Then give me the strongest case that the intuition is tracking something the theory leaves out—whether it be a virtue, a deontic commitment, a rational strategy under uncertainty about my own future willpower, or something else. Finally, give me a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked and dropped, should the theory be amended or restricted, or is there a reading on which they are compatible? I especially want to know what would justify each of these moves, not just which side you would pick.
