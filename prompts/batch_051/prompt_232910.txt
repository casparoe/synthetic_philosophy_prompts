I'm staying at a forest monastery in northern Thailand while finishing some remote work, and I keep getting pulled into a long-running debate among the monks about whether the monastery can accept a cryptocurrency donation. The relevant rule in their code of discipline (the Vinaya) is one the monks translate as: "If a monk receives gold or silver, or gets someone else to receive it for him, or allows it to be set aside for his use, he commits an offense in which the money must be forfeited." In practice the monastery is strict about this: no monk will even handle coins or paper money, and the abbot let's a lay steward run a bank account that pays for electricity and medicine.

Recently a supporter offered to donate quite a lot of Bitcoin, to be held in a wallet whose private key the lay steward would control until it is exchanged for supplies. The younger monk, who worked as a software developer before ordaining, says the rule can't reach this: a seed phrase is just a string of words, and in any case no monk would be "receiving" anything, since the steward holds the key. The older monk says that's sophistry. Bitcoin is money in the only sense the rule cared about, except you can hoard it invisibly and check the price five times a day, so the attachment it invites is worse, not better. I care less about the practical answer than the interpretive one. Give me the textual reading of the rule, the purposive reading (not handling money, staying free of wealth, not feeding greed), and a guess at what the monks who first framed the rule would have said if someone had explained a blockchain to them. Then say which approach should govern here and why, given that this tradition sometimes defends rules by their consequences and sometimes treats them as binding regardless of consequences.
