I'm trying to reverse-engineer a made-up parenting-licensing board's logic and I want a second read. In this country you need a license to raise a child, and the board has to decide whether applicants pass. Their recorded decisions so far:

- They approved an unemployed couple living with relatives, on the condition they complete a basic child-development course. The couple planned public school, routine medical care, and lots of contact with extended family, and there was no history of abuse or neglect.

- They rejected a couple where one partner had a child removed eight years earlier after repeated physical abuse, even though that partner has since completed a treatment program, a current psych report rates them low risk, and the new partner seems very stable. The board said the removal history by itself was enough.

- They rejected a couple with no abuse history who wanted to raise a child on a remote off-grid homestead, no formal schooling, no same-age friends, no contact with outsiders until adulthood. Their written plan said the child would be trained in subsistence farming and the family's religious tradition, and that children should not be given choices before they know how to choose correctly. The majority opinion kept coming back to the idea of an open future, saying the child would reach adulthood with most life paths already closed.

- They approved, with two years of monitoring, a single woman with an old shoplifting conviction and a period of unstable housing. The board stressed that she had held the same job for three years, finished counseling, and her plan included a standard school curriculum and freedom for the child to join whatever activities the kid wants.

- They rejected a couple whose older child had run away twice after reporting that they locked him in his room for missing prayers. A psychologist said the new household would probably be safe, but the board said it wasn't willing to take that chance.

Now the new application. Two former professional tennis players want to raise their daughter as a tennis prodigy. The plan: from age three she's in daily training, by age eight it's about thirty hours a week, with a private tutor covering reading, writing, and just enough math to keep her eligible for junior competition. No school teams, no non-tennis clubs, and they'll strongly discourage friendships that interfere with training. They say she can quit any time and they'd support a switch to normal school, but by then she'd enter years behind academically and socially. The family is warm, financially secure, no abuse or neglect in any ordinary sense, and a sports psychologist thinks the plan gives her a real shot at a pro career but will cost her most of a normal childhood.

What should the board decide if it wants to be consistent with itself? Which past case is the closest analog? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine this, what principle would the board be adopting with each ruling, and what later case would cleanly seperate the principles? I'm less interested in the exact rule they'd write down than in seeing the two or three rules that fit the record, where they disagree on the tennis case, and which one you'd actually bet on.
