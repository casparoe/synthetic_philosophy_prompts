Here is a parallel I cannot settle. In reinforcement learning, the reward signal is a scalar with a sign and a magnitude, set by the designer, that the agent is trained to maximize over time; nothing in the formalism requires the signal to be felt, and the setup is compatible with controllers no one would regard as sentient. In affective psychology, valence is a dimension of felt experience — pleasantness or unpleasantness — with an intrinsic good-or-bad character, and it is at least conceptually distinct from the incentive salience that makes a stimulus wanted; the two can come apart in ways that matter for animal behavior. Debates about whether RL agents can suffer seem to run these together, treating a negative reward or a negative reward prediction error as if it were itself a bad experience. Is the identification between RL reward and hedonic valence deep, partial, or a category error? Act as a philosopher of cognitive science who also knows the RL literature. Before settling on a verdict, brainstorm two or three candidate mappings between the formal roles of reward in RL and the psychological roles of valence, salience, and reinforcement, then choose the mapping that holds up best. Spell out what would have to be true of an RL system for facts about felt valence — and therefore welfare — to transfer to it, and name at least one prediction the identification makes that you think comes out false. Throughout, separate what is reasonably settled in affective science and RL from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
