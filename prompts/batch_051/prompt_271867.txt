I just came from a three-hour argument with a friend about the German thing of waiting at a red light at 3am with no cars around versus the American thing of deciding for yourself. He's German and kept saying the rule-following is what makes institutions trustworthy and that my whole objection is just American chaos with a conscience. I kept saying no, a decent person has to check the rule against their own judgment or you get people doing harm because "it's the rule." We got nowhere, but on the drive home I realized the argument was basically my work life.

I'm a shift supervisor at a chemical plant. We have a few hundred written operating and safety procedures, and every couple of days an operator wants to deviate: they think the procedure doesn't fit the actual situtation, or it'll waste an hour, or sometimes they think following it to the letter is actualy more dangerous right now. I'm the one who says yes or no, and every deviation has to be logged and can be reviewed by the plant manager, so the procedure has to leave a paper trail a non-technical boss can follow. The stakes: allow too many deviations and the safety culture rots and a regulator eventually nails us; allow too few and someone follows a rule straight into a bad outcome and says "I was told to follow the rule." No philosopher on staff, the operators won't read theory, and I need to make most of these calls in under ten minutes.

So turn whichever insights the two folk moralities actually have into a reusable decision procedure I can run before authorizing a deviation. I want a checklist or a set of tests in order, in words the night-shift lead operator can use at 2am. Show me which distinctions from the argument have to survive the translation and which ones are too fancy to keep. Build in the hard cases: the rule is safety-critical in general but this instance is genuinely different; the operator's "conscience" objection is really convenience; the rule conflicts with another rule; the rule is outdated or wrong on its face; one particular operator keeps having objections. Apply it to at least three worked examples, and make sure one of them comes out in a way I'm not going to like. Mark the steps where the procedure can't decide and a human has to. Then tell me whether what you've built ends up more German or more American, and whether one of the two instincts actually has the better of it in a setting like mine — or whether that's the wrong question to ask.
