I am a high-school student presenting later this week to my school's upper-level philosophy seminar. The seminar has spent the term on early modern philosophy and has worked carefully through Locke on ideas and perception, but Berkeley was assigned only as a brief closing unit, so for most members he has collapsed into the slogan that to be is to be perceived.

I am defending the following thesis: Berkeley's master argument — the argument that one cannot think of an object as existing unconceived, because in thinking of it one has conceived it — succeeds only on a weak reading. It shows that an object cannot be represented within thought under the description existing with no one conceiving of it; it does not show that such an object is impossible. Berkeley reaches immaterialism only by adding a premise the argument itself cannot bear, namely that what cannot be conceived cannot be possible, and that premise is in tension with the account of ideas and meaning he gives elsewhere.

My reconstruction has three moves: first, distinguish the two readings of the argument, one about the unthinkability of an unconceived object and one about its impossibility; second, grant the first reading but insist it is a fact about the limits of conceiving rather than about what the world contains; third, show that moving to the second reading requires the conceivability-possibility principle, which sits poorly with Berkeley's rejection of abstract ideas and his account of what a finite mind can meaningfully call possible.

The audience is not one bloc. Several members are working Lockean representationalists: they hold that perception is mediated by ideas that resemble mind-independent causes, and they will object that my second move already assumes the world can outrun thought, which is the very thing at issue. Two members favor the canonical reply that the master argument trades on confusing the act of conceiving with the object conceived; I expect them to say I have changed the subject from the thing to the thought of the thing. A smaller group is sympathetic to Berkeley and is happy to grant the conceivability test for possibility; they will press me hardest on the claim that this test conflicts with his rejection of abstract ideas, since they read him as allowing a kind of conception through images rather than through abstract representations.

Please write my prep sheet for the question period. I need the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each group would actually put them, each marked for how serious it is and whether it rests on a misreading of the master argument. For each objection, give me the briefest reply that could work and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede at the outset without surrendering the thesis, and include the objection I should most hope no one raises, with an honest account of why my position is exposed there. The reply that must land is the one showing why the limits of conceiving do not by themselves legislate for the world.
