I've been kicking around an essay idea about how heuristic arguments use independence assumptions, and I'd love some pushback. The working title is: The presumption of independence is a rule for changing models, not a probability assignment. Here's the sketch.

Most heuristic reasoning in math treats events as independent until proven otherwise: divisibility by different primes, the digits of pi, whatever. The usual gloss says we're not claiming independence literally holds; we're just using it as a tractable approximation, and if the heuristic prediction fails, that's evidence of a hidden coupling. I want to make that gloss precise as a nonmonotonic rule: from P(A) and P(B), plus the absence of any known mechanism linking A and B, infer P(A and B) = P(A)P(B). The inference is defeasible—if you later learn A and B share a common cause, the default gets retracted. So far, just default logic.

But here's the part I think is actually interesting. The condition "absence of a known mechanism" isn't a fact about A and B; its a fact about the reasoner's model of A and B. So the rule is sensitive to the structure of the model, not just to the probabilities the model assigns. That sets it apart from the maximum-entropy story, where given only the marginals, the max-entropy distribution just is the product distribution. Max entropy is a rule about which distribution to pick given constraints. Heuristic independence, by contrast, is a rule about which model to pick, and a model with an edge between A and B can still be numerically indistinguishable from the product model.

Example: I know two coins were struck by the same factory on the same press. Given only the marginals (fair, fair), max entropy says their tosses are independent. But a heuristic reasoner would be pretty reluctant to multiply probabilities here, not because the numbers force dependence but because the shared manufacturing process is a known mechanism that could couple the tosses. So you can have the same marginal constraints and still not grant the independence default—the difference is a model-level fact about which mechanisms you know. Conversely, you could have a fully connected model whose parameters happen to factor, and the default still shouldn't fire even though independence holds numerically. That makes heuristic independence an intensional notion, in something like the logic sense.

My proposal is to formalize the rule as a preference over factor graphs: among models compatible with your current knowledge, prefer the one with the fewest edges, and take that to be the independence default. Then the interesting open questions are (a) whether this collapses into algorithmic independence or minimum description length, or whether "mechanism" is doing separate work, and (b) how to handle colliders and conditioning-on-common-effects cases, where the graph structure itself induces dependence that isn't an edge.

I'm worried about a couple of things. First, is this actually different from just having a prior over models that penalizes complexity? If its equivalent to some MDL setup, the whole "intensional" bit might be melodrama. Second, does the factor-graph version get the retraction behavior right? In default logic you can retract a conclusion when new premises arrive, but a prior-over-models picture just conditionalizes—is anything lost there? Third, is my coin example doing real work or is it too toy?

I'd especially like comments on the essay's accuracy and structure: which section should go, where you'd push back hardest, and whether the max-entropy contrast is fair. Also suggestions for a non-math example where the model-dependence actually changes the heuristic's output.
