So I just spent a couple hours arguing with a friend about Pascal's stuff on force and justice — the passage in the Pensées where he says justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyranny, and that since you can't make the just strong, you have to make the strong just. My friend thinks this collapses into pure might-makes-right, and he laid out a chain of reasoning that I couldn't find a hole in. It ends somewhere I really don't want to go. Here's the argument, roughly as he put it:

(1) Justice by itself can't govern. People run on interests and passions, and their judgments about what's just flat out conflict with each other, so bare justice never gets a stable grip on anyone.

(2) Force by itself can govern, but not legitimately. Rule by nothing but force is just tyranny.

(3) So any stable, legitimate order has to unite justice and force.

(4) And there's only one stable way to unite them, which is the move Pascal himself makes: we can't make what is just strong, so we make what is strong just. The rules of whoever actually holds power end up being called just.

(5) So in any real society, "just" just means: whatever the effective force enforces.

(6) If that's right, then there's no gap between a law's being just and its being enforced. Any regime that can keep itself in power is just by definition.

(7) But then the whole practice of calling a law or a regime unjust is empty. "Justice" becomes another word for power, and a victorious tyranny can't be criticized at all.

So he says Pascal's position proves to much: it licenses calling any effective tyranny just, which is absurd, and that means the position refutes itself. I keep feeling like there's a slide somewhere but I can't make the objection stick. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can.
