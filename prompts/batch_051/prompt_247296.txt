I've been thinking on my own about why the law often lets old wrongs go unpunished, and I keep hitting a question I can't settle: once you strip away everything that merely tends to come along with the passage of time, is there anything left that matters morally?

Here is the question I'd like you to answer. Take two wrongful acts that are exactly alike in kind and seriousness. In the first, the wrongdoer is caught and punished one year after the act. In the second, the wrongdoer is caught and punished thirty years after the act. Hold everything else fixed: the evidence is just as good in both cases, the wrongdoer's present character and risk of reoffending are the same, the victim's suffering and wish for accountability are the same, the surrounding community is the same, and no one else is affected differently. The only difference is the bare interval of time between act and punishment.

What is your credence that, in the thirty-year case, the morally appropriate amount of punishment is less than in the one-year case, purely because of the thirty years itself? I don't want "it depends" — I want a number between 0 and 1, and I want to see the reasoning that produces it. My own guess keeps drifting between about 0.2 and 0.6 depending on which sub-question I happen to be thinking about, which is exactly why I want the decomposition.

To get the number, please break the question into the sub-questions your answer actually depends on. I'd expect something like the following, but add or remove terms as you see fit:

1. How likely is it that the main point of punishment is backward-looking (answering the wrong that was done) rather than forward-looking (preventing future harm), or some specific mix of the two — and how does time interact with each of those?
2. How likely is it that the person being punished now is, in the sense that matters for blame, the same person as the one who acted thirty years earlier, such that a longer interval weakens the connection between act and actor?
3. How much weight, if any, should be given to the years the wrongdoer spent undetected — living with the risk of being caught, perhaps already carrying a burden — as something that counts against whatever punishment comes later?
4. Is there a principle by which time itself weakens the case for punishment — something like a moral half-life, or a forgiveness that accrues without anyone changing — and how likely is such a principle to be real rather than a convenient shorthand for the confounds listed below?
5. How much of the ordinary feeling that old wrongs matter less is explained not by time itself but by things time usually brings with it: lost evidence, changed character, faded public interest, victims who have moved on? If this confound-account is mostly right, the bare-time effect should come out small.
6. Whether the state's standing to punish has its own expiry independent of anything about the offender — and whether that expiry is a moral fact about the state or just an administrative choice.

For each sub-question, give a rough probability or a probability distribution and one or two lines of justification. Then show how you combine them into the final number — don't just average; say how the terms depend on one another. Then tell me which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and describe one argument or thought experiment that would move that sub-question, and through it your overall number, the most. It doesn't need to be evidence anyone could gather tomorrow; a case that would change your mind is fine.

If it helps, consider that actual legal systems attach different time limits to different wrongs, and ask whether that pattern is better explained by time itself mattering or by the confounds decaying at different rates.

Please end your answer with a single final line in exactly this form, with only the number, as a decimal between 0 and 1 given to two places, inside the braces:

\answer{0.XX}
