I've been nursing a transcendental argument for months and cant figure out whether it proves something or just dresses up a bit of Kantian optimism. Here's my current reconstruction.

(1) To think of any of my experiences as mine—as something I undergo—I have to be able, at least sometimes, to distinguish how things appear to me from how they are. (2) I can do that only if I already have a conception of a world independent of my current experience, a world that could in principle correct me. (3) So the capacity to self-ascribe experience constitutively requires the conception of such a world. (4) A genuine capacity cannot constitutively require a conception of something impossible: you cannot have a real capacity whose successful exercise demands grasping, say, a round square. (5) Therefore an experience-independent world is at least possible. (6) And possible in more than a merely thinkable sense, because a merely notional world couldn't be what correctness of experience consists in.

I half-suspect (6), but I'm now more worried that (4) is doing an illegitimate slide from conceivability to possibility, or maybe that (2) is too strong: maybe I just need local corrections within experience, not one big independent world. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and on what reading? Also, if only (6) fails, does that matter—ie is (5) by itself enough to do the anti-skeptical work, or is it too weak?
