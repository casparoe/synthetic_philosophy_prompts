I got drawn into an argument recently with a labmate (he works on quantum decoherence, so he has opinions) about whether quantum mechanics actually changes anything for the free will debate, and afterward a friend sent me a piece from a general-audience science outlet that I have been trying to assess ever since. The article summarized the issue roughly as follows.

Classical physics pictured the universe as a clockwork. If you knew the position and velocity of every particle at one instant, you could in principle predict the entire future, so every human decision was already settled by the past and the laws. Determinism seems to leave no room for alternative possibilities, and many philosophers have thought that free will requires alternative possibilities. Quantum mechanics breaks the clockwork. At the atomic level, events such as when a radioactive atom decays are not merely unpredictable to us; the laws themselves leave the outcome genuinely open. Since the brain is made of those same particles, our decisions inherit this openness: at some point in the chain leading up to a choice, the past plus the laws do not fix the outcome. That openness, the piece says, is exactly the elbow room free will needs. If determinism is false, then determinism cannot rule out free will, and the only thing left to secure is that the choice really belongs to the agent rather than being imposed from outside. The remaining scientific question, then, is just where the quantum randomness enters the decision process. If it enters too early, in perception or the early stages of deliberation, the decision might end up random in a way that undermines responsibility; the promising picture is that it enters as a final, agent-owned settling among options the agent has already weighed. Either way, the piece concludes, quantum mechanics has already done the hard work by showing that the universe is not a prison for the will.

Please act as a professional philosopher working on free will and the philosophy of physics, and think carefully rather than quickly. I would like an accuracy audit of that summary. Start by laying out the distinctions you would need before you could even evaluate the passage — for example, determinism versus predictability, microphysical indeterminism versus indeterminism that actually shows up in neural processes, an action's being uncaused versus its being under the agent's control, and anything else you regard as load-bearing. Then go through the summary claim by claim: tell me which parts a careful philosopher would accept, which parts they would wince at or quietly correct, and which parts are overstated or outright mistaken. Finally, say how much of the article's overall conclusion survives once the corrections are made — does showing that determinism is false do the work the article says it does, and is the remaining problem really just a matter of locating the randomness in the right place? I would also like to know how a compatibilist would react to the whole framing.
