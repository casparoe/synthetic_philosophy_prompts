I am a novelist at work on a book that follows several families in an invented European country over two generations. To give the place a texture distinct from its neighbors, I keep reaching for sentences of this kind: these people are indirect in their dealings; these people treat hospitality as a point of honor; these people distrust eloquence. I am aware of the standard objections: such sentences can sound like the old essentialism of national character, they seem to reify an average into an essence, and they risk the very sort of generalization that, applied to real peoples, has underwritten prejudice. Yet I also suspect that refusing all such sentences leaves fiction unable to represent shared ways of life—the very thing that makes one place unlike another.

Please argue that national character is a coherent notion and that it is sometimes acceptable for a novelist to generalize about a people in this way. In doing so, address three points on which I remain unsure. First, specify the logical form of the claims involved. I do not mean that every member of the people is indirect or distrustful of eloquence; I mean something closer to a generic claim, along the lines of "mosquitoes carry malaria," which tolerates exceptions while still saying something real about the population. Say whether that is the right model, and what the truth conditions of such a claim would be: a difference in the central tendency or variance of a trait across populations, a fact about shared institutions and public culture, a fact about the typical situations and incentives people face, or something else. Second, separate the descriptive question whether national character exists from the normative question whether an artist may make use of it; then defend a criterion distinguishing acceptable generalization from mere stereotype. Third, be candid about the dangers: say what would make an otherwise coherent national-character claim morally or aesthetically defective, and why the argument does not license those defective forms.
