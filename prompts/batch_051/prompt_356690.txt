I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm stuck on a piece about the argument inside philanthropy between people who say fund the proven, measurable thing (bednets, deworming, vitamin-A drops, the stuff with tight trial evidence) and people who say that's mopping the floor while the tap is still running, and you should fund systems change—boring institutional work, policy shops, procurement reform—even though you can't put error bars on it. I dont want to strawman either side. What I want is a thought experiment that forces the disagreement down to one clean variable that I can actually show a reader.

You are an expert on both the philosophy and the economics of doing good—comfortable with expected value, discount rates, disability-adjusted life-years, and also with institutional design and dynamic systems. Think carefully. Before you write anything final, brainstorm three candidate mechanisms that could make a marginal intervention win under one analysis and a systems intervention win under the other, then pick the cleanest one. Grant B is a systems intervention: funding a small policy-analysis team inside a health ministry, a procurement-reform pilot, training a cohort of civil servants—something whose benefits, if any, arrive late and diffuse, but which compounds by making the system itself more effective.

3. The flip is caused only by the horizon plus the persistence assumption for B. Not by one side being risk-averse and the other risk-neutral, not by A's evidence quietly weakening on closer inspection, not by "measurement bias" against systems work. Both analyses should use the same discount rate, the same underlying data, and the same moral premises.
