I want an analysis of the European Stability and Growth Pact — specifically the 2003 moment when France and Germany breached the Maastricht deficit limit and the Council of Ministers declined to sanction them — read through the Ulysses-contract or constitutional-precommitment tradition. I know Jon Elster's use of the Homeric episode in the background; I want you to test the model against this case rather than restate it.

Here is the passage the tradition leans on, from Homer, *Odyssey*, Book XII, in Samuel Butler's translation, where Circe instructs Odysseus about the Sirens:

"Therefore pass these Sirens by, and stop your men's ears with wax that none of them may hear; but if you like you can listen yourself, for you may get the men to bind you as you stand upright on a cross piece half way up the mast, and they must lash the rope's ends to the mast itself, that you may have the pleasure of listening. If you beg and pray the men to unloose you, then they must bind you faster."

The model this image anchors is roughly: a rational agent anticipates that a future self will respond to a temptation the present self would rather not yield to, so the present self removes the future option or raises its cost, with the result that the future self's preference never gets a chance to determine the outcome. Constitutional and treaty-based precommitment is supposed to be the political analogue: the sovereign at time t0 ties the hands of its successor at t1, not because the successor is irrational, but because it is predictably responsive to incentives that t0 wants kept out of the decision.

Now the case. In the Maastricht Treaty (1992), EU member states agreed to keep budget deficits below 3 percent of GDP and public debt below 60 percent of GDP as conditions for joining the euro. The Stability and Growth Pact (1997) added a surveillance-and-sanction mechanism, with the Council of Ministers as the enforcer. In 2002–2003 France and Germany ran deficits above the 3 percent line; the Commission pressed for the excessive-deficit procedure to move forward; in November 2003 the Council voted to hold the procedure in abeyance rather than move toward fines. The rules were later revised in both directions — more automaticity and more flexibility — and were suspended in 2020 under the general escape clause.

I want a disciplined mapping before any verdict: who or what is Odysseus; who are the crew with waxed ears; what are the Sirens; and what are the ropes, the mast, and Circe's reliable forecast about the temptation? Then tell me where the model genuinely illuminates the Pact — the ex ante logic of binding future governments for credibility with markets and voters, the attempt to make the later temptation irrelevant rather than merely forbidden — and where it misdescribes the case or goes silent. In particular: the future self here can simply refuse to be bound, or can choose the identities of the people who hold the ropes; the enforcers are political peers rather than a crew committed to ignoring the captain's pleas; the Sirens are partly the very governments and electorates whose future conduct is being bound; and there is no Circe who knows the future temptations in advance. End by saying what the episode does to precommitment theory itself: is the Pact a failed Ulysses contract, a contract that was never actually binding, or a case the theory has no clean category for?
