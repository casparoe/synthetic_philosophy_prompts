I am preparing a long essay on deflationary theories of truth, and before I evaluate any particular version I want the full specification of what a deflationary account of truth actually has to do. Write me that specification: a numbered list of requirements that any adequate deflationary theory must meet, with the case or consideration that makes each a requirement, and with each requirement classified as non-negotiable or as a demand specific to one camp. I am not asking for a survey or a bibliography; I want the criteria themselves.

Be sure the list renders a verdict on at least the following pressure points, and add anything else you think belongs.

1. The generalization role. A truth predicate earns its keep by letting us express things like Everything the witness said is true or All the axioms of Peano arithmetic are true when we cannot enumerate the relevant claims. The requirement is that the account explain exactly how such blind or quantified ascriptions get their content and their logic; if the mechanism works only for displayed sentences, it has failed.

2. The equivalence data. Any candidate must preserve the validity of the schema S is true if and only if S for ordinary declarative sentences, and must say what that preservation consists in. It also has to explain why the schema itself is not a hidden correspondence relation.

3. The paradox constraint. The unrestricted schema together with ordinary logic and a sentence that says of itself that it is not true produces contradiction, and Tarski's theorem rules out a fully general untyped truth predicate obeying the schema for a sufficiently strong classical language. A deflationary account must therefore take an explicit stand among the possible responses — stratifying or typing the predicate, weakening the logic, restricting the schema — and the stand it takes must not, in the process, break the generalization role.

4. Embedded and anaphoric occurrences. Truth ascriptions occur inside conditionals, negations, attitude reports, and quantifiers (If the first thing he said is true, then ...; She believes that what I said is false). The account must handle these uses as part of its core machinery rather than treating them as an afterthought.

5. Cross-language ascription. Disquotational machinery is tied to the language of the home speaker, yet we routinely call sentences of other languages true. The theory must either extend the account across languages without smuggling in language-independent propositions, or justify a retreat to language-relative truth.

6. The norm of truth. Any theory must explain or explain away the intuition that truth is the standard of correct assertion and belief: the link between truth and what one ought to say or think. It is not enough to ignore the datum; the account has to say whether the norm follows from the logical role of the predicate or is merely a norm governing the underlying claims themselves.

7. Truth's work in semantics and realism. A deflationary theory must preserve the legitimate uses of truth in giving the semantics of connectives and quantifiers, in defining validity, and in inferences such as If the theory that electrons exist is true, then electrons exist. It should explain how it earns the right to these uses while denying that truth is a substantive word-world relation.

8. Conservativeness as a possible constraint. A natural deflationary thought is this: adding a truth predicate to a base of non-semantic claims should not let one prove new non-semantic claims, otherwise truth is doing substantive work rather than serving expression. But fully general truth theories over arithmetic often are such that they add new consequences, such as consistency or reflection principles. Decide whether conservativeness is a genuine adequacy condition on deflationism or a formal demand that should not be built into the specifications.

For the list as a whole, give me a committed verdict, not a menu. I want you to: (a) identify which of the above pressures encode genuine adequacy constraints and which are borrowed expectations from correspondence or pragmatist theories that a deflationist may reasonably reject, with reasoning; (b) mark the final requirements as non-negotiable, desirable, or optional; (c) say whether any subset of the non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable and, if so, exactly which requirement must be weakened and in what way; and (d) name the single requirement that does the most to separate adequate deflationary accounts from failed ones.

Close with a very brief scorecard for three canonical deflationary families — the disquotational account, the minimalist/axiomatic account, and the anaphoric or prosentential account — noting only where each meets or fails the non-negotiable requirements. Keep that part short; the criteria are the point.
