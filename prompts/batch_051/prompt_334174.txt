For a paper I am writing on changing attitudes toward work, I read an explainer article that summarized Max Weber's argument about the Protestant work ethic as follows:

"Weber argued that because Calvinists believed salvation was predestined and could never be earned, they looked for signs of divine election in worldly success. Hard work and disciplined profit-making became a way of proving to themselves and others that they were among the saved, and this eventually hardened into a secular 'spirit of capitalism' that treats work as good in itself and leisure as waste. We can see its afterlife in the modern 35-hour work week, which is usually read as a secular rejection of the old ethic: once work lost its religious significance, people began trying to reclaim their time. Japan's karoshi, or death from overwork, shows the same logic operating without Protestantism — a cultural obligation to work becomes so strong that it expands until it destroys people, which is essentially the Protestant work ethic in secular form."

I have since seen the Protestant work ethic described in several incompatible ways, so I am not sure how much of this paragraph to trust. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful scholar of Weber would accept, which parts are simplifications, which parts a defender of his thesis would object to, and which parts are just wrong? I would especially like to know whether the comparison with karoshi at the end actually follows from an accurate version of the thesis, or whether it conflates a few different things. Please be specific about where the summary overstates and where it is merely compressed.
