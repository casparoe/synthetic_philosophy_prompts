I'm a philosophy of math PhD student, and I keep building this argument about computer-assisted proof that sounds airtight and lands somewhere I can't live with. Honestly I'd rather keep (4) then (1), but I can't see what I'm paying.

(1) A proof gives us knowledge of its conclusion only if a human mind can survey the proof as a whole — hold the chain of steps together, check each link, and see that the conclusion follows.
(2) The four-colour theorem's proof has no such survey in it. The proof reduces the theorem to an enormous finite case analysis, and the cases are disposed of by a computer: a person can read the program and the general strategy, but the actual confirmation that no configuration survives is executed, not read or worked through by anyone.
(3) So by (1), the four-colour theorem has not been proved in the sense that matters — the proof has never been surveyed, so it can't give us knowledge.
(4) But the four-colour theorem is a theorem. Mathematicians know it, use it, and would find it insane to treat it as merely conjectural.
(5) So (1) is too strong.
(6) But if (1) is too strong, I don't see what's left of the line between proof and experiment. Once a proof can outrun any single human's survey, why isn't "the machine checked the cases" on a par with "the apparatus detected the neutrino," with math becoming a high-stakes natural science? The Kepler conjecture proof has the same shape, except with more cases. And if we don't draw the line at what one mind can survey, then the classification of finite simple groups — which no single mathematician has read end to end either — is in the same boat, and so is a lot of ordinary math that leans on results nobody has personally re-checked. So the argument seems to show that either the computer proof isn't knowledge, or mathematics has been experimental all along. Both feel wrong.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I have to reject a step, which one, and what does that rejection cost? Bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, no restating the question.
