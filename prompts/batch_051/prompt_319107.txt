I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am drafting an essay on emergency measures — pandemic entry bans, migration-related border restrictions, and the first-wave lockdowns — and the relation between their legality and their legitimacy. My working claim is that emergency conditions reveal legality and legitimacy to be not merely distinct properties but properties supported by different justificatory registers, and that public argument keeps sliding between them unnoticed.

The essay tries to develop two ideas. The first is a fourfold scheme: an emergency measure can be (a) legal and legitimate, (b) legal but illegitimate, (c) illegal but legitimate, or (d) neither. The traffic that interests me runs through (b) and (c), because emergencies make them central cases rather than marginal anomalies. The second idea is a phenomenon I have been calling borrowed legitimacy: a measure is authorized under one title — a broadly delegated statutory power, a public-health order, a plenary sovereign power over entry — while it is publicly defended under a different title, such as protecting the vulnerable or preventing system collapse. The substantive defense does most of the legitimating work, but the legal basis is what carries the action; objections addressed to one title get answered by re-describing the measure under the other. My worry is that this diagnosis may be too neat, and that I may be missing what makes the legality-legitimacy gap stable rather than merely rhetorical.

Before I send the essay to an editor, I would welcome comments on its spine. Specifically: (1) Does borrowed legitimacy mark a single coherent phenomenon, or does it run together legal validity, normative justifiability, and popular acceptance in a way that obscures more than it explains? (2) Is my central case too convenient, given that many emergency statutes and public-health laws themselves require exactly the substantive findings I am treating as borrowed — so that the legal basis and the public justification are less separable than I claim? (3) Does the fourfold scheme earn its keep, or does it merely restate the familiar point that legality and legitimacy can come apart? (4) What is the strongest version of the objection that the essay is giving a journalistic name to an old distinction, and can the argument survive it? I would rather discover the fracture now than after publication, so candor over encouragement.
