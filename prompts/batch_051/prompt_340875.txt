I am a third-year undergraduate taking a course on health economics and distributive justice. We read Kenneth Arrow's classic 1963 article on the economics of medical care, and a question occurred to me that I want to work through with help. The article argues, in compressed form, that medical care cannot be treated as an ordinary commodity: demand for it is irregular and unpredictable; the patient cannot judge the quality of the care they receive; physicians are not pure profit-seekers; licensing and entry restrictions are endemic; and many health risks cannot be fully insured because of moral hazard and adverse selection. From this, it concludes that a laissez-faire market in medical care will systematically fail, a conclusion often taken as an argument for universal or heavily socialized provision.

Now consider a specific new development. Suppose a country introduces a scheme of continuous actuarial monitoring as the default for all health insurance. Every resident wears or carries a device that continuously records heart rate, blood glucose, activity, sleep, medication adherence, and similar markers, and the data flow, under a national standard, to insurers and providers. Underwriting is done by algorithms trained on this longitudinal record rather than self-reports or an annual exam, and opting out raises premiums sharply, so participation is nearly universal. Patients also receive continuously updated, risk-adjusted quality scores for clinics, physicians, and treatment plans. The claim on the table is that this development largely solves the information problem Arrow identified: insurers now know their enrollees' risk, and patients now know what they are buying.

Please act as a health economist with philosophical training, not as a generic commentator. Think carefully before committing. Begin by rehearsing the distinct premises of the Arrow argument and identifying precisely which of them the monitoring scheme bears on. Then assess whether the broad conclusion—that medical care cannot be left to a well-functioning market and requires non-market financing—survives this development, needs repair, or is dead. Address whether the monitoring scheme only transfers the information problem, for example to the design, ownership, and oversight of the scoring algorithms, or whether it creates new market failures of its own. Give a definite verdict rather than saying that things are complicated.
