I've been thinking about what makes every 'a' an 'a'. A newspaper 'a', a cursive 'a', and a rushed shopping-list 'a' share almost nothing measurable—one-story or two-story, loop open or closed, top flat or curved. My homemade idea is the skeleton view: a letter is a small family of stroke-patterns (a one-story 'a', a two-story 'a') that all play the same role, and style or font is just one smooth deformation applied to every skeleton in the alphabet at once. The essential 'a' would then be whatever stays put when you take the style away—which starts to look less like a shape and more like a slot in a system, the letter doing the same job in the same network of contrasts however it's drawn. I cant tell if I've invented a real concept, reworded common sense, or hit something linguistics already has a boring name for. Is it coherent, does it exist under another name, and where does it break? The thing that nags me is that an 'a' in a font I've never seen can be closer, shape for shape, to an 'o' in that font than to any 'a' I know, and I still read it instantly—does the skeleton view explain that or just push the problem back?
