A friend who knows I have been reading around in eighteenth-century philosophy recently gave me the following account of Rousseau's views on theater. In his Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre, written in response to a proposal that Geneva open a public theater, she said, Rousseau delivers a sweeping indictment of theater as such. On her reading, Rousseau holds that tragedy and comedy inflame the very passions they claim to purge; the spectator leaves more attached to his vices than when he entered, having spent what pity he had on imaginary suffering while real duties go unserved. Rousseau therefore concludes, she argued, that every well-ordered society should prohibit theater, and since his hostility to the arts is so thorough, he proposes nothing constructive in its place. She added that the heart of his case is a moral condemnation of the actors themselves.

This summary struck me as too neat, but I do not know the text well enough to say where it goes wrong, and I would rather not absorb a distorted version of the view. Could you audit the summary claim by claim? I would like to know which claims a careful reader of Rousseau would accept as stated, which are subtly inaccurate, and what Rousseau or a sympathetic defender would say in response to each questionable part. In particular, do his arguments against establishing a theater in Geneva generalize into a universal condemnation of theater, and does what remains accurate in the summary still support the conclusion that a well-ordered society should ban theater?
