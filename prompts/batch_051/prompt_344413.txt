I'm a retired atmospheric chemist, and after forty years of building models that try to say something about the next century, I've gotten very into philosophy — longtermism especially, because it keeps making claims about the far future that remind me of how climate models get misused once policy people get hold of them. My reading group has two people who have spent a year argueing past each other about whether taking the far future seriously ends up licensing authoritarian control in the present. One is a longtermist consequentialist. The other is a critic who thinks the whole longtermist style of reasoning is a standing invitation to totalitarianism. They both want to do a proper adversarial collaboration, and they've asked me to help draft the document. I'd like you to write that document for them.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics and political philosophy, and you have a reputation for running honest adversarial collaborations. Please think carefully about each side's best version before you draft anything — sketch the possible cruxes to yourself, try to find wording each side would actually sign, and don't let either side become a strawman. Where part of their dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. Then produce the joint statement.

The longtermist camp holds roughly this: because the future may contain an astronomically large number of lives, sufficiently large expected benefits to those lives can in principle justify real infringements of present-day rights, liberties, and democratic or legal constraints, provided the epistemic case is strong; she is emphatically not a totalitarian and thinks the bar for such infringement is very high and met almost nowhere in practice, but she refuses to say it could never be met. The critic holds roughly this: decisions that bind people must be justified to and authorized by the people they bind, and expected far-future benefits cannot by themselves justify stepping outside democratic constraints or violating rights; a theory that allows such trade-offs is a reductio of the theory, not a discovery about how much the far future matters.

The document should do four things in order. First, it should state the claims both camps can sign, and these should be substantive rather than the kind of thing nobody could reject: they might include agreements about the formal structure of maximizing theories, about how authoritarian regimes have in fact used utopian future-talk, about which present-day risk-reduction policies are worthwhile even on both views, and about what a good epistemic standard for far-future claims would look like. Second, it should state their precise disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording — I expect the central one to be whether expected far-future value can ever make it permissible to infringe a right or override a democratic decision, and a secondary one about whether any far-future expected-value calculation could be reliable enough to cross that threshold. Third, it should identify the cruxes, the smaller questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. I suspect these include an authorization crux (whether expected benefit to future people can substitute for authorization by the people coerced), an epistemic crux (how fast the reliability of expected-value forecasts decays with time horizon), and a fanaticism crux (whether the longtermist can avoid Pascal's-mugging conclusions without ad hoc patches). Fourth, for each crux the statement should give a concrete case or test both camps agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

For the authorization crux, a good test might be the historical record of twentieth-century projects justified by appeals to a future transformed humanity or utopia: the longtermist predicts those failures came from sloppy or ideological forecasting rather than from future-regarding reasoning itself, while the critic predicts that any such justification is structurally available to authoritarians. For the epistemic crux, both might agree that a well-defined forecasting tournament bears on it — say, asking a panel of experts in 1900 to estimate the moral and demographic landscape of 2000 and the value of various interventions — with the longtermist predicting that modern probabilistic and scenario-based methods would do substantially better, and the critic predicting a cliff in accuracy beyond a few decades no matter what method is used. For the fanaticism crux, both might accept a hypothetical institutional test: an appointed Future Ombudsman with power to override a local democratic objection to an urgent pandemic-prevention or geoengineering project whenever the body's model says the expected averted existential risk passes some threshold. The longtermist predicts such a body, with veto points and sunset clauses, could remain uncaptured over thirty years across several countries, while the critic predicts capture, mission creep, or both.

Write all of this as continuous prose, the way a careful joint letter or memo would read, not as a formatted document. No headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold, no tables. Since adversarial-collaboration statements often use a numbered claims section and a disagreements table, do the nearest thing in prose: open with the agreed claims as a flowing series of sentences, then give each disagreement its own paragraph, then lay out the cruxes and each side's predictions in ordinary sentences. I care about precision: the wording should be tight enough that both camps could in principle sign it, and the residual disagreement should stay genuinely sharp rather than defanged. As a retired scientist I also want the crux tests stated concretely enough that we could in principle run or score them, not just gesture at them.
