I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of science and have been trying to update the unit on underdetermination in light of what contemporary machine learning has done to the problem. The classic argument, in the form usually associated with Duhem and Quine, runs roughly as follows: any finite body of evidence is compatible with indefinitely many mutually incompatible theories that fit that evidence equally well, so the evidence alone never forces a unique choice of theory. In its traditional presentation the rival theories are creatures of a skeptic's say-so; they are gerrymandered, unelaborated, and easy for a practicing scientist to set aside as not serious competitors. What has unsettled me is that modern model fitting seems to have industrialized this very possibility. Under labels like the Rashomon effect and underspecification, researchers routinely report that a fixed dataset can support many different models—produced by different runs, seeds, or architectural choices—that achieve essentially identical held-out accuracy while differing sharply in their internal features, learned representations, and predictions on inputs that depart from the training distribution. The choice among these models is often settled by incidental details such as the random seed or the order of minibatches, rather than by any evidential consideration.

I cannot decide between two readings of this development, and that is what I want you to resolve. On the first reading, it vindicates underdetermination and makes it worse: it shows that even within a well-controlled inductive practice, the data do not single out a unique generalization, so theory choice really is underdetermined by evidence in an everyday, practical sense. On the second reading, it drains the thesis of its skeptical force: the original argument traded on the rivals being hidden and possibly divergent in important but unspecified ways, whereas now the empirically equivalent rivals are actually on the table, fully specified, and open to inspection—so we can simply check whether they disagree on anything we care about, and if they do not, the mere existence of many models is no threat to scientific knowledge. Please work through the structure of the underdetermination argument—what it assumes about evidence, about theories, and about the relation between them—and identify exactly which assumption or premise the machine-learning development bears on. Then tell me whether the argument survives intact, survives only in a repaired form with a weaker conclusion, or collapses under the new circumstances. I want the mechanics of the argument itself, not a survey of the literature or a list of things to read.
