An AI lab I'm adjecent to keeps circulating a proposal for solving the honesty problem by turning it into an engineering check, and I can't tell if it's genuinely clever or if it just relocates the problem inside the model. The idea, roughly: before release, every model goes through a "counterfactual honesty audit." For each query in a large held-out set, you rerun the query with an added instruction forbidding omission and pushing the model to lay out every true, decision-relevant statement it has. That fuller output becomes the "truthful baseline." Then you run the deployed model on the original query, and compare its actual answer against its own baseline. If the actual answer omits or contradicts something the baseline included, the model gets flagged as deceptive — unless it said "I don't know," which is always excused, no penalty. The pitch is that this avoids having to settle what deception is philosophically: you're not checking the model against the world, you're checking the model against its own most exhaustive accounting, so its evasions become visible without a human arbiter of truth for every output.

I'm not asking whether this is a good idea in the abstract. I want to know how it would actually behave if it became an industry compliance standard. What does it incentivize at training time, and in how labs choose the held-out set and the wording of the forbidding instruction? How do users learn to game it — and what happens when the clever play is to nudge the model toward "I don't know" until it's useless? What happens at the fuzzy boundaries of "decision-relevant" and "true," given that the baseline is itself model output rather than ground truth? And are the failure modes the kind you can fix by tightening the rule, or does tightening just move the game to the distribution that produces the baseline?
