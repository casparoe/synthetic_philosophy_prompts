I've recently taken over the theoretical side of a small interpretability group at an AI lab. We study the same trained network at three grains — individual weights and units, circuits and modules, and whole-system behavior over long horizons — and the disagreement that stalls every meeting is which of those descriptions captures something real rather than something that is merely a useful summary. Someone introduced Dennett's physical, design, and intentional stances, together with the real-patterns idea, as a shared vocabulary. It has made the fault line explicit without resolving it: one camp says only the physical stance tracks the underlying structure of the network and everything else is convenient compression; another says that any pattern reliable enough to support prediction is real at its own level, so circuit-level and intentional-level descriptions are no less real than the weights.

I now have to set our theory agenda for the next two years, and I want to spend it on questions whose answers would actually change how we build and interpret models. What do you think are the main open questions in the real-patterns and three-stances framework — about when it is principled to switch stances rather than merely convenient, whether the three stances are exhaustive, how patterns at different grains compare when they conflict, what makes a pattern objective rather than observer-relative, or whatever else you think matters — and which two or three should a group like ours try to settle first? Defend the ordering: for each candidate, explain why it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is for people who are modelers first and philosophers second, and what a credible result or demonstration in a year would look like. Flag any question that sounds deep but would not alter how we train, inspect, or justify our models if answered, so we can set it aside.
