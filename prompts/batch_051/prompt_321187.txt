I write about language and the mind, and I've come around to a position I'd like you to pressure-test. The position is roughly Davidson's: a metaphor has no special metaphorical meaning over and above what the sentence literally means. A sentence that calls a person the sun says something literally false, and the metaphor's work is to make us notice similarities rather than to convey a hidden proposition. On this view, conceptual-metaphor theory may describe patterns in what gets noticed, but it is not itself a theory of what metaphors mean. What would have to be true for this position to be false? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: the systematic, cross-linguistic regularities in conceptual mappings; the speed at which novel metaphors are processed; dead metaphors that seem to have acquired new conventional meanings; children's metaphor comprehension; and the ease with which we paraphrase a metaphor into a proposition. Then give an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated by a narrow definition of the word meaning — and, if it has, whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind. Lead with the bottom line — how exposed this position actually is — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
