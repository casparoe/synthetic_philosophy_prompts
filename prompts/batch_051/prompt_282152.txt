At a dinner party last month two friends of mine spent half the night arguing about something another guest told us: she and a stranger at an academic conference of about fourty people had discovered they'd both spent a summer as teenagers in the same village of roughly 400 people in northen Norway. Marcus kept saying this was a staggering coincidence that couldn't just be chance; Leila kept saying it was the birthday paradox in disguise. I genuinely couldn't work out who was right. Could you act as a careful epistemologist with good intuitions for probability — start by brainstorming the strongest version of each side, and then adjudicate?

The back-and-forth, as best I remember it:

(1) Marcus: The odds that those two particular people share that particular tiny village are astronomically low. That's a one-in-a-million event, roughly. Events that unlikely don't just happen for no reason.

(2) Leila: That's the birthday-paradox mistake. In a room of forty people there are 780 possible pairings, and any two people can match on any of hundreds of things — hometowns, summer trips, employers, favourite books, obscure bands. With that many shots, the chance that at least one 'what are the odds' coincidence shows up is actually quite high.

(3) Marcus: But we didn't observe 'some coincidence or other'; we observed this one. You can't make a specific event probable after the fact just by putting it in a larger class. If I'd written down in advance, 'these two people will share that village,' you'd be impressed, and rightly.

(4) Leila: The class isn't arbitrary. We'd have been equally amazed by any of the hundreds of memorable matches that could have occurred, and the hundreds of boring non-events that could have occurred never get retold at dinner parties. So the relevant event was 'some amazing coincidence at this conference,' and that was probable. Your after-the-fact specificity is selection bias.

(5) Marcus: But a shared birthday is boring precisely because every day is roughly equally likely under the obvious chance model. A shared obscure village isn't like that. If you compare the probability of the data under a causal story — a common acquaintance, similar family backgrounds, whatever — versus under pure chance, the likelihood ratio might be enormous. So the evidence should substantially raise the probability that there's some connection.

(6) Leila: Raise it from what? A large likelihood ratio does nothing without a prior. Before the conference, the probability that these two particular people were connected by a hidden cause was vanishingly small, while the probability that one of 780 pairs would hit an available 'amazing' feature was not. So the posterior probably still favours chance, and the feeling of significance is just a bias.

Please go through the six moves one by one: for each, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and identify at least one thing the whole exchange is missing. I'm especially interested in whether the distinction between pre-registered and post-hoc observations should matter, and whether that distinction is defensible.
