My flatmate keeps going on about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and I've been trying to show him the view can't even state it's own threshold without collapsing. The view, as I understand it from him: below a certain threshold of suffering intensity, pain and pleasure trade off against each other in the ordinary cost–benefit way; but at or above that threshold, pain becomes lexically prior to pleasure, meaning no amount of pleasure, and no amount of merely mild symptom-relief either, can outweigh even one instance of the severe suffering. I came up with the following argument that the position is incoherent:

(1) Below the threshold, the view is committed to pain and pleasure being commensurable: a mild headache can be outweighed by a good enough evening, and so on.
(2) But the only difference between a pain just below the threshold and a pain just above it is a difference of intensity, and intensity is a matter of degree.
(3) A mere difference of degree cannot produce a difference in kind when it comes to commensurability. Two goods are either commensurable or they aren't; there's no in-between.
(4) So pain and pleasure are either commensurable everywhere or commensurable nowhere.
(5) If they're commensurable everywhere, then even a bout of extreme agony is commensurable with pleasure, so some astronomically large amount of pleasure could outweigh it — which is exactly what the lexical-priority part of the view denies.
(6) If they're commensurable nowhere, then not even the mild tradeoffs below the threshold make sense, which is the other half of the view.
(7) So lexical threshold negative utilitarianism has to give up either the lexical part or the threshold part.

My flatmate just says step (3) begs the question, but I can't see how. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails exactly, and what should a charitable version of his view say about (3) and (4)?
