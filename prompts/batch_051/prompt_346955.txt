Consider the standard open-access tragedy of the commons. Ten herders graze animals on a shared pasture. Grazing one animal costs 1; if N animals graze in total, each animal yields K − N, where K is the pasture's carrying capacity for the coming season. The herders know this functional form but not K. Each herder privately observes K plus independent, unbiased noise before the season begins. In the usual analysis, the open-access Nash equilibrium puts more animals on the pasture than the joint-profit-maximizing herd size.

Now change the governance rule to a miniature futarchy. Before grazing begins, the ten herders trade two securities. A share of the cap-security pays an amount equal to the total realized profit of all ten herders next season if a costlessly enforced cap of N ≤ 10 is adopted, and pays 0 if it is not adopted. A share of the status-quo-security pays an amount equal to the total realized profit of all ten herders if no cap is adopted, and pays 0 otherwise. All ten herders may trade, and no outsiders participate. After trading closes, the rule is selected mechanically: the cap is adopted if and only if the closing price of the cap-security is strictly greater than the closing price of the status-quo-security; if the prices are equal, the status quo remains.

Work through the standard analysis of this altered commons. Does the market necessarily adopt the cap when the cap maximizes expected total profit? Can a herder who would profit more under the status quo push the cap-security's price below the status-quo-security's price even when the cap would be more efficient? Does the classic overgrazing result still hold, or does the market price, by making the grazing rule contingent on itself, change the herders' grazing choices too? State explicitly what must be assumed about market microstructure, budgets, risk aversion, and strategic behavior to make the answer determinate.
