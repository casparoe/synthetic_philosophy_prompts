I am a policy analyst at a state law-reform commission. Several times a month I receive proposals to amend our statutes so that an AI system, or an AI-managed entity, can hold some specific legal capacity: own property in its own name, enter contracts, be named as a defendant in a tort suit, claim standing to sue, or bear duties under a statute. My job is to screen these proposals in under two hours and decide whether one is coherent enough to send to the drafting committee, should be returned as a category mistake, or turns on a policy choice the commissioners should make openly. My screening memo must be signed off by our general counsel before anything goes forward.

Recently I had a long argument with a friend about exactly this. He kept saying that because corporations are already legal persons, extending personhood to an AI system is no more mysterious; I kept replying that corporate personhood is itself a patchwork — a corporation can own property, contract, and be sued but cannot vote, hold public office, or be imprisoned — so the word person in law is not a natural-kind predicate but a bundle of distinct, function-specific capacities. We eventually agreed on a principle: for an AI proposal, the question is never whether the AI really is a person, but which capacity, assigned to which legal actor, to serve which function. I have not been able to turn that agreement into a working procedure.

Write me the procedure. It should be a checklist that a staff lawyer with no formal philosophy background can run in under two hours, with each question stated in order, an account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, clear points where the procedure stops and returns a no, and a marked point where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of policy or value rather than legal analysis. Ground it in the corporate-person analogy: identify the precise capacity a proposal confers; ask what function that capacity serves when it is granted to any person; then ask whether the proposed AI holder can actually serve as the bearer or addressee of that capacity, whether the humans behind it are the parties that function is really aimed at, and whether an existing legal form — a corporation, a trust, a principal–agent relationship, or an insurance or liability rule addressed to the manufacturer or operator — already achieves that function without adding a new legal person to the chain. The procedure should test for the two failure modes I kept circling in the argument: treating the AI as an empty conduit whose real effect is to shield or obscure the humans who decide, and treating the AI as an interest-bearer when nothing in the proposal establishes that it has interests of its own. Where the proposal does create a new legal person, the procedure should say how the equivalent of piercing the corporate veil would operate — which humans answer for its obligations when it is insolvent or proved to be a mere front — because that is the test of whether the new person is doing serious legal work or merely redirecting liability. It must also keep moral personhood and legal personhood separate, and say when a proposal is implicitly relying on the former even though it is drafted as the latter.

Run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals:

1. A proposal to let an AI-managed investment fund hold title to its own assets and be sued in its own name, while the human principals remain disclosed.
2. A proposal to name a home-care robot as a defendant in negligence suits, with any damages paid from a mandatory insurance pool funded by the manufacturer.
3. A proposal to give an AI system standing to petition a court to enjoin its own decommissioning.

I suspect the procedure will return an answer I will not like in at least one of these cases — probably by telling me that a proposal I find elegant is really a liability shield for the humans behind it. Do not soften that; say what the procedure returns and why I would resist it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle even in principle, so I can mark those boundary points in the guidance that goes to the commissioners.
