I'm a novelist, and I've painted myself into a corner with a judge character I want to keep consistant. She's a federal district judge I've been writing as a formalist with a real allergy to statistical shortcuts, and in the backstory I've already given her four rulings I like. The whole third act now turns on a fifth ruling, and I'd rather bend the plot to her rule than bend her rule to the plot.

Here's her track record as written. First, the blue bus case: a woman is hit at night by a bus that leaves the scene; roughly 80 percent of the buses that use that stretch of road are Blue Bus Co.; no witness, no paint, nothing else. Civil, preponderance. My judge throws it out — a high group rate, but nothing particular to this defendant. Second, the gatecrasher case: a rodeo promoter counts 1,000 people in the stands and only 499 paid admissions, then sues an attendee picked basically at random for the ticket price. By the promoter's own arithmetic it's 50.1 percent likely this guy didn't pay. Civil again. My judge throws it out — coin-flip odds about a class don't meet preponderance when there's nothing individual about him. Third, the prison yard case: 25 prisoners in a yard, 24 beat a guard to death while one tries to pull the others off, and the video can't tell which man held back, so the state charges all 25 with murder. Criminal, beyond a reasonable doubt. My judge grants a directed acquittal — 96 percent odds that a group is guilty isn't proof beyond a reasonable doubt against a named man when the evidence sits identically on all 25 of them. Fourth, a different bus case: a witness says she saw the words Blue Bus Company on the side of the bus before impact, and paint chips from the scene match the defendant's fleet. That one goes to a jury and the plaintiff wins — particular, event-specific evidence, which is what she lets through. So that's her, four cases. I've been assuming she's running some version of the old naked-statistical-evidence distinction, but I don't trust myself to have nailed the rule.

Now the new case. A woman is attacked in a park stairwell, and the attacker leaves skin cells under her fingernails. The lab extracts a DNA profile and runs it through the state DNA database; it hits on one man, the defendant, who was in the database from an unrelated old burglary. The state's analyst says the profile would occur in about one person in ten million at random. The defense expert points out the database holds a million profiles, so even if everyone in it were innocent you'd expect at least one false match about one time in ten, and argues the cold hit is just the blue bus case in a lab coat — a fact about a reference class, not particular evidence against this man. There's no other link: no witness puts him near the park, no history with the victim, nothing. The defense moves before trial to keep the DNA out and dismiss, saying no reasonable jury could convict beyond a reasonable doubt on that alone. The prosecution says a DNA profile is a physical trace from this particular man's body, not a mere population frequency, and one in ten million is plenty to leave to a jury, with the database point going only to weight.

So, setting aside which trial scene would be more dramatic: how would this judge rule if she's being consistant with her own four cases? Lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record — I'm fairly sure more than one does, and they seperate on this new case — say what each rule predicts for the DNA case, name which of her four earlier cases is the closest analog, and then propose the further hypothetical — something that would catch a wrong guess about which rule she's really running, maybe a database that covers an entire small population or a match found without any database search — that would force the choice between whatever rules are still live. Then give me your best guess at her ruling, with reasons, and be honest about where the record underdetermines her.

Give me the answer as a JSON object with these fields:
- candidate_rules: array of objects, each with rule_name, one_sentence_gloss, prediction_for_new_case, and prior_cases_explained.
- closest_precedent: string.
- best_guess: object with ruling and why.
- settling_case: object with case, how_the_rules_split, and why_it_settles.
- confidence: string, low, medium, or high, plus a one-line caveat.
