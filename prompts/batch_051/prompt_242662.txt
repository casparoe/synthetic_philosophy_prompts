I have been developing a notion I call the load-bearing surface of a concept, and I would like an honest, rigorous assessment of it.

The idea is this. Every concept sits inside a network of other concepts, classifications, institutions, and everyday practices that depend on it. By the load-bearing surface of a concept I mean the full set of things that would have to change, or would come under pressure to change, if that concept were substantially revised or replaced. Some concepts carry little weight. The concept planet, before its 2006 redefinition, was load-bearing mostly for a modest corner of astronomy education and popular taxonomy; revising it meant updating textbooks and museum labels, not rewriting law or reorganizing kinship. Other concepts carry enormous weight. Marriage, race, and person are load-bearing across law, medicine, property, policing, family structure, and ordinary coordination: change them, and the cascade reaches everywhere.

My central claim is that the load-bearing surface, not the concept's fidelity to the world, determines whether a concept is a good candidate for conceptual engineering. A concept is ripe for revision when its load-bearing surface is small or already eroding. A concept with a large, intact load-bearing surface should be left alone no matter how poorly it tracks reality, because replacing it triggers a cascade whose costs almost always exceed the benefits.

A corollary I draw is that conceptual engineers should aim mostly at low-load, technical, or peripheral concepts and be far more conservative about high-load ones. This, I think, explains why the redefinition of planet succeeded quickly while attempts to replace or re-engineer race and gender concepts stall or yield only partial, contested changes. It also implies that the most ambitious parts of the ameliorative project — the attempt to re-engineer central social concepts — are misguided in practice even if right in principle.

I am aware this sounds like a recipe for political quietism, and that worries me, but I cannot locate the exact flaw.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher working in conceptual ethics and the theory of concepts. Think carefully, and begin by reconstructing my proposal in its strongest and most charitable form, tightening any definitions that need it. Spend a little time brainstorming the nearest existing distinctions and the sharpest counterexamples before settling on your final assessment. Then: (1) say whether the idea already exists in some recognizable form and, if so, what it is called and how mine differs; (2) assess what it gets right; (3) identify where it breaks, giving the strongest three or four objections and saying which, if any, are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. In particular, address whether the proposal collapses into plain conservatism, and whether the load-bearing surface of a concept is well-defined enough to do the work I want it to do. Push back on my planet and race examples if they are doing illicit work. Be candid rather than encouraging.
