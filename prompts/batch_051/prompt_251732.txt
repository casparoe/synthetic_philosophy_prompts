I am a novelist, and for several months I have been unable to work because of a question about an old promise. When I was thirteen, my closest friend was dying of a slow illness. We had spent that fall reading Greek myths together, and we became fixed on the one vow the gods themselves would not break: an oath sworn on the river Styx, where the penalty came with the oath itself rather than from any judge or tribunal, so that not even Zeus could revoke a promise he had sworn there. Shortly before my friend died, he asked me to swear by the Styx that I would become a writer and finish the epic novel we had been planning together—the great work he knew he would not live to write. I did. He dictated the words, and I repeated them with all the solemnity a thirteen-year-old can manage.

I kept the promise for a long time. I have published three novels, and until a few years ago I still intended to finish the epic. But the project has come to feel like a kind of ventriloquism: the book belongs to a child's imagination, my writing life has moved elsewhere, and continuing would cost me years I no longer believe the book deserves. A fellow writer whose judgment I respect tells me I am not bound. His view, as I understand it, is that an oath binds only where there is a genuine mutual undertaking and, at least in principle, someone or something to hold the swearer to account; here one party has been dead for thirty years, the other is a river in a story, and a thirteen-year-old had no authority to conscript the decades that followed. Since no one survives to collect on the oath, I am free and should release myself without guilt. I want to accept this, but it seems to hollow out the very idea of swearing. If only the promises an enforcer will hold us to truly bind, then the act of binding oneself—the reason we swear oaths at all—collapses. I am not asking a legal question; legally I am free.

What I am asking is what I morally owe, now, to a dead friend and to an oath made under the sign of an unbreakable river. Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order. Section 1: The case that I am still bound—set out the strongest possible version of the view that the oath continues to obligate me, at about 250 words. Section 2: The case that I am released—set out the strongest possible version of the view that I am no longer obligated, at about 250 words. Section 3: Where the disagreement actually lives—identify the one or two underlying commitments the two positions diverge on, and indicate what would settle them, at about 200 words. Section 4: My advice—tell me what you think I should do and what attitude I should take toward the oath if I decide not to finish the novel, at about 200 words.
