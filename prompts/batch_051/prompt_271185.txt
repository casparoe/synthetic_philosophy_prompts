In a philosophy of mind seminar I attended recently, someone invoked Dennett's Careenium thought experiment to defuse the worry that mental states are epiphenomenal. As he presented it, the Careenium is a closed two-dimensional toy universe filled with simple simulated balls ("simmballs") that obey basic collision laws and occasionally bind together when they touch, forming larger, relatively stable aggregates. The facts about those aggregates — their shapes, centers of gravity, velocities, and lifespans — are entirely fixed by the histories of the individual simmballs, and the question this colleague drew from Dennett is whether the aggregate-level patterns can literally be said to push the balls around. His move was to treat this as a model of the mind–brain relation: the simmballs correspond to the neural substrate, the aggregates correspond to symbolic or semantic-level states, and just as aggregate patterns in the Careenium push their constituent balls without violating any microphysical law, so beliefs and contents can push the neural substrate around without violating physical closure. On his view, this secures the robust truth of claims like "my belief caused my finger to move" rather than treating them as a mere manner of speaking.

I am skeptical that the analogy can carry that load. My worry is that simmball-aggregates are just glued-together collections of particles with no representational content, no normative constraints, and no semantics, whereas symbols are content-bearing in ways that may make the parallel break exactly where it is needed. I also suspect that "the aggregate pushes the balls" may describe a useful predictive compression of the micro-dynamics rather than a genuine causal relation, in which case the analogy would prove far less than my colleague wants. But I do not want to overcorrect into saying the macro level is causally inert either.

Please audit this analogy carefully: first, give an explicit mapping of what in the Careenium corresponds to what in the mind–brain case (a table of correspondences is fine); then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion and separate them from disanalogies that do not; say which features of the source image the analogy quietly imports into the target; and finally state which conclusion, if any, survives the audit and whether some other analogy would do the job better. I am less interested in exegetical fidelity to Dennett's own text than in whether the Careenium-style analogy can genuinely support the claim that symbols causally push the substrate around.
