I've been orbiting the wild animal welfare stuff for a few years now — the usual LessWrong/EA threads about whether nature is a horror show — and a friend at a meetup walked me through an argument that's been stuck in my head since, because it seems to prove way too much and I can't find the leak.

(1) If we're evaluating whether to destroy or preserve a habitat, what matters is the net welfare of all the sentient individuals affected, not "biodiversity" or "nature" for it's own sake. (2) In lots of real ecosystems — the r-selected species where hundreds or thousands of offspring get produced and only a couple survive — the vast majority of animals die young from starvation, exposure, parasites, or being eaten, probably before they've had enough positive experience to outweigh the pain. (3) So for those habitats, keeping the habitat around is plausibly net negative: it's a machine that churns out mostly short, painful lives. (4) Therefore destroying such a habitat, if done in a way that doesn't itself cause a drawn-out die-off (say, shrinking it gradually, or contraceptives if we had workable ones), could be a good thing, and restoring or protecting it could be bad, other things equal. (5) And the same reasoning doesn't stop at "some habitats": if short miserable lives are bad and we can cheaply prevent them, then the best thing we could do for wild animals — assuming the tech — would be to sterilize, rewild-then-block, or otherwise phase out huge swaths of wild animal life across the planet. (6) But basically nobody thinks we should depopulate the ocean or the Amazon because the fish and frogs are suffering.

So the argument seems to drive me from a mild "maybe we shouldn't romanticize nature" to a conclusion that sounds monstrous, and I can't tell whether the problem is a bad step in the middle or a premise I just don't want to pay for. I'd really rather keep both (1) — because it just says take animal suffering seriously — and (2), because denying it feels like the Disney-movie move of assuming wild animals are mostly fine. But holding both seems to hand me (6). Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If the chain is valid and the only way out is rejecting a premise, which one should I reject and what does that rejection cost me elsewhere?
