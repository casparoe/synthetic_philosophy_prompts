I've been working through the eliminative materialist literature, and the argumentative move I want pinned down is the analogy its defenders draw from the history of failed theories. In their hands, folk psychology — our everyday vocabulary of beliefs, desires, hopes, and the like — is a theory, and its central posits stand to mature neuroscience roughly as phlogiston stood to Lavoisier's chemistry: when the theory failed, phlogiston was not identified with oxygen or reduced to something else; it was dropped from the ontology altogether. The eliminativist then argues that we should expect the same fate for beliefs and desires, once neuroscience matures.

I'm not asking for a summary of eliminative materialism. I want a careful audit of how far this analogy can actually carry that conclusion. Begin by making the intended mapping explicit, term by term: what corresponds to the phlogiston, to phlogiston theory, to the phenomena the theory was trying to explain, to oxidation chemistry, and to the community that abandoned the theory. Then isolate the disanalogies. I'm particularly interested in the ones the analogy quietly imports: whether folk psychology's 'posits' are introduced in the way theoretical posits are; whether the everyday explanatory practices folk psychology serves are answerable to evidence in the way combustion experiments were; whether beliefs and desires are in the same kind of explanatory business as phlogiston was; and whether eliminating the posits of folk psychology would have the same relationship to ordinary life as eliminating phlogiston did to the laboratory. For each disanalogy, say whether it damages the eliminativist's use of the analogy or is merely cosmetic. Then state what the analogy, properly constrained, can and cannot support: at most a live possibility, at most a reason to fund alternative research programs, or something stronger? If a better comparison suggests itself — another episode from the history of science, or a different source domain — say what it is and whether it would on balance serve the eliminativist or their critics.

Throughout, mark the difference between what is settled and what is genuinely contested. That the broad history of phlogiston chemistry went as the standard story says is one thing; that folk psychology is a theory in the relevant sense, that its explanatory record resembles phlogiston theory's, and that dropping theoretical posits is the right lesson to draw from such episodes are all contested claims, and I don't want any of them presented as consensus.
