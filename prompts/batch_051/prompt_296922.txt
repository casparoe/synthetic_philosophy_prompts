I'm teaching a political theory course, and this week we're doing precommitment — the Ulysses contract stuff. We read the Sirens episode in the Odyssey, where Odysseus has the crew tie him to the mast, plug their own ears with wax, and row past, and the class keeps treating it as the classic ilustration of self-binding: you know you'll become irrational, so you tie your hands ahead of time. But the more I look at it, the less it seems like a clean parable for constitutions. Odysseus isn't actually bound by his own earlier self — he's bound by the crew, who are obeying orders from the person currently screaming at them, and they can't even hear the temptation they're supposed to protect him from. That's not a majority binding itself; it's more like one guy who has engineered a structure that keeps him in charge while no one else can even hear the argument.

I'd like an actual close reading of the episode through the lens of constitutional precommitment, pitched for a seminar, not just a list of parallels. Where does the analogy genuinely hold, where does it stretch, and where does it run in the opposite direction from the standard story? Specifically: is this precommitment at all in the sense the literature means, or is it really just credible delegation or coercion dressed up as self-control? Does the crew's deafness make a philosophical difference, and if so what does the episode end up saying about constitutions — that binding works only when the people enforcing it don't know what the bound person is giving up? I don't need a survey of theories; I want you to take both the poem and the concept seriously, and say where the analogy distorts one of them.
