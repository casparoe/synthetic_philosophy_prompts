I keep coming back to the old argument that a person who is as morally good as possible, all the time, would be a strange and vaguely unappealing ideal — not because she's bad, but because being that good crowds out all the other ways a person can be excellent: being funny, having taste, liking silly things, getting lost in beauty for no reason. A friend of mine insists the point is to discursive to make into a poem. I'm not so sure.

Write a villanelle from the point of view of someone who loves a moral saint — a child, a partner, an old friend, your choice. The two refrains should carry the two halves of the objection: one line for how good she is (the part that makes you feel small and lucky and a little guilty for wanting anything else), and one line for what's missing from the life (the part that makes you not want to be her, or not want your kid to turn out like her). The form should do the argumentative work: the refrains return and return the way her single-mindedness keeps reasserting itself, and when they finally meet in the closing couplet it should feel like the realization the original essay is pushing toward — that the problem isn't that she's too good, it's that she's only good. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the position recognizes it, and still work as a poem for someone who has never heard of it. And please don't let the word saint do all the heavy lifting — make the poem earn it.
