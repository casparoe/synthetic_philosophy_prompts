I spent my career as a chemist, so I'm used to the idea that once you fix your instrument and your procedure, a measurement question has a determinate answer. Lately I've gotten into philosophy and I keep tripping over the "is the world becoming less violent?" debate. I have a rough idea for an essay and I'd like comments before I sink real time into it. Sorry in advance for typos—I type with two fingers.

Working thesis: the question "is the world becoming less violent?" has no determinate answer as usually asked, because it's really a family of questions, and the choices that pick out one member of the family are partly normative or interest-relative, not empirical. Here's my notes.

1. What counts as violence. The headline claims usually count direct physical violence: homicides, war deaths, maybe state killings. But there's a whole literature, and ordinary usage, that also calls structural violence "violence"—deaths from preventable poverty, environmental degradation, neglect. Wether that's literally violence or a metaphor is a conceptual question, not an empirical one, and the trend answer flips depending on how you come down on it.

2. Weighting. Even inside direct violence you have to aggregate war deaths, homicides, genocide, domestic violence, and so on into one number. Each type has its own trend. There's no empirical procedure that tells you how to weight a death by drone strike against a death by intimate partner, so the "world trend" is partly an artifact of the weights.

3. Per capita vs absolute. If population doubles and violent deaths go up 50%, the per-capita risk falls but the total number of violent deaths rises. Which one is "the world becoming less violent"? Both, in different senses. The per-capita number answers something like "how likely is an arbitrary person to die violently?" and the absolute number answers "how much violence is there in total?" I don't see a fact of the matter about which question is the real one.

4. Aggregation weirdness. This is the part I'd most like checked. I think you can get a Simpson's-paradox-style effect: global per-capita violence could fall even if every region's per-capita rate rises, if population shifts toward lower-violence regions. If that's right, then even the global per-capita number isn't a clean summary of what's happening to people; it's partly a summary of where people happen to live. That seems to undercut the idea that there's a single "state of the world" being tracked.

5. Time window. Starting the clock in 1945 versus 1500 versus 10,000 BCE can give you different answers, and the choice of window is usually set by the question you care about, not by the data.

So the essay would argue: for each fixed package of choices—definition, weights, per-capita vs absolute, regions, time window—there's usually a determinate empirical answer, but the bare question has no determinate answer because the package isn't fixed by the world. I'm not saying the debate is pointless; I'm saying it's partly a debate about which package to adopt, and that part is philosophical or normative.

What I'd like from you:
- First, is the Simpson's-paradox point actually right? I worked through a toy example and I think it is, but I'd like the reasoning checked, not just the arithmetic.
- Second, is the overall argument too quick? A defender of the decline story might say they're explicitly asking about per-capita risk of dying violently, so the question is determinate once you grant their definition. Does my essay need to say more about why anyone should care about the bare, unpacked question rather than the sharpened one?
- Third, where's the weakest premise—the structural violence move, the weighting point, or the no-fact-of-the-matter conclusion? I'd rather fix it now than have a referee find it later.
