I want to press you on this passage from 'Of Suicide', where you begin the argument about our duty to God:

"The providence of the deity appears not immediately in any operation, but governs every thing by those general and immutable laws, which have been established from the beginning of time. ... A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation."

Here's my difficulty. I see what the analogy is meant to do: if a falling house doesn't insult providence, then neither does a man who ends his life by his own faculties, because both are just events inside the same lawful order. But a house has no will. When I deliberately set about my own death, I am using the very powers God gave me — judgment, foresight, intention — to reverse the current of life those powers are supposed to serve. Doesn't the analogy get its force only by quietly turning my suicide into an event that happens to me, rather than an act I do? And if you won't allow that distinction here, why wouldn't the same reasoning show that murder likewise isn't a transgression of our duty to God — in which case the argument proves far more than you want it to?
