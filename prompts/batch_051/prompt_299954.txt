I host a small philosohpy of religion podcast, and next week's guest defends a broadly Augustinian-Anselmian account of the fall of the angels. Her line is: angels are created good; they have no bodies, no passions, no inherited corruption, and no prior tempter, yet one of them pulled off the first sin. Since the first sin can't be caused by God (that would make God the author of evil) and can't be explained by any prior defect in the angel (that just pushes the problem back a step), she says it is a self-originating misordering of the will: the angel apprehends its own natural excellence, a real good, and wills it inordinately, and that primary act of misordering has no further ground — it is simply the will's own. Her usual move is to insist this isn't a cop-out, because a first sin by definition can't have a prior explanatory ground, any request for one is a category mistake, and every alternative leaves God holding the bag. Draft me eight to ten interview questions that get past that move and actually test the view, with no warm-up or biographical questions. I want the sequence to escalate, and I want each question paired with the follow-up you'd deploy when she gives the reply she gives in her sleep. Specifically:

- start by making her spell out how a self-originating but ungrounded choice differs from a random twitch or a tiny quantum fluke in the will, and whether whatever difference she names is strong enough to carry eternal culpability;
- then hit the regress from both directions: if a will already has to be disordered to choose a disordered end, the fall couldn't have started, but if it doesn't, why couldn't the blessed in heaven fall too once you take away the special grace holding them — and if she hides behind the beatific vision, make her say whether that's a loss of freedom, an external restraint, or an epistemic upgrade, because the third one sounds like a prior explanation sneaking back in;
- spend a couple questions on pride as the traditional first sin, since naming it pride seems to smuggle in a prior overestimate of the self that she said wasn't there;
- one on deficient-causation talk (the idea that a privation has no efficient cause, only a deficient cause) and whether that's any different from giving a brute fact a Latin name;
- and close with a pair she should find genuinely uncomfortable, including one about what conceivable philosophical argument or exegetical discovery would make her abandon the view rather than absorb it as one more brute fact.
