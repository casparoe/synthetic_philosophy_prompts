Let's get into the weeds on Mencius and Xunzi. The textbook contrast is that Mencius says human nature is good and Xunzi says human nature is bad — but that's too clean. Both of them grant that people mostly go wrong without the right conditions, and both grant that people can become genuinely good through the proper kind of cultivation. I want you to argue for a specific reading: that the real disagreement between them is over where to draw the line between xing (nature) and wei (deliberate activity / artifice), and that this is a substantive dispute about the metaphysics of moral development, not just a dictionary fight or a mere empirical disagreement about how many built-in nice feelings people have. Defend the claim that Xunzi can happily grant Mencius his four sprouts — the incipient feelings of compassion (the child-about-to-fall-into-the-well reaction), shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong that Mencius thinks need nurtering — while still denying they count as nature, because for Xunzi whatever has to be learned, shaped, or worked up is artifice by definition. Mencius, by contrast, treats the sprouts as nature precisely because they have a natural direction of growth, like a seed. Then use that contrast to explain why the two positions genuinely part ways over whether moral development is more like cultivating a pre-existing tendency or manufacturing it from outside, like steaming bent wood straight. Your conclusion should be that this isn't merely verbal — defend it, don't just split the difference. Aim for around 800–1000 words, and write it like you're explaining it to a sharp undergrad who's only read one-paragraph summaries.
