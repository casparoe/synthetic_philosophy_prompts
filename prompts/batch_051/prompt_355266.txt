I’m reading Mill’s *Utilitarianism* (the chapter on what sort of proof the principle of utility is susceptible of), and there’s a passage I can’t quite make cohere. He writes:

“Virtue, according to the utilitarian doctrine, is not naturally and originally part of the end, but it is capable of becoming so; and in those who love it disinterestedly it has become so, and is desired and cherished, not as a means to happiness, but as a part of their happiness.”

Then he says money is the same kind of case: originally just a means to other things, but people come to desire it for itself, sometimes more intensely than they desire the things it buys. I understand the basic claim that something can start as a means and turn into an end. What I don’t understand is whether this is still a consequentialist position in any interesting sense. If virtue can be desired for itself as part of one’s happiness, and if a good utilitarian ought to love virtue that way, then is the claim just that consequentialism says virtue’s value is ultimately derived from its effects but can be experienced as non-derivative? Or is it a deeper concession—that virtue has a kind of value that outruns its effects and makes the view halfway to virtue ethics? I’d like help drawing the line Mill is trying to hold. Also, tell me where your reading is least certain and what would change it.
