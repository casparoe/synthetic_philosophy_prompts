A mate of mine who works on interpretability and I spent most of Saturday evening down the pub going back and forth about this, and it's doing my head in that I still can't put my finger on where he's cheating. He was defending roughly this line, which I've written up as steps:

(1) An AI's output is legible only if a human can follow the reasoning that produced it.
(2) For any output, either that reasoning is human-followable or it isn't.
(3) If it is human-followable, then it's the kind of reasoning a human could in principle have gone through themselves.
(4) If a human could in principle have gone through it themselves, then the model isn't adding anything new: it's just doing, faster, what we could already do.
(5) So a legible model can never produce insight beyond human reasoning.
(6) But the whole point of training models on hard problems is to get outputs whose grounds we could not have produced ourselves.
(7) So any genuinely useful output is necessarily illegible.
(8) Therefore training for legibility isn't a coherent engineering goal; it just caps models at human level.

He swears this shows the whole 'make models understandable rather than merely correct' project is confused, because the two aims pull in opposite directions once the tasks get hard. I think that's to quick, but whilst I was objecting I kept getting tangled around steps 3 and 4. I'm not sure whether the issue is his definition of legibility, the move from 'a human could follow it' to 'a human could have done it,' or something later.

Where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? Be specific about the exact step or steps and say what the correct version would be. And if I've mischaracterised what people mean by 'legibility' in this line of work, tell me straight instead of politely working around it—I'd rather be corrected than have the setup treated as fine.
