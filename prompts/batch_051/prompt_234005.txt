I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting an essay on what the choices people make online can and cannot tell us about what they actually want. I want to treat a standard economic argument fairly before criticizing it, and I'd like your help identifying exactly where it gives way.

The argument, as I understand it, runs as follows. Revealed-preference theory begins from the weak and strong axioms of revealed preference: roughly, if a person's choices satisfy these consistency conditions—when one option is chosen over another the implied ranking is not later reversed, and these pairwise rankings do not cycle when chained together—then the person's choices can be represented as maximizing a utility function, with the chosen option always the one assigned the highest value. On the associated ordinalist reading, utility is not an independently measured mental quantity; it is simply a convenient numerical summary of a consistent pattern of choices. A further step, often left implicit, moves from that result to welfare economics: because choices reveal preferences, observed choices are treated as evidence about what makes people better off, a step that quietly supports cost-benefit analysis and standard consumer-welfare reasoning.

The development that unsettles me is the accumulated evidence that many choices are constructed on the spot rather than read off from a stable internal ranking. Framing effects, anchoring, default effects, and preference reversals all suggest that the choice situation contributes to the preference expressed. That concern intensifies in digital marketplaces, where the relevant situation is engineered: the default settings, the ordering of results, the recommended alternatives, and the wording of comparisons are all set by a platform with its own commercial reasons for shaping what gets chosen. Observed choice data are therefore produced jointly by the chooser and by whoever designs the choice environment.

My question is what becomes of the revealed-preference argument under those conditions: does it survive intact, does it need repair, or is it dead as a guide to what people want? Specifically, which premise or step does the context-dependent-choice evidence bear on? If the argument can be repaired—for example, by treating the choice context as part of the object chosen, or by restricting the argument to a purely formal claim about consistency—what would the repaired version still entitle us to say about utility, and what would it have to concede about the move from choices to welfare? I am less interested in any particular regulatory conclusion than in what happens to the argument itself.
