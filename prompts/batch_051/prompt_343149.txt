I'm taking a seminar called Kant on War and Peace this semester, and we've spent most of it on the essay Toward Perpetual Peace. I'm stuck on one of the preliminary articles, the one where Kant says (I don't have the exact translation with me, so I'm paraphrasing) that no state at war may permit hostilities that would make mutual confidence impossible for a future peace. He then names a few examples: using assassins, poisoners, breaching a capitulation, or inciting treason in the enemy state. I want to test it against a case that obviously wasn't in his head.

Suppose State A is at war with State B. State A's cyber command remotely compromises the industrial control system of a water-treatment plant in B's capital and alters the chemical dosing so the water is intermittently toxic for three weeks. People get sick, a few children die, and A's operatives also manipulate the logs to make it look like the plant operator simply made an error. No physical agent crosses the border, and no outside toxin is introduced; the software just makes the plant's own chemicals get released at the wrong times and concentrations. State A later denies that the preliminary article applies: it says it employed no assasin and no poisoner, and that poison has to be a substance, not a process.

I think the purpose of Kant's article pretty clearly reaches it, because being attacked through one's own infrastructure while the enemy hides behind a contractor and a software patch is exactly the kind of thing that would make the two states unable to trust each other during a later peace negotiation. But the literal words seem not to. Can you lay out what each approach—reading the article by its words, by its purpose, and by what Kant would probably say if asked—gives in this case, and tell me which one should win? I also want to know whether the fact that the harm is deniable and indirect changes the analysis, or if that detail is just a modern version of the old poisoner problem.
