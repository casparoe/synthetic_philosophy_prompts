I recently had a long and rather heated argument with a close friend about how societies ought to respond to the prospect of transformative artificial intelligence, and I would like a careful expert audit of the analogy that did most of the work in his position. His claim was that nuclear weapons are the only serious historical analogy. The story he kept returning to was this: once physicists understood that a chain reaction was possible, they did not wait for a city to be destroyed before treating the problem as urgent; they alerted governments, concentrated the work in a handful of state projects, and eventually built an international regime of inspections, non-proliferation agreements, and arms control. Frontier AI, he argued, is the same kind of thing — a dual-use capability that lets a small group concentrate enormous destructive power, developed under competitive pressure rather than deliberate foresight. His conclusion was that we should treat the most capable AI systems exactly as we treat fissile material: a small number of licensed actors, strict state oversight, an international body with inspection powers, and perhaps a moratorium on the most dangerous systems until safeguards exist. He dismissed the printing-press and Industrial Revolution analogies as excuses for complacency: the press, he said, disrupted the epistemic order only slowly and unevenly, and the Industrial Revolution, however disruptive, never placed the survival of the species in a small number of hands.

I have misgivings, but after several hours I cannot tell which of them are principled and which are just discomfort with a conclusion I find politically implausible. Nuclear weapons are physical objects made from rare materials; their production leaves visible industrial signatures; they do not improve themselves; and their danger is located fairly precisely in detonation. AI systems, by contrast, run on widely available hardware, can be copied at essentially zero marginal cost, are diffused throughout the civilian economy, and might, if even weak claims about recursive self-improvement are true, accelerate their own development. My friend insists these are surface differences, because the deep structure is the same: a dangerous capability that cannot be uninvented, held by parties that will race to stay ahead, in a world with no reliable enforcement mechanism.

Please act as a professional philosopher of technology with a strong background in both the history of science and nuclear history. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming before you settle on a judgement. What I would like from you is a systematic audit of the nuclear-weapons analogy. Please give an explicit mapping — a table of correspondences is fine — of what corresponds to what between the nuclear domain and the AI domain. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for my friend's conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Identify what the analogy quietly imports from its source domain, such as assumptions about chokepoints, state monopolies, or the inertness of the technology, that may not hold in the target. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether either of the analogies he dismissed, or something else entirely, would carry the same lesson with less distortion.
