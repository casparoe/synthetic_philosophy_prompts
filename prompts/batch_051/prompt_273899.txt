I cover AI and philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and our newsroom is doing a day of training on how to cover machine learning. I've been handed the ninety-minute does-it-understand slot: eighteen editors and reporters, none of them technical, but there smart and wary of both hype and doom. I don't want to open with a lecture on the Bender-Koller line or a Searle rehash. I want them to run into the skeptical position with their own hands: that a system can produce fluent, confident, even empathetic prose without any of the machinery they'd ordinarily call understanding. Then, in the second half, I want them to feel the opposite pull — that the skeptical story may underdescribe what changes when you add verification, interaction, or a fact-checking objective. My rough seed is a content-farm simulation in two rounds. Round one: teams write for a fake encyclopedia using only a deck of canned sentences and a fixed transition rule, and they get paid per fluent on-topic word, with nobody checking truth. Round two: add a fact-checker role and a different payoff structure so that the winning strategy flips. But I don't want to over-specify it; I'm after a design where the rules do the teaching and the phrase stochastic parrot is never spoken until the debrief. I'd like them to come away seeing that the disagreement is mainly about what the training signal rewards, not about whether a box is really thinking, and that the hardline skeptical position is a claim with real costs. Budget is about twenty dollars in snacks for stakes.

Please design the full runnable thing: setup, rules, timings, what I say when I hand out the cards, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the actual disagreement — generation without truth-tracking versus generation shaped by verification, why RLHF makes the parrot more persuasive rather than more honest, and what the hardline skeptical claim commits you to. Be explicit about what the game can't reproduce (the scale of pretraining, gradient descent, whether the internal states end up with anything like a world-model) and about what will predictably go wrong when it's run and how I should handle it.
