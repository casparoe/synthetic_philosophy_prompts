Suppose two nuclear-armed rivals agree to outsource brinkmanship to a machine. Each side builds a "crisis engine"; once either side declares a crisis, both engines run a shared public lottery. On each day of the crisis, with probability p the engine moves both sides one rung up a fixed escalation ladder (force dispersal, conventional skirmishes, a single small nuclear demonstration in the ocean, strikes on military targets, strikes on cities), and with probability 1-p it holds. p is fixed in advance and cannot be changed mid-crisis. The only way to stop the ladder is for one side to pay a pre-set "stop price" into an escrow account the other side keeps whether or not it also stops. Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral question. Walk through what it would actually do: to crisis bargaining, to the incentive to declare a crisis, to the stop-price rule specifically, and to the risk of a false alarm starting the engine by mistake. Where does the scheme break first, and which failure modes can be fixed by changing the rules versus which are just the old problem of credible threats sneaking back in?
