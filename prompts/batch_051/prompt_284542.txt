I am a philosopher of mind, and I find myself caught between a methodological doctrine and an intuition. The doctrine is heterophenomenology, in roughly Dennett's sense. Its virtues are systematic: the science of consciousness takes as data only what is publicly available, namely subjects' reports and other behavior, and constructs from those reports a third-person account of what it is like according to the subject, treating that account as a represented world rather than as a transparent report of private facts. This keeps the enterprise public and falsifiable, prevents disputes about qualia from contaminating the data, and still honors first-person testimony as a central thing to be explained. I find these virtues compelling, and I have defended the doctrine on their strength in my teaching.

The intuition is far harder to dismiss in my own case. When I attend to my own experience, say the pain in my shoulder or the visual field in front of me, the report I could issue is not the primary datum; the experience is. My access to it is direct in a way that no third-person reconstruction could be. On the heterophenomenological picture, the public report is epistemically primary, and the very experience I am directly acquainted with enters only as something reconstructed or posited. That strikes me as an inversion: the report is a fallible, lossy, later expression of the experience, and a method that refuses to admit the experience itself into the evidence base may have purchased scientific respectability by changing the subject.

I am not asking for a partisan resolution; I want a negotiated verdict. Tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, debunked as a Cartesian holdover rather than honored. Tell me what it would take for the doctrine to be the thing that gives way, amended or abandoned. And tell me whether there is a reading on which both survive, for example one on which heterophenomenology is neutral about the reality of first-person acquaintance and claims only that such acquaintance cannot be admitted as third-person data. For each move, say what would justify it rather than merely motivate it.

Please set out your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each item one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should address, in whatever order seems clearest: the strongest statement of the doctrine's position; the strongest statement of the first-person intuition; what would justify debunking the intuition; what would justify amending or abandoning the doctrine; and the compatibility reading, together with a concrete test for deciding among the three.
