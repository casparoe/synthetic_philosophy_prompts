After forty years of physical chemistry I've taken up philosophy, and I keep coming back to Hume's Enquiry because it reminds me of something from the lab: you never measure the force itself, just the regularities in what the force does. Here's what's got me stuck. Hume plainly says all we ever observe in causation is constant conjunction—A followed by B, over and over—and that the necessary connection is something the mind contributes. But then he repeatedly talks as if there are unknown powers in the objects themselves, hidden features of the world that do the real causal work, and in places he seems to say no reasonable person could deny that the cause has something in it that produces the effect. The "New Hume" readers take this as skeptical realism: Hume believed in a mind-independent causal power, just one we can never know. The older reading says no—Hume is a pure regularity theorist, causation is nothing over and above the regularity, and all the power-talk is just psychology, our expectations projected onto the world. My own credence in the New Hume reading is around 0.3, and a retired biologist I argue with is at 0.7.

So give me your credence that the New Hume / skeptical-realist reading is right, meaning Hume really did believe in mind-independent necessary connections or causal powers, however unknowable. Don't just hand over a number—decompose it. I want the sub-questions your number rests on: how you weight the realist-sounding passages against the regularity-sounding ones, whether the unknown-powers talk can be dismissed as just the background furniture of doing science, whether Hume's copy principle (all ideas come from impressions) even leaves room for him to have a coherent idea of a hidden power, and how much we should trust attributions of belief to a text built around skepticism in the first place. Give each a rough probability, say how they combine, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive too. Then say where you think my 0.3 and my friend's 0.7 most likely diverge—or whether our disagreement is really somewhere else—and what would move your own number the most, short of finding Hume's diary.
