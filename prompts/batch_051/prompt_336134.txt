I'm a researcher attached to a municipal working group that is redesigning how a mid-sized city regulates sex work. The city currently criminalizes selling, buying, and third-party involvement alike. We've spent months going over the standard templates — the Nordic model (which criminalizes buyers but not sellers), the German and Dutch approach (legal brothels with registration and inspection), and New Zealand's full decriminalization of selling and third parties — and we've now floated a hybrid we want stress-tested before presenting it. Under the proposal: (1) selling sex is fully decriminalized and treated as ordinary self-employment; (2) buying sex is legal only when the purchase takes place inside a licensed, worker-majority-owned cooperative venue, and remains a criminal offense elsewhere; and (3) every transaction in a cooperative venue carries a 4% levy, collected by the venue and remitted to a fund for exit services and annual safety audits, and a client who can produce a levy-paid receipt for the transaction is immune from the buying offense.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a moral position to be endorsed. You are an expert in institutional design and the political economy of sex work regulation; think carefully and brainstorm before answering. In particular, work through the incentives the scheme creates for workers who join the cooperatives, for workers who do not, for clients, for cooperative managers, and for police; identify where the scheme would be gamed first; consider second-order effects on prices, stigma, street-based work, and the black market; and say whether the main failure modes could be fixed without gutting the scheme. Then present your analysis as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
