I'm teaching a unit on what it means to say one civilization is more advanced than another, and my students keep suspecting that every scale on offer is just a disguised way of measuring energy use. To head that off, I've been collecting the axes people actually use to rank civilizations. So far I have five. (1) The Kardashev axis: total usable energy captured or expended, from planetary to stellar to galactic. (2) Barrow's downward extension: the smallest scale at which a civilization can deliberately engineer matter, from meter-scale artifacts down through genetic material, molecules, atoms, and nuclei to elementary particles. (3) An information axis: bits stored, processed, or transmitted per unit time, or total accessible information. (4) A velocity or spatial-reach axis: the fastest speeds routinely sustained and the radius of space made accessible, from orbital through interstellar to relativistic. (5) A durability axis: expected lifespan and resilience against self-destruction and natural catastrophes, the L in Drake-equation discussions.

Those five seem to me to cover the standard candidates, so please don't give me a refinement of one of them (a fractional Kardashev interpolation, say) or a weighted combination of the above. I want a genuinely new axis: a different quantity or capability whose growth would make us inclined to call a civilization more advanced, and which is not just energy, scale of manipulation, information, speed, or longevity in disguise. Describe the axis, say how civilizations would be ranked on it, and explain how it relates to the five I listed — where it overlaps with them, where it pulls apart from them. Then step back and say whether it is genuinely a measure of advancement or only of something adjacent, such as power, complexity, or flourishing. Please write the answer in American English.
