I'm a journalist working on a piece about why reasoning about mutable state turns out to be so much harder than it looks, and a programming-languages person I interviewed sketched this argument over coffee. I can't tell wether it's a real paradox or one of the steps is pulling a fast one, but it seems to prove that assignment is impossible, which can't be right.

Take the line:

x = x + 1

and suppose x currently holds 7.

(1) The two occurrences of `x` in that line are the same variable.
(2) If two expressions refer to the same variable, then they refer to the same thing.
(3) So the `x` on the right and the `x` on the left refer to the same thing.
(4) What an expression denotes is determined by what its parts refer to, so both sides of the assignment should denote that same thing.
(5) But when the line runs, the right side contributes 7 (the old value) and the left side is where 8 gets put (the new value).
(6) If both sides have the same denotation, then the old value and the new value are the same thing — in other words, 7 = 8.
(7) Since 7 ≠ 8, and yet `x = x + 1` plainly does something (after it runs, x is 8), at least one of these steps has to go.

So we'd be forced to say either (a) variables don't have stable identity through time, (b) assignment is just a notation trick and doesn't really change anything, or (c) 7 = 8. I really don't want to accept any of those: I'd like to keep the idea that assignment is real and that `x` is the same variable before and after the line runs. So where does the argument actually break? Which step should I give up, why did that step look so plausible, and what's the least costly repair? And if it's not too much: what does the diagnosis tell us about why mutation is so hard to think about — the distinction between a variable and it's value, or whatever is really going on? I'd also be curious what a variable is, so to speak, in languages like C or Python versus in math, and what a location/box picture of memory has to give up or add to make step (6) fail.
