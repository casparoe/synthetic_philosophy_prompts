I am a novelist researching an alternative-history novel set around 1760. My fictional state is a small maritime republic whose governing council has read David Hume’s essays ‘Of Money’ and ‘Of the Balance of Trade’ (from his Political Discourses, 1752) and has adopted a simplified version of them as policy. They no longer pursue a favourable balance of trade, they allow coin to leave freely, and they reject the mercantilist assumption that hoarding bullion is the same as national wealth. Their neighbours remain aggressive mercantilists, hoarding specie and protecting their own markets.

Near the beginning of ‘Of Money’, Hume offers a thought experiment that I can only paraphrase from memory. He asks us to imagine that four-fifths of a nation’s money vanishes overnight. His argument is that prices and wages would fall, foreign buyers would then find its goods cheap, coin would flow back, and the country would soon recover its former stock of money. I want my novel to test that argument over a thirty-year span under less convenient conditions: the republic begins with modest reserves, and its main trading partners are not open markets but hostile mercantilist states that raise tariffs against imported manufactures. Give me your reasoned prediction of how the republic fares. Which parts of the Humean adjustment mechanism are robust, which depend on assumptions about speed of price adjustment, wages, credit, war, or transport, and what would have to hold for the prediction to fail? Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. For each item, state how confident you are in that prediction, and make the fifth item identify the point you are least sure of.
