I have been trying to make sense of a puzzle about rational preference, and I keep finding that the difficulty comes down to what it means to say that one option is all-things-considered better than another. I would like a working definition of that phrase — all-things-considered better than, or, if you prefer, rationally preferable to — precise enough to sort a set of hard cases. A friend of mine insists that transitivity is built into the very idea of a rational preference, while another friend suspects the cases below show that it is not, and I am hoping a good definition will settle their disagreement.

The cases are these. (1) You are offered £20 or £10 with no further consequences; I take the £20 to be clearly better. (2) An agent chooses a dominated option purely out of habit, such as always buying the dearer train ticket because it is the one they have always bought; I take it that they have not thereby shown the dearer ticket to be rationally preferable. (3) A person faces a dial with 1,001 settings. Setting 0 delivers no money and no electric shock. Turning the dial up by one step adds £10,000 a year and increases a mild electric current by an amount too small to be felt; at setting 1,000 the income is enormous but the shock is searing and constant. For every adjacent pair, the person is confident that the higher setting is better than the one below, because the extra money is guaranteed while the added shock is too slight to register; yet the same person confidently prefers setting 0 to setting 1,000. (4) A smoker judges that the next cigarette is, right now, preferable to not smoking it, while also judging that a smoke-free year is preferable to a year of smoking; here the preferences seem to run in opposite directions depending on the time horizon. (5) A cautious person prefers a certain £500 to a 50% chance of £1,100, even though the gamble has the higher expected value; I am unsure whether this is a case of better than at all, or merely a taste for safety.

Please propose a definition, run it through these cases, and say where it gives a verdict you did not expect or leaves one of the cases unsettled. In particular, I want to know whether the definition requires better than to be transitive, and whether the dial case should be described as a failure of rationality, a failure of the definition, or a perfectly coherent set of preferences. If the definition does not handle a case, revise it or defend the surprising result. Please answer in British English.
