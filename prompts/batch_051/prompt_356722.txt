I'm not a professional philosopher — I work in public policy — and I keep noticing a pattern I've started calling "free-floating rationales," which I'd like you to assess candidly. The idea is this. When an institution or a person acts on some consideration and the action later unravels — a project is halted, a policy reversed, a promise retracted, a verdict overturned — the consideration that supported the original action doesn't simply disappear. It lingers in a strange in-between state: no one any longer treats it as a reason to act, and yet it still seems to do normative work. It determines what has to be explained, compensated, or repaired; it constrains how the reversal has to be staged, as when a board must publicly unmake the old rationale rather than just replacing it; and it shows up in verdicts like "they had good reason at the time, but they were wrong." I want to say such a consideration is neither an ordinary agent-neutral reason — it arises only from a particular prior commitment and has no grip on anyone's current deliberation — nor merely a historical fact about someone's psychology, because it outlives the change of view and can be invoked by people who never held it. Example: a drug is approved on evidence that later turns out to have been weak; the approval is withdrawn, but the old evidential rationale now functions as something the regulator must publicly dismantle. Another: a city widens a road because of a traffic forecast; the forecast fails and the project is stopped, but the reason for the widening remains something the planners are answerable for even though nobody believes it anymore.
