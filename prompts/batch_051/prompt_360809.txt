I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums and I keep seeing people do the golden rule dance: one group treats it as basically the one moral principle normal humans can actually use, another group treats it as a folk saying that collapses under pressure. I have two friends who have been going back and forth on this for months, and I want you to write the document they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration between their two positions.

Call the two positions roughly like this:

- The fixers: the golden rule has a recoverable core, and its failures come from loose formulations. Once you distinguish positive and negative formulations, add the right constraints (competence, relevant wants, idealization), or move to something like reciprocal role-reversal tests, it can do real normative work without collapsing into "just do whatever the other person happens to want."
- The abolishers: the golden rule is either vacuous or successfully substantive only when it silently imports a more demanding moral theory. Any version strong enough to generate duties is not the golden rule; any version that stays close to "do as you would be done by" is hostage to offenders' preferences, power asymmetries, and parochial intuitions.

The deliverable should start from Kant's complaint in the Groundwork, which both of them have been arguing about. There, as far as I remember, he says the rule can't ground strict duties because a criminal could appeal to it against his judge, and it can't ground duties of benefit to others because people differ about what they want done to them. I want the two camps to register that as evidence: either the fixer has to explain why their repaired version doesn't have that problem, or the abolisher has to explain why the repaired version stops being the golden rule.

As the professional philosopher in the room, please think carefully and start by brainstorming the candidate formulations and the precise failure modes before you commit to wording. Then write the joint statement they could both sign. Include:

- a numbered agreed-claims section, with claims that are substantive enough to matter but not slanted toward either side;
- a disagreements table where each disagreement is phrased so that its own side would accept the wording;
- a section on cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side;
- for each crux, one concrete case or test both camps would agreeing bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

I'm particularly interested in cases that distinguish "the problem is in the rule's logic" from "the problem is in how actual preferences are formed." Cases like the judge and the criminal, but also things like a partner who wants critical feedback while you want reassurance, or a community whose members disagree about what counts as the relevant likeness between persons. Don't just produce a taxonomy; I want the residual disagreement made exact enough that an outsider could see what experiment or argument would count as progress.

One more request: don't make the agreed-claims section consist of weak "reasonable people can disagree" statements. If the two camps wouldn't sign it, it doesn't belong there; if both would sign it only because it's empty, it also doesn't belong there. The hardest job is finding the non-obvious claims that survive the collaboration. I'll add citations to Kant later if I turn this into an actual post, so don't worry about footnotes.
