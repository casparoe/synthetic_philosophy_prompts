I keep going in circles on a puzzle I first saw years ago and never resolved. People with pain asymbolia will tell you the pain still feels like pain—they can localize it, rate its intensity, say it's sharp or burning—but they don't mind it, they don't find it aversive, and they won't pull their hand away from whatever is causing it. The standard philosophical move is to read those patients as showing that pain's badness is a separate evaluative layer that can go missing while the sensory core stays intact. The obvious reply is that it isn't a clean dissociation: maybe their sensation is subtly degraded, maybe their reports of intact sensation are confabulatory, or maybe the missing badness is just another symptom with no clean architectural lesson.

I want you to treat "pain's badness is intrinsic to the pain experience" as a testable-ish claim and tell me how you'd bring evidence to bear on it. Design the sharpest experiment or observational study you can—asymbolia patients, matched controls, maybe comparing noxious stimuli that load differently on sensory versus affective dimensions, whatever you think is strongest—and say which result would favor the intrinsic-badness reading, which would favor the dissociable-component reading, and what even a clear result would leave unsettled. My standing worry is that no behavioral or imaging outcome can decide between "badness is part of the experience" and "badness is a separate process that is just very tightly coupled in ordinary people," and I'd rather you take that worry head-on than waive it away.

Make the response exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion. And if you think the question as posed is itself confused—say, that it reifies badness in a way no test could get at, or asks evidence to settle a purely conceptual point—say so plainly instead of humoring me with a fake experimental desing.
