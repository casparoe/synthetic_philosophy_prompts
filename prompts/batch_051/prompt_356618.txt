I've been running a graduate reading group on non-consequentialist ethics, and the member who always pushes back on deontology brought in an argument that I can now nearly recite from memory. It doesn't dispute the standard cases from the outside; it argues that deontological constraints, understood as restrictions on how an agent may pursue even good outcomes, are self-defeating on their own grounds. I want to keep the constraints. I find it simply not open to me to frame an innocent person to pacify a mob, kill a healthy bystander to harvest his organs for five patients, or torture a suspect's child to extract information that would save hostages. But the argument makes those prohibitions look like a rule that forbids the best way of conforming to itself. Here is the reconstruction we settled on.

(1) A deontological constraint against an act-type, such as intentionally killing an innocent person, says an agent may not perform that act, even when performing it would prevent several other agents from performing instances of that same act-type in the near future.  
(2) Any such constraint must be grounded in the features that make its prohibited instances wrong; otherwise the prohibition is arbitrary.  
(3) The feature that makes each intentional killing wrong, namely the violation of an innocent person, is present in each victim: five such killings involve five instances of that wrong-making feature, and five such violations are morally worse than one.  
(4) Therefore, the reasons grounded in that feature weigh more in favor of preventing five such killings than they weigh in favor of refraining from one such killing. An agent who must choose between a world containing one such killing and a world containing five has decisive reason, from the ground of the constraint itself, to choose the world with one.  
(5) But in the riot case, the agent can prevent five killings only by committing one; the constraint nevertheless forbids the agent to commit the one, directing her to bring about the very state of affairs, five violations of the constraint, that the constraint's ground ranks as worse.  
(6) The same structure applies to promise-breaking, torture, and perjury: a constraint that forbids the least-bad way of realizing fewer instances of the wrong it tracks is self-defeating in form.  
(7) So either the constraint's ground has some special agent-relative shape that blocks the move from (3) to (4), or deontological constraints are really just goals in disguise. The first option looks ad hoc, and the second is consequentialism by another name.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would rather reject premise (4) than give up the ban in the riot case, but I do not know what I then lose: perhaps the entire thought that the constraint is there for the sake of the victims. I want a precise diagnosis of the exact bad step or inferred transition, and then a pricing of the main ways a deontologist could stop the argument: the agent-relative constraint, the victim-centered grounding, and threshold deontology. I would also like to know whether any portion of this dispute is verbal rather than substantive.

Please structure your answer in four sections, in exactly this order, with rough lengths: (1) Diagnosis, stating which step is false or where the reasoning slides (about 150 words); (2) Why it looked right, explaining the idea doing the work and its appeal (about 200 words); (3) The price of each repair, covering at least three ways a deontologist could stop the argument and what each concedes (about 350 words); and (4) What I should believe, giving your preferred position and the residual tension it leaves (about 150 words).
