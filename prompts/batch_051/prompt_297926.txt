Got into a long argument last night with a friend about whether left and right are still useful categories, and he spent half of it drawing a horseshoe on a napkin. His claim: the farther you go left or right, the more the two sides bend toward each other, until at the tips they practically touch — same taste for policing speech, same allergy to internal dissent, same certainty about who the enemies are, just different labels on the jerseys. His conclusion was that the whole left-right axis is mostly aesthetic and that people who defend it are confusing team colors with real positions. I argued back but couldn't quite say *where* the picture went wrong, which annoys me because im sure it does somewhere.

Can you audit the horseshoe image for me? Lay out the correspondence table first — what in politics is supposed to play the role of the two arms, the open end, the curvature, the nearly-touching tips — and then go through where the mapping breaks down. I want to know which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are just complaints about the drawing, like the fact that real political space isn't literally one-dimensional. Does his conclusion — the left-right distinction is overrated because the extremes converge — survive once the breaks are on the table, or was the horseshoe carrying the whole argument? And if there's a better image for what actually holds each side together, or for the way they do and don't converge, I'd take that too.
