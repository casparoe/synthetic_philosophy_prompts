Last month I sat in on an argument between two friends, Clara, who works on formal logic, and Anton, who studies historical Christian theology. The question was whether the doctrine of the Trinity can be stated without formal contradiction. They agreed on a minimal set of claims that any orthodox statement must preserve: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God, there is exactly one God, and the Father, Son, and Spirit are really distinct from one another.

Clara opened with a purely formal point. If the copula in the Father is God expresses identity, then by symmetry and transitivity the Father is identical with the one God and the Son is identical with the same God, so the Father is identical with the Son, which the doctrine denies. Therefore the five claims cannot all be true if that copula means identity. Something must give: one of the claims, the reading of the copula, or the assumption that identity behaves normally in this domain.

Anton replied that the copula should be read as predication rather than identity. To say the Father is God is to say the Father possesses the divine nature, just as Socrates is human and Plato is human without the two being identical. The distinctness of the persons is compatible with their each being divine in the same sense.

Clara answered that this buys consistency only by giving up monotheism. If the divine nature is a kind that several individuals can each possess, then the Father and the Son are two distinct divine individuals, which is two gods, or three. The doctrine must affirm one divine individual, not merely one divine kind, or the word God has changed its meaning.

Anton said that this was exactly why the tradition distinguishes person from substance. The three are not three instances of the divine nature; the one divine substance exists as three persons, and the persons are distinct without dividing the substance. To say the Father is God is to identify the Father with the one divine substance, while to say the Father is not the Son is to mark a distinction among persons rather than substances. Consubstantial means one and the same being.

Clara pressed him from the other side. If the Father is identical with the one divine substance and the Son is identical with that same substance, transitivity again makes the Father identical with the Son. If the Father and Son are, as persons, not strictly identical with the substance but only related to it in some looser way, then the copula in the Father is God is no longer identity and the tritheism worry returns. And if the distinction among the persons is real while each is the one substance, then the one substance has conflicting attributes at once: as Father it is unbegotten, as Son it is begotten, as Spirit it proceeds. That is a failure of the substitution of identicals, which is about as basic as logic gets. The only way out, Clara said, is to hold that the distinctions are not real distinctions in God but merely three ways of thinking or speaking about one substance, and that abandons the real distinctness of the persons.

Anton's last proposal was to deny that identity is absolute. He said that the word same is always incomplete until one specifies same what: same God or same person. The Father and the Son are the same God but not the same person, and the apparent contradiction arises only because we assume a single identity relation indifferent to the kind of thing being counted. If identity is relative to a sortal term, all five claims can be true without contradiction.

Clara replied that this was a heavier cost than Anton admitted. Abandoning absolute identity would mean that an ordinary truth such as the morning star is the evening star would stop being a strict identity and become only a claim about the same planet under different descriptions, and most ordinary counting and inference would need to be rewritten. Moreover, even if one accepts relative identity, saying that the Father and the Son are the same God but different persons still leaves unexplained why three persons each of whom is God should count as one God, since counting has itself become kind-relative. She suspected the proposal was a restatement of the problem rather than a solution.

At that point they were going in circles and asked me to referee. I could not decide which of them had the better case. Please adjudicate the argument move by move in continuous prose: say where each move is strong or mistaken, identify the philosophical commitments each position takes on, say what, if anything, both sides are missing, evaluate Clara's final objection to sortal-relative identity, and give a clear verdict on whether either of them has stated all five claims consistently, and if not, which revision is least costly. One request about the form: please write the reply entirely in continuous prose, as if you were writing a letter, and do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
