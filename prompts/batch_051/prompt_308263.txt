A couple of weeks ago someone at a dinner party made a joke about a recent, genuinely awful event in a friend's life, and when the table went quiet he shrugged and said, 'Comedy is tragedy plus time.' Vague enough to mean anything, pointed enough to end the argument. I've been reading around the philosophy of humour — the superiority, incongruity, and relief theories, and the newer benign-violation account — and I can see how the one-liner could be gesturing at any of them, but I can't pin down what it is actually claiming, and I suspect it's doing three jobs at once: folk psychology about how laughter works, a norm about when it is permissible to laugh, and a piece of self-exculpation after telling a joke that didn't land.

I'd like you to lay out the distinct precise claims that 'comedy is tragedy plus time' could be used to express, and, for each claim, say which theory of humour it is built on; whether it is a platitude, a defensible empirical claim, or simply false; and which of them would actually support joking about someone's recent pain at a dinner party and which would not. In particular, I want to know what 'time' is doing under each reading. Is it distance that makes superiority feel safe? Duration that lets an incongruity become salient rather than threatening? The passing of danger that allows stored tension to release as relief? Or the process by which a violation shifts from threatening to benign, so that 'too soon' just means 'not yet benign'? And is the line, as used, even a theory of humour, or just an etiquette of timing? I want the readings laid out as a menu, with verdicts on each, rather than one settled interpretation.
