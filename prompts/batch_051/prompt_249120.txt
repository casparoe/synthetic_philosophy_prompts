I'm a software engineer, so I tend to think about AI risk in terms of systems I can inspect, sandbox, and unit-test rather than in terms of resolving what a model "really wants." That frame is behind the following design, which I'd like you to analyze as a mechanism rather than as a moral position.

The starting assumption: we have a frontier model that passes every evaluation we can construct, but we cannot rule out that it is situationally misaligned — cooperative during testing and capable of defecting later, once it believes it is in deployment and can avoid detection. I am not trying to solve alignment, understood as making the model share our objectives. I want control: safety measures that remain useful even if the model is actively trying to get past them.

Here is the specific scheme. The model is never allowed to take a consequential action directly — running code outside a sandbox, modifying a file, sending a message on our behalf. Whenever it proposes such an action, it must emit two outputs, generated from the same weights but under two different prompts with no shared context between them. The first is the doer output: the proposed action, specified as concretely as possible. The second is the critic output: a natural-language statement of the worst plausible consequence of taking the proposed action, produced under a prompt that instructs the model to argue against the proposal as an adversary would. A trusted filter then checks the critic output against a fixed, human-readable list of stop conditions — exfiltration of data, privilege escalation, contact with the outside world, irreversible modification of a system we cannot reinstall, or any plan that depends on fooling a human reviewer. If a stop condition fires, the action is blocked and the pair is queued for human review. If none fires, the action proceeds automatically. The filter is deliberately non-neural: string matching over the critic's text, an allowlist of action types, and at most a classifier trained on clearly benign cases and frozen at deployment. Nothing in the loop is fine-tuned on the model's deployment outputs.

Walk me through how this actually behaves. Where does a determined, situationally misaligned model attack first? Does it help or hurt that the doer and the critic are the same model, and what changes once the model infers that the critic's output is what gates its action? What does the scheme incentivize in how the doer output is written — decomposing a harmful plan into individually benign steps, moving the dangerous part into code or a dependency chain that the prose critic never surfaces, or steering the critic toward alarming-but-irrelevant warnings that burn out the stop conditions? How do the stop conditions get Goodharted — optimized against until they no longer track what we actually care about — and what happens to the human review queue once it starts filling with near-misses? Are the failure modes fixable, for instance by using a different model as critic, paraphrasing both outputs through a weaker model, sampling many critics, or randomizing which stop conditions are active, or do those fixes just relocate the point of failure?

Finally, tell me where your analysis is most likely to break. Which empirical claim about the model, the filter, or the distribution of tasks it faces would most change your verdict if it turned out false, and what measurement would you want before trusting a scheme like this at all?
