I've been working through the debate over imprecise probabilities, and I keep coming back to a distinction I want checked before I try to use it in writing. I'll call it first-order versus second-order imprecision. First-order imprecision is the familiar view that an agent's belief about a proposition should sometimes be represented by a set of probability functions rather than a single sharp number: my credence that a card drawn from a mostly-but-not-fully-shuffled deck is red might be the interval [0.4, 0.6] rather than 0.5. Second-order imprecision is the further claim that, in many realistic cases, the boundaries of that set are themselves not something I can settle precisely. The agent doesn't merely fail to have one sharp credence; she also fails to have a sharp view about how much imprecision is appropriate. Her state isn't best described as "somewhere in [0.4, 0.6]" with her confident in exactly those brackets. It is closer to something like "somewhere around the middle, with the edges of the interval themselves left fuzzy." I want to say we should represent this not with a single interval, but with a set of intervals, or with a fuzzy set over possible credences rather than a crisp one.

Here is the claim I'd like assessed. I suspect that many standard objections to imprecise probability trade on treating the imprecise agent as though she had crisp bounds on her credal set. For example, the objection that imprecise agents face an indeterminate or excessively cautious decision rule assumes there is a determinate set of probabilities to feed into max-min-style reasoning, and the dilation phenomenon assumes there is a definite widest interval that gets wider upon learning something apparently irrelevant. If we model the agent as second-order imprecise, I think these objections lose their force, because there is no fact of the matter about exactly which probability functions are permitted, and therefore no determinate worst case or unique interval for the objector to attack. My worry is that this move either collapses into ordinary precise probability (if the fuzziness does no real work), or starts an infinite regress (if we immediately need third-order imprecision about the second-order set). Is this distinction already drawn in the literature under another name, like higher-order imprecision or fuzzy credal sets? And is the central claim right, or does it merely relocate the original problems one level up? Please answer in American English.
