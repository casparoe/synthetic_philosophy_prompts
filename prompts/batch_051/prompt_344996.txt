After retiring from a career in biochemistry, I have been reading legal philosophy with the time I used to give to laboratory work, and I keep returning to the maxim that ignorance of the law excuses no one. I tried to spell out the contrary intuition as an explicit argument, and it led me to a conclusion that seems hard to reconcile with how legal systems actually operate. My scientific training taught me to be suspicious of derivations whose intermediate steps feel too smooth, yet here I cannot locate the step at which the meaning of the words changes. Here is my reconstruction:

(1) It is unjust to punish a person for violating a rule if she could not reasonably have been expected to know the rule at the time she acted.
(2) The body of law now in force in the United States — federal statutes, agency regulations, state and local ordinances — is so vast and technical that no one, not even a careful lawyer, can know more than a small fraction of it.
(3) Therefore, for any ordinary citizen, most of the rules that actually apply to her are rules she cannot reasonably be expected to know.
(4) Therefore, when an ordinary citizen is punished by the state for violating a rule that actually applies to her, the rule she violated is, in the typical case, one she could not reasonably be expected to know.
(5) Therefore, the ordinary practice of punishing citizens for violating the law is largely unjust.

I find steps (1) and (2) hard to resist, and the chain seems to draw the conclusion by itself, yet even jurists sympathetic to fair-notice concerns would balk at step (5). Is the argument valid as it stands? If it is not valid, I would like to know precisely which step or steps fail, what a corrected version of the failing step would say, and whether any phrase carries two different senses across the argument. If it is valid, I would like to know which premise I should be most reluctant to grant and why.
