Peirce has this bit I keep coming back to: inquiry only gets going when a belief you actually lean on stops working and produces a kind of irritation — the doubt has to be alive, not the fake Cartesian thing where you "doubt" the external world for an afternoon and then carry on exactly as before. I want a working definition of *genuine doubt* — the state that actually launches inquiry — that classifies the following cases, some of which I have a view on and some of which I genuinely don't:

- A physicist who notices a well-corroborated theory keeps mispredicting in one narrow domain, and starts checking whether they've made an error somewhere.
- Someone who announces "I doubt free will exists" in a forum argument, but everywhere else in their life continues to get angry, blame, plan, and hold people responsible exactly as before.
- A person who flips a coin between two options they've already decided are exactly equal in expected value.
- Someone who deliberately tries to talk themselves into uncertainty about whether their charitable giving is driven by altruism or by self-image, on the theory that it will make their motives more honest.
- A member of a community where everyone accepts a textbook consensus but nobody can reconstruct the evidence, who starts asking "why do we actually believe this?"

Give me the definition, run each case through it, and where the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, say whether you'd revise the definition or bite the bullet on the verdict. Then tell me how confident you are in the definition and which single verdict you're least sure of.
