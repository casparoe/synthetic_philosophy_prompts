John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government*, Chapter VIII, section 95:

> Men being, as has been said, by nature, all free, equal, and independent, no one can be put out of this estate, and subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent. The only way whereby any one divests himself of his natural liberty, and puts on the bonds of civil society, is by agreeing with other men to join and unite into a community for their comfortable, safe, and peaceable living one amongst another, in a secure enjoyment of their properties, and a greater security against any, that are not of it.

Construct a thought experiment built around this passage. The scenario should be a private city or seastead, brought into being tomorrow, whose founders claim that every adult under its authority has explicitly consented in Locke's sense. Build it to the following specifications: no one may be born into membership; children raised within the jurisdiction must, upon reaching majority, perform a deliberate, revocable act of consent before acquiring full political membership; no resident may be physically restrained from departing; the jurisdiction must be economically and administratively plausible without fantastic technology; and it must be situated within territory or waters claimed by a functioning liberal-democratic state that has never obtained the consent of the city's members.

The task is not to argue in general for or against private cities or seasteading. It is to produce the strongest possible case in which the opt-out succeeds on Locke's explicit-consent criterion while the surrounding state nevertheless continues to assert jurisdiction over the same people. After presenting the scenario, show the reasoning available to each side: why the city's governing body can honestly claim explicit consent from its members, why the state can still claim legitimate authority over them, and whether the quoted test yields a determinate answer or simply stops short. Then identify the structural feature of the scenario that does the real work in creating the deadlock. Predict what would happen if that feature were removed: does the case collapse into a standard social-contract problem, an ordinary conflict-of-laws dispute, or something else? Finally, state the strongest attack that a defender of the state's jurisdiction could make against your scenario.
