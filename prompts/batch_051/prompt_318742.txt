I'm in a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century British moral philosophy, and we've just finished a unit on Henry Sidgwick. After the last session, a classmate compressed Sidgwick's "dualism of the practical reason" for me roughly as follows: Sidgwick was a hedonistic utilitarian who held that the fundamental axiom of morality—that each person's happiness matters, and that no one person's happiness counts for more than another's—is grasped by rational intuition. Yet he also thought that reason equally licenses making one's own happiness the organizing end of one's life, and he could find no proof that this egoistic standpoint is irrational. So both "rational egoism" and "rational benevolence" carry the authority of practical reason, and they issue incompatible demands. On my classmate's telling, this means Sidgwick held that each of us can ultimately be moved only by the prospect of our own pleasure, that the conflict is therefore built into human psychology, and that Sidgwick took this to show that utilitarianism rests on an illusion and that the only honest conclusion left to him was a deflationary ethical skepticism.

I've done the assigned reading, and while the broad outline seems right, the slide from "two rational demands conflict" to "ethical skepticism" feels unlike the Sidgwick we actually studied, and I suspect the claim about being moved only by one's own pleasure smuggles in a motivational thesis that the dualism itself doesn't require.
