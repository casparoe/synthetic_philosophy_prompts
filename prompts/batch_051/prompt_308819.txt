The "boundary problem" in democratic theory concerns who should make up the demos: who is entitled to participate in a democracy's binding decisions. Two leading answers are the all-affected principle (everyone whose interests are affected by a decision should have some say in it) and the all-subjected principle (everyone subject to the state's coercive enforcement of its laws should have some say in that state's government). Consider a counterfactual that tests the all-subjected principle in a relatively pure form.

In 2028, a consolidated parliamentary democracy of about 40 million people—call it Esperia—with a proportional electoral system, high public trust in courts, and annual net immigration of roughly 0.8% of its population, adopts a constitutional amendment. Starting in 2029, every adult lawful resident of Esperia who has lived in the territory for at least five continuous years may vote in national elections, regardless of citizenship. At the same time, Esperian citizens who have lived abroad for more than ten consecutive years lose the vote in national elections until they re-establish residence in the country. No other country changes its franchise rules, and dual citizenship remains permitted.

Predict how Esperian politics evolves over the following fifteen years. Make your reasoning visible: trace the mechanisms through parties, candidates, voters, and institutions rather than just listing outcomes. Address at least these questions: (1) How do political parties and candidates adjust their platforms, coalition strategies, and mobilization efforts to the newly enlarged electorate? (2) What happens to immigration, naturalization, and diaspora-related policy? (3) How do the newly enfranchised residents and the disenfranchised expatriates react, and what does this do to the perceived legitimacy of elections? (4) Is the amendment stable, or does the boundary get renegotiated—through further constitutional amendment, court interpretation, or informal practice—and in which direction? Throughout, separate the effects you expect robustly from effects that depend on contingent features of Esperia's party system, economy, and administrative capacity. End by stating how confident you are in your overall prediction, identifying the single point in your chain of reasoning you are least sure of, and saying what fact about the world would most change that assessment.
