I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've been reading Aristotle's *On Interpretation* for a story I'm drafting about a prophet whose predictions all come true, and I can't shake this argument. In chapter 9 he talks about the sea-battle: tomorrow there either will or won't be a sea-battle, but from that alone it doesn't follow that the one or the other is already fixed. I think I understand him but here's the argument that keeps bugging me, my own version:

(1) Any meaningful assertion about the future is going to turn out true or false. Even before the time arrives, it already has a truth value, because the future will be what it will be.  
(2) If an assertion about tomorrow is already true, then the fact it describes is already in place, at least as something that cannot be avoided.  
(3) The sentence "There will be a sea-battle tomorrow" is a meaningful assertion about the future.  
(4) So either the sea-battle is already unavoidable, or its non-occurrence is already unavoidable.  
(5) If that's so for every future-tensed assertion, then everything anyone will ever do is already unavoidable before they do it.  
(6) Therefore none of the choices my characters make are genuinely open when they make them.

I have a strong pull toward (1) because I can't see how a proposition could lack truth value, wich just sounds like it's neither true nor false and that breaks my brain. But I also can't accept (6) because then the novel becomes a puppet show, not a story. I want you to tell me whether this argument is valid, and if not, precisely which step fails. I half suspect (2) is doing the work by slipping from "it's true that it will happen" to "it's now unavoidable that it will happen." If that's right, give me the strongest reading of (2) on which it's true and check whether the argument still goes through. Also, if you think Aristotle's own distinction can be rebuilt without truth-value gaps, say how.

Please end with a final answer in the form \answer{...} where the content is either "valid" or "invalid at step N" with a short label for the failing step.
