In my early modern philosophy class we've been reading Berkeley's *Principles of Human Knowledge*, and somthing in the opening has stuck with me. Berkeley says: "It is a hard thing to suppose that right deductions from true principles should ever end in consequences which cannot be maintained or made consistent." Then later we got to the so-called master argument, where Berkeley seems to say: try to conceive of an unperceived tree; you'll find you're conceiving it, so it's impossible for anything to exist unperceived. That move seems like exactly the kind of unmaintainable consequence he warns us about earlier—it looks like he's going from "I can't mentally do X" to "X is impossible." But maybe I'm the one taking the master argument too atomistically, and the real argument doesn't let you help yourself to that reading.

I'd like a committed take: is the standard reading of Berkeley's master argument just a bad deduction from a true-ish premise, or is the absurdity a product of misreading the passage? What's the strongest reconstruction of what Berkeley is actually arguing there, and does it avoid the psychological-to-metaphysical leap? Don't just lay out interpretations—defend the one reading you think gets it right, and tell me if my framing is off.
