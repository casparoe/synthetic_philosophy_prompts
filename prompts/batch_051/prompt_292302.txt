I've been in a democratic theory reading group for a couple years now, and there's one argument that keeps coming back no matter what we're reading: who should get to take part in a democratic decision in the first place? Everyone agrees a decision is only democratic if the right people make it, but there's zero agreement about who the right people are. One camp says anyone whose interests are affected by a decision should have standing, because otherwise you're being ruled without a say over things that harm or benefit you. Another says standing belongs to anyone who will be subject to the resulting law or coercion, because what democracy legitimates is coercion, not interests. A third says the demos just is the existing citizenry of a state, because you need an already determinate membership before any procedure for counting voices can even run. The running example in the group is whether long-term residents who aren't citizens should vote in local elections, and the three camps give three different answers without any of them moving. I know this is a running dispute in political theory, but what I can't find is anyone stating what kind of disagreement it is, rather than just arguing for a side. My working hypothesis is that it isn't really about facts and isn't even straightforwardly about values: the phrase 'who belongs to the demos?' is doing several different jobs at once, and each camp is answering a different question under the same heading. There's also a circularity nobody wants to admit out loud, namely that any answer to who-should-decide-who-decides already presupposes a determinate group doing the deciding.

So I don't want a survey or a verdict for one camp. Please give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion. Make them: (1) a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, (2) the precise version of the different questions each camp might actually be answering, (3) the strongest version of the view that the debate is merely verbal or just different questions, (4) the strongest version of the view that there's a real substantive disagreement underneath, and (5) what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a case, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
