In my philosophy of language seminar we have been circling the question of why the sorites debate has locked into entrenched camps without resolution. Epistemicism saves bivalence and classical logic by positing sharp but unknowable boundaries; supervaluationism keeps classical logic while abandoning bivalence for borderline statements; contextualism keeps both by making the extension of 'heap' shift with context and standards of tolerance. Since all three accommodate the same ordinary judgments about piles of sand, counterexamples do not move anyone. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not a verdict on the paradox itself. My working hypothesis is that it is a clash of bedrock commitments — a dispute about whether logic, semantics, or ordinary usage is negotiable — which would make it immune to empirical or formal resolution. But an alternative is that the camps are simply answering different questions: one about determinacy in the world, one about the logic of vague discourse, one about actual speaker behavior. Please diagnose, using what each camp actually says, whether this is verbal, factual, or a conflict of non-negotiable intuitions, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a formal result, an experimental finding, a distinction, a change of question, or an argument that none should be expected.
