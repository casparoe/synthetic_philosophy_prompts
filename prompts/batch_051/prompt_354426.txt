I am finishing a novel in which a single character is known by three different names across the book. The invented society uses family names in public and given names only within the household. My protagonist first appears as the public figure called solely by her family name; in the middle chapters, when she returns to her mother's house, the narration uses the given name her family has for her, which has never appeared before; later, after she is stripped of her position and joins a cloistered order, she lives under a new name the order bestows. These are not disguises, and my intention is that the reader should gradually recognize the public figure, the daughter, and the novice as one and the same woman, without the narrator ever saying so. A careful early reader did not recognize this. She assumed that the woman known by the family name and the woman known by the given name were two people, and that the woman under the order's name was a third, so the convergence that was meant to resolve the book struck her as an unearned trick. My editor now wants one explicit early line linking the family name to the given name; I resist, because the novel's method is to show a person re-described and renamed while remaining herself. I am trying to understand what I was quietly asking the reader to do. What does a reader ordinarily rely on to keep the same person before her when a name changes, and which of those supports did I withhold? Is an overt announcement the only honest repair, or can the narration make the names do this work itself, without a narrator's stepping in to state that the different names belong to one person?
