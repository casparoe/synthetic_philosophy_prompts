I've been working out a view I call recognition-bounded randomness, and I want it stress-tested rather than just validated. The claim is that randomness is not a property of a finite sequence but of an encounter between a sequence and an observer's recognition repertoire: the finite stock of compressors and prediction rules the observer can already competently deploy before seeing the sequence. Say a sequence S is random for observer O just in case every procedure in O's repertoire compresses S no better than it compresses a typical fair-coin sequence of the same length, and no procedure in the repertoire predicts the next bit of S at better than chance; S is merely unrecognized, not random, when some procedure outside the repertoire would have handled it as a pattern. I actually want to defend the consequences: the same string can be random for one person and patterned for another (a lottery draw that encodes someone's birthday in binary; the binary digits of π for someone who has never met π); a pseudorandom stream is random only for observers whose repertoire lacks its generator; and a sequence can stop being random for an observer without changing at all, when the observer learns a new recognizer — the randomness is lost retroactively even though nothing in the sequence changes. I know this brushes against the standard algorithmic treatments — incompressibility, effective statistical tests, computable betting systems — but those all seem to make randomness a property of the sequence (or of all bounded agents at once), whereas mine makes the observer part of the definition and treats "is this finite sequence random?" as incomplete in the way "is this place far?" is incomplete.

I honestly cannot tell whether this is a real distinction or subjectivism with formal decoration, and I want an honest assessment. Please (1) reconstruct the strongest version of the view and say exactly what it commits me to, (2) identify which existing positions it amalgamates and where, if anywhere, it is genuinely distinct rather than relabeled, (3) give the two most damaging objections with concrete examples where the view says something false or useless, (4) compare it with sequence-relative definitions by saying which cases it handles better than they do and which it handles worse, and (5) give a verdict on whether the central notion is doing real work or should be abandoned as a metaphor. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
