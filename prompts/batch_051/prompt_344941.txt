Switzerland maintains no large professional standing army. Nearly every able-bodied Swiss man is conscripted into a citizen militia: he trains in short installments spread over years, keeps his issued service rifle at home during his service obligation, and remains liable to call-up for years after his initial training. Women may serve only as volunteers, and conscientious objectors may perform a longer civilian alternative service. The institution sits inside a policy of armed neutrality rather than alliance membership, and it has remained broadly intact while many neighboring states professionalized their forces or joined military alliances. Reconstruct the political philosophy this institution presupposes. In particular: (1) What does the male-only character of the obligation presuppose about the relation between gender, citizenship, and the distribution of the risks and burdens of common defence? (2) What does storing military rifles in private homes presuppose about the state's trust in its citizens, and about where legitimate violence may properly reside in a democracy? (3) Are the presuppositions consistent with one another — above all, can a system be understood as an institution of civic equality while it distributes its central burden so unequally between the sexes? Throughout, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and finish by saying what would have to change in law and civic culture if the underlying view were replaced by one in which military protection is a voluntary, market-priced service supplied by professionals.
