I'm a novelist, not a professional philosopher, but for the current book I need to get a certain scene right, and I've ended up reading Bernard Williams on integrity. My character saves his wife from a fire in full view of a neighbor he can't reach, and then has to explain himself afterwards; I don't want the scene to collapse into either he was just doing his duty or he doesn't really love her. I've been working through what Williams means when he says the husband in the drowning case, if he finally justifies saving his wife by citing duty or rightness, has had one thought too many. My notes so far list four readings. (1) The alienation reading: the extra thought shows he stepped outside the relationship and treated his wife as one claimant among others. (2) The integrity reading: impartial morality asks him to replace his particular commitment to his wife with a general reason, and the replacement itself is the damage Williams is pointing at. (3) The justification reading: the problem isn't that he stops to think—he might have to—it's that he reaches for an impartial justification after the fact, when the unforced answer is just that she's his wife. (4) The character-flaw reading: the impulse to launder a personal motive through duty or rightness is a defect of moral taste, not something a good husband would feel the need to do. I keep feeling theres a fifth reading I've half-overheard, something about what the demand to justify itself does to him, or to the relationship, before he opens his mouth. Give me that one (or a better fifth), make it genuinely different from the four above rather than a rewording or a mix of them, and say what it would change in how I write the explanation scene.
