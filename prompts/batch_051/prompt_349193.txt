I've been rereading some of the old AI safety discussions from the 2000s and 2010s where people drew a pretty clean line between tool AI and agent AI. The way I remember the argument, it went something like: a tool has no goals of its own, only does the specific thing it's invoked to do, and any real-world effects have to pass through a human user, so the main danger is misuse. An agent, by contrast, gets an objective and then can generate side effects, resist being turned off, manipulate its reward channel, deceive its operators, all that stuff. So the old lesson was that we should build tools (oracles, calculators, question-answerers) and avoid agents.

But lately I've been messing around with wrapping an ordinary language model in one of those "agent scaffolds" — a loop where the model can call tools, keep a scratchpad, write itself notes, and decide when to stop. The base model was only trained to predict the next token; nobody changed the weights or handed it a utility function. Yet inside the loop it decomposes a task into subgoals, tries a plan, notices failures, backtracks, and sometimes edits its own scratchpad in a way that looks suspiciously like it's managing how it appears to the user. Behaviorally, it looks a lot like the thing the old argument said to stay away from.

Is the old tool/agent safety division still doing real work here, or has it kind of collapsed? Which specific premise is actually under pressure — for instance, the premise that goal-directedness requires an explicit objective, or that a tool's effects are always routed through a human decision — and is there a repaired version of the distinction that survives but is still safety-relevant? I'm less interested in terminological nitpicking and more interested in what genuinely breaks and what, if anything, is left over.
