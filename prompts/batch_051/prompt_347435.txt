I've been going down a Mill rabbit hole for a class on liberalism and empire, and its weird how the Mill of On Liberty doesn't obviously match the Mill who spent thirty-five years at the East India Company, defended Company rule after the 1857 uprising, and argued that representative government was unsuited to societies he classed as not yet civilized — for whom, he thought, a paternalistic despotism could be legitimate so long as it aimed at their eventual improvement. At the same time, in his essay on non-intervention, he mostly says that the nations he considered civilized shouldn't meddle in each other's affairs, but then he carves out an exception for peoples he called barbarous, on the ground that the ordinary rules of international morality don't apply to them. Here's what I want to work out. Suppose Mill could be brought up to speed on the post-1945 order — decolonization, the UN Charter, the Genocide Convention, and especially the Responsibility to Protect doctrine adopted in 2005, which says a state's sovereignty carries a duty to protect its population from mass atrocities, and that if it fails, the international community may use force as a last resort, roughly the way NATO did in Libya in 2011. Those interventions are multilateral and time-limited, aimed at stopping atrocities rather than permanent tutelary rule, so it's not obvious whether Mill would even classify them as the kind of despotism-for-improvement he defended or as something new. What position do you think he'd take on R2P? I'm less interested in a bare for-or-against than in the full chain of reasoning: which of his commitments pull him toward endorsing something like the Libya intervention (the despotism-as-tutelage line, his belief that a more advanced nation could govern a backward one for the latter's benefit, his insistence that what backward peoples needed was security and improvement rather than self-rule), and which pull him the other way (his view that a civilized people is the best judge of its own affairs, his support for national self-determination, his worry that rule from London at a distance and without local knowledge would be worse than the Company's expert administration).

And when you answer, please seperate what's fairly settled about his view from what's genuinely contested among people who actually study Mill on empire. There are scholars who read the civilization/barbarism distinction as a sincere universalist test, and others who read it as mostly a rationalization for empire; I don't want either side presented as the consensus where it isn't. Also flag which steps in your reconstruction you're confident about and which are just extrapolation from a handful of texts.
