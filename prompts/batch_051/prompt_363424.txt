So I've been tinkering with a scheme for giving emergency border controls and lockdowns some legitimacy without handing the executive a blank check, and I want it stress-tested as a mechanism rather than argued as a moral question. The proposal: in a declared public-health emergency, the government can close borders or impose stay-at-home orders for two weeks on its own authority. After that, the measure has to be re-approved every ten days by a citizens' assembly of 150 adults drawn by lot, excluding anyone currently employed by the health ministry, the police, or the executive branch. The assembly meets online for one paid day, gets shown the same internal epidemiological modelling the cabinet sees, hears one advocate for keeping the measure and one for ending it, and votes by secret ballot. Individual votes are published five years later, but names are never attached. A no vote cannot be overruled, but the government can call a fresh assembly the following week and try again. Walk me through how this thing actually behaves if it were real. What does the ten-day renewal cycle do to compliance and to the politicking around each vote? Who bothers to sit through the assembly, and how do both sides game the lottery, the evidence briefing, or the assembly's composition first? Where does it break first — in the first bad pandemic, or somewhere the designers didnt think about — and are the breakages fixable without gutting the point of the mechanism? Would the five-year release of individual votes change how a selected citizen votes even if their name is never published, and does the rule end up making lockdowns more legitamate or just more annoying?
