I've been chewing on this for a while and can't tell whether it's a real distinction or just a social habbit. In the circles I move in—grad students, scientists, ex-religious rationalist types—nobody blinks if I say I value truth for its own sake. I can spend a weekend running down whether some minor historical claim is true, with no stake in the answer, and people call it intellectual integrity. But if I say the same about tradition—that I want to keep doing something simply because it was passed down, without needing a further payoff—people immediately ask what the tradition is for: does it build community, does it encode old wisdom, does it make people happy? "It's just how we've always done it" is treated as a non-answer. Purity is even more suspicious: almost nobody will say they value it for its own sake anymore, and even religious friends reframe it as health, discipline, or closeness to God, which are all further goods. So I have three things that all feel, from the inside, like final values—worth wanting for themselves—but only truth gets to stay final when we talk out loud. The other two have to earn their keep by producing something else.

Is there a defensible basis for that asymmetry? I'm not asking which of the three values is correct in some objective sense. I want to know whether the asymmetry itself is justified, or whether it should collapse: maybe truth-seeking is usually instrumental too and we're just flattering ourselves; maybe tradition and purity deserve the same intrinsic status; maybe the three aren't even comparable in the right way. And I'd want you to be explicit about what would change your verdict: which argument or consideration would flip you the other way, or where in your own case you're least confident.
