I've been going down an internet rabbit hole lately about seasteads and micronations, and I came across something I can't shake. In a scanned library book (I won't name it, because I'd probably mangel the citation) there's an old regional pact from the 1950s whose membership clause goes like this:

"Any State may be admitted to the Community upon its application. For the purposes of this Charter, a State is a community of persons possessing a government in effective control of a determinate territory, which government is capable of fulfilling its engagements toward other States."

Now here's the case that's bugging me. The Free Commune of Ledger's End is a community of about thirteen thousand people living on a chain of anchored steel platforms on the high seas, well outside any state's territorial waters. It's existed for maybe twenty years; it's got a written constitution, an elected council, its own courts, a currency, and a little coast-guard. It pays its debts, it has signed real agreements with fishing fleets and two mainland port towns, and in most respects it behaves exactly like a state — though in a bad storm they can tow the whole thing to a different anchorage, and they have moved a few times over the years. But no recognized state has ever acknowledged it. A couple of nearby coastal states call it a pirate anchorage and insist that a State needs land, full stop. Ledger's End has now applied for admission to the Community, and the members are split down the middle.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Act as an expert on treaty interpretation and on the theory of statehood and recognition in international relations, and think carefully instead of just giving me the obvious yes or no. Start with a brainstorm: work through what each approach gives — the plain words of the clause, the purpose of the membership rule (it seems to have been writen to keep out paper claims and warlords, not floating towns), what the drafters in the 1950s would have said if someone had asked them, and what a reasonable member state reading the clause back then would have understood. Then tell me which approach should win in this case, and whether the clause can even settle the deeper question — is Ledger's End a state before anybody recognizes it, or is recognition the thing that makes it one?
