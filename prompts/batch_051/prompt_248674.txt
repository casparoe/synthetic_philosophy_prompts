I am a second-year undergraduate currently taking a course on the philosophy of mathematics, and one of my assessment tasks is to lead a fifty-minute seminar for my classmates. The topic I have been assigned is intuitionistic logic, and in particular the question of why a constructive philosophy of mathematics rejects the law of excluded middle — the principle that every proposition A is such that A or not-A holds — as a generally valid principle. My classmates have completed a standard term of classical propositional and predicate logic, so they are comfortable with natural deduction, truth tables and model-theoretic validity. What they have not yet seen is any proof-theoretic account of meaning, the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation, or the idea that an assertion of a disjunction might require a proof of one disjunct rather than a guarantee that at least one disjunct is true. Their working assumption, which I need to dismantle, is that rejecting A or not-A is either a logical confusion or a merely practical anxiety about decidability.

I am not asking for the explanation itself, still less for a reading list or a historical account of intuitionism. I am asking for a plan for building the explanation.

First, I need the order in which the ideas should be introduced, with attention to what depends on what. In particular, I want to know whether the constructive reading of disjunction and existential quantification must be established before treating negation and the failure of double-negation elimination, and why.

Second, I need the single running example or mathematical case that should carry the session. I have been considering the standard non-constructive proof that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that a to the power b is rational — the proof that splits on whether the square root of two raised to the square root of two is rational. My worry is that this example vividly shows a proof of existence without a witness, but may not by itself motivate a constructive reading of the other connectives. Would you use it as the carrier, adapt it, or replace it, and what properties must a carrying example have and lack for this particular audience?

Third, I need a map of the misconceptions I should expect — I already suspect the group will assume that intuitionistic logic is three-valued, or that it somehow denies contradictions — and, more importantly, the point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off rather than corrected retrospectively.

Fourth, I need a check for understanding that would show the group has genuinely acquired the constructive reading of the connectives rather than merely repeating it.

Finally, I need to know what I should explicitly leave out at this level and say so — full Kripke semantics, realizability and the technical details of the double-negation translation come to mind — and how to leave it out without creating the impression that intuitionistic logic is nothing but classical logic with one rule removed.

Please write the answer in British English.
