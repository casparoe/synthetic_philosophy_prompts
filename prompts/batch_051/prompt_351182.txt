I have been unable to settle a tension, and I would like you to walk me through the costs of each way of resolving it rather than simply pick a side.

In my statistics class this year we worked through Bayes' theorem, and I have since been reading about the Bayesian claim that priors "wash out." The idea is that if two people begin with different prior probabilities but update by the same rule on the same body of evidence, then as the evidence accumulates their posterior probabilities should converge, because the effect of the likelihoods eventually dominates the effect of the priors. I find this picture very attractive. It supplies a clean, non-arbitrary story about rational disagreement: two honest inquirers who see the same data and process it the same way should end up in the same place, and if they do not, then at least one of them is mis-updating or is seeing a filtered stream of evidence. I know the formal results carry conditions — roughly, the two people must share a likelihood function, must assign at least some initial probability to the hypotheses the other side considers live possibilities, and must observe a common dataset — but those conditions sound on paper like exactly the conditions a rational inquirer is supposed to satisfy. So the theory has real systematic virtues, and I am reluctant to give it up.

Yet I have an intuition I cannot shake. In actual public disputes — the safety of a vaccine, the causes of climate change, whether a diet works, whether a school policy improves student learning — people are exposed to more studies, data, and arguments over time, and they often do not converge. They get more convinced of their own side and more dismissive of the other. Sometimes the same piece of evidence is read by each side as supporting its own position. Far from washing out, the priors seem to compound. I cannot bring myself to believe that every such person is simply failing to update correctly, or that their evidence streams are so different that the comparison tells us nothing. My intuition is not that the mathematics is false; it is that the cheerful picture the mathematics suggests about real disagreement must be wrong, or at least radically incomplete.

I can see three possible ways out. First, the intuition could be debunked: maybe every real case of polarization fails one of the theorem's conditions in an ordinary way — the two sides do not actually share a likelihood function, or each side assigns zero prior probability to what the other side regards as a live option, or they are not looking at a genuinely common dataset — so the convergence results never promised anything about those cases. Second, the theory could be amended or abandoned as a description of actual agents: perhaps the conditions are so stringent that the formal results have no practical reach, or perhaps convergence occurs only "in the limit," which is compatible with any amount of divergence over any finite amount of evidence. Third, the two could be compatible under the right reading: the theorems tell us what would happen under conditions that almost never hold among real people, while the intuition tracks how routinely and how badly those conditions fail. I do not know which resolution is correct, and I worry that my liking for the tidy view is making me rationalize.

Please present the strongest version of the case for the theory and the strongest version of the case for the intuition, and then give me a negotiated verdict. What would justify giving up the intuition rather than narrowing the theory? What would justify concluding that the convergence results are idle as a picture of real inquiry? Is there a reading on which both the formal results and the real-world stubbornness of disagreement survive, and if so, what work does the "washing out of priors" idea still do for us?
