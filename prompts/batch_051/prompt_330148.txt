I've been working through the construction of the reals for a seminar and I've hit something that feels like it can't all be right. We build the reals as equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals, and we prove they're uncountable by the usual diagonal argument. But I also keep reading that there are only countably many definable real numbers, since there are only countably many finite descriptions in any fixed language. I can't get these two pictures to line up.

My confusion is this. A Cauchy sequence of rationals is just a function from N to Q satisfying the epsilon condition, and the construction includes all of them, not just the ones anyone has written down. But the functions we can actually name or specify are countable — a finite definition, an algorithm, something like that. So isn't the uncountability of the reals just being smuggled in by allowing totally arbitrary functions from N to Q into the construction? If we built the reals only from computable or definable Cauchy sequences, we'd get a countable set. So it looks like the continuum isn't something we discovered; it's something the powerset axiom handed to us by fiat. Which step in that reasoning is the bad one? Or, if none of it's bad, why is the version with all possible functions the right object to call the continuum?
