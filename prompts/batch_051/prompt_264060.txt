I am drawn to a purely probabilistic account of confirmation. On that account, a piece of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H just in case P(H | E) > P(H); support is nothing over and above probabilistic relevance. One consequence I initially found elegant is that a fact I already know with certainty cannot be evidence for anything, because conditioning on a proposition with probability one leaves all other probabilities unchanged. The theory thereby avoids unexplained appeals to novelty, explanatory power, or consilience, and it gives a single, non-arbitrary test for when evidence supports a hypothesis.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake: when a new theory turns out to entail, without gerrymandering, an anomaly that has been sitting in the literature for years, that counts as a serious mark in the theory's favor, and it does so before the theory has any new predictions to its credit. My standard way of putting the case to myself is a phenomenon that was known in advance and not understood, followed by a theory built from quite different principles that then derives it. I am sure this is support and not merely pleasing bookkeeping.

The usual reconciliations strike me as inadequate, though I cannot tell whether that is a fair assessment or just stubbornness. Saying that what really confirms the theory is the fact that H entails E, and that this logical fact is itself new evidence, looks like moving the goalposts, because the confirming relation is still a relation between the theory and the old fact. Saying that I should rerun the history with a prior in which E was not already known looks like I am evaluating a different epistemic situation from my own. And biting the bullet, declaring that old evidence never confirms and that we were merely impressed by the explanation, seems to throw away a central feature of how theory choice actually works.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know, as precisely as you can manage: what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes and for us to accept that old evidence is never itself confirmatory; what it would take for the pure probabilistic-relevance account to be amended or abandoned; and whether the compatibility readings are genuine solutions or just relabelings of the old evidence. If the only stable position is to amend the theory, what is the smallest amendment that still preserves its systematic virtues?
