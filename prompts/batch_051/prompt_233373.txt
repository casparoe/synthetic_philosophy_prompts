I spent my working life as an ecologist and, I'm embarrased to admit, I used to think of consciousness as either there or not: a vertebrate has it, maybe an octopus, certainly not a grasshopper. Since retiring I've been reading some philosophy of mind, and the idea I can't shake is that sentience might be a matter of degree. Not a switch, more like resolution or temperature. A bee may have a dim, compressed kind of experience; an ant less still; and by the time you get to a thermostat or a simple feedback controller, maybe the gradient has hit zero—or maybe it hasn't and it's only vanishingly close. If a person actually takes that degreed view seriously, what are the most important implications for the modest everyday stuff: swatting a fly, putting out sticky traps in the greenhouse, turning off a heat-seeking gadget? I'm not after a ranking of species or a citation. I want the structural upshots. Does degreed sentience force us into expected-welfare reasoning across lots of small uncertain minds, or does that over-moralize ordinary life? And does drawing any threshold for mattering become arbitrary on this picture, or is there a principled way to draw one? If I'm smuggling several different distinctions into the one word 'sentience', say so plainly; I'd rather have the question broken and rebuilt than answered politely.
