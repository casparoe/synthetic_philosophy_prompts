I need a build-to-order example for a handout on Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility. The thing I want to isolate is that the departure from ordinary expected utility isn't one-directional: same agent, same weighting function, can be more risk-seeking than EU on some bets and more risk-averse on others, and I want that shown by clean arithmetic rather than asserted.

Set the theory up this way. For a two-outcome gamble where the best outcome G has probability p and the worst outcome L has probability 1−p, risk-weighted EU is r(p)u(G) + (1−r(p))u(L). Take u linear in money over the stakes, so ordinary EU is just expected value. Use this risk function:

r(p) = 3p for 0 ≤ p ≤ 0.2
r(p) = 0.6 + (p − 0.2)/6 for 0.2 ≤ p < 0.8
r(p) = 0.7 + 1.5(p − 0.8) for 0.8 ≤ p ≤ 1

Now build one ordinary-life scenario with two seperate decisions in it, same person, same u, stakes under a few hundred dollars, probabilities kept at 0.1 or above. Decision A should be a long-shot gain where RWEU says take it and EU says dont; Decision B should be a high-probability gain where EU says take it and RWEU says dont. Then do the check: for each decision, state the option, its two outcomes, the relevant r(p), and compute both verdicts, so the verdicts fall out of the numbers rather than being stipulated.

Finish with: (i) exactly which feature of r is doing the work, (ii) what would happen to both decisions if r were the identity r(p)=p, and (iii) how a defender of plain expected utility would attack this weighting function — independence axiom, money pump, whatever seems strongest — and what a defender of risk-weighting would say back. The handout is about how risk-weighting can violate the independence axiom in an everyday-looking case, so cheating means picking utilities or r after the fact just to force the verdicts; the choices should generate them.
