I have been developing an argument for a seminar on the boundaries of sentience, and I half-suspect it contains a trick, though I cannot localize it. Since sentience fixes the boundaries of moral patiency, the stakes are high. Here is the argument:

(1) What makes a physical system conscious, and what gives it the particular experiences it has, is the causal-computational structure of its parts: the pattern by which inputs cause internal state transitions, while the chemical or biological material matters only as a vehicle.
(2) This structure is substrate-neutral. Any system that runs through exactly the same pattern of causal transitions thereby has exactly the same experiences, whatever it is made of.
(3) A pattern of causal transitions among finitely many states can be written down abstractly as a transition table; once it is written down, realizing that structure is just a matter of running through those states, in that order, under those conditions.
(4) Any moderately compact transition table can be realized as a proper sub-computation of almost any sufficiently large computation. A fine-grained weather simulation, a turbulent fluid model, or a large spreadsheet will, over a long enough run, pass through sequences of states that mirror the table.
(5) Therefore, if the brain's experience-inducing transition table is what matters, then the same experiences are occurring inside weather simulations and spreadsheets whenever their states wander through the matching sequence.
(6) So computationalism implies that consciousness is massively over-attributed: moments of experience pop up inside computations that no one designed to be mental, and there is no principled line between one sentient subject and the larger computation surrounding it, nor any guarantee that the system as a whole is the subject rather than some scattered sub-sequence of its states.

That conclusion seems absurd to me; I take it we do not want to grant little minds to spreadsheets on the strength of a state-sequence match. So I suspect at least one premise must be false, and I keep wanting to blame (2), but I cannot say exactly what the fault is.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under which reading of the key terms? If it is valid, which premise is the least costly to give up, and what does abandoning it do to the broader computationalist project in the philosophy of mind?
