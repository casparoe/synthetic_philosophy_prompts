Okay so I got pulled into an argument with a friend who's deep into Fomenko-style chronology (the stuff where Rome, Egypt, and most of the middle ages are supposed to be later fabrications, timelines doubled up, etc.). I couldn't immediately find the flaw, so I tried to formalize the underlying argument, and now I honestly can't tell whether the formal version is sound or whether there's a sneaky slide somewhere. Here it is:

(1) Historians can only work from texts, ruins, coins, carbon dates, and other traces that happen to have survived.
(2) Those surviving traces are compatible with a huge number of mutually incompatible histories; you can keep the traces fixed and still rewrite enormous chunks of the past.
(3) So standard history and fringe alternatives (Fomenko, Atlantis, ancient aliens, whatever) are alike in at least this respect: none of them is uniquely forced by the evidence.
(4) If a claim isn't uniquely forced by the evidence, then the choice between it and its rivals isn't an epistemic choice at all—it comes down to tradition, authority, aesthetics, institutions.
(5) Therefore the preference for mainsteam history over Fomenko or Atlantis is not an epistemic preference; it's just institutional bias.

I half-suspect step (4) is doing something dodgy, but I want someone to pin down what. So: is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step is the first one that fails, and under what reading of it? And if you think step (4) is fine, what's wrong with the conclusion?
