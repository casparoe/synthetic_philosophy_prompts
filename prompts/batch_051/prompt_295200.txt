I am a final-year undergraduate taking a module on phenomenology, in which we have been working through Division One of Heidegger's Being and Time. We have spent several weeks on the analysis of equipment. On my understanding of the text, a hammer in use is encountered as ready-to-hand: it is absorbed into a network of assignments and purposes and does not show up as a separate object. When the hammer breaks, goes missing, or becomes an obstacle, it surfaces as a present-at-hand thing with determinate properties; Heidegger distinguishes the ways this happens as conspicuousness, obtrusiveness, and obstinacy.

My seminar tutor and the study guide both then draw an analogy. They say social norms are like the hammer. While everyone is complying, the norm stays in the background, unnoticed and uninterrogated; when someone commits a faux pas, interrupts a ceremony, breaks a promise, or refuses a routine request, the norm suddenly stands out as an explicit object of attention. From this they conclude that Heidegger's real lesson is that a social practice is best understood through its breakdowns, and that smooth, unproblematic compliance is systematically uninformative.

I am writing an assessed essay on whether that conclusion is defensible, and I need to know how much weight this analogy can actually carry.

Please act as a professional philosopher working on Heidegger and on social ontology. Think carefully, and before giving a final judgement, brainstorm the main correspondences and mismatches between the two cases. Then give me an audit of the analogy that includes:

- an explicit mapping between features of tool-use and features of norm-following;
- the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion, separated from the disanalogies that do not;
- the features of the tool case that the analogy quietly imports into the social case without argument;
- whether the conclusion that breakdown is the privileged route to understanding a practice survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and
- if it does not survive, a better analogy or a more defensible version of the claim.

Please write your answer in British English.
