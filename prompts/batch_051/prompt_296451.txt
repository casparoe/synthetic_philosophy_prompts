I recently read an article that tried to explain the difference between negative, positive, and republican liberty, and I want to know how much of it I can trust before I repeat it. The article's account, as I remember it, went roughly like this. Isaiah Berlin distinguished negative liberty, the absence of interference from other people, from positive liberty, which the piece glossed as having a share in the political decisions that govern you. The modern republican tradition, associated especially with Philip Pettit, holds that political freedom is really neither of these but non-domination. On this view, a slave whose master never actually interferes with him is still unfree, because he lives at the mercy of the master's goodwill; the fact that someone else has unchecked power over him is itself the unfreedom, whether or not the power is exercised. Non-domination therefore demands more than being left alone: it demands institutions that prevent anyone from holding arbitrary power over you. The article concluded that, since domination consists in subjection to another's unchecked will, the republican remedy is democratic self-government — the more thoroughly democratic a society is, the freer its citizens are, because democratic accountability is what keeps power from being arbitrary. It ended by saying that the slave case shows Berlin's negative liberty is a stunted ideal that counts a lucky slave as free, and that non-domination should replace it as our central political ideal.

I find the slave example persuasive, but I am suspicious of the jump from non-domination to the claim that more democracy always makes people freer, and I cannot tell whether the gloss on positive liberty is right. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a careful negative-liberty theorist, a careful defender of positive liberty, and a neo-republican such as Pettit would each accept and reject in it. In particular, I want to know whether the republican view actually entails that a more democratic society is a freer one, whether the slave example works against negative liberty in the way the article claims, and how positive liberty should be characterized if the article's description is off. Where the article errs, say what the corrected point would be, and then assess whether the article's final verdict survives those corrections.
