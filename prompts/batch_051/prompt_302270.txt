I have been taking a course on the historical Jesus, and we recently spent several weeks on the criteria of authenticity — multiple attestation, embarrassment, discontinuity, coherence — and the recurring charge that the whole apparatus is circular. I have noticed that, even though my professor hedges about all of this, I have drifted into a private rule for reading scholars who disagree about what Jesus probably said or did. I treat a conclusion as much more credible when the scholar reaching it seems to be conceding something against their own confessional or cultural stake: a Christian scholar arguing that a beloved saying is likely a later addition, or a secular scholar arguing that some miracle story preserves an early tradition. Conversely, I heavily discount conclusions that sit comfortably with the author's prior religious or anti-religious commitments. Since I cannot check the philology myself, this rule functions as my substitute for evaluating the arguments.

A friend in the course says my policy is really just the criterion of embarrassment transferred from the first-century sources to modern scholars, and that it inherits that criterion's problems — what was awkward for the later church does not by itself preserve an earlier fact — while adding a new one: the policy systematically rewards dramatic self-undermining and punishes conclusions that align with a scholar's commitments for good evidential reasons. I find this partly persuasive, but I also cannot shake the sense that a conclusion running against an author's strongest incentives is genuine evidence about where the arguments point.

Is my rule defensible as a non-specialist's epistemic shortcut, or is it a bias dressed up as a method? What exactly would need to be true about motivated reasoning, selection into the field, and the direction of scholarly error for the policy to track truth rather than merely reward contrarian personalities? And is there a restated version — for example, using the interest-against-itself heuristic only as a tiebreaker, or only when the scholar has a public reputation at stake — that keeps whatever is good in the rule without letting the sociology of the field substitute for the arguments?
