After retiring from a career in experimental physiology, I have been reading the philosophical literature on death. The 1968 report by the Ad Hoc Committee of the Harvard Medical School, A Definition of Irreversible Coma, is often treated as the founding document of the brain death criterion. The report characterizes irreversible coma by four clinical findings: unresponsiveness, absence of spontaneous breathing, no elicitable reflexes, and an isoelectric electroencephalogram. I have tried to reconstruct the report's central reasoning as follows.

(1) An organism is a living whole only if its various parts are integrated into a single functioning system.
(2) In a human being, the brain is the organ that integrates the other parts into such a single functioning system, because it directs respiration, circulation, and wakeful interaction with the environment.
(3) A patient in irreversible coma has permanently lost the brain functions that produce this integration.
(4) Therefore, such a patient's parts are no longer organized into a single functioning system; at most, individual organs are kept operating separately by artificial means.
(5) Since death is the cessation of the organism as a whole, the patient in irreversible coma is dead, even if a ventilator maintains a heartbeat.

I have checked the steps several times, and the conclusion seems to follow if the premises hold. But the conclusion is weighty, and I am not sure the notion of an integrated whole is doing honest work. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails and why? If it is valid, which premise should receive the hardest scrutiny, and what would it cost to reject it?
