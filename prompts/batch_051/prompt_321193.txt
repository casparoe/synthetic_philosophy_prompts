I'm a novelist, and I've got a translators panel next month, and a friend told me I should sort out what I actually think before I get up there and contradict myself. So here's the position I'd defend, more or less:

(1) A lot of what my novels do lives in the grain of the language itself — rhythm, syntax stalling or rushing, puns and echoes that only work in my language. Some of that just can't be carried over, and I wish more people would admit it.
(2) Faithfulness isn't word-for-word; it's whether the translation does to a reader over there what the original did to a reader here. I guess you'd call it fidelity to effect.
(3) But I also dont want translators to smooth out the strangeness. When my prose is meant to feel difficult or slightly foreign to my own readers, the translation should make the new reader work that same way, not make it cozy.
(4) And since something is always lost, I think a translator's main virtue is humility: they should stay close to what I wrote and not treat my book as a prompt for their own writing.
(5) At the same time, I honestly believe the translated book is a different book. It isn't mine in the same way, and where it's better in another language, that's the translator's achievement, not mine.
(6) But I would still be furious if a translator rewrote a whole chapter because they thought it made a better book.

I'd like a consistency audit. Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest way to fix the whole set? Please rank the problems by severity rather than telling me everything's equally bad, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
