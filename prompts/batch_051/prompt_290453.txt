I keep running into the sentence "that's too improbable to be a coincidence" doing an awful lot of silent work. My uncle uses it about a vivid dream that roughly matched something that happened the next week, and then a skeptic friend just answers "birthday paradox" as if that settles the matter in the other direction. Neither of them will say exactly what claim they're defending. You're a philosopher who works on probability and evidence and who knows the epistemology of coincidence. Before you write anything, take the time to brainstorm a bunch of genuinely different precisifications of that sentence, including ones that are compatible with the birthday-paradox point and ones that aren't. Then lay them out for me as a menu: for each reading, say whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false or needing heavy qualification, and say which reading my uncle would actually need for his dream-anecdote to be evidence for something non-chance, and which reading the skeptic's "birthday paradox" reply does and doesn't undermine. I especially want the distinction between "this particular event was wildly unlikely under chance" and "some event of roughly this kind was unlikely across all the occassions someone could have noticed it," plus how selection effects from anecdotes should enter. Where the epistemology of these arguments is genuinely disputed among people who work on this, mark it as contested rather than settled; where it's just a theorem or a textbook point, you can say so plainly.
