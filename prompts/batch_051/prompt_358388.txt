I run a small community mediation practice in a city where a lot of our referrals are two groups with genuinely different value systems — a mosque and a music venue that share a wall, a tenants' association and a landlords' group, a dog-park coalition and a native-plant restoration society. The standard "find common ground" training has been worse than useless, because the conflict is precisely that each side thinks the other side's ask is an injustice, and asking them to split the difference feels to both of them like being told to sell something they shouldn't sell. We see two or three of these a week; the settlements aren't legally binding, but the city's licensing board tends to adopt our recommendation when deciding on permits and noise variances, and I'm the one who signs our recommendation before it goes to the city, so if we bless a rotten comprimise it does actual harm.

A friend who knows more economics than I do told me that moral compromise can be thought of as gains from trade: when two value systems place different weights on different things, there are sometimes swaps where each side gives up something it regards as important-but-not-central and gets back something it regards as much more central, so both sides are better off measured by their own standards — you don't need a neutral judge with a shared metric to call the swap an improvement. I want to know whether there is a real procedure hiding in that idea, because I can also see the failure mode: people get talked into trading away things their own value system treats as non-negotiable, or the "trade" only looks like a trade from the mediator's spreadsheet and not from inside either value system.

Please write me a one-page decision procedure my volunteer mediators could actually run on a proposed compromise. They have a weekend of training and no philosophy background, and they have to get through it in one afternoon. For each step, say what question to ask, what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question can't be settled by facts. Then run the procedure on these three proposals and show the work: (1) the mosque and the venue propose that the venue installs soundproofing and keeps silent during Friday prayer, in exchange for the mosque signing a letter supporting the venue's extended-hours variance; (2) the tenants' association proposes to accept a permanent rent-increase cap in exchange for waiving the right to a full hearing before eviction; (3) the dog-park and the restoration group propose to divide a single disputed meadow exactly in half, giving each side half of the only contested resource. I'd like at least one of these to come out as do-not-broker, even if both sides would sign it — and tell me which steps are just judgment calls with the noise removed.
