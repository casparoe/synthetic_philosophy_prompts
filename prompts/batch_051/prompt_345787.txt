I'm head of strategy at a mid-sized AI company. Once or twice a month somebody hands me a proposal to make an irreversible commitment: announce a launch date before the product is ready, publish our safety evals before the rival does, open-source a model as a moat-destroying move, or sign a customer contract with a penalty clause that would cost us a quarter of revenue if we back out. I've read the usual posts on precommitment, costly signals, and Moloch-style traps and I mostly buy them, but I keep noticing that a commitment does not happen in a vacuum: if it works, the other player often answers with a counter-commitment, and the two of you can end up in a race neither side can walk away from without loosing face. I want a decision procedure my team can actually run in the 48 hours before the board call.

Write it as a checklist or ordered set of questions, grounded in the actual logic of commitments — cheap talk versus costly and observable commitments; whether the move shapes the other player's expectations or just ties our hands; first-mover advantage versus starting a race we can't win — but usable by people who haven't read the theory. The procedure should ask questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where it stops with a no, and say where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run it on three of the proposals above: the launch date announcement, the open-sourcing move, and the penalty-clause contract. Make sure at least one of the worked applications ends in a no that I will hate, and say honestly what the procedure cannot do and where it will systematically misfire.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
