I used to run a spectroscopy lab before I retired, and lately I've been sitting in on a philosophy discussion group at the local university. Last week two members, Dana and Marek, got tangled up over a case Dana brought in. The case: a research consortium wants to run a long-wave communications array from a site near town for six months. Their own model predicts it would very slightly raise the risk of cardiac arrhythmias in the nearby residential strip — roughly one additional event spread over eight million exposed people during the run. The consortium has no plans to notify anyone, on the grounds that the risk is negligable. Dana said that's obviously wrong; Marek said it's obviously fine. As near as I can reconstruct it, the argument went:

1. Dana: You can't impose any risk on people without their consent, full stop. If one person in eight million has that event, that's a person whose heart became a cost of the project. "Negligible" is a conclusion, not a permission slip.

2. Marek: That standard would make ordinary life impossible. Every time you drive through a neighborhood you impose a tiny risk on pedestrians without asking. We don't demand consent for those risks because they're reciprocal and too small to chase down.

3. Dana: Driving is reciprocal; this isn't. The benefit flows to the consortium and to whoever uses the array. The residents get the risk and nothing else.

4. Marek: That actually concedes my point. What bothers you isn't the missing consent, it's the unfair distribution. If the consortium paid each resident, or funded better local arrhythmia screening, the complaint would collapse — no actual asking required.

5. Dana: Money doesn't fix it. You can't compensate someone whose event actually happens. And there's no amount that buys my consent to a lottery ticket on my own heart, because I never agreed to be handed the ticket at all. Compensation after the fact isn't consent.

6. Marek: But ex ante, each resident loses only about one eight-millionth of a life. Monetized, that's a dollar or two, not ten thousand. We don't normally say you've wronged someone every time your expected-value effect on them is under the cost of a sandwich.

7. Dana: So a drunk driver who makes it home safely did nothing wrong? The risk never materialized and the ex ante expected loss is small. No — the wrong is in exposing people to a risk they didn't accept.

8. Marek: The drunk driver imposes a risk that's orders of magnitude larger and illegal for good reason. You're lumping culpable risk in with the micro-risks a complex society runs on. Not the same category.

9. Dana: But now you've left consent behind and made it about magnitude and reasonableness. Fine — then tell me how small counts as small enough, and who gets to decide. Because "negligible to us" is exactly how the consortium justified not telling anybody.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Could you go through it move by move and say who's right about what, where each one overreaches, and who is more right overall? I'd especially like a clear statement on whether a non-arbitrary line between "small enough" and "too big" can be drawn at all, and whether the fact that in most cases the harm never materializes does any real work or is a red herring.
