Over the course of the school year, my biology teacher has made a series of off-the-cuff judgments about what should count as one organism, and I have kept a record of them because they seem to resist any simple rule. She has said that an ordinary mammal, such as a dog or a human being, is one organism. She has also said that a Volvox colony is one organism, even though it is only a hollow ball of a few thousand genetically identical cells, with the small cells beating their flagella to move the ball and the larger cells doing the reproducing. She has said that a Portuguese man o' war is one organism even though it is traditionally described as a colony: it grows from a single fertilized egg, and its float, stinging tentacles, and reproductive structures are specialized buds of that one egg. On the other side, she has said that a lichen is not one organism, even though the fungus and the alga in it are so integrated that the whole looks and behaves like a single living thing. She has said that a large aspen clone is not one organism, even though every trunk above ground is a genetic copy of the others and the trunks share one underground root system, with each trunk still an ordinary tree carrying its own leaves and flowers. She has said that a termite colony is not one organism, even though it is organized into a queen, sterile workers, and soldiers, and the workers and soldiers are siblings rather than genetic copies of one another. And she has said that a human being together with her gut bacteria is not one organism: the human alone is one organism, but the human-plus-microbes package is not.

The new case I want to predict is a coral head. A single larva settled on a rock and founded the colony, so every polyp in the head is a bud descended from that original founder. The polyps share one continuous sheet of living tissue and a connected gastrovascular system, so food captured by one polyp can reach its neighbors. But each polyp also has its own mouth, tentacles, and gonads, and each can capture and digest food for itself; none of the polyps are specialized as feeders, defenders, or reproducers, except that some are older and larger than others. If she is being consistent with what she has already ruled, should she call the coral head one organism or not? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning.

I think there are at least two distinct principles that would both fit all of her earlier judgments, and they seem to point in opposite directions on the coral head. Please state those principles, show how each one would handle her earlier rulings, and say what each predicts about the coral head. Then give your best guess about which principle she is actually tracking and why, and name the additional case or specimen I could ask her about that would most clearly separate the two.
