I have been trying to settle a disagreement with myself about whether societies "collapse," and I keep going back and forth. I am drawn to the revisionist position that has become influential in archaeology and ancient history: that the very idea of collapse is a loaded metaphor, borrowed from bodies and buildings, and that what it picks out is usually better described as transformation, reorganization, or dispersal. The attraction is not merely rhetorical. The revisionist view explains continuities that catastrophic accounts struggle with—people keep farming, making pots, speaking languages, and burying their dead across the supposed break—and it dissolves puzzles about how quickly "collapsed" regions were reoccupied or reorganized. It also has the virtue of treating people in the past as adaptive agents rather than as passive victims of a systems failure they could not understand.

And yet I also have a firm, pre-theoretical conviction that refuses to give way: in at least some cases, something really did come apart, and the word "transformation" feels like a euphemism. When I think of the end of the Mycenaean palaces—Linear B writing stopping, monumental building stopping, long-distance exchange drying up, populations falling sharply—or of the abandonment of the great Classic Maya centers in the southern lowlands, or of the familiar account of Rapa Nui in which deforestation and population decline mark something more final than a rearrangement, I find myself wanting to say that these were not merely reorganizations. Someone who described the burning of a house as "a change in heating arrangements" would be technically correct and substantively evasive.

What I want is not a verdict handed down, but a map of how the disagreement could honestly be resolved. Please present the strongest version of each side, then tell me what it would take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped, what it would take for the revisionist theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a defensible reading of "collapse" on which both survive—perhaps one that distinguishes political and demographic rupture from long-term social continuity. For each possible resolution, make explicit which evidence, arguments, or case studies would justify it, rather than simply choosing a side.
