People in my Spinoza reading group keep using "intellectual love of God" (amor Dei intellectualis) as if it were obvious, but we obviously mean different things by it — one person uses it for the twenty seconds of euphoria after finishing a proof, another for a kind of pantheist afterglow after a long hike. So last week we hammered out a rough list of criteria for whether some occurrent state of mind counts as the intellectual love of God in the sense of the later parts of the Ethics:

(i) it's a genuine affect of joy/pleasure, not just a bare assertion that "all is necessary";
(ii) the joy is accompanied by the idea of God — understood as the one infinite substance — as its cause, rather than by the idea of some local object, your own gratification, good luck, etc.;
(iii) it arises from adequate cognition, the kind where you grasp why the thing could not have been otherwise, as opposed to hearsay, imagination, or a passive emotion like hope or terror;
(iv) it leaves you with an increase in power of acting and a more stable, less reactive sort of gladness, not the kind that collapses when circumstances change.

Now the borderline case. One member of the group, M., reports a state she reached after some years of studying dynamical systems and also dealing with a genuinely awful breakup. She says that when she really sees that the breakup, her own bad choices in it, and her present grief all follow necessarily from the whole causal order, she feels a deep, quiet, almost funny joy — not relief, exactly; she says she feels more capable, not less, and that what she loves is "the fixedness of the whole thing." But she refuses to call it God, and she says she isn't sure she can think of it as a single substance rather than just... laws plus initial state plus time, taken together. Under the four criteria, does M.'s state qualify? Go criterion by criterion, say where our criteria underdetermine the answer, and say whether the hard criterion here is doing real work or is a leftover piece of theism we should probably drop or amend.
