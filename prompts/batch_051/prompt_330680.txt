Right, here's one I keep going round in circles on. I've been arguing with a colleague about how much it matters that the assumptions in our models are false. She's what you'd call an F-twist person: the view that a model should be judged on its predictions, and the realism of its assumptions is beside the point. I'm more sympathetic to the thought that a model with false assumptions genuinely explains something only when the false bits are, in some sense, harmless idealisations rather than plain errors — but I'm not sure that distinction can be made to stick.

Here's the proposition I want a number on:

A model whose central assumptions are known to be false can nevertheless provide genuine understanding of its target system, not merely accurate predictions — where 'genuine understanding' means something like correctly capturing the causal structure, or the reasons the system behaves as it does, rather than just getting the outputs right.

Give me your credence in that proposition, and decompose it. I'd want at least these terms separated: (a) how likely it is that understanding and predictive accuracy are different epistemic achievements in the first place; (b) how likely it is that at least some false assumptions do representational work that yields understanding rather than mere curve-fitting; (c) how likely it is that the harmless-idealisation/plain-error distinction can be made principled rather than post hoc. Add any other terms you think are doing real work, give each a number and a sentence of justification, and say how you combined them — including whether you treated them as independent, since that itself is a modelling assumption. Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it most.

I don't want a survey of positions — pick a number and stand behind it, even if its a bit arbitrary.
