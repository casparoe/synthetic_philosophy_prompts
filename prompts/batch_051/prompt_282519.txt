I am a philosophy professor preparing a research colloquium for a psychology department, and I would like to arrive with a worked-out plan for the question period. The talk defends a neo-Aristotelian account of wisdom: wisdom, I argue, is best understood as an intellectual virtue that combines two strands—Aristotle's sophia, systematic understanding of the most fundamental matters, and phronesis, deliberative good sense about human affairs—and it cannot be taught as a body of techniques or debiasing routines, though it can be cultivated through study, experience, and institutional design; accordingly, wisdom remains a legitimate educational aim rather than a folk category awaiting replacement by better-measured constructs. I open the talk with this passage:

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.7: "For it is absurd to think that Political Science or Prudence is the loftiest kind of knowledge, inasmuch as man is not the highest thing in the world. … This is why people say that men like Anaxagoras and Thales ‘may be wise but are not prudent,’ when they see them display ignorance of their own interests; and while admitting them to possess a knowledge that is rare, marvellous, difficult and even superhuman, they yet declare this knowledge to be useless, because these sages do not seek to know the things that are good for human beings."

The audience is a research psychology department dominated by people who study wisdom empirically: they treat wisdom as expert competence in navigating difficult and uncertain life problems, operationalize it through think-aloud responses to standardized vignettes scored for criteria such as factual and procedural knowledge about the pragmatics of life, recognition of uncertainty, and appreciation of multiple perspectives; and they care most about construct validity, longitudinal training studies, and whether interventions produce real change. A few members doubt that "wisdom" names a single trait at all, and one educational-policy researcher will press on what universities should do differently if wisdom cannot be directly taught. Draft my Q&A prep sheet as a numbered list of exactly eight items. Each item should be two sentences: first the objection phrased in this audience's voice, ordered from most damaging to most easily handled, and second my two-minute reply together with a note on where that reply is soft. At least three of the objections should engage the Aristotle passage directly—for example, whether it concedes that the contemplative strand of wisdom is useless for human good, and how I should interpret the passage to keep that concession from becoming fatal. Include, among the eight, the question I most hope nobody asks. Give no introduction or conclusion.
