i'm taking a course on game theory and rational choice this term, and after the lecture on backward induction two people in my study group got into an argument about the centipede game that i honestly couldnt settle. The version we were looking at has, say, six decision nodes: at each node the player whose turn it is can either take a larger share of the pot now or pass, and if they pass the pot grows and the other player faces the same choice. Backward induction says player 1 should just take on the very first move.

Anna said that conclusion is inescapable. At the last node, player 2 will take because the pot is bigger then and taking beats passing. So at the second-to-last node, player 1 should take too, because taking then gives them more than what they'd get if they passed and player 2 took on the final move. And that logic unwinds all the way back to move one. The fact that grabbing immediately feels wrong is exactly the kind of intuition the theory is supposed to correct. Ben replied that backward induction isn't only being used as a norm here; it also gets treated as a prediction of actual play, and as a prediction it's awful. People pass early in these games all the time, and often both players end up with more than the backward induction payoffs. You can't just say "well, they're irrational" unless you can explain why near everyone is irrational in the same direction. Anna said he was moving the goalposts: backward induction is about what rationality requires, not about what people in labs do. Ben came back with this: if rationality really requires player 1 to take on move one, then reaching any later node is itself evidence against the assumptions that generated that requirement. So when player 2 is actually choosing at node three or four, she shouldn't simply follow the backward induction prescription for that subgame; she should update on the fact that she's choosing at all, and the update can make passing rational. Anna responded that this just lets the conclusion rewrite the game. If passing can be rational at a later node because the previous move already violated the theory, then passing could be rational at the first node too, and backward induction stops giving any definite result at all. You don't get to say player 1 ought to take and then, after player 1 doesn't, say player 2 ought to pass because player 1 didn't.

They went back and forth like this for twenty minutes and neither gave ground. Please adjudicate it move by move: who's right about which specific point, where each argument overreaches, and what should actually be said about backward induction in the centipede game when we hold it to both a normative and a predictive standard?
