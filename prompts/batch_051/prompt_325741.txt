I keep flip-flopping on something and I want it settled with actual arguments rather than vibes. I'm attracted to a pretty austere view of what a mathematical proof is: a proof is just a finite derivation from axioms to a theorem, where each step follows by a fixed rule. On that view there's no deep difference in kind between a proof I can hold in my head and a proof that only a machine can check—if a program has verified every step and every case, then that's a proof, full stop, and we know the theorem, full stop. This view is nice because it makes proof-checking a mechanical matter, it treats the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture as unproblematically solved, and it avoids all this mystical talk about 'understanding' that nobody can define.

But I also can't shake the feeling that something is lost. When I look at the Appel–Haken proof of the four-color theorem—over a thousand reducible configurations checked by computer—or Hales' proof of the Kepler conjecture, my honest reaction is: we've checked something, but have we *proved* it in the sense that matters? It feels more like we built an oracle and asked it. If no human being has ever seen the whole proof, and no human being ever could survey it, then calling it a proof seems to stretch the word past what made it valuable. Maybe what I want from a proof isn't just correctness but something like *being able to see why*, and unsurveyable computer proofs give the first without the second.

I don't want someone to tell me which side obviously wins. I want the strongest case for each: why the formal-derivation view should make me drop the intuition as a mere limitation of human memory and attention, and why the intuition is tracking a real distinction the formal view can't see. Then tell me whether there's a reading of 'proof' or 'knowledge' or 'understanding' on which both survive—mechanical verification gives me knowledge-that, say, while surveyable proof gives me something further—and what it would actually cost to accept that split.
