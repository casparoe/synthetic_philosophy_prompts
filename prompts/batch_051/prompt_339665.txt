I'm putting together a module on contested moral and political concepts, and I want a working definition of "corruption." Not a dictionary gloss — the kind of definition that would let you sort a pile of cases the way a thoughtful person actually would, and that explains why the hard ones are hard.

Here's my pile, deliberately a mix of obvious and not:

1. A building inspector takes $300 cash to sign off on a block of flats even though the wiring is nowhere near code.
2. A state legislator awards a no-bid contract to a company owned by her brother, at roughly twice the going rate.
3. A nurse gets her neighbor's son an internship at the hospital where she works, even though he's less qualified that other applicants.
4. A pharmaceutical rep buys lunch for the whole staff of a clinic and leaves branded pens and a gift basket, with no explicit request for anything in return; prescribing patterns for the rep's drug stay about the same.
5. In a country where a passport application can sit for months, a citizen pays a clerk $50 to move their file to the top of the pile. The clerk doesn't change any eligibility judgment, just the processing order.
6. During a famine, a village chief distributes the village's emergency grain stores to families from his own lineage first, on the principle that kin come before strangers.
7. A mayoral candidate hands out cash to individual voters in the run-up to the election, saying "vote your conscience." It's textbook clientelism, but her party has done it for decades and defenders call it a dignified way of sharing campaign resources.
8. After a favorable permit is issued, the permitted company sends the regulator's staff on a lavish holiday as a "thank you" — no prior agreement, just gratitude after the fact.
9. A professor raises a student's final grade by half a letter because the student's final exam was genuinely much better than their midterms. Presumably not corruption — but why not, given that it's an exercise of power that benefits a particular person?

I suspect "corruption" is doing two jobs at once: one descriptive (something like an office or a position of entrusted power being abused for private benefit) and one condemnatory (the label itself carries the disapproval, so people argue about the label as much as the behavior). I want you to give me a single working definition — or, if you think the word really does two jobs, give me two definitions and say which cases move between them.

In order, please:

a) State your best working definition (or two).
b) Run it against all nine cases and say what it yields: clearly corruption, clearly not, or no determinate answer.
c) Flag any case where your definition returns an answer you wouldn't want to defend, and either revise the definition or defend the verdict.
d) Spend a paragraph on case 6 and the broader worry it points at: is any definition built around impartial rules and public office just a Western, bureaucratized way of seeing things, or can it get a grip on gift economies and kinship obligations without simply relabeling them?

Don't survey other people's definitions — I only want yours, and I want it to earn its keep against the cases. Also don't give me a legal compliance answer (what counts under this or that statute); I'm after the concept, not the code.
