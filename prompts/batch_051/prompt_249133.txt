I'm a phd student in philosophy of science, and a friend in science policy keeps pitching a funding scheme that's supposed to make grant review honest about the pessimistic meta-induction. I dont want a philosophical verdict on whether the scheme follows from PMI; I want someone to game it out.

Here's the mechanism. A large private funder adds a mandatory "predecessor post-mortem" section to every proposal. The applicant has to name one historically successful theory in their own subfield that was later abandoned—the aether, caloric, phlogiston, something like that—and state the property F that, with hindsight, marked it as the kind of theory that gets abandoned. F could be "posits an unobservable substance with exactly the features needed to explain one narrow phenomenon," but applicants can pick their own F if they defend it. Then the applicant either (a) argues that the central theoretical terms of their proposed work do not have F, or (b) if they suspect the terms do have F, describes an experiment inside the proposal that would reveal F.

A panel scores the post-mortem 1–5 for how un-self-serving it looks. High-scoring (b) proposals get "PMI priority" and are funded ahead of otherwise equal proposals. Weak or evasive post-mortems don't make a proposal unfundable, but the applicant gets a visible risk flag in the funder's system for three rounds. Reviewers are rated every five years on calibration: they get a bonus if the theoretical terms they scored as likely to have F actually get abandoned more often than the terms they scored as safe, and a penalty if they score everything as risky to game the bonus.

Walk me through what this selects for among applicants, reviewers, and subfields. Where does it get gamed first? What happens to exploratory work and to fields without a clean gallery of dead predecessor theories? Are any of the failure modes fixable without turning the post-mortem into a box-ticking exercise?
