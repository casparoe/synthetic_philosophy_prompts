I’m a retired chemist, and after forty years of treating safety questions mostly as questions about reversibility, I’ve been trying to read Kant carefully. In Perpetual Peace, in the sixth preliminary article, he makes a claim I keep turning over: that a state at war must not allow acts of hostility that would make all mutual confidence impossible in a future peace, and he gives as examples using assassins or poisoners, breaching a capitulation, and inciting treason in the opposing state. His idea seems to be that even during war you have to keep open the possibility of a later peace, and some methods of fighting foreclose that possibility because the other side could never trust you again afterward.

I want to turn that into something a practitioner can actually use. Suppose a foreign ministry has a small office that reviews proposals for covert operations, so-called dirty tricks, and certain intelligence activities during an ongoing conflict, and its job is to advise whether a proposal would cross the line into making a future peace impossible or purely cynical. The staff are not philosophers and are often under real time pressure; they need a written procedure, not a seminar. Can you give me a step-by-step procedure, maybe a checklist or decision tree, for deciding whether a proposed wartime action violates Kant’s sixth preliminary article? It should help them handle not just assassins and poisoners but modern analogues: decapitation strikes, deepfakes aimed at a state’s own population, tampering with command systems, deliberately negotiating in bad faith as cover, and so on. Include two or three worked examples, and be explicit about the places where the procedure cannot decide and a human judgment has to intervene. I’m especially interested in whether the procedure would ever tell the ministry that something it wants to do is not allowed, because if it never does that, its probably just a rationalization engine.
