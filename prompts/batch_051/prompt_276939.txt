My colleague in philosophy of biology keeps a running list of her verdicts about whether a system is one biological individual—that is, one organism. Her record so far: she counts a Portuguese man o' war, a floating colony of specialized, mutually dependent zooids all budded from a single fertilized egg, as one organism; she counts a dog as one organism; she does not count a lichen, whose fungal and algal partners belong to different species and can be separated and grown apart, as one organism; and she does not count a honey bee colony, an assembly of many complete animals each with its own body and its own genotype, as one organism. Her next case is an insect that carries bacteria inside its own cells, passed from mother to offspring through the eggs; the bacteria make essential amino acids the insect cannot make for itself, and neither partner can develop and reproduce normally without the other. Is she likely to count this insect-plus-bacteria system as one organism? Please lay out the candidate rules that fit her record, spell out what each rule predicts about the new case, give your best guess about which rule she is actually running and why, and name a further case whose verdict would most cleanly settle which of the candidate rules is driving her.
