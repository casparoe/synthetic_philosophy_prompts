I sit on the data release committee of a longitudinal study on childhood asthma in three counties. Our job is to vote on requests from member researchers who want to post their own small extracts of the study data in a public repository. Each request sounds harmless on its own, because the researchers always say their slice is too small or to general to identify anybody, and they usually need to share the data because a journal or funder is pressuring them. But I've been reading about what Bostrom calls the unilateralist's curse, and it describes our problem pretty well: each person can reasonably say that their own little release won't change anything, but if dozens of people each act on that reasoning, the accumulated public archive becomes a re-identifiable shadow of the whole cohort, and there is no mechanism coordinating all those individual yeses. I want to know whether our past decisions already commit us to a rule, and what that rule implies for next week's vote.

Our track record, roughly:
- We approved Ma's 2018 request to post age-band, sex, and a 12-item respiratory symptom score for the full cohort. No geography, no dates, no rare categories; it struck everyone as genuinely safe.
- We rejected Okafor's 2019 request to post census tract plus exact birth month for 60 kids from the rare-disease subgroup, because with two other extracts already public it would be enough to triangulate down to a handful of households. We told him we assess each file against what's already out there, not as if it landed in an empty world.
- We conditionally approved Singh's 2021 request for an occupation-by-industry-by-lung-function table for 200 participants, but only with a six-month embargo that would lift only if no more than four other extracts were public by then. He was annoyed that his careful design had to pay for other people's future choices.
- When the same table came back in 2023 with exact age and exact month of test added, we rejected it even though it looked anonymous on a quick skim, because the accumulating archive had changed the baseline.

Next week we vote on Fontaine's request: a 175-row table with age in years, sex, and a 9-item depression scale for everyone who attended the year-five follow-up. Considered on its own, and even against the current archive, it adds very little risk; the variables are common and the follow-up draws from all three counties. But we have eleven other requests waiting in the queue that are all roughly as innocuous as Fontaine's, and the study coordinator has calculated that if we approve them all, the public archive crosses the threshold we implicitly set in the Singh decision. The committee is already splitting.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on aggregation, risk, and collective decision-making. Think carefully and start by brainstorming the distinct rules that could fit our past decisions, including rules we might not have consciously adopted. Then tell me: which precedent is the closest analogue, what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and are our precedents enough to force a decision or do they underdetermine it? If they underdetermine it, say what principle we would be adopting under each live option, and name the further case we would need to decide in order to tell the rules apart. Keep it concrete enough to use in the meeting.
