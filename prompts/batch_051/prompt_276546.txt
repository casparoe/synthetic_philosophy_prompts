I'm a PhD student working on moral status, and a friend and I got into it last week about whether a bee is sentient; the argument stalled because neither of us could say what a 'a little bit sentient' theory would even have to do. Most of what I read still treats sentience like a switch: a being either has an inner life and counts, or it's just a thing. That seems less and less defensible once you line up the cases—a dog, a honeybee, a feedback-loop gadget like a thermostat—because there's a pretty natural sense in which the light doesn't turn on all at once. But the moment you go continuous, you inherent a bunch of headaches: tradeoffs across degrees, aggregation (does a sufficiently large pile of barely-sentient things outweigh one clearly sentient thing?), and the fear that we've just dressed up a thermostat as a tiny moral patient.

Before I read yet another paper on this, I want to know what I should be demanding of a graded theory of moral status. Write the desiderata: what an adequate theory has to deliver, which cases it has to get right, and which distinctions it has to respect, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Keep the distinction between degrees of sentience and degrees of moral status explicit—I don't want a theory that quietly conflates 'how much experience' with 'how much it matters.' Flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied. Then, briefly, score the main contenders—binary sentience views, a scalar or valenced-consciousness view, and a multi-dimensional cluster view—against your list.
