I am a PhD candidate in comparative politics, and next term I will present a paper at our department colloquium. The paper takes up the idea of performance legitimacy in the study of authoritarian rule in China and Singapore: the claim that sustained prosperity, social order, and rising living standards can stabilize a regime that lacks democratic authorization. I argue that this familiar account misdescribes the underlying mechanism. It treats performance as a stock of accumulated public acceptance that a regime can draw on; I argue that under contemporary information conditions performance functions instead as a continuously refreshed audit. Outcomes now arrive in real time, are benchmarked across jurisdictions, and are attributed through social media rather than through state-managed narratives. The operative standard of comparison therefore shifts from a country's own past to the best performer currently visible, and the regime is held responsible for outcomes it only partly controls, such as global demand, exchange rates, supply chains, and demographic change. On this logic, performance becomes a source of volatility rather than a reserve of stability, and credit-claiming is undermined precisely by the information openness a regime needs if its performance is to count as credible. I am not predicting that either regime collapses; I am making a claim about the changing mechanics of performance-based legitimation. I also argue that the two regimes respond differently: Singapore manages the baseline by framing external constraints in technocratic terms, while China increasingly tries to control the benchmark itself. My claim is about the empirical mechanics of public acceptance, not about whether outcomes normatively justify rule.

The audience is a mixed political science colloquium. It includes comparative politics faculty who work on authoritarian resilience and will suspect any inference from observed stability to legitimacy; normative political theorists who think legitimacy concerns the right to rule and cannot be bought by outcomes; a China specialist who will object that the party-state actively manages indicators rather than passively enduring them; a Singapore specialist who will object that the city-state has long succeeded precisely by controlling benchmarks and that my account underestimates competent state capacity; and two methodologically conservative colleagues who will question whether high-frequency social media sentiment can support claims about legitimacy at all.
