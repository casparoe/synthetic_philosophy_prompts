I'm a philosophy of mind professor teaching a mid-level course, and we've just spent two sessions on the Aunt Hillary dialogue in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach — the one where an ant colony carries on a conversation about whether a colony can have a soul or a self, and the ants get compared to neurons in a brain. My students have split into the usual two camps. One camp says the colony obviously has a mind because it has memory, communication, and goal-directed behavior; the other camp says it obviously doesn't, because there is nothing it is like to be a colony and individual ants are far too simple. I'd like to give them a third position that isn't just a middle-ground muddle.

Act as a philosopher of mind who works on collective cognition and functionalism, and think carefully about how to build the case. It may help to begin by distinguishing three grades of mindedness — mere information processing, representation, and phenomenal conciousness — so the argument doesn't collapse them together before it gets started.

Please make a sustained argument for the following thesis: an ant colony has no phenomenal consciousness, but it does have a mind in a stronger sense than merely processing information. Specifically, the colony instantiates colony-level representations of its own states — caste ratios, trail strengths, queen pheromone gradients, and so on — and these representations are causally efficacious in guiding colony behavior in a way that is not reducible to the beliefs or representations of the individual ants. In making the case, explain why this colony-level self-model is not a little homunculus, and say something about the uncomfortable implication that aggregate systems like markets or human institutions might also possess this thinner kind of mind. Don't just survey possible positions; construct the argument step by step.
