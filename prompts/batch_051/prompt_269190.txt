I have been trying to work out whether the long-standing dispute among historians about who was to blame for the First World War is a genuine moral dispute or rests on a confusion. Here is the argument I keep returning to:

(1) To be morally responsible for starting a war, an entity must be an agent: it must be capable of forming an intention to go to war, deliberating among alternatives, and choosing one course of action rather than another.
(2) A nation is not an agent in this sense. What we call a state's decision is the output of bureaucratic routines, alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, and the partly independent choices of ministers, generals, and officials; there is no single national will over and above these.
(3) Therefore, no nation can be morally responsible for starting the First World War in the way an individual person can be responsible for an action.
(4) If no nation can be morally responsible in this way, then a question such as 'Which nation was to blame for the war?' is not a well-formed moral question, but at most shorthand for a distributive question about which individuals and institutions share blame.
(5) Therefore, the historians' debate over whether Germany, Austria-Hungary, or some other power bore the chief blame for the First World War is, insofar as it is intended as a moral debate, a category mistake.

I am drawn to the conclusion, but I suspect the argument may be equivocating on 'agent' or sliding between causal and moral senses of 'responsible.' Is the argument valid? If it is not valid, say exactly which step fails and why. If it is valid but unsound, identify the premise a defender of the claim that nations can be blamed should reject, and give the strongest reformulation of that premise on which the charge of category mistake no longer follows.
