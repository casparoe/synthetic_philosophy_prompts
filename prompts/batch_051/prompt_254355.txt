I teach a small undergrad political-theory seminar, about a dozen students, one 75-minute session, and I want an activity that makes them feel Arrow's impossibility theorem before I ever name it. The thing I want them to come away seeing is that the same set of sincere ranked ballots can elect a different one of three options depending on which perfectly defensible counting rule you use, so the choice of rule—not just the voters—is doing real work.

Design a game where they vote on something with genuine small stakes (which of three readings gets cut from the syllabus, where the seminar gets coffee the following week, whatever) with at least three options, and I'll collect ranked ballots from everyone before announcing any counting rules. Then split them into three groups, give each group a different rule (plurality, a runoff or Borda-style count, round-robin pairwise majority), and have the groups come back and announce their winner—with the ballot set pre-aranged by me so the three answers clash, ideally a Condorcet cycle. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the standard Arrow conditions (unrestricted domain, Pareto, non-dictatorship, independence of irrelevant alternatives, plus transitivity of the social ranking) and gets them to say which condition their own group's rule violated.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the theorem the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. Give me your plan as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
