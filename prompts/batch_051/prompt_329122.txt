In a seminar on the search for extraterrestrial intelligence, I laid out a proposal to expand the radio searches and add a survey for possible technosignatures, and a colleague cut me off with the one-liner: "Where is everybody?" — the question usually attributed to Enrico Fermi and the namesake of the Fermi paradox. He said it as though the Great Silence, by itself, settled the matter and showed that expanding the search would be pointless. I suspect that sentence is doing several different things at once: it could be a bare observation about what we have detected so far, a substantive inference that technological civilizations are absent, rare, or short-lived, a claim about what we should expect intelligent life to do, or a methodological claim about where the burden of proof lies. But those readings pull in different directions, and I doubt they all support his dismissal of the proposal. Lay out for me, as a menu, the distinct precise claims "Where is everybody?" could be expressing in this context. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial description of the current evidence, a substantive and defensible inference, or a questionable or even false leap. Then tell me which reading or readings would actually license the conclusion that expanding the search is pointless, and which readings are compatible with thinking the search is worth expanding.
