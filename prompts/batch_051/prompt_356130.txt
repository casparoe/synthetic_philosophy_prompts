Right, here's one. I'm a PhD student in philosophical theology, and my reading group has spent most of this term going round in circles on the problem of Satan's fall—how a sinless, good, free angel could make the first evil choice. Two of us have agreed to stop talking past each other and write an adversarial-collaboration statement, and I'd love a draft.

The two camps: (i) a privation theorist in the rough Augustinian/Thomistic line, who says evil is not a positive thing but a privation or defect of due order; on this view the first sin doesn't need an antecedent evil cause, because what God creates is the act's being while the disorder is the creature's own, made possible by its being created from nothing. (ii) a free-will defender who thinks that is just a restatement of the problem: God gave the angel libertarian freedom, freedom entails the possibility of moral evil even in a good creation, and the first sin is an underdetermined self-determination—permitted rather than caused, and allowed for the sake of genuine freedom.

Could you draft their joint statement? Aim for 900–1100 words, formatted as follows:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Make them substantive, not just 'the angel existed'—for instance, that the angel was created good, that God is not the author of sin, that the fall was a free act, and whatever you can genuinely say about whether the possibility of falling was already there in a mutable creature.
2. A table of remaining disagreements, with each row worded so its own side would accept it. I supsect part of the fight is whether the privation story actually explains the first bad choice or merely labels it, and whether 'freedom' is doing explanatory work or is just the thing to be explained.
3. The cruxes. For each crux, give a precise case or thought-experiment both sides agree bears on it, and state each side's prediction in advance. Don't let the cruxes collapse into 'so is evil a privation?'—push down to things like: whether a will that can fail is already imperfect; whether an angel confirmed in grace from its first moment would have been unfree; whether the first evil choice needs a reason and whether any such reason must already be defective; and whether a first moment of hesitation is coherent without there already being a defect.
4. A short paragraph saying which parts of the disagreement, if any, you'd diagnose as merely verbal.

One constraint: the agreed claims need to be worded so the free-will defender isn't quietly conceding the privation story, and so the privation theorist isn't conceding that God could make sin inevitable. Keep it British English and a bit informal—it's for our reading group, not a journal.
