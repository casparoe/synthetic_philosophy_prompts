I am a professor of philosophy and I sit on the academic freedom and doctrine committee of a small theological college. The committee has been asked to adjudicate a complaint against one of our lecturers, a philosophical theologian, who has published an article arguing that the free-will defence — roughly, the view that a good creator may permit evil because evil arises from the free choices of created beings — cannot, whatever its merits for human sin, explain the fall of the angels. His argument runs as follows. An angel is created wholly good, without a body, without a history of temptation, and in the direct presence of God; there is therefore nothing in its nature or circumstances that could incline it towards evil. If its first evil choice has no prior inclination behind it, that choice is left unexplained; and if the only remaining explanation is that the defect was already present in the creature as created, then the creator, not the creature, becomes the source of the first sin. Our college's statement of faith affirms that God created all things good, including the angels, and that evil entered the creation through the free choice of creatures. The lecturer does not explicitly deny that statement, but he argues that the usual way of holding these two claims together is philosophically unstable, and he ends by saying that the tradition owes a better account than it has so far given.

A group of colleagues, together with one member of the governing council, has filed a formal complaint. They contend that the article undermines the confession to which every member of the teaching staff has assented, introduces doubt where the college has promised its students clarity, and ought not to have been published by a teacher bound by that confession. They ask the committee to require the lecturer to withdraw the article, or at least to publish a correction bringing it into line with the college's teaching. The lecturer's defenders reply that the article is precisely the kind of philosophical theology the college exists to support, that it identifies a genuine conceptual problem rather than asserting a heresy, and that disciplinary action would damage the college's intellectual honesty and its standing in the wider scholarly community.

The committee has agreed not to resolve the matter merely by citing the statement of faith, since that would be to assume the very point at issue. I need to circulate a written opinion before our meeting, and I would like you to draft it. It should recommend one concrete course of action, with a time frame and any conditions — for example, to dismiss the complaint, to uphold it and require a withdrawal or a clarification of specified scope, or to remit the matter to a working party with a defined brief. The opinion should give the reasons for the recommendation and engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the side it rejects, so that the losing side can recognise its own case in the document. The committee wants reasoning rather than a survey of sources, so the opinion should be self-contained and free of citations, quotations, and bibliography. Please write it in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write a memorandum or an opinion to be read aloud at a meeting.
