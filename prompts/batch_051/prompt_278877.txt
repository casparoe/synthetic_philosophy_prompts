I'm on the exploratory-science grants comittee of a small private foundation — we fund projects that wouldn't survive NIH or NSF review, but we're not a crank shop; every round we fight about where "interesting and risky" stops and "unfalsifiable" begins. We vote next month on a proposal from two physicists and an artist-programmer who want to do a systematic sweep of small one-dimensional cellular automata (binary cells, tiny neighborhoods), classify their long-run behavior, and look for rules that reproduce patterns we see in the natural world — leaf arrangements, zebra stripes, stop-and-go traffic waves, maybe branching in neurons. Their pitch leans hard on Wolfram: the universe is computational, simple rules generate complexity, and the principle of computational equivalence says that anything not obviously trivial is computationally equivalent to anything else — so a rule with a three-cell neighborhood is, in principle, as sophisticated as a brain, and sweeping through rule-space is exactly the kind of science A New Kind of Science called for. One reviewer wants to reject: he calls it numerical stamp-collecting with Wolframian branding and says there is nothing in the proposal that could ever falsify it. Another reviewer insists NKS was mostly ignored rather than refuted, and that demanding hypothesis-driven, falsifiable work is normal-science chauvinism; she thinks we'd look gutless to kill it on that ground. I have to circulate a short decision memo before we meet: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with criteria the rest of the committee can use next time something like this comes up — not just an "interesting debate, both sides" punt. Please draft that memo itself. And make sure you honestly engage the strongest argument on the side you turn down.
