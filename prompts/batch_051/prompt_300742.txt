I've been reading around in epistemic game theory and have more or less come to accept the standard line on Aumann's agreement theorem: two ideal Bayesians with a common prior can't agree to disagree—if their posterior probabilities for some proposition become common knowledge, they have to be equal. I get the proof, and I get why people treat it as a deep, almost no-go result for a certain kind of persistent disagreement: if you and I both see each other's credences and know that we both saw each other's, and we started from the same prior, any remaining gap is supposed to be impossible. But I realize I've never actually asked what would count against this. So play it straight for me: what would have to be true for the standard conclusion to be wrong, or true but much less interesting than people take it to be? Which assumptions could in principle fail—the common prior, common knowledge of the posteriors, common knowledge of rationality, partitional information, finite state space, whatever else matters—and for each, what would the world have to look like for that failure to matter in practice? Which of these escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like a sense of which ones a commited defender of the theorem's significance would themselves concede count against it (not just what its critics say). And please actually commit: give me your honest, definite verdict on how exposed the standard conclusion is, and defend that verdict rather than laying out the options and leaving me to pick.
