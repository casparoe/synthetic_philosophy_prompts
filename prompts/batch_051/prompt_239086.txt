Here is an argument I reconstructed from a policy debate. It concludes that the standard warning about money printing — that it cannot make a country richer, only change the numbers on the accounts — is itself an accounting mistake. I cannot tell whether the argument is a genuine insight or a disguised fallacy.

(1) Net wealth, for any sector of the economy, is assets minus liabilities.
(2) A newly created banknote or newly created reserve balance is an asset to the household that ends up holding it, and no private-sector agent acquires a matching liability at the moment the money is created.
(3) Suppose the central bank prints new money and transfers an equal amount to every household. Households' assets rise, and their liabilities do not.
(4) Therefore the private sector's net wealth rises by the face value of the transfer.
(5) If the private sector's net wealth rises and no other domestic sector's net wealth falls by the same amount, then national wealth rises.
(6) No other domestic sector's net wealth falls. The central bank has not given up anything it previously owned, and its new liability is costless to it, since a non-interest-bearing note never has to be redeemed in anything except itself.
(7) Therefore the transfer increases national wealth by exactly the amount printed. Printing money does create wealth.

Please assess the argument: is it valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading of liability and wealth? If the matter depends on how one consolidates the central bank with the government, say so explicitly, and say whether there is a defensible national-accounting convention on which the argument goes through. If you think the argument is valid, defend the conclusion against the standard objection.
