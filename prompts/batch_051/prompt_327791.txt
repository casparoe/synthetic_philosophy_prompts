The Marwick County Historical Society's acquisitions policy, adopted in 1927 and never amended, reads: "The Society shall collect and preserve only such documents and objects as relate to persons of established historical importance or to events of decided public consequence. Materials of merely private, local, or genealogical interest are outside the Society's mission and shall not be accessioned."

A historian offers to deposit the only known papers of a seventeenth-century village midwife who practised for roughly forty years: an account book and a set of case notes carrying the midwife's marginal comments on the births, debts, quarrels, and household arrangements of her neighbours. These are among the very few surviving records produced within the labouring population of the county. The historian argues that the midwife is of demonstrable historical importance precisely because her records make recoverable a part of the past that elite and state archives systematically failed to preserve, and that refusing the deposit would simply re-enact that old bias. The Society's own 1927 committee minutes record the drafters' worry that the Society would otherwise be buried in trivial papers.

You are the Society's director and must rule on the deposit. Interpret the policy (a) by its plain words, (b) by its evident purpose at the time of adoption, (c) by what the 1927 committee would most plausibly have said if asked about a midwife's account book, and (d) by what a reasonable present-day reader would take the words to permit. Then say which of these approaches should govern the decision and why, and address directly whether the phrase "established historical importance" can, on any coherent reading, include a person whose importance becomes visible only through the very records whose accession is in dispute.
