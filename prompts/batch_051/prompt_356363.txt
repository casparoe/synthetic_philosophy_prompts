Me and my friend got into a long argument last night about how Christians are supposed to tell which commands in scripture were meant only for the original audience and which still bind people now. His whole position was that there's a clean split between moral commands and cultural or ceremonial stuff: the moral ones carry over, the rest don't. I kept saying it can't be that clean once you go command by command, but I couldn't hand him a better principle, and it annoyed me. So I'm writing it out properly.

Here are my honest gut calls on the cases, in no particular order; I'd rather find the principle that fits them than defend every single one.

- The Levitical food rules (pork, shellfish, the whole list). Not binding on me, no hesitation.
- The bans on mixing fabrics and trimming the corners of a beard, from the same part of Leviticus. Not binding, and if someone tried to make them binding I'd think they'd lost the plot.
- The Sabbath command. I keep a loose Sunday rest but I don't think it's literally the same command, and I'm genuinely undecided about whether it binds the way "stealing is wrong" binds.
- Women praying or prophesying with their heads covered, from 1 Corinthians 11. My friend says this one has to be transcultural because Paul backs it with a creation-order argument. I think its local enough that we don't have to observe it. That odd line about the angels makes me less sure than I'd like, though.
- The instruction a bit later in the same letter that women should stay quiet in the assemblies, even though the letter already has them praying and prophesying. I'm okay treating the quiet instruction as aimed at something local in Corinth, but I can't give you a crisp reason for treating that one as local without treating the head-covering one the same way, other than that one feels cultural and the other feels like it's doing real theology.
- The commands to greet one another with a kiss, in Paul and Peter. It's New Testament, it's repeated, and nobody I know treats it as binding. We turn it into a handshake and don't even notice we did.
- The Jerusalem council's letter in Acts 15, telling Gentile believers to avoid blood and strangled animals. In the text it's apostolic and aimed exactly at non-Jewish churches; today virtually nobody I know keeps it, and I eat my steak on the rare side without guilt. This one makes my friend visibly uncomfortable.
- The commands addressed to slaves to obey their masters, and the household-code stuff around them. Not binding as a direct instruction, and I'd go further: treating them as permanent rules has done real harm. But the underlying posture—that Christians should be a certain kind of presence inside unjust institutions—does feel binding, though I can't point to the words that carry the permanent part.
- The commands to care for widows, orphans, and the poor, and to love enemies. Binding, full stop, no cultural caveat.
- Charging interest. The Old Testament bans it between Israelites but permits it toward outsiders; the New Testament mostly doesn't take it up, as far as I can tell. I wouldn't think twice about interest on a savings account, and I also wouldn't dream of charging interest to a broke sibling. I can't tell whether that's a biblical distinction or just sentiment.

My best attempt at a principle, after arguing until 2am, was something like: a biblical command still binds when the human good it protects still exists and the form it prescribes isn't just packaging for that good in the ancient world. But that feels like it lets me bracket anything I'm uncomfortable with. The head-covering command comes with a "because" attached, and the blood rule came from the apostles to Gentile churches; if both of those can count as packaging, I worry my principle is just a machine for dressing up my intuitions as reasons.

So here's what I want. Is there a principle—or a small set of candidates, stated precisely—that captures most or all of these verdicts? It does not have to save all of them. Tell me which of my gut calls it forces me to drop and why, and show me which case pushes each part of the principle. Then tell me what your principle says about a case I keep refusing to decide: Jesus telling the rich young ruler to sell everything and give it to the poor. Is that a transcultural command, a call specific to him, or something else, and does your principle say so without special pleading?

Finally, tell me how confident you actually are in the principle you land on, and point out the single move or judgment in your answer that you're least sure about.
