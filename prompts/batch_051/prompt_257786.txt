You are an expert on social epistemology and the economics of information, with particular knowledge of rational herding and information cascades. I have an argument from a graduate seminar whose conclusion seems far too strong, yet each step looked compelling at the time. Please think through it carefully. Before settling on a final answer, brainstorm at least three distinct places where the argument might break; then give your considered diagnosis. In your final answer, go through the argument step by step, check the arithmetic where it matters, identify the precise premise or inference to reject, explain why it looked acceptable, and say what rejecting it costs.

Consider a hiring committee that votes sequentially in a fixed order on a single candidate. Each member receives exactly one private signal, H or L, and the signal is 60 percent reliable in each direction: P(H | good) = P(L | bad) = 0.6. The prior probability that the candidate is good is 0.45. Each member observes all earlier votes but not the earlier members' private signals. Each member votes yes exactly when their posterior probability that the candidate is good, given all their evidence, is greater than 0.5.

Now the argument:

1. Member 1 votes yes exactly when they see H, because P(good | H) = 0.27 / (0.27 + 0.22) ≈ 0.551, and votes no exactly when they see L, because P(good | L) = 0.18 / (0.18 + 0.33) ≈ 0.353. So a yes vote from member 1 publicly reveals that member 1 saw H.

2. Suppose the first two votes are both yes. Member 2, having seen member 1's yes and therefore inferred H, would have voted no if member 2 had seen L, since P(good | H, L) = 0.108 / (0.108 + 0.132) = 0.45. Because member 2 voted yes, the two yes votes publicly reveal that the first two signals were H and H.

3. Member 3 observes two yes votes and, by step 2, can infer that the first two signals were H and H. If member 3 sees H, then P(good | H, H, H) ≈ 0.734; if member 3 sees L, then P(good | H, H, L) ≈ 0.551. Both exceed 0.5, so member 3 votes yes regardless of which signal they see.

4. Since member 3 would have voted yes under either signal, member 4 learns nothing about member 3's signal from the third yes vote. Member 4 therefore faces the same evidential situation as member 3: two informative yes votes plus one private signal. By the same arithmetic, member 4 votes yes regardless of which signal they see.

5. By induction, once the first two members have voted yes, every later member votes yes regardless of their private signal. Even if the next twenty members all privately observe L, all twenty vote yes, and the committee never corrects course.

6. The only assumptions used are these: voting proceeds one by one in a fixed order; later voters observe earlier votes but not earlier signals; votes are binary; private signals have bounded strength; and the prior and signal reliability are common knowledge. None of these assumptions is exotic. They seem to hold, at least approximately, in plenty of real sequential settings: juries, hiring pipelines, technology adoption, promotion queues.

7. Therefore, in any such real setting, once two early deciders happen to agree, the group should rationally lock in and discard all later contrary private information. But real juries and committees sometimes do change course, and later dissent sometimes flips an early majority; real groups are not this epistemically brittle. So the argument proves too much and at least one step must go wrong.

Where does the argument go wrong, if anywhere? If I had to keep something, I would most like to keep the arithmetic in steps 1–3 and the induction in steps 4–5, and to reject the claim in steps 6–7 that the assumptions are mild and that the conclusion transfers to real settings. But I worry that this reduces the model to a toy with no real application. What is the load-bearing assumption, why did it look innocent, and what exactly is lost, and what is preserved, by rejecting it?
