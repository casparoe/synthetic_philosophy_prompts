I'm drafting a paper on scientific and everyday reasoning, and I want to begin by getting the constraints on the table rather than defending one more account of inference to the best explanation. The working assumption is that such an account should do two jobs at once: it should be recognizable as a description of how people actually rank rival explanations when they say one is "better" than another, and it should be defensible as a norm — that is, it should explain why believing the best available explanation is, at least sometimes, a way of moving toward truth rather than merely toward a satisfying story. Write the desiderata such a theory must meet. For each requirement, give the concrete case, puzzle, or failure mode that forces it. The following are the sort of thing I have in mind, but the list should not be limited to them: curve-fitting that succeeds by tracking noise; ad hoc additions that keep a theory consistent with any possible observation; the availability of a rival hypothesis for any finite body of data; explanations that are highly satisfying while being antecedently very unlikely, as when a motive makes a crime intelligible without making the suspect much more likely to have committed it; and the fact that real inference often has to proceed without a pre-enumerated set of candidate hypotheses.

Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of a particular camp, and then say explicitly which pairs or subsets of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied as stated. That conflict part is the deliverable I care most about: I suspect the obvious list hides at least one deep tension, and surfacing it would be worth more than another taxonomy. Keep any scoring of named accounts brief; the criteria and their incompatibilities are the point.
