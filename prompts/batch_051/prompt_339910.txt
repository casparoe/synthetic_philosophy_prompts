I'm a PhD student in computer science, working on reinforcement learning—mostly on how to get agents to behave sensibly when full optimization is computationally hopeless. People in my lab keep using "satisficing" to mean any time an agent settles for less than the best, and I'm pretty sure that's not what it means, but I'm not confident enough to say what exactly they're missing. I know the term comes out of Herbert Simon's work on bounded rationality and I know the rough idea—set some threshold and take the first option that clears it rather than optimize all the way—but I've never seen anyone actually spell out the difference in the vocabulary I work with day to day.

So here's what I want. Take satisficing—aspiration levels, search, stopping rules, the bounded-rationality claim that "good enough" can be more rational than "optimal" for agents with limited time and compute—and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of RL: policies, value functions, reward, Bellman updates, compute budgets, planning horizons, exploration/exploitation, regret. I don't just want a glossary mapping; actually run the translation and show me what a satisficer amounts to in RL terms. Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto something RL already has, which parts you had to fudge or stretch, and which parts just drop out. In particular I want to know whether an aspiration level is just a threshold on a value estimate, or whether it does something RL genuinely can't express, and what the "objective function" of a satisficer is even supposed to be if it doesn't have one in the usual sense. Finally, tell me your honest read on the residue: is RL too thin to capture something important about how bounded agents should choose, or is satisficing just less precise than people like to pretend?
