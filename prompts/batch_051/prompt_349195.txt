I'm helping organize a two-hour session for an undergraduate reading group of about sixteen students with mixed backgrounds in philosophy, anthropology, and religious studies, and the guiding question is how communities come to treat guilt as if it were a transferable substance, something that can be loaded onto an animal, a person, or an effigy and then driven out, destroyed, or killed. I'd like you to design a concrete, runnable group activity — a structured role-play or small-stakes simulation, not a lecture — that lets participants feel the pull of ritual substitution from the inside. The effect I'm after is that participants should find themselves genuinely relieved when the group "succeeds" in moving its accumulated fault onto a substitute, and only later, in discussion, notice two things: first, that no one can explain the mechanism by which the guilt actually left the original wrongdoer and entered the substitute, and second, that the relief did not depend on the substitute's being guilty of anything. Ideally the selection of the substitute should happen under real, if token-sized, pressure — a threshold, a deadline, a threatened cost to everyone — so that the group discovers for itself how easily the choice lands on whoever is marginal, quiet, newest, or least willing to object. There should be no framing lecture up front, and the participants should not encounter the technical vocabulary for what is happening until the debrief.

Give me the full setup: the group size and time allocations within our two-hour window; the rules and materials, such as cards, tokens, a public ledger, role assignments, or a "stain" or "debt" currency; the distinct phases of play; the facilitator's moves; and a debrief script. In the debrief, map what happened at each stage onto the underlying ideas — the difference between individual responsibility and communal pollution, the possibility that a ritual can relieve a group even if no one can make the literal transfer of guilt intelligible, the function of the substitute, and what it costs to resist the ritual once it is underway. Include a short "what can go wrong" section: participants may refuse the premise, treat it as comedy, game the system, or feel genuinely hurt if they get singled out, and the design should say how to modulate that. Finally, be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce — above all, the real stakes and the background belief system that give historical scapegoat practices their power — and how the facilitator should supply that missing piece without lecturing up front.
