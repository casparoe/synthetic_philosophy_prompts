I'm a PhD student working on the semantics–pragmatics boundary, and I've noticed I follow a policy I can't defend to my own satisfaction. Here it is: when someone implicates something rather than saying it outright, I refuse to update on the implicated content; I only store the literal content as learned. If a colleague lists five flaws in my draft and than says "I'm not saying it's bad," I'll update on the flaws but not on "my colleague thinks the draft is bad." If someone answers "do you plan to run?" with "I have no plans to run," I walk away holding only that they said they have no plans; I don't form the belief that they won't run. My rationale is that implicature is deniable by design—people can hand me a belief while dodging responsibility for it, and I've been burned by updating on a hint that they later disowned. But a Gricean would say, I think, that once I've recognized the speaker intended me to infer P from their saying Q, then P is part of what they meant, and refusing to update is willful miscomprehension, not epistemic caution. So which is it: a defensible rule about deniable evidence, or an enormous information tax I pay to avoid a rare betrayal? When does the rule serve me and when does it make me obtuse in ways other people rightly find obnoxious? Is there a version that works—maybe update on the implicature but file it under "asserted? no; deniable? yes" so it can't later be weaponized against me? And what would I look for from the inside to tell whether I'm being prudent or just protecting my views from uncomfortable implications? Assume ordinary cooperative conversation, not courtrooms, politics, or PR statements, where I suspect the rule is obviously fine.
