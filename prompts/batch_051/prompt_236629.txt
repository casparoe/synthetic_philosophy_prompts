In a reading group I attend, two members got into an argument after we discussed the color-phi effect. The effect is as follows. A red dot is flashed briefly at one position, and then, a short distance away and a short interval later, a green dot is flashed. With the right timing and spacing, observers do not see two separate dots; they see a single dot that appears to move smoothly from the first position to the second and to change from red to green partway along its path. The puzzle is that the green flash, which arrives only at the end of the apparent trajectory, is what supplies the color for the later portion of that trajectory. The system appears to use a later stimulus to fill in the content of an earlier part of the perceived event.

We had just distinguished two ways of describing what the system might be doing with this late-arriving information. On the Orwellian description, the subject first consciously sees a red dot moving the whole way; once the green flash arrives, the system retrospectively rewrites the memory or record of that experience, so the subject sincerely recalls and reports a color change that was not present in the original conscious experience. On the Stalinesque description, the unrevised red-only trajectory never becomes conscious in the first place; the construction of experience is held back until both flashes are available, so the subject's only conscious experience is already the red-to-green moving dot.

The argument that followed is numbered below.

(1) Aaron: The Orwellian description must be correct, because revision requires something to revise. Before the green flash arrives, the system must already represent a red dot traversing the full path; otherwise the later arrival of the green would have no earlier record to alter. That earlier representation just is what we mean by the subject's initial conscious experience.

(2) Mei: That is the step I reject. The earlier representation need not be conscious; the brain produces many representations below the surface of awareness. The point that something must be there to revise shows only that some early red-trajectory representation exists, not that it has already crossed the threshold into consciousness. The edit may occur before that threshold.

(3) Aaron: But then you owe an account of where the threshold is and why consciousness waits. On your view the system apparently delays awareness until the green flash can be incorporated — but how long is the delay, and what fixes its length? If the machinery can wait for one later stimulus so that the story comes out smoother, why not wait even longer for still more context? Your delay is ad hoc.

(4) Mei: It is not ad hoc. Any account of apparent motion, yours included, must keep the first flash in some buffer long enough for the second flash to arrive and be integrated into a single continuous trajectory. The delay is simply that integration window: the minimum required to turn two successive flashes into one coherent event. The only difference between us is whether the content in that buffer is called conscious or not.

(5) Aaron: But that difference is doing all the work. If the buffered red-trajectory content is available to the system in the way your own description requires, then the subject has, in every functional sense that matters, already had the experience of a red dot moving the whole way. Adding a later official stamp of consciousness merely relabels the same process. The green flash still revises content the subject already had, and that is Orwellian revision.

(6) Mei: The relabeling runs both ways. If conscious is a line drawn across a continuous stream of processing, then one cannot settle this by appealing to what the subject truly experienced, because the subject's reports and behavior are identical on both descriptions. That is the substantive conclusion: the Orwellian/Stalinesque question is not a question about the brain at all, but about where we choose to apply a word.

Please adjudicate the disagreement point by point. For each of the six numbered moves, rate it as clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, sound but incomplete, or clearly right, and give a sentence or two of justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the most important consideration neither participant has fully articulated — in particular, what a genuinely decisive test for one description over the other would require, and whether the notion of the time of an experience can carry the weight both sides place on it.
