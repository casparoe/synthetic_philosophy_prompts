I'm about a hundred pages into a novel with two point-of-view soldiers on opposite sides of a war where one side is, I'm fairly convinced, in the wrong — a war of aggression, basically. The book won't settle the ethics, but I need my own view coherent enough that the narration doesn't quietly contradict itself. The problem is my gut reactions to my own characters don't add up. Running through the cases as I've drafted them:

- A nineteen-year-old conscript on the invading side shoots back during a firefight to keep his squad alive. I don't blame him at all.
- The defending soldier who shoots him in that same firefight. Also fine.
- A supply clerk in the invading army never touches a weapon but falsifies records she knows keep the invasion running. I condemn her more than the conscript, even though she kills no one.
- A defending soldier kills an enemy medic to stop him reporting the squad's position. Not fine, even though his side's cause is just.
- An invading officer has killed defending soldiers in earlier engagements, then refuses an order to shell a town and is executed for it. The refusal I find admirable. The earlier killing barely makes me wince. And that's the one I can't make sense off: the officer knows the war is unjust better than the conscript does, yet somehow the knowledge makes the clerk culpable but not him.

My half-baked rule is that there are two separate judgments — a combat judgment (can you shoot back, is this murder) and a war judgment (are you responsible for the war as a whole) — and I'm smuggling my characters across the boundary to get the verdicts I want. I want you to tell me what principle actually fits, or mostly fits, these calls, test it on a case or two I haven't thought of, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. And say how confident you are in that principle, and what part of it you're least sure of.
