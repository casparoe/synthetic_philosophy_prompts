I'm a postdoc in the foundations of quantum mechanics, with three years of funding and a background that lets me follow and teach the technical arguments but not originate new formal physics. I want to spend those years on the Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation, and I need help deciding which subproblem or line of attack is most worth prioritizing, and why.

To fix the question: assume the standard modern Everettian framing. The universal state evolves deterministically by the Schrödinger equation; decoherence picks out an approximately classical branch structure; all branches are equally real. Yet working quantum mechanics assigns probabilities equal to the squared moduli of the amplitudes. The problem is to explain, from within the theory, why an observer should expect to find themselves in a branch with probability given by the Born weights rather than, say, equal weight per decoherent branch, or no probabilities at all.

The live approaches I know of are roughly these. (1) Decision-theoretic derivations in the Deutsch–Wallace style, which treat branch weights as the betting odds of a rational agent and try to force squared-amplitude weighting from axioms of rational preference. (2) Self-locating-uncertainty and epistemic-separability arguments, on which the agent is genuinely unsure which branch they occupy and the Born weights follow from constraints on that uncertainty. (3) Envariance and symmetry arguments, which use entanglement-assisted invariance to derive equal probabilities for equal amplitudes and then generalize. (4) Deflationary positions that deny the problem is well posed, or that treat the Born rule as a separate postulate not in need of deeper derivation.

I can also see distinct subproblems within these: whether the decision-theoretic program can justify its axioms without quietly presupposing the Born rule; whether self-locating uncertainty is even coherent in a deterministic branching universe; whether envariance generalizes beyond the canonical cases; and whether deflationism merely relocates the difficulty into an unexplained notion of typicality or rational action.

Please rank the two or three lines of work you would prioritize for someone with my profile, and defend the ordering. I want a committed ranking, not a survey: if two options look equally promising, tell me which one you would still put first and on what tiebreak. For each entry, say (a) how far upstream it is—whether the other lines presuppose or depend on its resolution; (b) how tractable it is with current conceptual tools; (c) what result in the next few years would most change your ranking, up or down; and (d) which line or subproblem you would deprioritize on the ground that settling it would not change much downstream.
