In my climate ethics course we have been working through how much weight to give the welfare of people who will live centuries from now when we set climate policy. In the background is a technical distinction that matters enormously in practice: the difference between discounting future consumption because we expect the future to be richer, and discounting future well-being merely because it arrives later. The latter, the pure rate of time preference, is doing a great deal of work in the numbers, and one side of the debate in the readings tries to show that any positive pure rate of time preference is ethically indefensible. The argument they lean on is an analogy. They say that discounting a person's welfare merely because that person lives in the future is exactly like discounting a person's welfare merely because that person lives far away. Since almost nobody accepts geographic discounting, the thought is that no one should accept temporal discounting either; we do not think the same suffering matters less when it occurs on another continent, so we should not think it matters less when it occurs in another century. From this the conclusion is drawn that cost-benefit analyses of climate policy should use something close to a zero pure rate of time preference.

Please audit this analogy rather than the conclusion directly. First, make the analogy's structure explicit: construct the correspondence between the spatial and temporal cases, including what plays the role of the person, the distance, the evaluator's position, the discount factor, the relevant beneficiaries, and the intuition being appealed to. Then identify the points at which the two cases come apart. Among the things that strike me as potentially relevant, but which you should judge for yourself: future people do not exist yet, and whether, which, and how many of them exist depends on the very choices the discount rate is being used to evaluate; uncertainty about the remote future may differ in kind from uncertainty about distant strangers; and the causal pathways from present policy to temporally distant welfare versus spatially distant welfare are very different. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the intended conclusion from those that are incidental. Then assess what the analogy can actually carry: does it show only that the pure rate of time preference is an ethical parameter rather than a market fact, does it establish the stronger conclusion that it should be near zero, or does it unravel once the disanalogies are on the table? If a different analogy would do the intended work better, present that one and explain what it improves.

Throughout, I would like you to distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and economists who work on intergenerational justice, and not to present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
