Consider a version of Rawls's original position in which the veil of ignorance is thinned in exactly one respect. The parties still do not know their sex, race, class, natural abilities, conception of the good, or generation, and they still do not know which station in society they will occupy. Behind the veil, however, the following facts are made common knowledge. The society that follows from each candidate set of principles will contain a fixed number of positions, ranked by the level of income and wealth each position receives; to keep the comparison tractable, suppose the alternative basic structures differ only in their distributions of income and wealth, while basic liberties, fair equality of opportunity, and the social bases of self-respect are held equal across alternatives. For each candidate set of principles, the parties know the complete list of income-and-wealth levels that would go to the best-off position, the next-best-off position, and so on down to the worst-off position. They are also told that each of them will be assigned to exactly one of these positions by a lottery that treats every position as equally likely. Finally, they share a common utility function over their own income and wealth: it is increasing and strictly concave, so they are rational, risk-averse, and will choose so as to maximize expected utility.

This differs sharply from Rawls's own setting, in which the parties are deliberately denied the information that would let them assign probabilities to social positions; Rawls argues that, because they cannot assign probabilities, the parties will reason as maximiners and select the difference principle, which permits inequalities in income and wealth only insofar as they improve the level of the worst-off position relative to every available alternative.

Work through what the parties in this modified position would choose, and show how the choice depends on the degree of risk aversion and on the shape of the distribution they are told to expect. Say whether the difference principle still gets selected, and under what conditions: does it require a special, extreme form of risk aversion, or does ordinary diminishing marginal utility suffice? Then assess what this variation does to the moral force of the original-position argument. Does it show that the difference principle rests on the device of suppressing probabilities and on a controversial maximin rule, or does it show instead that the expected-utility framing changes the subject by smuggling back in the sort of morally arbitrary information the veil was meant to exclude? Present the strongest version of each side of that last question before giving your own assessment.
