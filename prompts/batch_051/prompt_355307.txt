In reading Langdon Winner’s “Do Artifacts Have Politics?” I have returned repeatedly to his treatment of the Long Island parkways. Winner reports that Robert Moses had the overpasses built unusually low, so that buses could not pass under them, and suggests that this was a deliberate way of keeping poorer and Black riders off the parkways while private automobiles moved freely. That case has become my touchstone for deciding whether an artifact has politics. My working rule is this: an artifact’s politics must be traceable to a specific design decision whose differential social effects were plausibly intended, or at least foreseeable, by an identifiable planner or designer. If I cannot identify such a decision and such a source, I classify the artifact as having social consequences but not politics in Winner’s sense. Last week, for example, I declined to call the layout of a campus building political even though the accessible entrance is far from the main door, because I could not identify a point in the design process where someone decided to make access worse for wheelchair users; I said it had an uneven consequence but not politics. I know this may be too stringent, and I worry that I am using intent as a shield against broader structural claims. How would I distinguish a defensible version of this rule from a bias toward requiring smoking-gun intent? Under what conditions does a designed system have politics even when no particular design decision was intended or foreseen to produce the differential outcome? Is there a restatement of my rule that keeps the epistemic discipline without treating intent as the only locus of politics?
