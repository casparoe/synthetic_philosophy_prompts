I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've ended up with a view about words and I'd like someone to knock it about a bit. For a long time I treated the so-called etymological fallacy the way I was taught to: the fact that decimate comes from a Latin word about taking a tenth, or that nice used to mean something closer to foolish, tells you nothing about what those words mean now, and people use words perfectly well without knowing their histories. But whilst redrafting a book whose central character is a bit obsessed with origins, I've drifted towards a position I didn't expect to hold. I don't mean the crude claim that a word's true meaning is its root. I mean something softer: words carry the residue of their histories, and appealing to that history isn't automatically a mistake — it can pick out real constraints on how a careful writer may use a word, and on what a word can do without the writer lying to herself. I've started defending this to freinds, and the standard objections (speakers don't know the histories, meanings change, etc.) don't really move me, which makes me suspicious that I've just made the view unfalsifiable.

So here's what I want. Treat my position as the thing on trial and tell me what would genuinely count against it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and draw the line between what a defender of my view would have to concede is a problem and what she could reasonably dismiss as beside the point. Where is my position most exposed — in borrowings, in semantic drift within a single generation, in dead metaphors, in technical coinages, in the fact that most usage is unreflective? And is it possible the whole thing is really a stylistic preference dressed up as a claim about meaning? If it turns out my view is well insulated, say so, and say whether that insulation is itself a reason to abandon it. Keep it under 550 words — I'll actually read it all.
