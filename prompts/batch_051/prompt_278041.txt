I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of risk and formal epistemology: twenty students, one seventy-five-minute session, no student computers, and only an instructor's projector. The next topic is the distinction between time averages and ensemble averages in repeated multiplicative gambles. The phenomenon I want the students to feel before I name it is this: a gamble can have a positive expected value computed across the group and yet a negative growth rate for the typical individual who takes it repeatedly, so that a class-level average rises while most students' balances fall.

Design an in-class game that stages exactly that conflict. Each student should manage an individual balance of points, starting equal. Each round, every student is exposed to the same multiplicative gamble involving a fair coin or shuffled cards; choose the two wealth multipliers so that their arithmetic mean is clearly above one, their geometric mean is clearly below one, and the divergence becomes visible within roughly forty minutes of play. The stakes must be motivating but not monetary: propose a point scheme with small token rewards, and be explicit that course grades must not be tied to performance. During play, keep two statistics visibly updated every round — the arithmetic mean balance of the room and the median balance — so that students watch their own balance sink while an aggregate statistic rises. Arrange the rules, pacing, and incentives so that students initially judge the gamble favorable and are visibly puzzled by the divergence before any explanation is given. If it strengthens the design, include a later phase in which students choose what fraction of their balance to put at risk each round, and debrief accordingly.

Then script a debrief for the remaining time that maps what happened to the formal ideas: the ensemble average as a sum over parallel trials, the time average as the growth rate of a single trajectory over repeated trials, expected log growth and the geometric mean, the arithmetic-geometric mean gap, and the claim that the two averages diverge because the process is non-ergodic. By the end, students should see that a policy can be good for an ensemble in expectation while being ruinous for most of the actual individuals who pursue it, and that expected-value reasoning by itself does not settle which repeated gamble a person should want. The debrief should also say, honestly, which feature of the idea no classroom game can reproduce and the facilitator must supply. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — sampling noise in a small class, students disengaging after a losing streak, the median behaving erratically in the early rounds, or anything else — and how to handle each failure without smoothing over the concept.
