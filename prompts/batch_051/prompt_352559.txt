I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting an essay about the vocabulary we should use when a generative AI system produces a false statement. In particular, I want to know whether lie, bullshit, or error is the right word for what a chatbot does, and I have found my own usage less settled than I would like. To steady it, I have written down my verdicts on a series of cases. I would like you to tell me whether a single principle captures these verdicts, or comes close, and whether the principle forces me to revise any of them.

Consider a witness who testifies that she saw the defendant at the scene at ten o'clock, when she knows she saw him across town. I call that a lie, and I would call it a lie even if she told it for a kind reason. Consider a television pundit who announces on air that a proposal will cost forty billion dollars a year, a figure he has never checked and does not care about one way or the other, because it makes his argument sound quantitative. I call that bullshit rather than lying. A press secretary who knows the unemployment figure he is about to quote is false, but repeats it because the chief of staff told him to, is lying in my view, even though the message is not his own. Yet a second press secretary who repeats the same false figure without checking it, because his job is to defend whatever line the party hands him and he is genuinely indifferent to whether the figure is accurate, strikes me as bullshitting rather than lying, even though the sentence he utters is identical to the first secretary's. So far I seem to be tracking something like the distinction Harry Frankfurt drew: a liar must be responsive to the truth in order to depart from it deliberately, while a bullshitter simply does not care about it.

Now the cases that actually motivated the piece. A lawyer asks a chatbot to summarize a recent appellate decision, and the chatbot returns a fluent paragraph with a plausible case citation that does not exist. I do not want to say that the system lied, because it had no belief to betray and no aim to mislead; but I also hesitate to call it bullshit in the same sense as the pundit, because the pundit is indifferent to truth in a way a trained model cannot be, and I am not sure there is a bullshitter anywhere in the picture. A paralegal who pastes that same summary into a memo without checking it, because the citation looks right and checking would slow her down, is harder for me: I lean toward saying that she is bullshitting, perhaps more culpably than the model. And there is a case I keep going back and forth on: a nurse gives a patient a drug interaction warning that she got from a health chatbot, the warning sounds right to her, but it is false. I do not think she lied, but I cannot decide whether she bullshitted the patient or merely made a negligent mistake, and I am not sure whether that distinction should matter to the patient.

I am tempted by a principle something like this: lying requires knowingly attempting to mislead, bullshit requires indifference to truth, and mere error requires having tried but failed to get it right. But the chatbot case appears to fit none of those, since the system cannot know, attempt, care, or try, and I do not want to call a fabricated legal citation a mere error as if it were a slip while aiming at the truth. So I am asking whether there is a better principle, stated precisely, that captures my verdicts; what it says about the nurse case and about similar cases in which a person passes along a machine's output without checking it; and which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate. In particular, I want to know whether bullshit can properly be said of an output when there is no bullshitter, and what my essay should say happened when the chatbot produced the false citation: that it lied, bullshitted, fabricated, or erred. I would like the entire answer in American English and in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; write it the way you would write a letter or an email.
