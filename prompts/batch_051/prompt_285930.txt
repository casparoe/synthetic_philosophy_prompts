I'm taking a course on markets and moral character, and we keep going back and forth on whether the market corrupts or civilizes us. The civilizing side is the old doux commerce idea, the line of argument that runs roughly from Montesquieu to Hirschman, at least as my professor laid it out: commerce was supposed to be a school for the passions. Repeated face-to-face exchange forces you to keep promises, show a bit of courtesy, and take the other party's perspective if you want them to trade with you again, so market participation should make people more reliable, less violent, better at getting along with strangers. The corrupting side says the opposite, that markets turn relationships into calculations and train us to treat everyone as a means.

Here's what's bugging me. Almost none of my actual economic life works like the face-to-face, reputation-dependent exchange that the civilizing story seems to lean on. I buy things with one click, from anonymus sellers I'll never interact with; trust is handled by the platform's refund policy and a star-rating sytem, not by anything like a relationship. People I know who do gig work are matched through apps and managed by scores. So my question is whether the doux commerce argument survives that shift. If the mechanism that was supposed to do the moral work is repeated, personal, reputation-dependent dealing, and that mechanism has largely been replaced by anonymous, algorithmically mediated exchange, does the argument still go through, does it need a repair, or is it basically dead? I'm not asking whether these platforms are good or bad; I want to know what the change does to the original argument: what part of it is really under strain, and whether there's a rebuilt version that says something weaker but still worth defending.
