I keep noticeing a weird asymmetry in how people (including me) respond to stories, and I'm trying to figure out whether it's defensible or just an inconsistency we've settled into. Suppose a novel says that on a certain planet water runs uphill as a matter of course, or that some people can turn into wolves, or that history went completely differently at some well-known point. Nobody objects; we accept those things as true-in-the-fiction and move on. But suppose an otherwise realistic story says that a character's calculated murder of a child was the uniquely right thing to do, or that cruelty is the highest virtue in that world, and the narrator presents this as a plain fact of the story rather than as the villain's opinion. Readers push back hard: they say the author overstepped, the story is badly written, or that the author can't just make that true in the fiction the way they can make water run uphill. Factual absurdities get a pass, but moral ones don't. That looks like an asymmetry in how much stipulative power we grant an author over descriptive facts versus moral or evaluative claims. I gather there's a name for this — imaginative resistance — but I'm less interested in the label than in whether the asymmetry can be justified, and if so, on what grounds.

I can think of a few candidate justifications, but none of them feels obviously right to me. One is that we literally can't imagine the moral inversion — that there's something about moral concepts that prevents us from coherently simulating a world where cruelty is good. Another is that we can imagine it but we won't, because going along would feel like endorsing it, so our resistance is a kind of self-defense rather than a limit on imagination. A third is that the author's authority only extends to what the world is like, and never to how we ought to feel about it; but then I don't know wether that distinction really holds up, because a story can also tell us that a character is hilarious or a meal is delicious, and we don't resist those nearly as hard. I'd like you to test these candidates and any others you know against nearby cases: a moral claim that's wrong but not evil, a claim about what's funny or disgusting, a logical impossibility, a false claim about the actual world smuggled into otherwise realistic fiction, and anything else you think separates the cases. Does the asymmetry survive those variations, and should we keep, refine, or reject it?
