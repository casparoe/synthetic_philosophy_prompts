I'm putting together a handout for a seminar on historical injustice and collective responsibility, and I want a decision tree that sorts a person into a defensible position on the question: can a nation, a people, or a generation be guilty — and if so, in what sense? The deliverable should be a branching instrument: a sequence of diagnostic questions, each answerable yes/no or by a short forced choice, leading from a starting commitment to a named leaf position.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First work out the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart; decide which combinations of answers are coherent; only then locate the actual positions in that space. Mark any combination that no one occupies, and say in one sentence whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any leaves the tree cannot separate even in principle, rather than pretending they are distinct.

Use, as your raw material, the fourfold of criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt associated with Karl Jaspers — but treat the categories as revisable material, not as a fixed schema to defend. As I understand the four, in my own words: criminal guilt attaches to a person for an act that violates positive law and is proven in court; political guilt is the liability citizens bear for the deeds of their state's regime simply in virtue of membership; moral guilt is what a person answers for before conscience — what they did, tolerated, or failed to do; metaphysical guilt is a felt shame at remaining alive and safe while others are wronged, even when one could not have prevented the wrong. Paraphrase freely; do not quote Jaspers, and do not lean on any particular edition or text.

The diagnostic questions should be anchored in concrete cases, and each should be phrased so that a respondent can answer it without already knowing which position the answer routes them to. Use at least these five cases somewhere in the tree:

1. A mid-level railway official in a genocidal regime whose job is only to schedule trains; he never touches a victim, but he keeps the network running.
2. A person born forty years after her country's atrocities, who has benefited materially from the post-atrocity economy and carries the country's passport.
3. A citizen who voted against an unjust war, joined protests against it, and still paid taxes partly used to fund it.
4. A bystander who survived a massacre in which her family was killed, and who could not have saved anyone, yet feels she did something wrong merely by surviving.
5. A successor state that refuses to pay compensation that has been awarded for a predecessor regime's wrongs.

The leaves must include at least: a strict individualist position on which only direct wrongdoers can be guilty and nothing normatively important carries across generations; a Jaspers-style position that keeps the four categories distinct and gives each a different bearer and source; a forward-looking beneficiary position on which later generations carry responsibility, but not guilt, in virtue of benefiting from or participating in structures that perpetuate the wrong; and a strong collective-identity position on which members of a nation or people inherit the group's guilt and shame as such. Include any additional leaves the logical space requires, and if a leaf has no standard name, say so and describe it rather than inventing a label.

Give each leaf a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Aim for roughly four to eight leaves and no more than four branch levels, so the whole thing stays tight enough for a two-page handout. No bibliography is wanted; the handout should stand on its own.

Finally, please separate what is settled about these positions from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested reading of Jaspers, or of any named thinker, as the consensus.
