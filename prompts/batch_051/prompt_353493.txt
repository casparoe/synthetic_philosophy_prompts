I moved to the US last year from a country where service is included and queueing is basically a religion, and now I'm writting up my views on everyday conventions for a social philosophy course. Could you act as a philosopher who works on social norms and moral obligation, and check whether my commitments hang together? I genuinely don't know where the problem is. Please brainstorm a little about how the pieces fit before judging, then give me an audit: which pairs really pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what the cheapest revision would be in each case. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad.

Here's the set I've settled on:

1. Conventions like queueing, tipping, and walking on one side of the sidewalk bind me only insofar as I've actually participated in them and others rely on my participation — an implicit-agreement view.

2. Because of that, tourists and recent transplants are mostly off the hook for local conventions they haven't taken part in; you can't be bound by an agreement you never made.

3. But I also think anyone traveling somewhere has a real duty to learn the money-related conventions in advance — tipping culture especially — because getting those wrong harms workers, and "I didn't know" isn't an excuse for avoidable harm when the information is easy to find.

4. Tipping 15–20% at a sit-down restaurant in the US is strictly obligatory, and not mainly for politeness reasons: servers are legally paid a subminimum wage on the assumption that tips make up the difference, so not tipping leaves them underpaid.

5. Jaywalking against a red light on an empty street at 2am is completely fine. Traffic rules exist to prevent collisions; when the risk is absent, treating the rule as sacred is rule-worship.

6. Queue-jumping is wrong even when the line is tiny and nobody seems bothered, because queues are fragile cooperative practices: they only keep working because people follow them without recalculating costs and benefits each time.

7. Not every convention deserves that kind of respect. If a convention is pointless or harmful — weird dress codes, standing when someone enters, whatever — people are free to flout it so long as they dont hurt anyone.

One more thing: I suspect I'm blurring the line between a convention's being morally required and its merely being expected, so if that ambiguity is doing any work in the commitments above, say where.
