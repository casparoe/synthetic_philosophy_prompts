My brother and I spent most of last year deciding whether to sell our mom's house, and the whole thing felt like a centipede game — every time we got an offer, whoever had the "turn" could accept it and end things, or pass it to the other with more money on the table but also more lawyers fees eating it. It got me reading about backward induction, and now I've collected my gut reactions to a bunch of cases. I want to know if there's one principle that makes sense of all of them, or which of my verdicts I have to give up.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Standard centipede game: a pot that doubles each time someone passes, six alternating moves, two players who both know the game. Backward induction says the first player should take on move one. I think that's wrong, or at least not what I'd do — I'd go a few moves in, and I think most reasonable people would too, even though I cant say exactly where I'd stop.

2. Same game, but I'm told the other player is a computer program that always plays backward induction and I know it. Here I think taking immediately is right. So my answer changes depending on who I'm playing, which makes me suspicious of my own first verdict.

3. Finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, 20 rounds, both players know it's the last round. Here I actually think defecting from round one is rational, and I dont have the same resistance I feel in the centipede game. I cant tell you why these feel different to me.

4. Chain store game: a monopolist faces 20 potential entrants one after another, and backward induction says never fight any entrant. I think the monopolist should fight the first few entrants, even against rational entrants, to scare off the rest. But I also know that's exactly the reasoning backward induction says unravels.

5. Traveler's dilemma, the way I learned it: two people each claim a number from 2 to 100; the lower claim gets that amount plus a $2 bonus, the higher gets the lower amount minus $2, and if they tie they both get that amount. The Nash equilibrium is both claim 2. I'd claim something in the 90s, and I dont think I'm being irrational.

6. Chess endgames. Here I have no problem with backward induction at all: if a forced line loses, it loses, and I'd never say "a reasonable player would play a losing move and hope." Backward induction is obviously right.

7. Surprise exam. A teacher announces there will be a quiz sometime next week and the students won't be able to predict the day. The students run the backward induction — can't be Friday, so can't be Thursday, so can't be Wednesday... — and conclude there can be no quiz. I think the students are wrong and the quiz can happen, say, on Wednesday, and still count as a surprise.

So: is there one principle about when backward induction is the right guide to action that gives all of these? Or do I have to throw some out? If you can find a principle, tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed — e.g., a centipede game where the player who moves last has signed a contract before the game that fines them more than the extra they'd get by taking on the last move, so everyone knows they'll pass. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't keep.
