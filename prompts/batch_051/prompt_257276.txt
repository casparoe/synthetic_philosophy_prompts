I've slid into a personal policy for my own thinking that I honestly can't tell is wisdom or motivated laziness. I split uncertainty into two buckets. When there's a proper reference class — repeated events with frequencies I can check, base rates that actually apply — I'm fully on board with putting numbers on things and doing expected value reasoning. But for one-offs and radically novel cases, like whether this specific startup survives, whether this particular country goes to war, whether a new battery chemistry gets to production on schedule, I refuse to attach any number. When someone else does attach a number to that kind of event, I treat it as a fact about their psychology, not about the world, and I stop taking the argument as seriously as they'd like. I also won't allow an expected value calculation where the probabilities are basically chosen by feel to override a robust qualitative consideration, because multiplying made-up probabilities by large payoffs makes the uncertainty look smaller than it is.

I think this is roughly the Keynes/Knight risk-versus-uncertainty idea, and it feels like its protecting me from false precision. But the standard bayesian line is that you always have some subjective credence and you need one to make coherent choices, and that refusing to quantify is just a way of avoiding trade-offs instead of facing them. So: under what conditions is my policy actually rational, and when is it a bias wearing epistemic humility as a costume? Is there a refined version that keeps the protection against fake precision while still letting me reason about one-off events at all? And how should I decide, from the inside, whether a given case is really in the no-numbers bucket or whether I'm just uneasy about the answer a number would force on me?
