I'm leading a ninety-minute seminar in a philosophy-of-science course for third-year biology and neuroscience majors. The assigned topic is reduction and emergence, and the specific idea I need to leave them with is this: establishing that a biological phenomenon is implemented by molecular mechanisms does not, by itself, show that the higher-level description is dispensable, because the reduction had to borrow the higher-level description to say what was being reduced and when it had succeeded. I don't want you to explain this to me; I want you to plan how I should teach it. Give me the order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the conclusion feels forced rather than asserted, and say what depends on what at each step. Pick the single example that should carry the whole session, and say what it must have and what it must not have; if neither the standard water–H2O example nor the temperature–mean-kinetic-energy example will work, say so and replace them. Tell me which misconceptions these students will bring or form—I expect at least "emergence just means we can't predict it yet," "reductionism says biology is a convenient fiction," and "if there's no extra force, talk of higher-level causes is just metaphor"—and identify exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. End with one question I can ask that would show whether they have the distinction rather than just the vocabulary, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level. Take definite positions: pick one sequence, one carrying example, and one final check, and defend each choice against the alternatives you rejected.
