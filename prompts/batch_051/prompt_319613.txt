I've been in a working group arguing about recommender systems, and one member has proposed the following scheme, which he calls an inversion of the usual design logic. A large video platform would stop collecting likes, star ratings, and preference surveys entirely. Instead, it would infer each user's preferences by inverse reinforcement learning: treat every observable action — a click, an early exit, a rewatch, a video saved to a list and never opened, a moment when the viewer mutes or doubles the speed — as the choice of an agent maximizing some unobserved reward function, and estimate that reward function for each individual. Once a month, the user receives a "preference statement": a set of weights the system has inferred over features such as novelty, density, duration, difficulty, humor, conflict, pacing, and emotional valence. The user can adjust any weight, and the adjustments are registered. But here is the proposed twist: the corrections do not override the behavioral estimate. They enter as additional, explicitly lower-weight evidence, because the designer holds as a matter of principle that observed choices are more honest than stated ones, since choices carry opportunity cost and statements do not. The feed is then generated to maximize each user's current inferred reward function, not their predicted click probability.

What I want to know is how this system would actually behave once deployed. Specifically: what viewing behavior would it incentivize once users understand that they are being modeled — for example, would people strategically watch demanding or aspirational content to move their inferred weights, and would the system's discounting of stated corrections make that strategy futile or effective? What happens to users whose revealed behavior and stated values systematically diverge, such as the person who sincerely values challenging material but reliably abandons it when given the chance? How would creators adapt their videos to a population being scored this way, and what would that do to the catalog, to the platform's revenue, and to the gap between what people watch and what they later say they valued? Where does the system break first, and could any of the failure modes be fixed without abandoning the designer's premise that behavior is the more truthful evidence?
