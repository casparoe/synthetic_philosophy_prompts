Hey, I've been tinkering with an essay idea on greed and acquisitiveness and I'd like someone to poke holes in the argument before I commit. The core claim is that the familiar defense of greed as an engine of prosperity works by bait-and-switch. When people defend greed, the examples they actually reach for — starting a business, saving, investing, competing for customers — all seem to fall under ordinary self-interest or ambition, which I'd call acquisitiveness. Greed proper, I want to say, has to involve something extra: an insatiable wanting, or wanting far more than one can use while others go without, or treating accumulation as the point rather than a means. If that's right, then the defense only succeeds because its quietly swapped a thin, pretty uncontroversial motive for the thick, condemnable one.

The twist I'm considering is that this split might not be an objection to the pro-greed argument at all. The defender can just accept it: fine, the engine runs on acquisitiveness, and the word greed was rhetorical garnish. But then the interesting question is whether the extra something that makes greed greed is a real moral difference or just a pejorative residue we staple onto motives we already dislike for other reasons.

What I'd like comments on: (1) does the greed/acquisitiveness distinction carry real moral weight, or am I just gerrymandering the concept to make the defense of greed look worse? (2) where does this argument most obviously fail? (3) can the extra something in greed be specified in a way that doesn't collapse back into ordinary wanting-more, or is the concept of greed basically just acquisitiveness plus disapproval? (4) is the essay to inside-baseball, or would it land for someone who isn't already deep in moral philosophy? Be blunt.
