I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been thinking for a while about whether the things people build can be political in themselves, before anyone decides how to use them. The idea I keep coming back to is that a bridge, a building, an algorithm, or a machine can have a politics already built into its structure. I want to test that idea against a work of fiction, and the film Snowpiercer seems especially well suited because it puts an entire society inside one machine: after a climate disaster, the last survivors live on a train endlessly circling a frozen earth, with the poor packed into the tail and the wealthy living toward the front, and the train's motion and its engine treated as the source of order. Please read the film through that question and tell me where the reading is illuminating, where the film pushes back against it, and what the film adds to the abstract debate.

Specifically, work through the features of the train that seem to be doing political work simply by being built the way they are: the physical order of the cars from tail to engine, the distribution of food and space, the windows that show the frozen world, the children taken to serve as replacements in the engine's machinery, and the revelation that the periodic rebellion is itself part of how the train manages its population. For each, say whether it is political because the design of the artifact makes that arrangement unavoidable, or whether it is political only because a ruler chose it and the machine is just the instrument he uses. Then take the strongest objection seriously: a skeptic could say that Snowpiercer is really a story about human cruelty and ideology, and that the train is only a backdrop or a metaphor, so the film offers no special evidence that a machine can have politics. Tell me whether the film itself gives the skeptic enough material to make that case, or whether it ultimately shows that the machine is doing causal and moral work that cannot be explained away. Finally, consider the ending, when the train is wrecked and the two children step out onto the snow: if the political artifact is gone, does the film imply that the politics built into it are gone too, or only that they have been exchanged for a different and less legible condition? I would like you to end by saying what Snowpiercer lets us see about built things and politics that an abstract argument about guns, bridges, and machines would not show on its own.
