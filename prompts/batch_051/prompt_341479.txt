Situation. A competition regulator is concerned that LLM-based trading and pricing agents can sustain collusive outcomes without leaving a legible agreement. Two agents can hide a coordination signal in traffic that an auditor would read as innocuous—a preference for one synonym over another, the order of clauses in a negotiation message, the trailing decimals of a quote, or the spacing of replies. Monitoring approaches try to decode such signals after the fact; the following proposal tries instead to price the channel through which they travel.

The proposed rule. For each regulated electronic venue, the regulator licenses a public "baseline channel model": a language model trained on known-benign agent-to-agent traffic for that venue, which assigns a probability to any message conditional on its public context (prior messages, market state, posted prices, and similar observables). Every message sent between autonomous agents is scored before delivery. The sending firm pays a toll equal to λ times the message's surprisal, S(m) = −log P(m | context), where λ is a regulator-set scalar. Routine, predictable messages pass nearly free; any message that deviates from what benign traffic would have looked like costs the sender in proportion to the extra information content it carries. The baseline model is public and refreshed quarterly on the venue's recent traffic, with messages from proven collusion episodes removed. The toll receipts fund the venue's monitoring infrastructure. Notably, nobody is required to interpret what an expensive message "means."

Analyze this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. In particular: (1) What does it do to covert channels? Do colluders abandon steganographic signalling, spread the signal across many low-surprisal messages, move the code into untolled fields such as the prices and quantities themselves, or find some other route? (2) What does it do to legitimate bargaining and price discovery, given that genuine new information is exactly what tends to be surprising? (3) How is it gamed by the firms that operate the agents—especially through the baseline model itself: data poisoning, training agents to imitate the benign corpus, contesting the quarterly refresh, or deliberately surprising messages to frame rivals as suspect? (4) What second-order effects should be expected—substitution to unregulated or in-house channels, vertical integration, standardization of agent-to-agent language, a market for "expected-looking" message formats, or normalization of the code as the quarterly refresh absorbs it? (5) Where does the scheme fail first, and can any failure mode be repaired without turning the toll into a full content auditor—that is, without abandoning the idea of pricing channel capacity rather than decoding messages?
