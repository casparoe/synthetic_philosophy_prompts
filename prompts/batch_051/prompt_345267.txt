Over the years I have soaked up a fair amount of Bayesian epistemology from blogs and forums, but I still lack a clean statement of when, if ever, I should defer to my own future credences. So I am going to do what I would do with a calibration dataset: write down my case-verdicts and ask what principle they add up to.

The candidate principle I start from is van Fraassen's Reflection principle, which I understand roughly as follows: my current credence in any proposition should equal my current expectation of my future credence in that proposition; if I am certain that later-me will assign 0.8 to P, then I should assign 0.8 to P now. For this exercise I am working with that expectation form, not the conditional form sometimes given.

Here are the cases.

1. The rested analyst. At the end of a long day I am 0.6 that a certain argument is sound, though I cannot locate any specific worry. I know that tomorrow morning I will be well rested and will spend an hour working through the argument line by line. From long experience I know that morning-me is the more reliable reader, and I am confident morning-me will land at 0.8. I think I should move to 0.8 now rather than treating my current 0.6 as authoritative.

2. The surgical amnesic. I am 0.95 that a mathematical result has been correctly proven, having just followed the proof step by step. I know the drug I will be given before surgery will erase my memory of that proof specifically, while leaving my general knowledge intact, so that afterward I will be back at 0.5 on the result. I do not think I should lower my current credence toward 0.5 in anticipation.

3. The acquiescent meeting. I am 0.7 that a proposed policy change would be harmful. I know that tomorrow in a meeting I will encounter strong, unanimous social pressure in favor of the change, and I expect my credence to rise to 0.9 through conformity rather than through any argument or new evidence. I do not think I should raise my credence now to 0.9. If anything, knowing the pressure is coming makes me want to treat my current judgment as worth protecting.

4. The imminent trial result. I am 0.6 that a tutoring program improves reading scores. Tomorrow a colleague will give me the result of a clean, well-run randomized trial. If it is positive I expect to move to 0.85; if negative, to 0.35; I think the two outcomes are roughly equally likely. My current expectation of my tomorrow credence is therefore about 0.6. I think the right response is to leave my credence where it is until I see the result, then update on it.

5. The post-publication commitment. I am 0.5 that a certain empirical claim about habit formation is true. I know that once I defend the claim in print, my credence will rise to 0.7 over the following months purely because people grow attached to positions they have publicly defended, and no new evidence will arrive. I do not think I should adopt 0.7 now, before publishing, just because I can anticipate the rise.

6. The cherry-picked dossier. I am 0.55 that a colleague of mine has acted badly in a dispute. I know that later today a mutual friend who is angry at that colleague will walk me through a long, all-true, but deliberately unrepresentative collection of stories about his worst moments. I expect that afterward my credence in the colleague's bad conduct will be 0.85, even though the information is not representative and the friend knows it is not representative. I am unsure whether I should now move toward 0.85, hold steady, or do something else entirely.

The tension is this: case 1 pushes me toward a general policy of cashing out predictable future shifts now, while cases 2, 3, and 5 push against precisely that, and case 4 suggests that sometimes the expectation of one's future credence already equals one's current credence, so nothing changes. Case 6 is where I get stuck.

Can you articulate a principle that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? It will presumably be some repaired version of Reflection, though it need not resemble van Fraassen's original beyond the basic idea of anticipating one's future credences. Please state the principle precisely, including any conditions under which I should or should not defer to an expected future credence, and say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate if it cannot get them all. Then apply it to case 6, and also to at least one new case of your own construction that is designed to discriminate between the candidate principles.
