As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I keep noticing a striking asymmetry in the way we treat damaging public statements. In U.S. defamation doctrine and in ordinary moral judgment, a false assertion of verifiable fact—that a scientist fabricated data, that a company already missed a debt payment—counts as a serious wrong and can be actionable. Yet a harsh evaluation, a prediction, or a rating is usually treated more gently. A publication can call the scientist's work sloppy or third-rate, predict that it will be retracted within five years, give a research group a ruinous ranking, or assign a credit rating that proves wildly wrong, and this will typically be defended as nonactionable opinion or forward-looking assessment, even when the reputational damage is worse. Is there a principled distinction underwriting this fact–opinion and fact–forecast divide, or is it a pragmatic compromise that shelters reviewers, analysts, and rating agencies while leaving their targets exposed in inconsistent ways? Pick the harder of the two divides and say whether you would keep, revise, or abandon it.
