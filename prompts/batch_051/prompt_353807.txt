Suppose a mid-sized city just got a council majority in a Burkean mood — they keep saying inherited rules carry the accumulated trial-and-error of everyone who came before, and they keep citing the old Chesterton-fence line about not tearing down a fence until you know why someone built it. So they write a repeal-by-suspension clause into the city charter. Any ordinance more than 40 years old can't be repealed in one vote. Instead it goes into a two-year suspension trial: the rule stays on the books but isn't enforced, any resident can write in a public log any problem they think the suspension caused, and at the end the council votes to make the repeal permanent or to resume enforcement. I'm not asking whether this is a good idea in principle. I want to know how the thing would actually behave. Who is best positioned to game the two-year window? Whos problems will fill the log, and whos will never show up in it? What kinds of ordinances does the mechanism systematically kill even when they were doing real work, and what kinds does it keep even when they were dead weight? Can any of that be fixed — by pre-registering what counts as a problem, by running staggered trials in different wards, by stretching the window for slow-moving harms — without turning the whole thing back into ordinary repeal? And what do those fixes do to the council's own incentives and to the quality of the log?
