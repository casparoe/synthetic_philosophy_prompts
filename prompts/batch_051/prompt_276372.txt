A friend and I argued until late last night about a New Caledonian crow that bent a piece of wire into a hook to retrieve food: he insisted the behaviour was just operant shaping plus a lucky generalization, while I insisted the bird's actual history made that reading strained. Throughout, he appealed to a principle from C. Lloyd Morgan's comparative psychology, and I have since looked up the passage. It reads as follows, in the form in which Morgan's canon is usually quoted:

"In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development."

I would be grateful for a strict reading of this passage, because my friend treated it as though it settled the argument, and I want to understand how much it really settles. Three points in particular trouble me. First, what exactly are "higher" and "lower" psychological processes here — is the "scale" meant as an evolutionary sequence, a hierarchy of complexity, a ladder of consciousness, or something else — and does the canon itself supply or justify that ranking, or does it simply assume it? Second, the word "fairly" appears to me to carry the whole weight of the sentence: does "if it can be fairly interpreted" collapse into "if any lower description can be made to fit at all," or does it set some standard of adequacy that the lower interpretation must meet, and who bears the burden of showing that the interpretation is fair? Third, I cannot tell whether the canon is a methodological counsel of restraint addressed to the scientist or a substantive claim about what is actually true of the animal; my friend moved between these two readings as the argument required. Could you help me distinguish what the passage, read carefully, actually commits someone to from what it merely advises, and say how someone defending a "higher" interpretation ought to reply within the canon's own terms rather than merely dismissing it?
