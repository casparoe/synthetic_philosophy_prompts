Ok so I've been stuck on the Epicurean argument that death can't be bad for you cause when death is there you aren't, and when you are death isn't. I think I found a way around it and I want someone to tell me if its actually new or just dumb. I call it the owed-days account. The idea is that you don't need to be around after death for death to harm you, because the harm lives in the gap between the days your own plans and projects had already claimed and the zero you actually get. Example: my cousin has her next ten years mapped out for med school and she talks like those years are already hers. If she died tomorrow I'd say the badness of her death is the size of that gap, not something she has to experience after. But my grandpa is 87 and says he's done everything he wanted, so on my account his death tomorrow would be barely bad or not bad at all. That seems to get the intuition right that a kid dying is a tragedy but an old person dying peaceful is just sad. My questions: did I just reinvent something that already exists, and is my version doing any actual work? Also, what are the strongest objections — where does it break? Like if someones plan is to count to a million by hand, does their death become a massive harm? And does Epicurus still have a move left, or did I actually kill his argument?
