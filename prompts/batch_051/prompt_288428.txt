I cover artificial intelligence and philosophy of mind for a general-interest science magazine, and I keep returning to a dispute I cannot get a clean empirical handle on. The philosopher John Searle's familiar line is that a symbol-manipulating system, however fluent its outputs, has only derived intentionality: its meanings are borrowed from its designers and interpreters, whereas a human speaker's intentionality is original. In reply, Daniel Dennett has argued that the distinction marks no real difference in kind — that it dissolves once you specify what causal history, internal organization, or evolutionary story is supposed to ground it. I want to treat this as an evidentiary challenge rather than a definitional standoff. Design a concrete test, measurement, or observation that would operationalize at least part of the claim that a system's intentionality is original rather than merely derived. Say what outcome would count in Searle's favor, what outcome would count in Dennett's, and then be explicit about the residue no such test can settle — in particular, whether even a favorable result would license saying the system really means something, or only that it has a particular kind of causal ancestry.
