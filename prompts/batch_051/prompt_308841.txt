I keep having this argument with my bandmates about when two pieces of music are "the same song," and I'm hoping someone can help me turn my gut calls into an actual rule. My working theory has always been that a song basically is its melody, the bit you'd hum. That explains why I'm happy to say a kazoo version of the birthday song at double speed and in a weird key is still the same song, and why I call a cover that keeps the melody but puts completely new chords under it a different arrangement but same song. It also explains the other half: a friend wrote a brand new tune over the exact chord chart of an old jazz standard, and I refuse to call it the same song even though the harmony matches bar for bar.

But some of my other calls don't fit that theory. I heard a rap track that used a four-bar loop of the actual vocal recording from a pop song as its main hook — no new instruments, just the looped snippet — and I have no problem calling that a new song, even though the part you'd hum is literally the same few seconds of the old one. And then there's the opposite case: a slowed-down "sad cover" of an upbeat hit, same words, same chords, but the melody gets bent around so much you can barely recognize it unless somebody tells you what it is, and I still want to call that the same song. Meanwhile, when a computer renders a song's MIDI file through different instrument sounds and no human plays anything, I'd say it's the same song but I'm not sure anything got performed. So what principle would actually deliver my verdicts? Is "a song is its melody" salvageable if I patch it, and if not, which of these calls should I drop? Also tell me what the resulting rule would say about a piece that has a groove and a production style but no real melody to speak of.
