I am a PhD student in the philosophy of mind working on creativity, and I have been defending a broadly Hofstadterian position: that creative production—a sonata, a proof, a poem, a scientific theory—is at bottom a matter of varying some already-existing structure along dimensions that structure already provides. On this view the difference between the routine and the brilliant is not that the brilliant work introduces fundamentally new content, but that it twiddles the inherited knobs farther, in more combinations, or on knobs no one had thought to touch. Even the most apparently discontinuous innovations, I maintain, can in principle be reconstructed as chains of small knob-twiddles that only look like leaps from the outside; the leap is an artifact of retrospect rather than a feature of the process itself.

What I want to know is what would count against this position and how much each kind of challenge would actually damage it. I mean this broadly: psychological findings about how experts and novices actually produce new work, historical or experimental cases, and conceptual arguments. I am less interested in the stock objections from critics who never shared the view than in the counterevidence a committed defender of the knob-twiddling account would have to acknowledge as counting against it. For example, a creative act that seems to add a new dimension to the space of possible variation, rather than merely repositioning a work along existing dimensions, would threaten the view's explanatory purchase; so would a work whose interest lies chiefly in the choice of theme rather than in its treatment; and so would a case so remote from its antecedents that a post hoc reconstruction of it as recombination begins to look unfalsifiable. For each challenge you identify, I want it ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know whether it forces a revision of the view or can be absorbed, and whether you think something of that kind has already occurred.

Finally, tell me honestly whether the position as I have stated it is insulated against refutation, and if it is, whether that insulation is a defect in a claim that purports to explain how human beings actually produce new work, or merely the predictable result of its having been converted into a conceptual point about where creativity should be located. I would also like your judgment about which single type of case or argument would move you most, and whether there is any familiar kind of creative achievement you would be prepared to treat as an exception to the model rather than an extreme instance of it.
