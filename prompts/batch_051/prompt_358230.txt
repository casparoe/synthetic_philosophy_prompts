I am a doctoral student in philosophy of religion, and I am convening an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who have argued about petitionary prayer for the better part of a year. One defends a broadly Thomist account: petitionary prayer remains coherent and purposeful even when God's omniscience, immutability, and ordering of all effects through secondary causes are granted in advance. The other defends a revisionary critique: once those doctrines are granted, petitionary prayer as ordinary believers practise and describe it is superfluous, misleading, or unintelligible, and the practice must be reframed as something other than asking God to bring about an outcome. They have agreed to take as their shared starting point a passage from Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Secunda Secundae, Question 83, Article 2, in the English Dominican Province translation:

'For we pray not that we may change the Divine disposition, but that we may impetrate that which God has disposed to be fulfilled by our prayers.'

I would like you to draft the joint statement this collaboration should produce, written as a document both parties could sign. It should contain the following.

(1) A numbered section of agreed claims. These should not be vacuous: they should cover what the standard objections from omniscience and immutability actually establish, what the passage above asserts and what it leaves open, what a minimally adequate account of petitionary prayer must explain, and what partial insights each side is willing to concede to the other.

(2) A disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give the neutral statement of the question; then the Thomist's formulation of their position, worded as the Thomist would accept for their own view; then the critic's formulation, worded as the critic would accept for theirs. The wording matters more than the conclusion: phrase each side's position precisely enough that the two sides can no longer talk past one another.

(3) A short diagnosis of which apparent disagreements are verbal and which are substantive, attending especially to the terms 'petition', 'change', 'answer to prayer', and 'secondary cause'. Treat the Aquinas passage as a test case for this diagnosis: each side should be able to state in their own words what claim about prayer Aquinas is making, and which reading of 'fulfilled by our prayers' they would reject.

(4) A list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, for example a scriptural petition, a prayer for an event already past whose outcome the petitioner does not know, a petition in which the petitioner expects no independent change in the world, or an intercessory prayer whose efficacy could in principle be tested. Record in advance each side's prediction and what result would count in the other side's favour.

(5) A closing statement of what would change each mind. Each side should write, in the first person, the argument or consideration that would most plausibly lead them to abandon or substantially revise their position, and the evidence they would recognise as counting against their own view.

(6) An unsigned methodological note from you as drafter, identifying where your own judgement is most likely to have failed in the document: in particular, which of your verbal-versus-substantive classifications or which choice of cruxes you would revise if either side pressed you, and what textual or philosophical consideration would lead you to reverse it. Indicate also where the drafted crux list most risks depending on a contestable reading of Aquinas's phrase 'fulfilled by our prayers'.

Do not cite secondary literature or attempt a bibliography, and do not invent references; work from the passage above and from the structure of the arguments themselves.
