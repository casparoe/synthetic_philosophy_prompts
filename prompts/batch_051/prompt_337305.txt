I am a doctoral student in philosophical theology, and a dispute that broke out in our reading group has reached an impasse none of us can settle. We were discussing the Muʿtazilī and Ashʿarī treatments of the relation between divine command and moral value — what is sometimes called the Islamic Euthyphro. As background, the Muʿtazilīs are usually taken to hold that actions have good-making and evil-making features that unaided reason can in principle discern, and that God commands and prohibits in conformity with those features. The Ashʿarīs are usually taken to hold that moral value is constituted by divine command and prohibition, so that apart from revelation there is no standard against which a divine act could be assessed. The focal question was whether the Ashʿarī can avoid the Euthyphro dilemma: if God commands an act because it is good, the standard of goodness is independent of God, while if the act is good merely because God commands it, the command itself looks arbitrary.

One participant, call him A, defended what he called the wisdom-identity reading. He argued that the dilemma is a false dichotomy for the Ashʿarī, because a third option is available: God's commands are not measured against any standard external to God, but neither are they arbitrary, since they issue necessarily from God's essential wisdom (ḥikma). Wisdom, on this reading, is not something God consults; it is identical with the divine nature. So the command is good because it is God's, and since God's being is wise, the objection that God could have commanded cruelty and made it good has no grip.

A second participant, call her B, replied that this relocates the dilemma rather than dissolving it. Either "wisdom" has content that can be specified independently of the commands, or it does not. If it does — if it rules some candidate commands in and others out — then the real ground of rightness is that content, and A has accepted the Muʿtazilī horn in disguise, with God's nature playing the role of the independent standard. If it does not, then "wisdom" does no argumentative work beyond decorating the will: calling a command wise because it comes from a wise being tells us nothing unless we can say what that wisdom excludes. The charge of arbitrariness therefore returns intact.

A answered that B's second horn depends on a picture of God as an agent who weighs reasons and then decides, and that this is precisely the picture the Ashʿarī rejects. For such a view there is no gap between God's nature and God's command in which the dilemma can gain a foothold. He said that asking whether God commands the good is like asking whether the standard metre is one metre long — the question is not so much false as inapplicable. On this picture it is the Muʿtazilī who creates the gap in the first place by treating divine reason as though it operated like ours.

B responded that this is where the position becomes too expensive. The whole point of calling God just, good, and merciful is to distinguish God from a maximally powerful tyrant. If "just" means no more than "whatever God commands," then the sentence "God commands justly" is a tautology, and the very terms the Ashʿarīs rely on in theodicy and in praise lose their content. The position may be internally consistent, she said, but it cannot then explain how the believer's trust in divine justice differs in kind from submission to arbitrary force. Either the Ashʿarī keeps determinate content for those terms, in which case that content is doing normative work and the first horn reappears, or he empties them of content, in which case he has stopped offering an account of justice at all.

A's final move was to say that B was importing a human rationalist standard into a tradition that expressly rejects it. For the Ashʿarī, he said, the alternative to divine-command ethics is not a better theory of justice but a misplaced confidence in the power of unaided reason to judge God. The believer's trust in God's self-disclosed character is a datum of revelation, not a conclusion of moral theory.

We could not agree on a scorecard. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of this exchange, not a general survey of the two schools. For each move above, say where that participant is right and where the move fails. In particular: does the wisdom-identity reading genuinely add a third option to the Euthyphro dilemma, does it merely relabel one of the two horns, or does the dilemma itself rest on an assumption that at least one side should have challenged? And is there anything both participants are missing — for instance, a way for the Ashʿarī to keep normative content for "just" without thereby reintroducing a standard independent of God?
