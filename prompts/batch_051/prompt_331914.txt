I have a standing policy in arguments about why some nations are rich and others poor, and I would like to know whether it is defensible. The arguments I keep having are always an auction among master explanations—institutions, geography, culture, luck—and each person supports their preferred cause by telling a coherent story about a few famous cases while ignoring cases that cut the other way. My policy is this: I do not allow myself to update in favor of any macro-historical causal claim unless its advocate can state, before the evidence is inspected, at least one observation that would count in favor of a named rival explanation and against theirs; I refuse to update from cross-country correlations that are not accompanied by a concrete mechanism at the level of individual choices; and I treat any explanation that cannot say which earlier fork would have changed the outcome as a story rather than a causal claim. I call this my "no counterfactual, no update" rule.

The rule feels like it protects me from just-so stories. There are only so many countries, history runs once, and the candidate causes are mutually entangled, so most claims seem to fail my test. But lately I have suspected the rule may be rigged to produce agnosticism no matter what the world is like, since almost no one can satisfy its demands in an ordinary conversation. Is my rule rationally defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as epistemic hygiene? If it is too strict, what is the most defensible version of it that keeps the protection without making me permanently unpersuadable? And can someone without country-level data in front of them reasonably hold any view at all about institutions versus geography versus culture as causes of national wealth—or is the honest stance just that the question is, for now, underdetermined?

I would like you to approach this as an epistemologist who thinks carefully about causal inference in the historical and social sciences. Start by brainstorming the strengths, failure modes, and possible restatements of the policy before committing to a verdict. Then give me your assessment in continuous prose only, the way you would write a long letter or email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
