I've been calling this thing 'promissory shrinkflation,' which is probably a terrible name, but here's the idea. When people break small promises, the break seems to happen twice, and only the second one matters morally. First you don't do the thing. Then—usually so fast you don't notice—you quite reclassify the promise as something softer: 'I didn't say I'd read it, I said I'd try,' 'we never set a firm time,' 'that was more of a loose plan than a promise.' The claim is that for small promises, the second move is where the actual wrong lives. Not showing up is just behavior; the retroactive shrinking of the commitment is what turns it into a pattern. It also explains why serial flakes don't feel guilty: by the time the promise is broken, their memory of what they promised has already been edited down to something they didn't break.

An example: my roommate asked a friend to read a draft before a deadline, and the friend said 'sure, I'll take a look this weekend.' The weekend came and went. When my roommate followed up, the friend said with what looked like total sincerity, 'well I said I'd take a look, not that I'd definitely get to it.' I don't think he was lying. I think he had genuinely rewritten the record of what was promised, retroactively, so that there was nothing left to feel bad about. Big promises can't be shrunk like this—if you skip a wedding you said you'd attend, the promise survives and you feel its weight. Small promises get shrunk precisely because the words leave room to reinterpret them.

So is this a real distinction or just motivated reasoning with a cute name? Has someone already worked it out under another heading (speech act theory, Scanlon on promises, the epistemology of memory, anywhere)? And where does it break—for instance, are there cases where the shrinkflation is actually the correct reading, because the small promise never was more than a stated intention, and the person is just catching up to that fact? If so, how do you tell that apart from the self-serving version? Does the concept make ordinary flexible social language look more sinister than it actually is?
