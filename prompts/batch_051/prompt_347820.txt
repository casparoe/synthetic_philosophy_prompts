I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together a piece on why smart people make decisions under risk that look irrational on paper. For background I read a popular book that summarizes the risk-weighted expected utility theory associated with Lara Buchak. Here's the summary as I understand it:

Expected utility theory says a rational person should pick the option with the highest average payoff, where each outcome is weighted by its probability. But lots of smart people — including some of the people who built the theory — make choices in cases like the Allais paradox that break its independence axiom. Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility is a descriptive account of how people actually choose under risk. It keeps the expected-utility machinery but adds a nonlinear probability weighting: instead of weighing outcomes by their raw probabilities, you weigh them by a distorted probability reflecting how much the agent fears the worst outcomes. This is basically the same probability-weighting move Kahneman and Tversky made in prospect theory, except Buchak argues it's rationally required rather than just a bias. The upshot is that once you weight probabilities by risk attitude, any amount of risk aversion turns out to be rational — there's no line between prudent caution and outright inconsistency. So expected utility isn't just too simple as pscyhology; it's broken as a theory of rationality.
