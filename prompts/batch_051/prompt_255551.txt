Last month I joined the board of a small tenants' union that has split into two factions: one wants our limited budget spent on direct eviction defense, the other on a citywide rent-control campaign, and each side treats the other's priority as a distraction and any concession as a betrayal of principle. The dispute has made me think carefully about the idea that parties with different value systems can sometimes both come out morally ahead by trading concessions — each giving ground on a value it weights less to secure a value it weights more, in the way trading partners gain from exchange.

I have been asked to lead a one-hour discussion for the rest of the board on this idea, and I want a teaching plan rather than a lecture. The members are not philosophers, but they are practiced at arguing about strategy and values. Lay out the explanatory arc: which concepts must be in place before "moral gains from trade" can even be stated, and in what order they should be introduced because of what depends on what. Supply the single worked example that should carry the session — it should feel close to our situation without being our actual dispute — and say what it must contain and what it must avoid. Identify the misconceptions the board is likely to bring or form (for example, that any concession is a betrayal, that compromise presupposes a shared scale of value, or that a gain means getting everything one wanted) and specify at which point in the sequence each should be headed off and how. Tell me what I should observe or elicit by the end to know that they have distinguished trading across values from merely capitulating on one, rather than having picked up the vocabulary. And state what should be deliberately left out at this level and how to flag that it is being set aside.
