I was in the grad lounge and two friends got into a long back and forth about a specific section of Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence — the subsection near the end of the objections where he takes up the argument from extra-sensory perception. The paragraph they were stuck on is the one where Turing says the reader probably knows the four ESP items (telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis), says these phenomena seem to upset our usual scientific picture, says the statistical evidence at least for telepathy is not easy to dismiss, and then imagines running the imitation game with a telepathic human against a digital computer, where the human guesses cards at well above chance and the machine is just guessing. He says if the interrogator is allowed to use ESP, the machine fails, so the test would need a telepathy-proof room. That's the passage.

Here's roughly how it went.

One friend said Turing is clearly treating ESP as real, or at least as well-enough established that it's a live empirical problem for the test. He pointed out Turing calls the evidence overwhelming and says the ESP objection is to his mind quite a strong one — and that he doesn't dispose of it the way he does the theological or consciousness objections. So Turing's own view, he argued, is that an interrogator could in fact distinguish a machine by ESP unless you physically block it.

The other said no: the whole section is written from inside the objector's position. Turing is giving the strongest version of a bad objection to the imitation game, and the point is to show what would follow if the objector were right about ESP. He admitted the passage sounds more sympathetic than the others, but said that's just because Turing's real target is the design of the test, not the existence of ESP. The telepathy-proof room is a condition for a fair test, not an admission that telepathy is real.

First one came back: if you're willing to redesign your test around the possibility of telepathy, you've already stopped treating it as a non-starter. Maybe that's not full belief in ghosts, but it's a lot more than "suppose for the sake of argument." Turing was known to be interested in parapsychology, and the paper could have cut the section entirely if he thought it was irrelevant.

Second one replied: the paper can't just cut it, because the imitation game is supposed to be a behavioural replacement for the question Can machines think? If ESP reports are part of the intellectual climate in 1950, a serious proposal has to say what its test does about them. You can design a control for a phenomenon you think is probably not real, because the experiment should not be hostage to a dispute you can't settle inside the paper itself.

They kept going in circles. Can you adjudicate move by move? Rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say what each is getting right and what each is ignoring, and then say what both of them are missing about what Turing's paragraph is actually doing in the structure of the paper.
