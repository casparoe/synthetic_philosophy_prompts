I'm a PhD student in philosophy of private law, and I'm writing a chapter on whether online click-through agreements involve genuine agreement or merely manufactured assent. I'd like you to act as a senior philosopher of contract and consent, work through the issue carefully, and avoid the canned "courts say X, philosophers say Y" answer.

The classic argument I'm wrestling with runs roughly like this. Under the objective theory of contract, what matters is not what a party privately understood but what a reasonable person would infer from her outward conduct. On that view, clicking "I agree" after being shown a link to the terms manifests assent: the user had notice, had an opportunity to read, and performed an act whose public meaning is acceptance. So even if nobody reads, the click counts as agreement, because the law allocates the risk of unread terms to the party who chose not to read.

I'm trying to figure out whether that argument survives a development it did not anticipate: the choice architecture around the click is now itself a product, engineered by the counterparty. Buttons are A/B-tested for color, placement, and wording; "Accept all" is a glossy one-click path while rejecting is a grayed-out link or a multi-step chore; pop-ups use confirm-shaming, with the decline option framed as something like "No thanks, I prefer paying more"; terms change after signup with notice buried in an email; and the interface is personalized in real time. The very party that benefits from assent designs the signal and tests it to maximize the click rate.

Here is what I would like from you. First, identify which premise of the objective-manifestation argument this development bears on, and say whether the argument can be repaired by, for example, cleaning up the interface, mandating comprehension checks, or treating the click as a formal act like signing a document while under informational or economic pressure. Second, give a considered verdict: does the argument still go through, does it need repair, or is it dead as an account of genuine agreement — even if it remains workable as a legal fiction about allocating the risk of unread terms? I'd like the verdict to be yours, argued for, rather than a survey.

As you answer, please distinguish carefully between (a) what is settled in contract doctrine — roughly, that clickwrap is enforceable when there was reasonable notice and an opportunity to review — and (b) what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and legal theorists about whether such a click amounts to morally significant consent or the exercise of autonomy. Don't present a contested view as the consensus. Before committing, briefly brainstorm two or three distinct ways one might repair or replace the argument, including at least one that concedes the click is not real agreement but argues the obligation comes from somewhere else.
