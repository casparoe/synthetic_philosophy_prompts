I've been going back and forth on a practice that economists and policy people do all the time, and I can't shake the feeling it's built on some unspoken philosophical commitments. Here's the practice: a central bank (or a finance ministry, or an IMF report) looks at the gap between the yield on an ordinary government bond and an inflation-linked one, calls it "break-even inflation," and reports it as what the market expects inflation to be. Same move with futures, interest-rate swaps, prediction markets, you name it: read a price off a screen and treat it as a forecast, often as the best forecast available.

But then there's this other result in economics, sometimes called the no-trade theorem, that seems to pull the opposite direction. The rough idea: if you're trying to sell me an asset and I'm trying to buy it, and we're both rational, your willingness to sell should be evidence to me that you know something bad. Under some assumptions—everyone has the same prior, everyone is rational, and all of this is common knowledge—you can argue that purely information-driven trade shouldn't happen at all. Any trade would refute itself, because my willingness to accept your price would already make your price revise. So the same broad framework that makes it plausible that markets aggregate information also seems to say we can't just read a price as the sum of everyone's private information.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit epistemology of the first practice. When someone treats a market price as a forecast, are they presupposing that traders have different priors? That a chunk of trading comes from liquidity needs or confused money, and the informed trades ride on top? That the market knows more than any individual but only if prices aren't actually efficient in the strict sense? Try to say which commitments the practice strictly needs, which ones it merely sits comfortably with, and where it's underdetermined—that is, where several different stories about why trades happen would all license reading forecasts off prices, and the price itself doesn't tell you which story your in. And if someone actually swallowed the no-trade line, what would have to change? Would central banks have to stop saying "market expectations," or could they keep the practice with alot more caveats?

Also, at the end, tell me how confident you are in the reconstruction and which single point you're least sure of.
