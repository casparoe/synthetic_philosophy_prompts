Secondary schools often state that a central aim is to form morally good persons and responsible citizens, and many require community service for graduation. But they also cap that requirement at a modest number of hours, and the same institutions organize their resources around academic breadth, athletics, music, and the arts. A student who devoted every spare hour to service, or to maximizing the good more generally, would likely be commended, but not held up as the model for everyone; an academic record consisting almost entirely of service would be treated as unbalanced rather than superlative. Reconstruct what this practice presupposes about the desirability of moral sainthood — understood as being as morally good as possible, with moral considerations dominating one's character and conduct. Specifically, does the practice strictly presuppose that a life organized entirely around moral concerns would be lacking, unhealthy, or otherwise undesirable, or is it equally consistent with the view that we should all aim at moral sainthood but that schools are constrained by facts about development, the limits of compulsion, or the value of an institution's being pluralistic? State where the practice is philosophically underdetermined between rival views of the place of moral goodness in a good human life, and say what would have to change in the design of such schools if one held that moral sainthood is not a rational ideal for human beings, versus if one held that it is the ideal but simply not teachable by these means.
