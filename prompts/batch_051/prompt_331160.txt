I'm drafting a chapter on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and its use as a rationalist argument for utilitarianism, and I keep encountering the same handful of objections. I want something that is not already sitting in the standard toolbox. Let me fix the target. The theorem says: if each person's preferences over lotteries obey the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and a social evaluator's preferences over lotteries also obey those axioms, and the evaluator is indifferent between any two lotteries between which every person is indifferent, then the evaluator's utility function is an affine transform of a weighted sum of the individual utility functions. Add the Pareto condition that the evaluator strictly prefers one lottery to another whenever at least one person strictly prefers it and no one strictly prefers the other, and the weights come out positive. The standard reading takes this to show that a consistent social evaluator who respects everyone's preferences must be, at minimum, a weighted utilitarian.

The objections I already have, and do not need restated, are these. First, the scaling objection: von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities are unique only up to positive affine transformations, and because the theorem permits rescaling each person's utility independently, the coefficients in the weighted sum have no ethically meaningful value until an interpersonal scaling is supplied, which the theorem itself does not supply. Second, the arbitrary-weights objection: even granting the theorem, it yields a weighted sum rather than the equally weighted sum utilitarianism needs; equal weights must be imported through a separate anonymity or impartiality premise, so the distinctively utilitarian content is assumed rather than derived. Third, the Diamond-style fairness objection: there is no independent reason the social evaluator must obey the expected-utility axioms, since the evaluator can be indifferent between a benefit going to each of two equally situated people for certain while strictly preferring a fair lottery that awards the benefit to one of them, because the fairness of the chances themselves matters; the theorem rules that concern out by fiat. Fourth, the ex ante/ex post objection: the setup asks the evaluator to rank lotteries from an ex ante standpoint, where only expected individual utility is visible, whereas an evaluator who cares about ex post distributions across persons, and about how risk actually falls on people, need not aggregate ex ante utilities additively. Fifth, the single-profile objection: the theorem fixes one profile of preferences and shows that the evaluator's utility can be written additively within that profile, but it does not identify a social welfare rule across profiles, so it underdetermines the claim that social evaluation must always take the additive utilitarian form.

I take those five to be the standard objections, and I want an objection to treating Harsanyi's theorem as a justification of weighted utilitarianism that is not on this list and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything on it. Give me the objection, then assess it honestly: does it genuinely undercut the theorem's justificatory force, does it only narrow its scope, or does it collapse back into one of the listed objections under pressure? Say how it differs from the listed ones.
