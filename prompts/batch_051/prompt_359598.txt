Ok here's a position I've ended up half-believing because I work with inverse reinforcement learning a lot: that if you give me enough observed behavior — ideally choices across a huge spread of contexts, including counterfactual choices — I can recover the agent's underlying preferences (or reward function), and the only ambiguity I have to accept is the usual IRL one where different reward functions rationalize the same optimal policy. I want to stress-test the underlying idea, not the formalism: the claim that behavior reveals what the agent cares about.

Assume I have best-case behavioral data but no self-reports, no neural data, no asking the agent why. Under that setup, what would count against the view? Give me the defeaters sorted by how serious they are: things that are fatal even in the limit, things that are serious but fixable with auxiliary assumptions (about beliefs, constraints, planning horizon), and things that are merely awkward. Then walk me through the hard cases a defender should have to handle — addiction and akrasia, choices under false beliefs, social masking or acting from fear, time-inconsistent choices, performative behavior, and pairs of very different values that generate identical choice behavior. For each, say whether a committed 'behavior reveals preferences' person should treat it as a genuine defeat or a measurement problem.

If the honest answer is that the view survives only by being qualified into unfalsifiability — any behavior gets rerationalized as revealing some hidden preference — tell me whether that's fatal for someone who wants to use this in engineering, or just the normal cost of an interpretive stance. And if I've set the question up badly, for instance by running together preferences, values, and reward functions and letting the confusion do the work, say so plainly instead of politely answering the question as posed.
