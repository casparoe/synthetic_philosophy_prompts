I'm a novelist—literary fiction, mostly—but I've taken a contract to write a branching narrative for an app: the kind where the reader finishes a passage, chooses between two actions, and the story forks. I'll be making this call dozens of times over the next year, and each choice is a place where the reader either feels the agency doing real work as part of the art, or catches me faking it. The problem is I don't know in advance wich choices are actually doing something as choices and which are just decoration, and my editor, who has to sign off, keeps saying some of my branches "play themselves" without being able to state the standard. I want a reusable procedure I can run on each choice before I write it, grounded in whatever the aesthetics of agency has actually sorted out, but translated for a novelist with no game-design training. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to go ahead, where the procedure says "cut this and write a straight passage instead," and where it says "this is a judgment call, stop and think." Then run it on three choices from my draft. (1) The reader chooses to forgive a brother or stay cold; either way the same events follow, and only the register and interior of the next chapters differ. (2) The reader chooses a boat or a road to the same city; each leads to a different ally with different information, so the plot genuinely diverges, but at the moment of choice the reader has no reason to prefer either. (3) The reader chooses whether the protagonist burns a letter or sends it after all; the immediate scene changes a lot and the choice reveals character, but the long-run state of the story reconverges and no other character ever learns what was chosen. Tell me what the procedure returns for each, including any case where it gives me an answer I won't like. And please seperate what people who study games and agency are fairly agreed on from what is genuinely contested; if the field is split on something, flag it rather than presenting one side as the consensus. Plain continuous prose only, please—no headings, bullets, bold, or tables—the way you'd write an email.
