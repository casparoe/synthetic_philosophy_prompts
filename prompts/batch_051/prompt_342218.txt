Two students at my school need roughly the same amount of extra money each month to be adequately nourished, and I cannot tell whether the difference in how we treat them reflects a genuine principle or only a settled habit. Marco has celiac disease. The school covers part of his meal costs because gluten-free food is expensive, and everyone, rightly in my view, describes this as meeting an unchosen medical need. Lena has no diagnosis, but for as long as she can remember she has found most inexpensive food so repulsive—something she and her family describe as just the way she is wired—that she cannot stay focused at school unless she spends more on food. The school gives her nothing. She is expected to absorb the cost, and people tend to treat her condition as a preference even though it is as stable and as unwilled, from her perspective, as Marco's.

The adults I ask say the difference is that Marco's condition interferes with how his body converts resources into wellbeing, while Lena's is just a fact about what she wants. But both are unchosen, both are stable, and both make the same bundle of resources yield less wellbeing for the person who has it, so I worry the distinction is being drawn after the fact to protect our ordinary judgments. Give me the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating Marco and Lena differently as a matter of justice, not merely as an administrative matter of what is easy to verify. Then test it against nearby cases: a student whose body requires twice the usual calories to maintain his weight, a student with a deep and unchosen commitment to the violin that requires expensive instruction, and a student with a lifelong involuntary aversion to noise who must rent a quieter apartment. If the principle cannot hold those boundaries without collapsing into one of the standard positions in the debate over whether equality is a matter of welfare, resources, or capabilities, tell me whether the ordinary asymmetry between medical need and expensive taste should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
