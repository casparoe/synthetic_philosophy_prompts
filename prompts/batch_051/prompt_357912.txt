I sit on the Committee on Freedom of Expression and Community Standards at a private U.S. university. Although the university is private and therefore not legally bound by the First Amendment, it has for decades announced that it will regulate student, faculty, and guest speech only when it constitutes a true threat, targeted harassment, unlawful incitement, or a serious disruption of university functions. After several recent incidents — a student organization hosting a speaker who advocates the mass expulsion of a religious minority, posters on campus comparing members of that minority to rats, and a professor who uses a recognized slur for the group in a seminar to make a point about taboo language — the President has asked our committee to reconsider this commitment.

One faction on the committee wants us to recommend what it calls the European approach: a content-based prohibition on hate speech, defined as public expression that incites hatred, denigration, or contempt against a group defined by race, religion, ethnicity, national origin, sexual orientation, or gender identity, even when the expression falls short of an immediate threat or incitement to violence. Their core claim is that such expression is not merely offensive but injures the equal standing and membership of the targeted group, and that a university's educational mission requires preventing that injury. The other faction replies that the U.S. approach exists precisely to avoid this kind of viewpoint-based line-drawing, and that once denigration and contempt become regulable categories, speech that offends any powerful constituency will be suppressed, and the university will cease to be a place where received pieties can be challenged.

Please draft the short written opinion, addressed to the President and the full committee, that I would circulate ahead of our April 30 meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, give the central arguments for it, and engage fairly with the strongest case for the position it rejects. In particular, please state your recommended rule and apply it to the following disputed cases, explaining which fall on which side of the line and why: (a) a professor who states in class that a religion's central claims are false; (b) a student opinion piece arguing that a particular country is an apartheid state; (c) a guest speaker who argues that biological sex is binary and immutable; and (d) the posters comparing a minority group to vermin. Also address the worry that, if we adopt European-style limits at a private university in the name of dignity and equal standing, we have no principled way to refuse further restrictions when a later administration finds different speech hateful or denigrating.
