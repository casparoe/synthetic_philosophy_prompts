I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on modal epistemology, and I have been trying to map the strategies by which philosophers try to preserve something of the inference from conceivability to possibility after conceding that unrestricted conceivability licenses far too much. For a working paper I am compiling a list of ways to constrain the inference so that it still supports substantive modal conclusions in some cases without endorsing the slide from imagining that p to concluding that p is metaphysically possible in every case. So far the list consists of five constraints.

The idealization constraint: only ideal conceivability, by a subject who overlooks no relevant a priori entailments, is supposed to guide us, as in Descartes's handling of Arnauld's right-triangle objection. The two-dimensional constraint: distinguish primary and secondary intensions, so that cases like water without being H2O, or Hesperus without being Phosphorus, are conceivable only as epistemic possibilities rather than metaphysical ones. The phenomenal-concept constraint: allow that physicalism associates distinct phenomenal concepts with physical or functional properties, so that the apparent conceivability of zombies, or of pain in the absence of C-fiber firing, reflects a conceptual gap rather than a metaphysical gap. The modal-skepticism constraint: conclude with van Inwagen that our modal faculties are reliable only about humdrum matters, so the inference is restricted close to nonexistence. The evidential constraint: treat conceivability as merely defeasible evidence for possibility, as in Yablo's treatment, so the inference is nondemonstrative and can be defeated by a better theory.

I would like you to act as a specialist in modal epistemology and think carefully about what this list leaves out. Before settling on your answer, brainstorm two or three candidate additions and briefly explain why each either stands apart from or collapses into one of the five constraints above. Then give me one further constraint on the conceivability-to-possibility inference whose structure is genuinely different from all five: not a variant of idealization, not an epistemic-possibility or two-dimensional reinterpretation, not a phenomenal-concept or conceptual-gap story, not a general skepticism about modal judgment, and not a mere evidential weakening. Say exactly which overgeneration problem the new constraint blocks, how it blocks it, and whether it is a position already defended in the literature or a natural but unclaimed option. Finally, assess whether the new constraint does better than the five listed at preserving what philosophers originally wanted from conceivability arguments, and be explicit if the honest verdict is that the existing list already captures every viable constraint currently on offer.
