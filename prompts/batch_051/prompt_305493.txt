I am a TA for an upper-level early modern philosophy course, and I am grading a midterm question on Hume. The question was:

"Using the passage below from Section VII, Part I of Hume’s An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, reconstruct Hume’s argument that our idea of necessary connexion arises from an impression of the mind rather than from an impression of external objects. Then evaluate whether this passage better supports an Old Hume or a New Hume reading of Hume’s causal realism."

The passage given to students was:

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

A student wrote the following answer:

"Hume’s copy principle requires every simple idea to be copied from a prior impression. In this passage he looks for the impression of necessary connexion in external objects and finds only succession: one billiard ball’s motion follows another, but no quality tying them. Since no impression is present in the objects, the idea of necessary connexion is empty. He then argues in Part II that repeated observation of constant conjunction produces a mental habit: the mind feels determined to pass from cause to effect, and this feeling is the impression from which the idea is copied. So the idea of necessity is not derived from objects at all. This is why Hume rejects causal powers: there is nothing in the cause that makes the effect follow. The New Hume reading, according to which Hume is a realist about necessary connexions, is therefore excluded by this passage, because if a real necessary connexion existed in objects it would be an impressionable quality, and Hume explicitly says we never discover any such quality."
