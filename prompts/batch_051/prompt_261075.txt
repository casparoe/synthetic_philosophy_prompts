I am drafting a short essay whose working thesis is that the familiar contrast between natural selection as a stupid, blind optimizer and gradient descent as an intelligent, gradient-informed learner is drawn at the wrong level, and that both are better understood as points in a larger space of search procedures distinguished by how they handle evaluation, variation, and representation. My notes for the essay are set out below. I would like your comments on three things: first, the technical accuracy of the contrasts, especially anywhere I may be mischaracterizing either natural selection or gradient descent; second, whether the central thesis is genuinely interesting as opposed to a restatement of points already familiar in discussions of evolutionary computation; and third, which individual note is weakest and how you would strengthen or replace it. Please also state the strongest objection to the thesis that occurs to you, and say whether any note needs qualifications I have not supplied.

Working thesis: The impression that evolution is a stupid optimizer comes from comparing evolution's selection step with gradient descent's update rule while ignoring evolution's variation mechanisms and the cost structure under which evolution operates. Once those are included, evolution looks less like an incompetent implementation of gradient descent and more like a different kind of search procedure, one that trades gradient efficiency for representational flexibility and robustness to noisy, non-differentiable, or non-stationary evaluators.

Note 1. The usual contrast is asymmetric. Gradient descent is a point-based, first-order procedure whose variation is implicit in the gradient computation over a fixed, differentiable parameterization. Natural selection, to the extent it is treated as an optimization procedure at all, is a population-based, derivative-free procedure composed of variation, differential reproduction, and inheritance. Comparing natural selection's selection step alone with gradient descent's update rule ignores the part of evolution—mutation, recombination, gene duplication, regulatory rewiring, developmental change—that actually generates new candidates.

Note 2. The deeper difference concerns what can be varied. Gradient descent improves within a fixed space of parameters and a fixed mapping from parameters to behavior. Evolution can also alter the genotype-phenotype map itself, so that later selection operates over a different encoding than earlier selection did. This makes evolution's search space non-stationary in a way gradient descent is not normally designed to accommodate, and it is one reason talk of evolution's loss surface is at best a loose metaphor.

Note 3. The two procedures face different evaluation regimes. Gradient descent succeeds when the loss is smooth enough that local derivatives carry reliable information and when evaluations are cheap enough to amortize across batches. Evolution's apparent wastefulness—large populations, many dead ends, no direct gradient signal—is better read as a strategy suited to noisy, coarse, non-differentiable, and possibly non-stationary evaluators, where a population serves both to average over noise and to maintain several simultaneous bets.

Note 4. Each procedure looks stupid under the other's assumptions. Gradient descent is brittle when the surface is rugged or when the assumption of differentiability hides the structure that actually matters, because its updates are guaranteed only under smoothness conditions that no longer hold. Evolution is inefficient precisely when accurate gradients are available and cheap. Calling either procedure intelligent or stupid without specifying the problem class and evaluation budget amounts to comparing them in different currencies.

Note 5. The practical lesson is not that gradient descent should imitate evolution. Adding populations, mutation, or diverse initializations helps only when the problem actually resembles evolution's evaluation regime. The more general lesson is that optimizer comparisons should be made along explicit axes: what information guides variation, what can be varied, how search is distributed across candidates and time, and how noisy and costly evaluations are.

I would particularly welcome comments on whether Note 2 overstates the adaptive significance of changes to the genotype-phenotype map, whether Note 3 invites the objection that populations have other roles such as sexual recombination, and whether Note 5 dismisses evolutionary algorithms too quickly.
