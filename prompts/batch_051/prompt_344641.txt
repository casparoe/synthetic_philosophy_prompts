I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about how our institutions separate acceptable selection from forbidden discrimination. Here is the asymmetry I want to examine. In the legal systems most familiar to Western readers, a nightclub may refuse entry to a man because the bouncer finds him too short, overweight, or not good-looking; in most places this is treated as crude taste, not as unlawful discrimination. That same man may be turned down for a job as a bus driver because he scores below a cutoff on a reaction-time test; that is treated as ordinary competence screening. But if either the club or the bus company rejects him because of his race or because he is a woman, the moral and legal category of the act changes: what was mere selection becomes discrimination, with penalties attached. It is not obvious which feature of the second set of traits does this work.

I am not asking for a history of the protected classes or a country-by-country list of which traits are covered. I am asking whether any principle draws this line, or whether 'suspect ground' is simply the residue of past political campaigns. The obvious candidates each break somewhere. Immutability cannot be the test: religion is chosen and yet protected, while age is unchosen and yet routinely priced into insurance. Predictive relevance cannot be the test either: in some jurisdictions sex-based insurance pricing is forbidden even though sex is a statistically informative predictor of claims, while age and credit history are accepted for very similar reasons. And a group-subordination history would make the line depend on which groups happened to win recognition rather than on anything about the ground itself. Take whichever candidate is strongest, or build a better one, and state it precisely. Then test it against hard cases: an accent that sometimes counts as national-origin discrimination and sometimes as a legitimate qualification; genetic risk, which is protected in health insurance but not in life insurance; height and weight, which are protected in a few cities but almost nowhere else; and age, which is protected in employment but openly priced in insurance.

I want an honest verdict, not a survey. Does the principle you defend actually hold the line between the nightclub's two exclusions and the bus company's two rejections, or does it force you to condemn some of the practices we currently treat as innocent? If the line cannot be held, say where it should be redrawn, or whether we should stop treating 'suspect ground' as if it named a real kind at all.
