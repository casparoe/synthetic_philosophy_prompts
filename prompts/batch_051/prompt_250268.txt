In discussions of moral motivation I repeatedly encounter a configuration for which I have no label. A person is moved to help by what he calls compassion, and the immediate act looks benevolent. But the help seems structured around the continued suffering of the recipient: it relieves distress enough to keep the recipient as a suitable object of concern, while avoiding measures that would make the recipient independent; the helper's interest cools once the recipient begins to recover or assert competence. At the same time the helper experiences the encounter as a confirmation of his own sensitivity, and interprets the recipient's resistance or ingratitude as a failure of the recipient rather than as information about the help. A public variant appears when displays of pity for a group function as a credential of moral seriousness, while the group's own agency is treated as an inconvenience. I am aware this overlaps with the familiar charge that pity is patronizing, and that Nietzsche offered a sharper version of that charge against Schopenhauer's elevation of compassion. But I am after something more specific: the way compassion can depend on, and quietly preserve, the suffering it claims to want to eliminate. Does this phenomenon have an established name in philosophy or moral psychology, and what separates it from ordinary compassion, condescension, and straightforward hypocrisy? If no term exists, what should it be called?
