I am a novelist, not a philosopher. The recurring craft problem I want solved is this: when I write a scene in which a character acts against their own settled, better judgment—a relapse, a betrayal, a broken promise—the failure nearly always comes out as either unmotivated plot mechanics or the stale cliché of being "overwhelmed." I want a reusable procedure I can run on any such scene, in drafting and in revision, to check whether the akratic act is psychologically credible and whether I have dramatized the right failure rather than merely asserted it. The stakes are the same every time: if the psychology is false, the reader stops believing the character, and the novel has lost that character for good.

The procedure should be built around a specific passage from Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VII.3 (in H. Rackham's translation), which I will quote here:

"Their using the language of knowledge is no proof that they possess it. Persons in the states mentioned repeat propositions of geometry and verses of Empedocles; students who have just begun a subject reel off its formulae, though they do not yet know their meaning, for knowledge has to become part of the tissue of the mind, and this takes time. Hence we must conceive that men who fail in self-restraint talk in the same way as actors speaking a part."

The "states mentioned" are the conditions Aristotle has just named—sleep, madness, drunkenness—but the sentence that matters to me is the last one. I read it as saying that the akratic person often has the words of his better judgment the way an actor has a script: he can produce them fluently, but the knowledge has not grown into him, so the words do not do the work they would do for someone whose knowledge is embedded. What I want is a craft procedure that tests whether a drafted scene is actually an instance of that picture. And I want it put alongside the other account I have been reading, hyperbolic discounting: the finding that people tend to prefer a smaller-sooner reward over a larger-later reward, with the preference reversing as the smaller reward becomes imminent. That account suggests the knowledge may be perfectly genuine and stable; what changes is the steepness of the discount curve, not whether the principle has become part of the person's tissue. Your procedure should say, scene by scene, which model—scripted knowledge or temporally imbalanced discounting—better explains the failure I have drafted, and what I must revise if neither does.

Give me a checklist of no more than ten tests, in order, that a non-philosopher can apply to a drafted or outlined scene in under an hour. For each test, say what in the scene counts as evidence: what to examine in the character's dialogue, interiority, past behavior, and the timing of the choice relative to the rewards. Mark plainly which steps derive from Aristotle, which from hyperbolic discounting, and where the two conflict; I want the procedure to catch the specific mistake of treating an ordinary preference reversal as if it were akrasia, or treating a character's fluent moral vocabulary as evidence of genuine conviction. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls that cannot be mechanized.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

1. A man who has sincerely and repeatedly sworn off gambling places a bet the night before a debt comes due.
2. A woman who has spoken with genuine fluency and conviction about loyalty for years betrays a close friend the first time loyalty costs her something she badly wants.
3. A writer with a finished first novel accepts a flattering, well-paid magazine commission that will delay the second novel by only a few months, then never returns to the second novel.

At least one of these worked cases should produce an answer I will not like—for example, that the scene is not akrasia at all, or that the character's stated principle was never more than performance, or that the credible version of the scene is plainer than the one I wanted. Tell me explicitly which case that is and what the procedure would force me to change. Finally, say what the procedure cannot settle: the cases where the evidence in a draft is too thin to distinguish scripted knowledge from discounted preference, and where only a decision by the novelist can close the gap.
