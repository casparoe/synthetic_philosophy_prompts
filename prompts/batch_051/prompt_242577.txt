For years I've been keeping notes on how my old evidence professor answers the standard "naked statistical evidence" cases, and I still cant pin down the rule she's actually running. Here's the record:

- Blue bus. A pedestrian is hit by a bus that doesn't stop. On that route, Blue Bus runs 80% of the buses, and the plaintiff sues Blue Bus on a preponderance standard. She would not find liability.

- Gatecrasher. A rodeo sells exactly 1,000 tickets; 499 people paid and 501 snuck in. The organizer loses the stubs and sues a randomly picked attendee for sneaking in. It's 50.1% likely he didn't pay. She would not find liability.

- Prison yard. 100 prisoners are in the yard when a guard is killed. 99 joined the attack and 1 was across the yard with a visitor, definitely not involved. The footage only shows a tangle of bodies, so the state charges one prisoner at random. There's a 99% chance he was involved, but she says that's not proof beyond a reasonable doubt and acquits.

- Blood on the window. A burglar cuts himself breaking in. DNA from the blood matches the defendant, and the lab puts the chance of a random match at one in ten million. No other evidence ties him to the scene. She says she'd convict.

Now the new one, which is supposed to be like blue bus but at the DNA-level probability. There's a warehouse where ten million parcels move through in a single day, and all but one of them are handled by Acme Logistics. A worker is badly injured when a parcel falls on him, and nobody saw which parcel it was. So there's a one-in-ten-million chance it wasn't Acme. Civil case, preponderance, like blue bus.

What should I expect her to say? I can see at least two rules that fit her whole track record: she's running a "no bare statistics, ever, no matter how high" rule, or she's running a "statistics are fine once the probability gets overwhelming" rule. Those two pull apart on the new case, but Im not sure which reading of the DNA case is doing the real work. Give me your best prediction for her verdict, which of her old cases is the better analog for the new one, the principle you'd attribute to her, and the cleanest extra hypothetical that would settle which rule she's actually using.
