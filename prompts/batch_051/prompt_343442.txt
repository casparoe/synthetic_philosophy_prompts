I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I've been going back and forth on how much the tautology objection actually hurts the propensity interpretation of fitness. Roughly: the tautology objection says that if fitness just is actual reproductive success, then the claim that the fittest survive is empty—you can't identify the fittest independently of those who survived. The propensity view was supposed to fix this by defining fitness as a probablistic disposition to leave offspring, so an organism can be fitter than another even if, by bad luck, it leaves fewer offspring. But I keep worrying this just moves the bump in the rug: we estimate the propensities from the same realized frequencies, so maybe the explanatory circularity is back at the epistemic level even if it's gone at the defintional level.

Give me your actual credence that the propensity interpretation genuinely dissolves the tautology objection (rather than just relabeling it), with the reasoning decomposed. Terms I'd want in there: (1) whether the tautology objection is still live against any serious modern formulation of natural selection, or whether it's only a criticism of a slogan nobody in the field actually uses; (2) whether a single-case propensity is coherent, or whether fitness-as-propensity falls apart once you press on the reference class problem; (3) whether the propensity view gives us empirically testable claims that aren't just read off actual outcomes; (4) whether the semantic fix even matters if the circularity reappears when we estimate fitness from realized frequencies. Put rough probabilities on each, say how they combine into your final number, and tell me which term you're least confident about and what evidence would move it most. My advisor is at about .8 that the propensity view works; I'm closer to .35, so if you can say which sub-question most likely explains a gap like that, I'd appreciate it.
