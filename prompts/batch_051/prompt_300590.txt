In discussions of Jain epistemology, the analogy people most often reach for is the story of the blind men and the elephant: each blind man feels one part of the animal and reports a different object, a pillar, a rope, a winnowing fan, while a sighted observer sees the whole animal. The analogy is made to do real argumentative work: it is used to gloss anekāntavāda, the doctrine of many-sidedness, and to support the conclusion that no single standpoint captures the truth about a thing, that each limited view possesses only a partial truth, and that the appropriate epistemic and practical stance is therefore qualified and tolerant rather than absolutist.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds, not another rehearsal of the story. As I understand the doctrine, anekāntavāda gets worked out through two connected apparatuses: nayavāda, the doctrine of standpoints, and syādvāda, the doctrine of conditional predication, standardly expressed through a sevenfold scheme in which any assertion is made under the qualifier syāt — in some respect it is, in some respect it is not, in some respect it is indescribable, and the combinations of these. If I have that summary wrong in a way that matters, correct it before going on.

Treat the analogy as a piece of argument rather than as a loose illustration. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the blind men, their individual tactile reports, the parts of the animal, the elephant as a whole, and the sighted observer, within the machinery of nayas and syāt-qualified predications? Then identify where the mapping breaks. Which features of the source story does the analogy quietly import that the target doctrine may not possess — for example, a single privileged observer who is not one of the partial knowers, a whole composed of spatially separable parts, and partial knowers who are all straightforwardly wrong rather than partly right? Say which of these disanalogies are harmless and which matter for the conclusions the story is being used to support.

Then assess whether those conclusions survive. Does the story support the claim that truth is many-sided in the way anekāntavāda says, or does it smuggle in a milder, less technical perspectivism? Does it support the further move from many-sidedness to tolerance, or does it overstate the case by implying that every view is equally partial? Does the analogy help at all with the familiar difficulty that a non-absolutist doctrine can seem to assert its own absoluteness? And if the story cannot carry its load, say what a better analogy would need to represent — in particular, how it would need to handle the difference between being true under a qualification and being merely partly true, and whether it should include anything corresponding to a fully adequate though still conditional cognition.

Throughout, separate what is settled in the study of these doctrines from what is genuinely contested among informed interpreters — for example, whether anekāntavāda is best read as a relativism, a pluralism, a realism about many aspects of a single reality, or something else — and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
