I'm a novelist, working on a book set in a hospital that gets cut off by a flood, and I keep tripping over the triage scenes. I don't want the doctors to be mouthpieces for an ethics textbook; I want to know what a real person's gut does in these moments, because my protagonist is an ER nurse and she has to make the calls. So I've been keeping a list of my own verdicts, and they are all over the place.

If there's one ventilator and I have to choose between a 22-year-old with a solid chance and a 78-year-old with diabetes, heart failure, and a much worse chance, I give it to the 22-year-old and barely lose sleep. But if the 78-year-old is my protagonist's father, I want the nurse to override the protocol and save him, and I think the reader would want that too, even though the nurse is a stranger to both. I'd also spend a fortune digging out one trapped child on live news, but I'd grumble at spending the same fortune on guardrails that would statisically save more children I'll never see. Between a liver for a man whose drinking wrecked his own liver and a woman with a congenital disease, I'd pick the woman, and I'm not sure the argument about personal responsibility actually does the work, I just feel it. But if I have to pick between ten extra years of full health for one person and twenty extra years at a quality the scales would rate around 40 percent for another, my instinct is the twenty real years, even though I know the QALY arithmetic prefers the first. And I'd take a patient off a ventilator who has almost no chance if someone with a better chance needs it, but if that patient has been on the vent for three weeks and the staff know her family's names, I flinch.

What I'd like from you is not a reading list. I want the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, that could sit behind these calls. Should I think in QALYs, or in unadjusted years of life, or in something about identified versus statistical lives, or some mix? What does the cleanest principle say about cases I haven't put on the list, say spending the same money on one adult with a rare cancer versus many adults with treatable hypertension, and which of my gut verdicts does it force me to give up?
