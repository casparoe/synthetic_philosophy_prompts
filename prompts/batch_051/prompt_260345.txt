I work as a software engineer, mostly on optimization and planning systems, and I have no formal background in philosophy. A colleague recently described to me a thought experiment that, I've since learned, is called Parfit's hitchhiker. When I tried to translate it into the language I use for building agents, I ended up with an argument that seems to prove that an ideal agent should sometimes deliberately make itself non-ideal. I can't tell whether the argument contains a bug or whether I have to accept the conclusion, and I'd like someone to locate the failure point exactly.

Here is the argument, as I've reconstructed it.

(1) An action is rational just in case it maximizes expected utility relative to the agent's preferences and beliefs at the time the action is performed.

(2) A traveler in the desert will be picked up by the only available driver only if the driver is confident the traveler will pay him upon reaching town. The traveler knows he will prefer to keep the money once he is safe in town, and the driver knows this as well. Therefore an unaltered traveler—one whose only guide later will be his later expected-utility calculation—cannot both be rational and credibly promise to pay.

(3) The traveler can make the promise credible only by changing himself so that, after reaching town, he will in fact pay: by precommitment, by adopting an enforceable rule, or by directly modifying his own preferences so that paying has the highest expected utility for him at that later time.

(4) From the traveler's present position, the expected utility of changing himself and being driven to town is higher than the expected utility of remaining as he is and not being picked up.

(5) Therefore, the act with the highest expected utility for the traveler now is to change himself into an agent who, later, will not take the act with the highest expected utility relative to his present, unchanged preferences.

(6) Since the traveler's present, unchanged preferences are the ones that define his present good, it follows that a rational agent is sometimes required to make himself irrational—to deliberately build a worse optimizer. But an agent that is sometimes required to be non-optimal no longer looks like the clean optimizer I set out to build; it looks like a system with a deliberate bug.

I am most reluctant to give up (1), since it is the definition of the utility-maximizing agent I know how to implement. I also find (4) hard to deny. But I cannot see how to stop the slide to (6), and (6) seems to undermine the entire project of writing down an objective function and optimizing it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I should not reject any step, explain what (6) actually shows and why it only appears to be a bug, and say what sense of "rational" is left for the later agent.
