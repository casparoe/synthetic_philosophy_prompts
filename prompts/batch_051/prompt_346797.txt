I've come to a position I want stress-tested. I think the everyday homonymy of natural language — the way 'pitch' and 'tone' mean completly different things depending on context — is mostly a feature, not a bug. The story is: short, frequent forms are scarce and valuable, and context is usually rich enough to do the disambiguation for free; on the rare occasion context fails, we just ask or restate. So ambiguity buys massive compression at a small repair cost, and languages should be most tolerant of it exactly where context is most informative and least tolerant where the cost of being misunderstood is high. That's a prediction the 'ambiguity is just tolerated noise' picture doesn't make so cleanly. Tell me what would count against this position — not just the usual fact that ambiguous sentences get misread, but the observations, experiments, theoretical or formal pressures, and unlikely cases that should actually move me. Work from fatal to merely awkward, and do it in continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write an email. Also say which of these a defender of the ambiguity-is-efficient view would honestly concede as counting against them (not just what critics would claim), and whether the view has been insulated by the ease of saying 'context handled it' — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
