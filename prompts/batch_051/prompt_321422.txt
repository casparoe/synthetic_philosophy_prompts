Two people in my office got into it after one of them, Elena, was reading Otto Neurath's International Picture Language (the 1936 booklet) and read us a bit from it. I didn't write it down, but the gist was: his picture language is meant only as a helping language, not a replacement for words, and it avoids ambiguity through fixed rules, for example showing larger quantities by repeating the same equal-sized symbol rather than drawing a bigger one. She said this was more radical than people realize: the internationalness isn't from pictures being self-evident but from the rules being economical and consistent. Tom said no, if you need fixed rules then it's just a notation; any international appeal comes from image-like resemblance to the thing, otherwise Esperanto with drawings is still contrived. Elena replied that's the point Neurath concedes: the signs don't have to be self-evident, they have to be standardised, and they work alongside ordinary language; the picture part keeps comparative relations visible. Tom said that shrinks the claim from International Picture Language to 'standardized chart supplement for blah text'. Elena said that's still more international than a paragraph of prose because the chart can be read by someone with minimal literacy. Tom said only after they learn the symbol set; a pictogram of a factory isn't a factory until you're told. Elena said exactly - once told, the repetitive same-size rule lets you compare without doing number work, which is a different kind of cognition than reading words. Tom said this is just the difference between graphs and prose, not a language. Please adjudicate move by move: who is right about what, where does the 'language vs notation' objection land, and does the passage as paraphrased support Elena's interpretation or Tom's (or neither)?
