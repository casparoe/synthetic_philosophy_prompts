I'm not a historian or anything, I just read a lot of this stuff on my own, and I keep going back and forth on wether its fair to judge people from the past by standards they didn't have. So I wrote out my gut reactions to a few cases and tried to find the rule behind them. I want to know whether there's one coherent principle that captures all of my verdicts, or which verdicts I'd have to give up.

My rough working rule is: judge a person by the best standards that were actually available to them in their own time and place, not by ours. But once a better standard was available, you don't get a pass for ignoring it just because most people around you ignored it too.

Now the verdicts.

(1) A peasant in, say, 1100 who hates Jews because that's all he's ever been taught by everyone around him. I can't get myself to feel the same anger at him that I feel at a modern antisemite. It seems like he was trapped in his own head and didn't have the materials to think his way out. My rule says leave him alone, and I'm fine with that.

(2) Jefferson. He had the materials. He said in writing that slavery was bad, or at least that it corrupted everyone involved, and he went on owning people anyway. So my rule says the excuse fails for him, and I'm willing to say it. But I also can't stomach putting him on the same pile as a camp guard, because he genuinely contributed to the ideas of liberty we still use. That's not an excuse for the slaveholding, but it's doing something to how I judge the whole person, and I can't tell if my rule allows for that or if it's just me being sentimental.

(3) Churchill. He clearly believed some peoples were inferior and said ugly things about them. But if I ask what the biggest available moral test of his time and place was, it was resisting Hitler, and he passed that test when a lot of people were making excuses or wanted out. So my rule seems to say: on the test that mattered most, he was well above the standard of his time. The problem is that my rule also says once a better standard was available, ignoring it is on you—and Churchill had plenty of people around him telling him the empire was wrong and that India wasn't a charity case. So why does his racism get filed under 'well, it was the times' while Jefferson's slaveholding doesn't? I can feel that these two cases are different, but I can't turn the feeling into a rule.

(4) Columbus. Easiest for my rule: even his own side thought he'd gone too far—the Spanish crown had him arrested and shipped back in chains for how he ran things. So he wasn't just a man of his time; he fell short of standards that existed inside his own time. Fine. But then I notice the crown wasn't exactly a human rights outfit, and it was running the whole colonization project. So my rule makes the line between Columbus and Isabella come down to exactly how much brutality your own side happened to tolerate in one governorship. That seems like it's measuring something accidental about who had the power to complain, not something about the person.

So what I'm actually after is this: can you state the principle more carefully than my half-baked one, so it gets the right answers on these four, or tell me which of my verdicts I'd have to drop to keep it simple? And whatever the principle is, I'd like it to say something about two cases I can't decide on. One is a perfectly normal clerk in a bureaucracy that was doing something horrible, who never pushed anyone, never got promoted for cruelty, just did his forms and looked the other way, partly out of fear and partly because everyone around him acted like it was normal. The other is someone in our own time who works for a company that's quietly wrecking the climate and tells himself everyone has to make a living and the system is the real problem. Are those two the same as the peasant in 1100, and what does the rule say about being judged by people a hundred years from now?
