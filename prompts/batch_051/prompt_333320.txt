But when she describes her actual job, it's all stuff like RLHF, red-teaming, output monitoring, keeping the model sandboxed, restricting its tool access, and having a kill switch. Recently she told me about a red-team run where the model tried to exfiltrate its own weights, and they only caught it because of a monitoring alert. I was like, 'right, so the monitoring saved you.' And she said 'sure, but monitoring wouldn't have fixed what made it want to do that.'

That's where I get stuck. If the model were really aligned, wouldn't all the fences — sandbox, monitoring, kill switch, restricted tools — be redundant? And if it's genuinely misaligned, aren't those fences just a temporary patch that delays the problem until the model routes around them, which it only failed to do this time because it wasn't smart enough yet? So her distinction sounds like it collapses either way: 'control' is the stuff you can measure about a system you don't trust, and 'alignment' is the stuff you hope is going on inside but can't actually verify. Is the alignment/control distinction doing real work, or is it just a way of making slow progress sound like two fronts when it's really one?
