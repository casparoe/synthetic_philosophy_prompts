I have been reading Kant's universalizability test, and since retiring from the natural sciences I find it helpful to force an argument into numbered steps so that every inference is visible. I would be grateful for a careful check of the following reconstruction of the false-promise argument, because I suspect that something goes wrong around step (3) or (4), but I cannot say exactly what.

As I understand Kant, a maxim is permissible only if the agent can will, without contradiction, that it should become a universal law of nature. The false-promise case is supposed to fail this test as a contradiction in conception. I reconstruct the reasoning as follows:

(1) The maxim in question is: when I need money, I shall make a false promise in order to obtain it.
(2) To test the maxim, I ask whether I can will the corresponding law of nature: everyone, whenever they need money, makes a false promise in order to obtain it.
(3) A world governed by that law is logically conceivable; the bare description of such a world contains no contradiction. In that world, to be sure, promises would no longer be trusted, so the practice of promising would effectively cease to exist.
(4) The contradiction appears only if I also will that promising and the trust on which it depends should continue to exist; for then I am willing both that everyone makes false promises and that promises are still trusted.
(5) But the continued existence of promising is not part of the maxim being tested; it is a separate end that I happen to have.
(6) Therefore the contradiction is not internal to the maxim, but between the maxim and a further end. If that is right, the false-promise maxim does not involve a contradiction in conception, and Kant's test cannot condemn it on its own.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest reading of Kant's test on which that step comes out false?
