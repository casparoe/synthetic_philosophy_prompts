Earlier this year I helped clear out a neighbor's flat after she died, and I was the one who sorted her papers. What struck me was how nearly every official trace of her — a 1970s eviction notice, a small-claims judgment, a hospital intake form — survived only because some institution had opened a file on her; her own account of those same moments was nowhere. I have started calling the underlying distortion "institutional refraction": an archive preserves an encounter between a person and an institution, but the institution's categories bend the record, so the person tends to survive as the object the institution acted upon — tenant, debtor, patient, defendant — rather than as an agent with her own descriptions and reasons. My working claim is that the density of records about a person is therefore a measure of how often they collided with record-keeping bodies, not of how much they mattered to the people around them. I want an honest assessment of this distinction. Is it coherent, or is it a metaphor doing the work of an argument? Does something like it already exist under another name, and where does it break — what kinds of records or cases does it describe badly?
