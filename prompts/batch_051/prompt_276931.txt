You are a professional philosopher working in Hellenistic ethics, with particular expertise in Stoic theories of emotion and the debate over whether apatheia, freedom from disturbance, is a good worth wanting and at what cost. I would like you to assess a distinction I have been developing on my own rather than anything I have read. Please think through the strongest version of my proposal before criticising it, and begin by restating the distinction in your own words.

I distinguish first-order disturbance from second-order disturbance. First-order disturbance is the felt response itself: the grief at a death, the startle at bad news, the flare of anger at an insult. Second-order disturbance is the additional distress that arises when I judge the first-order response to be itself bad: that I should not be grieving so much, that the anger is a failure of self-control, that the fear is shameful. My claim is that Stoic apatheia is best read as aiming at the elimination of second-order disturbance only, and not, as its critics commonly assume, at the elimination of first-order disturbance. On this reading, the person who has achieved apatheia still feels the grief or the anger fully; what has gone is only the second layer of commentary and self-reproach.

I think this absorbs the familiar objection that the Stoic ideal demands an inhuman deadening of ordinary human emotion, because the cost people fear, losing grief, the distress of love, or righteous indignation, is never actually exacted. Am I mistaken? I want an honest assessment on three fronts: whether the distinction is stable or whether the second layer inevitably seeps back into the first; whether anyone has already defended this reading of apatheia under another name; and whether the strongest Stoic position would in fact reject my move, holding that the first-order passions themselves must be revised and that the cost is real.
