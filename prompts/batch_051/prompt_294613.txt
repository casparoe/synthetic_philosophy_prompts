I've been reading Aristotle's account of phantasia in De Anima, and I keep noticing what looks like a structural parallel to a live debate in cognitive science, and I want help deciding whether the parallel is deep or superficial. Aristotle distinguishes phantasia from both perception and intellect: it is not simply seeing, and it is not simply judging or believing, yet it seems to be the medium through which thought gets its objects. On his account, when the intellect thinks about something, it needs an image-like presentation of the thing, even when the thing is absent; thought seems to operate on the residue of perception rather than on bare intelligible content. In modern cognitive science, one influential family of views says something structurally similar about concepts: conceptual thought is not executed in a separate amodal symbol system but reuses perceptual and motor representations, so that even abstract reasoning is carried out partly via modality-specific simulations. The common idea I think I see is that there is no clean break between perceiving and thinking; an intermediary format, neither fully sensory nor fully abstract, does the real cognitive work.

But the disanalogies worry me. Aristotle's phantasia is also closely tied to desire and animal movement, and it can be erroneous in ways that intellect is not; those functions are mostly absent from the modern imagery-and-concepts debate, which is framed around representational format and empirical predictions about reaction times, interference effects, and neural overlap. So my question is this: is the parallel genuinely illuminating, or does it just trade on the English word image? If it is deep, what exactly is the shared insight, and what would it force either side to say that it has not already said? If it is superficial, where precisely does the mapping break—what does Aristotle's phantasia do that no modern notion of perceptual simulation can capture, or vice versa?
