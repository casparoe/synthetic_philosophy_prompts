A philosophy magazine I read included the following paragraph summarizing the knowing how / knowing that distinction:

"Ryle's distinction is usually presented like this. Knowing that such-and-such is the case is factual, propositional knowledge, while knowing how to do something is a practical capacity that cannot be reduced to any stock of propositions. Ryle demonstrated this by noting that someone can know how to ride a bicycle without being able to state the rules involved; indeed, trying to think about the physics would make a skilled cyclist fall. He also argued that if knowing how were merely a kind of knowing that, an infinite regress would follow, because applying any proposition to a performance would itself require knowing how to apply it. Knowing how, therefore, is simply the possession of the relevant ability. After Ryle, the intellectualist claim that knowing how is a form of knowing that has been largely abandoned, since the knowledge that guides skilled action is not the sort of thing that could be encoded as facts. This is also why attempts to produce genuine know-how in artificial systems by feeding them more propositions are bound to miss what skilled activity actually is."

I suspect this paragraph is partly misleading, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong. I would like an accuracy audit: go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful epistemologist working on this distinction would accept, which parts they would find subtly wrong or overstated, and why. In particular, is it fair to identify knowing how with having an ability, and is the claim that intellectualism has been largely abandoned accurate? Does the final conclusion about artificial systems follow from whatever is true in the paragraph? Please write your answer in American English.
