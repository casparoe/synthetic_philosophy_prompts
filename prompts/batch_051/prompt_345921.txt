I'm writing a term paper on what makes a classification suspect in discrimination law and political philosophy, and I've hit a wall. Every account I read seems to reach for the same clutch of candidate features: (1) immutability, the person didn't choose it and can't easily exit it; (2) visibility, it's easy for others to sort people by it at a glance; (3) a history of group subordination, so using it tends to continue an old hierarchy; (4) irrelevance to almost any legitimate decision, like job qualifications; (5) the way it functions as a thick social identity rather than a thin administrative category. I'm not saying these all hang together, and I know they don't cover every case.

What I want is a new criterion for suspectness that is not on that list and not just a rewording, special case, or combination of the items above. It can be one someone has defended or one you come up with, but it has to do real work. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good — whether it helps with cases the standard five flub, like religion (not usually immutable or visible) or pregnancy (temporary and partly chosen), or whether it mostly restates things I already said.
