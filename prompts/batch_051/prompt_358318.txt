I lead the risk-assessment unit in a national emergency-management agency. Every two weeks or so we issue a one-page probabilistic judgment that determines whether to recommend precautionary measures: the probability that a circulating influenza strain will evolve resistance to the available antiviral within the next year, the probability that this monsoon season breaches the threshold for pre-emptive evacuation of a coastal district, the chance of a cascading power-and-fuel-grid failure during a heatwave. The officials above us feed our numbers into their own planning models and have made clear they want a single probability per judgment, not a discussion. But the evidence almost never fixes a single number: different defensible statistical models, priors, and methods of pooling expert disagreement produce different sharp probabilities, and sometimes the defensible spread is wide.

I am persuaded by much of imprecise-probability theory: that a rational state can be a set of probability functions rather than one, that there is a real difference between risk and ambiguity, and that reporting a point estimate can quietly turn ignorance into confidence. But that conviction stops exactly where my job begins. I still have to issue something tomorrow, and I have no rule for when the imprecision should surface as a reported interval, when as a point estimate with a stated caveat, when as "too imprecise to quantify," or how to set the endpoints of a range and document what was lost when we collapse it to a single number. I also know the framework is not unanimous about what to do when a decision deadline forces a scalar—maximin, E-admissibility, Hurwicz-style weighting are all on the table—and I do not know how to choose among them under time pressure, or how seriously to take the fact that in this framework new evidence can make an interval wider rather than narrower.

Turn the core commitments of imprecise-probability theory into a reusable procedure my team can actually run. I want a decision tree or checklist, not a survey of the view. It should tell us, for each assessment: which features of the evidence indicate genuine imprecision rather than ordinary model uncertainty, cross-expert disagreement, or our own hedging; what format to report in; how to fix endpoints when we report an interval and how to document the content lost when we sharpen to a point; and which decision rule to use when a scalar is unavoidable. Give two or three worked examples using the kinds of assessments I described, including at least one where the procedure yields a conclusion that an imprecise-probability purist on my team would resist. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and a person must decide. Please commit: give the procedure you would actually adopt, including where the literature genuinely disagrees, and say plainly when you are taking a contested stand. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—the one rule the whole procedure instantiates—then give the reasoning and the procedure itself, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
