I'm a junior in a regular public high school, and I'm writing a paper for civics on school choice. What's weird is I live inside the exact system people argue about, so I want to write about what the system itself assumes, not what politicians say about it.

Here's how it works where I live: you get assigned to a high school based on your address unless you win a lottery for one of two charters. The state also has a voucher program where a chunk of the per-pupil money follows you if you leave for a private school. And my older cousin is homeschooled; as far as I can tell her parents just file a one-page form once a year and nobody actually checks what she learns. Meanwhile, the same school board that runs my school keeps saying public education is the great equalizer and a public good we all pay into because everyone benefits from other people's kids being educated.

So my actual question: what view of education (and of kids, and of fairness) does this whole bundle presuppose? Like, is it treating education as a public good, a private service parents shop for, a child's right, a parental responsibility, or some mix? Reconstruct the implicit view the way you'd reconstruct someone's beliefs from their actions, not their slogans. I especially want to know whether the pieces—neighborhood assignment, charters by lottery, vouchers, basically unregulated homeschooling—presuppose the same thing, or whether the system is genuinely incoherent, or whether the policies don't actually force any one view. Then tell me what would concretely have to change if the system went all the way to education-as-pure-public-good, versus what would have to change if it went all the way to education-as-pure-private-good. I'm not trying to argue school choice is good or bad; I'm trying to figure out what its already committed to.
