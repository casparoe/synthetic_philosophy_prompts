Me and a friend just spent two hours arguing about whether a dog has the concept squirrel, and it turned into an argument about what "has a concept" even means. He said a concept is basically a pile of stored examples plus a similarity measure, so the dog has the concept because its stored a bunch of squirrel encounters. I said no, a concept is more like a little theory you hold about what a thing is and why it does what it does, so a lot of what he counts wouldnt count. Then his roommate wandered in and said we were both wrong, that a concept is just a summary of the typical features, a prototype. None of us could give a definition that handled the cases we kept throwing at each other.

So give me a working definition of "has a concept" (or "has the concept X") that gets these cases right:

- a person who, without hesitating, calls every chair in a room a chair and never calls a table or a bench one
- a thermostat that turns on the heat when the room drops below 68 degrees
- a dog that chases squirrels but not chipmunks, pigeons, or cats
- a two-week-old who stares longer when a ball on a screen seems to pass through a solid wall
- a chess engine that plays a fork and wins a knight and a queen. Does it have the concept "fork"?
- a single forward pass of a language model that answers "is a platypus a mammal?" correctly
- a blindsighted person who guesses the tilt of a line at 90% accuracy but says they see nothing and are just guessing
- a pigeon trained to peck at paintings in one style, which then pecks at new paintings in that style but not at paintings in other styles
- a kid who says a dolphin is a fish because it lives in the water and swims. Does she have the concept "fish"? Does she have "mammal"?

Then run the definition against each case, say where it gives a weird answer or no answer, and either fix it or defend the weird answer. Which of your verdicts is least secure? And if having a concept comes in degrees, say whether the definition is graded or all-or-nothing.
