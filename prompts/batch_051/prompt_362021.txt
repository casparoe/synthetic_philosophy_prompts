Before cheap generative AI, it was natural to treat photographic and audio recordings as an epistemic backstop. The argument was not that recordings are infallible, but that producing a convincing fake was costly enough that, in practice, a recording could settle disputes that would otherwise come down to conflicting testimony: a politician denying she said something, a video of an event contradicting official accounts, a society needing a shared record of what happened. On this view, hard-to-fake recordings function as a background condition for journalism, legal evidence, and public memory generally, even if any particular recording can mislead.

Now consider a new development: convincing synthetic video and audio of real people has become cheap and widely available, and for many files there is no internal feature by which even an expert can reliably distinguish a deepfake from a genuine recording at the point of encounter. Useful provenance data is often stripped from the file, and trusted provenance infrastructure is not yet operating at scale.

Does the epistemic backstop argument survive this change? I am asking about the fate of the argument, not about what policies we should adopt. Which premise of the backstop argument does this development actually bear on — the premise that fakes are hard to produce, or something deeper about how recordings function as public evidence rather than as private perceptual access? Does the argument fail outright, survive mostly intact, or need repair? If it needs repair, state the repaired argument precisely (for example, whether the backstop must move from the file itself to provenance tracking, institutional authentication, or the incentives of publishers), and say what the repaired argument now requires and whether it is stronger or weaker than the original.
