Here is an asymmetry in how we ordinarily think about language and translation. When an anthropologist studies a newly encountered language, we readily take seriously the thought that the word gavagai, uttered whenever a rabbit appears, might mean rabbit, undetached rabbit part, rabbit-stage, or rabbit-occurrence, and that no possible behavioral evidence would settle among those readings. Yet we do not extend the same doubt to our own word rabbit. We take ourselves to mean rabbits, full stop, even though a third party observing our linguistic behavior would seem to have only the same kind of evidence the anthropologist has about gavagai. In other words, we treat translational indeterminacy as a predicament of the outsider and exempt ourselves from it. Is there a defensible difference between the two positions that justifies this asymmetry? If you think there is, identify it and show why it applies to our case and not the anthropologist's. If you think there is not, say what follows: should we accept that our own words are indeterminate in the same way, and does that acceptance force us to revise our ordinary confidence in what we mean?
