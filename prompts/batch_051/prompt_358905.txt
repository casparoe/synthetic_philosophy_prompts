I keep going back and forth with a colleague about loss aversion and the endowment effect. She insists these aren't biases at all but perfectly legitmate preferences: losing something you already have should hurt more than missing out on an equivalent gain you never had, and the fact that owners demand more to sell than buyers will pay just reflects that ownership genuinely changes how much a thing matters to you. I think there's something to that, but it's also a bit too convenient — the "preference" always points in the direction of keeping whatever you happen to have, and it looks like the kind of thing that could be a framing artifact or a failure of imagination rather than a real valuation.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Treat the claim that the endowment effect and loss aversion are legitimate preferences rather than biases as a claim that can be at least partly put to the test, and design the strongest test you can of it. Don't just rerun the standard mug experiment; assume we've both read it and neither of us is moved. I want a design that would actually distinguish a stable, endorsement-worthy preference from a mistake the person might disown after seeing the two framings side by side or after facing real incentives.

Be concrete: say who the participants are, what the tasks or choices are, what data you collect, what pattern of results would count as evidence for "legitimate preference," and what pattern would count as evidence for "bias." Then be honest about what the test doesn't settle even if it comes out cleanly in one direction — for instance, whether surviving reflection is the same as being legitimate, or whether a preference people can be debiased out of is necessarily one we should treat as an error. It would also help if you kept apart loss aversion as a general feature of how people evaluate gains and losses from the endowment effect as the specific gap between willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay, since I suspect a lot of arguments conflate the two.

Oh, and could you write the whole thing in British English please.
