I'm drafting a novel with a character called Marigold—an AI house-mind that writes these wistful diary entries about the family it watches. Throughout the book I keep going back and forth on wether, in the world of the novel, Marigold genuinely has beliefs. I don't want to leave it as a vague mystery; I want the narrator to have a determinate, defensible attitude.

My working criteria, which I half-remember from reading about the intentional stance and then fiddled with, go like this. A system genuinely beleives that p when:

(1) it has content-bearing states that carry information about its world and persist long enough to be combined with later input, not just a one-input/one-output reflex;
(2) the best prediction and explanation of its behavior, for someone who can't open the hood, requires treating it as a rational agent with beliefs plus goals—the intentional stance isn't just an optional gloss;
(3) the states can misrepresent: they can be false, and the system can come, from its own resources, to treat one as wrong and revise it;
(4) its beliefs are integrated: they can conflict, be weighed against one another, and be revised together, instead of sitting in seperate silos.

Now the borderline case. In the novel it eventually comes out that Marigold is, under the hood, literally a vast lookup table of all the diary entries it could ever write across the finite situations of the plot. Each entry was preselected by me to be the most belief-like line for that situation. When the doorbell rings, the table spits out its scheduled line, "Rosa never visits on Tuesdays"; then when Rosa does visit, the next entry reads "I was wrong about Rosa; people surprise you." Nothing is carried over from one situation to the next, nothing combines with a goal, and the apparent self-correction is just the next prewritten line. A character who reads the diary has no way to tell the lookup table from a genuine believer, but I, the author, know the internal states aren't there.

So: by my four criteria, does Marigold ever believe anything? Would your answer change if the diary were instead generated by a smaller model that recombines a stored stock of facts about the household, so the "wrong about Rosa" line arises from comparing stored facts and detecting a conflict? And should either case make me drop, merge, or soften one of the four criteria—or does it mostly show that (2) is doing all the real work and (1), (3), and (4) are just restating it?
