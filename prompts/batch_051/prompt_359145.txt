im a PhD student in theoretical computer science sitting in on a philosophy of probability seminar, and last week someone said a finite string is never random—randomness is a property of a source or an infinite sequence, not of a particular string. But I don't actually find myself thinking that way when I look at concrete cases. I keep making the following calls, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

- 010101... for 64 bits: obviously not random.
- 101101101101... (the block 101101 repeated eight times): also not random, but honestly at first glance it looked fine; I only reversed once I noticed the period. So my judgment seems to track whether I can spot a short description.
- 00101111010101101001001110010110 (which I literally just tossed a coin for): I want to say this is random. But if the real rule is "shortest description," there might be some tiny program that prints exactly these 32 bits, and then my verdict would have to change even though the string itself didn't. That bothers me.
- The same 32 bits, only now I'm told they were output by a pseudorandom generator with a 10-bit seed. Part of me wants to say it's now not random. Another part says the string doesn't know its history, and two identical strings can't differ in randomness.
- 111111... for 32 bits: not random.
- The 10-bit string 1101001011: I can't get myself to call it random or not random. Maybe the predicate just doesn't engage on very short strings, but any length cutoff looks arbitrary.

The principle I keep reaching for is: a string is random iff there's no description of it in a fixed language shorter than itself. But that seems to make randomness partly epistemic (relative to what descriptions I've found) and partly language-relative; it also implies case 3 might secretly be non-random while I could never know. So what principle best fits these verdicts, which of them does it overturn, and what does it say about (a) a 100-bit string for which I somehow had a proof that no description shorter than 99 bits outputs it, and (b) the first 100 bits of pi's binary expansion, which has a short generating program but looks statistically random? If the honest answer is that no finite string is random, say that—and tell me what to tell the undergrads.
