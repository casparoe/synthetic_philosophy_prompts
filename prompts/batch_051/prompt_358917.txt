I recently joined the stewardship committee of a mid-sized regional land trust, and next month I am presenting a recommendation on how the organization should treat wild-animal welfare in its acquisition and restoration decisions. My proposal is that every project memo include a welfare impact note: a standing section stating, for each parcel, the expected direction, magnitude, and confidence of the project's effects on sentient wild animals, and committing the committee not to treat ecosystem restoration as automatically net-positive for welfare, nor automatically net-negative, but as an empirical question to be reassessed project by project.

The audience is the rest of the stewardship committee: seven career conservation biologists and restoration ecologists, plus a hydrologist and a former state wildlife official. Most built their careers when habitat restoration was framed as an uncomplicated good and intact habitat as the self-evident best outcome for animals. They care first about ecological integrity, second about donor confidence and the next capital campaign, and third about legal exposure; wild-animal welfare as such has never been on the room's list of criteria. Several are convinced that even officially acknowledging welfare trade-offs will be quoted back at the trust by developers, drainage districts, and anyone who wants to convert degraded land. One member has already told me privately that he suspects the proposal is a Trojan horse for anti-conservation arguments.

The committee meets for two hours, my item is third on a four-item agenda, and I can count on roughly fifteen minutes of questions after a short presentation.

Prepare a Q&A prep sheet for me, not a defense of the proposal in the abstract. Give me (1) the seven or eight objections I am most likely to get from this specific audience, each phrased the way a mid-career restoration ecologist on a land-trust committee would actually put it; (2) an ordering of the objections by likelihood and by damage, with a sentence on which ones will be raised but are relatively harmless; (3) for each, a spoken reply of no more than two minutes, including any concession I should make up front because the objection has real force; (4) a note on where each reply is softest, so I know which points cannot be left to stand unanswered; and (5) the question I should be most relieved nobody asks, with a sentence on why it would hurt the proposal more than it appears to.
