Put on the hat of a political scientist or legal-institutional designer and work through this carefully—brainstorm the players and their levers before you settle into a systematic answer.

A country adopts the following constitutional amendment: any statute or ordinance that goes 30 years without a single enforcement action under it—no arrest, no citation, no fine, no injunction, no successful prosecution—is automatically void at the start of the 31st year. The only way to stop the sunset is for the responsible enforcement agency, during the 29th year, to publish an enforcement plan that names an official, and then actually bring at least one action under the statute within the following two years. If that action is dismissed, dropped, or fails, the statute dies anyway. I'm not asking wether this is a good idea in the abstract. I want to know how the machinery would actually run.

Specifically: what does this incentivize for police, prosecutors, regulatory agencies, city councils, and ordinary people? Where does it get gamed first—token prosecutions, contrived citations, deliberate non-enforcement to quietly kill a law you don't like, re-enacting the same rule under a new number, emergency clauses, friendly defendants? What fights break out over what counts as an 'enforcement action'? What happens if a private right of action exists under an old statute—does a citizen suit keep it alive? And what does the rule do to the gap between law-on-the-books and law-in-practice, which is exactly where desuetude problems live? Does the rule of law get stronger or weaker, and for whom? Finish by saying which failure modes you would try to amend away and which ones are probably unfixable.
