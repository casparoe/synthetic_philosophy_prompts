I got into a long argument with a friend last night, and now I want to check my own rule. The rule is: when someone calls a person complicit in some harm, I won't accept the label unless that person's own act made a causal difference to whether the harm happened. So a person buying from a company that uses sweatshop labor isn't complicit in the sweatshops, because the factory would be just as bad without their one purchase; a person who votes for a candidate who later does awful things isn't complicit in those things, because the election wasn't decided by their single vote. I lean on this all the time because it stops what feels like runaway blame-by-association.

My friend said this is way too crude. He says complicity also includes doing your part in a wrong even when your part is causally inert: the lookout on a burglary that would have gone ahead without him, the shooter in a fireing squad whose gun was loaded with blanks, the low-level clerk who handles one file in a system that would keep running without him. He kept saying participation is a different relation than causation, not just a weaker version of it.

Is my rule defensable? I found the firing squad and lookout examples unsettling, but I can't tell whether they refute the rule or just show I need some exceptions. Is there a better version that keeps the benefit — not spreading blame to everyone remotely connected — without letting genuinely complicit people off the hook?
