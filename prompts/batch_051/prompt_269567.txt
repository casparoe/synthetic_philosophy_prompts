For a philosophy-and-literature unit I teach, I'm using A Clockwork Orange (the novel and the Kubrick film together, since students have easy access to both) to get at the moral bioenhancement debate. The obvious classroom move is to treat the Ludovico Technique as a failed moral bioenhancement: it rewires Alex so he cannot act on his violent impulses, which sounds like exactly what Persson and Savulescu are after. But I suspect that reading is to quick. They aren't proposing to nauseate people into compliance; their worry is that our moral psychology is built for small, face-to-face, near-term problems while our technology now gives us global and intergenerational powers, and so they argue we should at least explore biomedical ways of strengthening things like altruism and a sense of justice. I don't want my students leaving with the easy conclusion that moral bioenhancement is obviously dystopian because Alex ends up a clockwork orange.

So I'd like a careful reading that goes in both directions. Specifically: where does the Ludovico technique genuinely resemble what Persson and Savulescu defend, and where is it a strawman? Is the novel's real objection that the change is forced, that it leaves Alex's values and understanding untouched, or that it imposes gratuitous costs, like ruining Beethoven for him, that a more targeted intervention wouldn't? And then the other way: does Burgess dramatize something the philosophical debate underweights — say, the gap between being unable to do wrong and being able to do right, or the way a pacified Alex becomes defenseless and gets exploited by the people he used to terrorize? I'd like the reading to end by saying what the fiction adds to the philosophical argument that argument alone tends to miss, without reducing the novel to just an illustration.
