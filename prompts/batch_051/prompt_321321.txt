I have been trying to work out whether Goodhart's law, understood as the claim that once a measure is made a target for optimization it ceases to be a reliable measure of the underlying value, supports the much stronger conclusion that no metric can ever safely guide institutional decision-making. The following argument seems to get me there, but I suspect it is too quick. (1) A metric is reliable for guiding decisions only if it tracks, with reasonable accuracy, the target property it is meant to measure. (2) When agents are rewarded or penalized on the metric, they will alter their behavior to raise the metric. (3) Some of those alterations will improve the target property, but some will exploit gaps between the metric and the target. (4) The exploitative alterations are cheaper and more reliably available than the substantive improvements, so under pressure they will proliferate. (5) As they proliferate, the correlation between the metric and the target property decays. (6) Therefore, for any metric that is actually used as a target, the metric will cease to track the target. (7) Therefore, no metric can safely guide institutional decision-making. Where does this go wrong, if it does? I want to keep premises (1) through (4), but I have a feeling the move from (6) to (7) is doing something suspicious. Tell me exactly which step fails or whether the argument is valid. If I must give up a premise, what is the least costly revision, and what would it mean for the practice of using quantitative targets in organizations?
