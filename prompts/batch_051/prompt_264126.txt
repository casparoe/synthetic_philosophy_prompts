In my political philosophy seminar we've been reading Simone Weil on political parties, and I can't stop noticing an odd asymmetry in how we talk.

Take a political party: it has a permanent organisation, a line, a whip, and a good chunk of its energy goes into getting itself re-elected and growing. We treat all this as normal, even healthy — "that's just how democracy works." But now put the exact same structure somewhere else: a jury whose members are expected to vote as the jury's leadership instructs; a church congregation told to fall in with the committee's position; a university department where junior members get the message that they shouldn't publish doubts about the departmental line; a trade union branch whose members are told that, whatever they privately think, "we have a position" and they should back it. In every one of those cases we'd call the whipped judgement a corruption — a failure of the people involved to actually deliberate.

And the party case isn't just different in degree; we treat it as a different kind of thing, where loyalty to the line and swallowing your private doubts is a virtue rather than a vice. Is there a genuine principle that draws that line around party politics, or is it just that we've got used to parties and all the other cases sound sinister because they're unfamiliar? I keep thinking there's something about scale and aggregation that makes the exception real... but Weil's arguement seems to be that this is exactly backwards: the party is the worst case, because it doesn't just pressure a handful of people — it manufactures collective opinion and treats its own growth as a primary goal, so the thing we treat as the ordinary machinery of democracy is the thing that stops genuine thought from being exercised.

I'd like two things. First, find the strongest principle you can that might justify the asymmetry, and test it against a few nearby cases (a coalition, an internal party faction, a social movement, a union branch during a strike, an independent MP who accepts a whip) to see whether it genuinely tracks why we condemn the other cases. Second, give me an honest verdict: should we keep the asymmetry (parties are special), reject it (start judging parties by the standard we use for juries and churches), or reverse it (maybe whipped judgement is more defensible in some non-political settings than we assume)? I'd rather you land on a clear view than survey all the options, but I want to see where your principle fails as well as where it works.
