Here is a two-envelope argument I cannot fully diagnose. Tell me whether it is valid and, if not, exactly which step fails and why.

The game is this: one envelope contains twice as much money as the other. The smaller amount was chosen as a whole number of dollars between 1 and 100, by a rule I am not told, and the two filled envelopes were then shuffled so I cannot tell which is which. I choose the left envelope and, before opening it, am offered a switch to the right.

Consider this reasoning:

(1) Before opening, the two envelopes are perfectly symmetric, so switching cannot be better in expected value than keeping the left envelope.
(2) Yet suppose the left envelope contains x dollars. Then the right envelope contains either 2x or x/2 dollars.
(3) Since nothing in my evidence distinguishes those two possibilities, I should assign each probability 1/2.
(4) So the expected value of the right envelope, conditional on the left containing x, is (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 1.25x, which exceeds x.
(5) This appears to work for every possible value of x; so no matter what amount is in the left envelope, switching looks better in conditional expected value.
(6) Therefore switching is better than keeping the left envelope unconditionally — which contradicts the symmetry in step (1).

I suspect the failure is step (3), but someone else has told me the real mistake is the move from (5) to (6), since being better for each possible x does not entail being better overall. Which is right, or does the argument fail at another step, or perhaps not at all once the filler's rule is pinned down?
