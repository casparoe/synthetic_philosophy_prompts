A colleague and I have been working through what makes someone complicit in a wrong they do not cause, and we have produced an argument whose conclusion neither of us can accept. We would like a diagnosis.

(1) One can be complicit in a wrong without causing it. A getaway driver is complicit in a robbery even if the thieves would have found another driver and the robbery would have gone ahead without her; her driving is not a but-for cause of the theft.

(2) If a causal contribution is not required for complicity, then the relation that grounds it must be something weaker than causation — something such as knowingly making the principal's wrongdoing easier, more likely, or more profitable.

(3) That weaker relation is present across ordinary economic life. A consumer who buys a phone from a supply chain widely reported to depend on forced labor makes that arrangement more profitable and likelier to continue; a citizen who pays taxes to a state fighting an unjust war funds the war; a holder of an ordinary index fund helps capitalize a firm that conceals abuse in its supply chain.

(4) By (2) and (3), the ordinary consumer, the taxpayer, and the index-fund holder are complicit in forced labor, unjust war, and corporate abuse.

(5) Yet a concept that makes virtually the whole adult population complicit in serious wrongdoing in virtue of everyday economic participation cannot be what complicity is. Complicity is meant to mark out a distinctive and serious way of being answerable for wrongdoing, but on this account it no longer distinguishes the getaway driver from the person buying a laptop.

(6) Therefore either complicity requires a causal contribution after all, or almost everyone in a modern economy is complicit in serious wrongdoing and the distinction between the complicit and the merely present has collapsed.

Where does the argument go wrong? If it contains a false step, identify the step and explain why it looked sound; if it is valid, say which premise is least costly to reject and what giving it up would cost a theory of complicity. We are most reluctant to give up (1), and we find the second disjunct of (6) excessive; tell us what we are paying whichever way we turn. Please write your answer in British English.
