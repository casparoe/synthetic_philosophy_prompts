You are a professional philosopher who works on Habermas's discourse ethics and the ideal speech situation. Think carefully before answering; if it helps, begin by sketching the possible interpretations, but you must end with a committed position and the reasons for it.

I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I sit on my company's Engineering Council, which makes technical and operational decisions through an internal proposal-and-comment process. Section 6(c) of our Decision Procedure reads:

"Any engineer materially affected by a proposal may take part in the deliberation. Contributions shall be assessed on their merits alone, without regard to the author's level, tenure, team, or style of writing. No participant may use position, threats, inducements, or social pressure to influence the outcome. A decision is valid only if the participants could accept it on the strength of the reasons offered, setting aside every fact about who offered them."

The section was written many years ago, and the drafters clearly imagined people speaking for themselves in threads and meetings. I have always understood our process as an attempt to make the outcome turn on the quality of the argument rather than rank, which I take it is meant to resemble the ideal speech situation.

The case: we recently deliberated a change to the on-call rotation and escalation policy. Sam, an engineer who is clearly materially affected, posted a detailed objection showing that the plan would, through an interaction with the escalation path, force nearly all night pages onto the two engineers in a particular time zone. The numbers checked out, and nobody could answer the objection on the merits. During a follow-up meeting, Sam admitted he had not produced the argument himself: he had fed the policy documents to a large language model, asked it to locate fairness problems, and verified each factual claim in the result, but he could not reconstruct or fully explain the model's reasoning when a staff engineer pressed him on one step. The staff engineer moved to strike the objection, arguing that it was not a contribution from a participant at all, because Sam could not defend or redeem the claim he had posted. Another engineer replied that the rules require contributions to be assessed on their merits alone, and by that standard the objection is simply the best argument before us.

Does Section 6(c) require us to count the model-produced objection, or to exclude it? Work through the plain text, the purpose of the section, what the drafters would have said if asked about this case, and what a reasonable reader of the section today would understand it to require. Where those approaches diverge, tell me which should win and why. I would also like to know whether Habermas's idea that a speaker must be able to defend, or redeem, a validity claim makes the staff engineer's objection stronger than it first looks, and whether that idea decides the case or just reframes it. Please commit to one answer, and do not simply lay out the options.
