I am a high school student in a philosophy of mind reading group. Over the past term our group has split into two camps: phenomenal realists, who hold that our experiences have a distinctive felt quality—the redness of a ripe tomato, the hurtfulness of a stubbed toe—that is real and must be taken seriously by any theory of the mind; and illusionists, who hold that these felt qualities, as ordinarily understood, do not exist, and that the overwhelming sense that they do exist is itself a misleading appearance generated by the brain, which a correct theory should explain away rather than accept. After months of the two camps arguing past each other, we want the document we should have produced instead: a joint statement that an honest representative of each camp could sign. Please draft that document.

The document should contain a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Do not deliver a verdict about which camp is correct; the point is to produce a statement both camps could put their names to.

Part 1: the numbered agreed-claims section. These claims should not be vacuous. They should include claims about the testimony both camps are trying to explain—for example, what people sincerely report about the felt character of seeing, hearing, and pain; which of these reports both camps agree a theory must explain or accommodate; and which observed features of human cognition (ordinary misperception, inaccurate self-reports, or the like) both camps accept as real. Aim for the strongest formulations both camps can still genuinely endorse.

Part 2: the disagreements table. For each row, state a neutral question, then give the exact formulation each camp would volunteer as its own answer. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would make at least one camp revise its view. I suspect the cruxes will include (a) whether an appearance can be produced and sustained without any instance of the property it seems to present, on the scale illusionism requires; (b) whether the self-evidence of one's own current experience is a datum constraining theory or an appearance that theory is allowed to debunk; and (c) whether illusionism makes testable predictions or only re-describes the data after the fact. Adjust or replace these as your reconstruction requires.

For each crux, give a concrete case or observation that both camps would accept bears on it. Use only the following three cases, and explain which crux each best illuminates rather than mechanically assigning all three to every crux:

- Change blindness: observers often fail to notice large alterations in a scene while insisting that their visual field is rich and detailed.
- Blindsight: people with certain brain lesions deny seeing a stimulus while performing above chance on forced-choice tasks about it.
- The rubber hand illusion: when a visible rubber hand and a hidden real hand are stroked in synchrony, subjects report feeling touch at the location of the rubber hand.

For each case, state in advance what each camp would predict the case shows, and why. If a case supports both camps in different ways, do not hide that; write out the disagreement it exposes. Conclude the document by saying which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal—with a translation that would dissolve them—and which parts remain substantive. Do not cite the literature; rely only on the described cases and your own reasoning.
