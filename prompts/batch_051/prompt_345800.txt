I sit on my university's naming and commemorations committee, and a case has split us right down the middle. A donor wants to endow a "Truman Center for the Study of Executive Decision-Making in War," and the bequest comes with a requested plaque describing Truman as a leader who "made the hard choices that ended the war." Some members, mostly from peace studies and philosophy, oppose the naming outright. Their line is, in essense, G. E. M. Anscombe's: that deliberately destroying Hiroshima and Nagasaki was the deliberate killing of noncombatants, which no projected good consequences can turn into anything but murder, and that the problem isn't even primarily the historical debate but the honor itself, since a center with his name over the door treats as admirable a kind of act that just-war thinking categorically prohibits. The other side, mostly history and political science plus development, keeps saying that studying a decision is not endorsing it and that we can't just wave away the invasion and blockade deaths that never happened because of it.

Our policy allows naming for contested figures if the program provides "critical context" that "serves the educational mission," but nobody can agree what that could mean once the name is carved over the entrance. I'd like you to draft the actual decision memo I'd circulate before next Friday's meeting. It should recommend one of the options — accept, reject, or accept with conditions — with reasons the losing side would recognize as serious (so if we reject, engage the historical-study point honestly; if we accept, engage the Anscombe-style objection about what an honor does, not just the historical facts). Make the bottom line actionable: if there are conditions, spell them out concretely — plaque wording, programmatic requirements, a named lecture series, anything. No footnotes or citations; this is an internal document. And please don't just punt to "further study."
