I am a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and I want a diagnosis of the structure of a debate rather than a verdict in it. The platonism–nominalism debate strikes me as stalled in a peculiar way. The two sides agree on all the mathematics: the same theorems, the same proofs, the same applications in physics and engineering. The platonist holds that sentences such as 'there are infinitely many primes' are literally true and that their truth requires mind-independent abstract objects, at least of a minimal mathematical sort. The nominalist holds that no abstract objects exist; the sentence is either literally false, or true only under a paraphrase that replaces apparent reference to numbers with talk of concrete marks, possible constructions, or structural facts about the world. The disagreement therefore survives complete agreement about which mathematical statements are true, which are provable, and which science uses. That is what puzzles me: if the evidence is entirely shared, why does neither side move?

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that the debate is mostly a disagreement about what should count as a datum. The platonist treats the literal truth of accepted mathematics and the indispensability of mathematical vocabulary in mature science as data that ontology must accommodate; the nominalist treats ontological economy as a datum that semantics must accommodate, for example by giving paraphrases or fictionalist readings. On this diagnosis, the fight is not really about whether abstract objects exist; it is about whether the existential quantifier is a reliable guide to what there is, and about whether semantics owes more to successful practice or to prior standards of parsimony.

But I can imagine other diagnoses. It might be a substantive metaphysical disagreement that is simply hard, because no observation or proof bears on it. It might be a verbal disagreement about 'exists' that would dissolve under a sharper semantics. It might be a disagreement about explanation: whether the applicability of mathematics is best explained by objects or by structure. Or the two sides might be answering different questions — one about truth-conditions of mathematical talk, one about the ontology of the concrete world — under a single heading.

Which diagnosis best fits what each side actually argues, and what in their moves supports it? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result showing that some accepted piece of mathematics resists nominalistic paraphrase, a historical case where the quantifier criterion misled, a distinction between truth-conditions and grounding, or something else? Or should we expect the impasse to persist, and if so, what kind of impasse is it?
