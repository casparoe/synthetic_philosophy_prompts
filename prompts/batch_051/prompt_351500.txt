I cannot decide whether the following cleaned-up version of the quantum suicide argument is a genuine reductio of the many-worlds interpretation or a trick of perspective. I would like you to tell me exactly where, if anywhere, it goes wrong.

Set-up. A machine measures a quantum system that has two outcomes, U and D, each with Born weight 1/2. If the D outcome occurs, the machine instantly and painlessly kills you; if the U outcome occurs, you are unharmed. Run the machine on you once, and assume the many-worlds interpretation is correct.

(1) After the measurement, there are two real branches: one in which the U outcome occurred and you are alive, and one in which the D outcome occurred and you are dead.
(2) The dead version of you has no experiences; any experience continues only in the U-branch.
(3) So, looked at from within your own future experience, the only post-measurement experience you could have is the experience of being alive in the U-branch.
(4) Therefore, after the measurement, you should expect to find yourself alive in the U-branch — that is, you should be effectively certain you survived.
(5) Run the machine on you n times. The same reasoning applies at each step, so you should be certain you will observe a run of n consecutive U outcomes and survive all of them.
(6) But the quantum state assigns the all-U branch a weight of (1/2)^n; so the sequence you are now certain you will observe is, by the theory's own measure, vanishingly small.

I suspect step (4) is where the trouble is, but I cannot say whether it is a modal fallacy, a mistake about self-locating probabilities, or something else. Is the argument valid? If not, state the step that fails, the reading under which it fails, and the corrected version of that step. If it is valid, explain what the surviving version of me should in fact expect, and whether this commits us to abandoning the many-worlds interpretation.
