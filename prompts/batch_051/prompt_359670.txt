I do local history, mostly with parish registers, poor-law records, and manorial court rolls, and there's a pattern I keep running into that I don't have a word for. The people I study show up in the written record not when they were living their lives, but exactly at the moments when some institution needed something from them or wanted to count them. My great-great-grandmother appears three times in the whole surviving record: a baptism entry, because the parish needed to keep track of souls; a vagrancy examination, because she'd been picked up and questioned; and a death entry in the workhouse register. In between, nothing — and I don't read that as her doing nothing, just that no one with a quill and a reason to write needed her. A whole hamlet near where I grew up is the same: no letters or diaries from the tenants survive, but the hearth-tax assessments and manorial court rolls are beautifully complete, so I can tell you to the penny what people owed and when they were fined, and almost nothing about how they courted or mourned or got the harvest in. It seems to me the archive isn't just biased, but biased in one particular direction: it's fullest at the exact points where power pressed on a person, and quiet everywhere else. I've heard people talk about "silences" in the record, and I get that part. What I'm noticing feels different, almost like the archive's abundance is the footprint of coersion, so the people I can see most clearly are the ones being taxed, judged, or counted, not the ones being human. Is there an established name for this specific thing? If not, what would you call it? And I don't want just a label — I want to know how it differs from ordinary source bias on one side and from plain archival silence on the other, and where the edge cases fall: for instance, a love letter that survives only because it was filed as evidence in a lawsuit — does that count as the same thing or something else?
