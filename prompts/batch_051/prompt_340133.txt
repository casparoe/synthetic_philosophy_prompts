I've been reading Berkeley on money and its honestly scrambled my intuitions. I want to know whether there's one coherent principle behind the way I now react to cases, or whether I'm just pattern-matching to whatever sounds Berkeley-ish on a given day. My verdicts:

- If one island has a mountain of gold coins but everybody sits idle, and another island has no gold but everyone farms, builds, and trades, I call the second island the rich one. The first is just a heap.
- If a governement replaces all its silver coin with paper notes that buy exactly the same goods, I don't think any wealth has been lost. But if people stop producing because they don't trust the paper, that loss is real.
- A community that uses shells for money seems to me to have real money; they're no poorer for not using metal.
- A merchant who hoards coins under the floor is, to me, not making his country any wealthier, even though he personally has money.
- If a country's exports boom and gold pours in, I think the wealth was in the work and the goods leaving the place, not in the gold that came back.
- I get nervous about paper money backed by nothing, but paper issued against land and used to put idle people to work seems fine to me, even if the land can't be handed over coin for coin.
- A gold-rich country that imports luxuries while its own farms and workshops decay is, in my book, getting poorer.

My working slogan is: money is a counter, and real wealth is people working. I want that tested against the cases and repaired, not just illustrated.

Is there a single principle about what money is and where wealth comes from that gets all or most of these? Which of my verdicts does the tidiest version force me to drop? And am I actually tracking Berkeley here, or making him sound more modern than he was? Finally, what would the resulting principle say about a case I can't settle: a fully digital currency with no government backing that only works because lots of people use it.
