You are a philosopher of language and mind who works on assertion, belief, and the norms governing them. Please think slowly, brainstorm candidate principles before committing, and test each one against the cases below; then answer the question at the end.

I am trying to figure out what I actually think about the Moorean defect — the wrongness attaching to saying things of the form "p, but I don't believe that p." Here are my gut verdicts on a batch of cases.

If a colleague says flat-out, "It's raining, but I don't believe it is," I find the utterance defective in a way that a plain false statement is not. The sentence could be true, and if I point that out they can agree — "yes, it's consistent" — yet my sense that they have done something self-undermining survives. The defect seems to be in their asserting it, not in the weather.

If they say, "It's raining, but my daughter doesn't believe it is," nothing is wrong. Nor is anything wrong with "It was raining, but I didn't believe it was," which reads as a rueful confession of past error. But "It will rain tomorrow, but I don't believe it will" seems just as defective as the present-tense case.

"It's raining, but I don't know it is" also sounds wrong to me, and in a slightly different way: the problem seems to be knowledge rather than belief. "It's raining, but I'm not sure it is" is defective too, but milder. By contrast, "It's more likely than not that it's raining, but I don't believe it is" strikes me as mostly fine, perhaps because a probability claim can be made on the strength of credence alone without outright belief.

I also suspect the defect lives in the act of assertion rather than the sentence's content by itself. If a random text generator prints "It's raining, but I don't believe it is," I do not think the generator has done anything Moorean. If an actor says the line onstage, the actor is not self-undermining, though the character may be. And if someone says, "It might be that it's raining and I don't believe it is," that seems to be a coherent statement of self-ignorance rather than a Moorean blunder.

My working theory is that flat-out assertion expresses both belief and knowledge: it represents the speaker as believing the thing asserted and as knowing it. That would explain the first case and the "don't know" case, and it would explain why third-person and past-tense variants are fine. But it sits awkwardly next to the probability case, where I want to permit a claim about the balance of evidence without full belief, and it does not yet explain why the defect survives the reminder that the sentence is consistent.
