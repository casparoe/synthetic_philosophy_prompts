In the circles I move through — writers, editors, critics who take narrative seriously — the question whether video games can be art is now routinely settled by a single comparison. A game, they say, is at bottom a novel the reader walks around inside: the designer is the author; the player is the reader; the branching storyline is a plot in the subjunctive; the environment and the dialogue are description and voice. The conclusion they draw is that games are art in the same way novels are, and that the right instruments for judging them are the instruments of literary criticism — character, theme, structure, style. A playwright friend of mine is building an interactive piece on exactly this premise, treating each selected path as a verse she has already written, and she waves the comparison away when I press her on it.

As a novelist, I suspect the analogy is carrying more than it can hold. In my craft the text is fixed; the reader is not an agent inside the story; nothing the reader does changes what the work contains; and the aesthetic object is a shaped sequence of words rather than a space of possible actions with success and failure conditions. But I cannot yet say which of these differences matter for the conclusion and which are merely incidental — or whether the conclusion could be true even though the analogy fails.

I would like you to audit the comparison as one audits a metaphor that is doing argumentative work. Lay out the correspondence table: what stands in for the author, the reader, the text, the plot, the act of interpretation. Then mark the places where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not, and tell me whether the conclusion — that games are art and should be judged by literary criteria — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy is available, give it to me and say what it licenses instead.
