My sister is 31 and just got approved for a gastric sleeve. The clinic sent her a stack of documents and she has to sign a form saying she understands the risks, benefits, and alternatives before they'll operate. Watching her go through it, I was struck by how much the whole process treats her decision as if it were the same kind of informed choice as, like, picking between two car models. But the whole point of the surgery is that her relationship to food, her body, her moods, and even the way other people respond to her are going to change in ways she can't preview. So the person signing the consent form is in a weird position: she's making a decision on behalf of a future person whose preferences she doesn't currently have.

I've seen this kind of choice called a transformative experience, where you can't know what it's like from the inside until you're on the other side, and being on the other side changes the very values you'd use to evaluate it. So here's what I want to know: what theory of the self and of value does the practice of informed consent quietly presuppose? Does it assume our preferences are stable and knowable enough that present-me can speak for future-me, or is it actually just a risk-management ritual that doesn't take it's own stated rationale literally? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, and say whether the practice is strictly committed to it or just compatible with it. Then tell me what medicine would have to change if it actually took the transformative part seriously—trial periods, staged and reversible steps, consent informed by people who've already had the procedure, letting the later self ratify the choice after some time, or whatever else follows.
