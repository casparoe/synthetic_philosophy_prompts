I've been turning this over since a fight with my roomate, and I can't decide whether it's defensible or just a bias I should try to train out of myself. When I miss a deadline or snap at someone, I immediately reach for the situation: I was underslept, the instructions were ambiguous, the cat was sick, anyone would have lost their temper under that provocation. But when a coworker misses the same deadline or snaps at me, I reach for their character: they're disorganized, they're thin-skinned, that's just the kind of person they are. Same behavior, same lack of information about their week. It's not only for faults either — if I give a few dollars to someone on the street I think of it as a response to this particular moment, but if I see a stranger do it I'm ready to describe them as a generous person. I've asked friends about this and they all report doing it too, so it's not just me.

The obvious justification is epistemic: I have direct access to my own tiredness, intentions, and history, so of course I explain my behavior situationally, while for other people the trait is all I can observe. That sounds good until I test it on cases where I do know the other person's situation. I know my partner is exhausted and stressed, and I'll still read her snapping as evidence she's an irritable person more readily than I'd read my own identical snapping as evidence about me. Likewise I'll infer a stranger's disorganization from a single missed email while treating my own missed email as noise. So the differential-access story looks less like a justification and more like a rationalization.

The deeper reason I care is that a lot of moral thought leans on character. Virtue ethics in particular wants stable traits like honesty, generosity, and patience to do real explanatory and evaluative work. But if ordinary trait-attribution is systematically asymmetrical in this way, then maybe our character judgments are mostly a double standard — or maybe, and this is what I actually want help with, the asymmetry is tracking something real that could be rescued. Is there a principle that justifies treating my behavior as situationally caused and yours as character-revealing, or at least justifies the asymmetry in one direction but not the other? Test whatever principle you find against the partner case, the missed-email case, and the panhandler case, and give me an honest verdict on whether we should keep the asymmetry, drop it, or run it in reverse.
