I am trying to settle my view on prosperity: whether material abundance is a good in itself, or only good for what it buys in health, security, and satisfaction. My present, untutored verdicts on cases are these. Clean water and refrigeration are plainly good, because they prevent disease and misery. A cheap, abundant energy source that heats homes and powers transport is good, even if it also enables frivolous uses. Extending the average lifespan from eighty to ninety-five is good. Raising everyone's income while also widening inequality is on balance good, but the value does not seem to me to lie mainly in the extra purchases. Cheap long-distance travel is good. An endless annual churn of new consumer goods—telephones replaced every year—strikes me as not good, or only barely good. Luxury fashion and collectibles are not good in themselves, at most aesthetically so. A society in which everyone's material needs are easily met and people turn to art, sport, and science seems good; a society equally rich whose consumption is mostly status competition seems not good. The case I keep going back and forth on is a drug that makes people content with very little, so that a materially modest society stops wanting to produce or acquire more. Is that a loss, or no loss at all?

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? Work it out in reflective-equilibrium fashion: state the principle you judge best, defend it, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why those verdicts should go, and test it against cases I have not listed. I want a committed response, not a survey of options.
