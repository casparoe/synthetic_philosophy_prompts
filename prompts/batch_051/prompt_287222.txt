I am a novelist, at work on a book about a people who slowly turn their own founding figures into gods, and while reading for it I have kept noticing a pattern in real traditions that I cannot properly name. A person first known as a human—a lawgiver, an ancestor, a healer, a king—gradually accumulates a tomb and an altar in the same town, then a genealogy in which his parents are already divine, then a place in the hymns; and the older, plainer memory of the man sits unremarked beside the god. In Greek tradition I find Heracles, born and dead as a man and afterwards said to dwell among the Olympians, and Asclepius, a healer struck down for overstepping and afterwards worshipped. In Rome the senate could vote a dead emperor into the company of the gods and endow his worship as a state institution. In later Scandinavian material I have read accounts in which the gods themselves are reworked into human chieftains and workers of magic, whose descendants gradually came to treat them as divine. And I have found the traffic running the other way as well: a figure first met as a god is later supplied with a birthplace, parents, and a death, as though the tradition were trying to ground the worship in something human.

These seem to me related but not identical: a claim about the distant origin of an entire pantheon, an administrative act concerning a recently dead ruler, a hero who crosses over, and a later rationalizing impulse that gives gods human careers. I want the carving, not just a label. Does the first of these have an established name in the study of religion or myth, and how do the others stand to it? Is the precise claim that many gods began as humans, or is it that myths are garbled history—and are those the same idea or two ideas that have been run together? Where is the boundary between a powerful dead ancestor who receives rites and a full god, and between a god who was once human and a god who merely has a human-shaped biography? Which of my examples belong inside the concept, and which are only adjacent? If no established term matches the whole configuration, I would rather have a definition with edge cases.
