I am a novelist, and I am drafting the climactic trial scene of a novel set in a near-future city. I want the scene to be legally and epistemically serious, so I am trying to determine what my trial judge should permit a jury to conclude from evidence that is, at bottom, probabilistic.

In my novel's jurisdiction, conviction requires proof beyond reasonable doubt, and I have written myself four criteria for deciding when a statistical inference can meet that standard:

1. The inference must raise the probability of the defendant's guilt above 0.9.
2. The prosecution must offer at least one item of tracing evidence — evidence caused jointly by the crime and by the individual who committed it, such as a print, a recording, or residue left at the scene — rather than merely a base rate about some class to which the defendant belongs.
3. The probability must be robust across reference classes: it should not rise or collapse simply because we redescribe the defendant in another equally natural and equally available way.
4. The inference must be of a kind that a conscientious court could apply across all similar cases without knowingly convicting innocent people at an intolerable rate.

Now consider the case at the center of my novel. A murder leaves a single trace: a partial biometric profile on a window latch. The city keeps a compulsory, complete database of all one million adult residents. For a person chosen at random from the population, the chance of matching this profile is one in a million. The defendant was never a suspect and has no connection to the victim; he was identified solely because his was the only profile in the database that matched. No other evidence links him to the crime.

The prosecution argues that a one-in-a-million match is overwhelming. The defense argues that when the comparison was run against a million citizens, a chance match somewhere in the database was to be expected: on these figures, roughly one accidental match would appear even if the true culprit were not in the database at all, so the bare fact of one match cannot by itself put this particular defendant's guilt above 0.9.

That is why I chose this case for the novel: I genuinely cannot tell which side ought to win. Does the evidence satisfy my four criteria? Go through them one by one, giving each side its strongest version; say explicitly where the criteria underdetermine the answer or point in opposite directions; and then tell me whether the case should make me revise the criteria — in particular, whether criterion 2 draws the line where I imagine it does, and whether criteria 3 and 4 do any work that criterion 1 misses.
