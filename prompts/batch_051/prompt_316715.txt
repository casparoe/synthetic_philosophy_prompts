I've been reading rationalist blogs long enough to have picked up the habit of trying to separate "the crowd is evidence" from "the crowd is a wage or status incentive," but I've never seen the distinction drawn cleanly.

Here is the pattern I have in mind. People choose in sequence, and each person holds some private scrap of evidence, but each also observes what all the earlier people chose. Once enough earlier people have chosen the same way, later people can rationally discount their own evidence entirely, because the earlier choices are themselves evidence about what earlier people knew. I understand that, even when everyone updates like a perfect Bayesian, the group can lock onto one option even though their pooled information would have pointed the other way. The restaurant with the queue and the startup that everyone funds are the standard illustrations.

Next to this I keep noticing a pattern that feels different: a person joins the majority not because the majority's choices carry information about the world, but because there is a direct payoff to siding with the crowd. A fund manager who backed a failed startup along with everyone else is treated better than one who went against the crowd and lost. And then there is the cruder case where no one has meaningful private evidence and people simply copy.

My question is whether these are two or three distinct phenomena, whether the purely informational case has a standard name of its own, and where exactly its edges are. I am not satisfied by a bare label; I want the carving: which features are load-bearing, which boundary cases fall outside the core, and whether the informational and payoff-based cases are genuinely separate mechanisms or the same behavior described at different levels. Please also keep explicit track of what is settled among specialists and what remains contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
