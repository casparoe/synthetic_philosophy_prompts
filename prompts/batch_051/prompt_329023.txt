I'm auditing a philosophy of biology seminar, and last week we spent the whole session on the old charge that natural selection is an empty theory because its central slogan, "survival of the fittest," just says that the survivors survive. The instructor's reply was to introduce the propensity interpretation of fitness: fitness is not an organism's actual reproductive output but a probabilistic disposition it has in a given environment to survive and leave offspring, so that "the fittest tend to survive more often than the less fit" becomes a substantive empirical claim rather than a definitional truth. But I keep noticing that the slogan gets made to do very different jobs in different discussions—sometimes defining fitness, sometimes asserting a causal claim about why certain traits spread, sometimes making a claim about optimality or long-run improvement, and sometimes something almost normative about who "deserves" to prevail. I want to get this sorted out before I write my paper.

Could you lay out, as a menu of readings, the distinct precise theses that "survival of the fittest" could be used to assert? For each reading, I would like a verdict on what kind of claim it is (a tautology, a definitional stipulation, an empirical generalization, a near-mathematical consequence of reproduction, a normative claim, or something else), whether it is plausible or defensible, and whether it is the reading the tautology objection is actually attacking or the reading the propensity interpretation actually defends. In particular: which readings of the slogan are genuinely empty, which are empirical but doubtful or false, and which, if any, come out as substantive empirical claims about how heritable traits and environments interact? It would help if the list ran from the most trivial reading to the most substantive, and if you flagged any pair of readings that are especially easy to conflate when people argue past each other.
