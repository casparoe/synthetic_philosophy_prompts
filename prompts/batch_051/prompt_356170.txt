I'm reporting a story about what should happen to software when its creator disappears, and I keep coming back to one case. A shareware license from 1994 reads: "You may copy and distribute the software for personal or educational use, but you may not include it in any collection or make it available on any network without the written permission of the author." The author, a one-person developer, died in 2008; the company was dissolved, and no heir, estate, or successor-in-interest can be located. A preservation group now wants to include the program in an online archive that serves it through a browser-based emulator — a preservation use the clause's author never had in mind. Work out what the clause requires here under each of the main approaches to interpretation: the plain meaning of the words, the purpose the clause was serving, and what the author would most plausibly have said if asked. Then say which approach should govern, and whether the archive needs a permission that no one can now grant or whether the clause is better read as not reaching this case.
