At a linguistics department happy hour I overheard two graduate students arguing about the English possessive marker, and neither side had conceded by the time they moved on. The first said that the -'s in "the Queen of England's hat" cannot be an inflectional suffix, because it attaches to the whole phrase "the Queen of England" rather than to the head noun, so it must be a clitic. The second replied that it is the same element found in "Elizabeth's hat", where it is phonologically welded to a single noun and written as part of it; and even if it were a clitic, the grammar still has to say which word inside the phrase hosts it, so relabeling it does no explanatory work. The first came back with "the man I met yesterday's umbrella" and "somebody else's problem": if the marker were an inflectional affix of the noun, that kind of phrasal attachment should be impossible, but speakers produce such forms routinely. The second answered that "children's toys" shows the marker still respects the head noun's morphology, attaching after the irregular plural exactly where a suffix would go, and that in "the dogs' owners" the possessive is not even separately pronounced after a regular plural — which is affix-like behavior, not free-standing-word behavior. The first then said a true case ending should affect the shape of its host, whereas the English possessive leaves the whole preceding phrase untouched, which suggests it is placed by syntax rather than built inside the word. Go through this exchange and adjudicate each move: which arguments are sound, which overstate their evidence, and what the dispute reveals about the boundary between inflectional affixes, derivational affixes, and clitics — in particular, which tests actually locate the edge of a word, and what a sharper account would need to say to settle it.
