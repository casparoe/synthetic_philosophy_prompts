I am a novelist, and I am trying to understand a disagreement with my publisher that has now run for the better part of a year without either of us moving. It concerns the effectiveness of advertising for my books, and I have come to suspect that the obstacle is not the arithmetic but something about what question each side takes itself to be answering.

I write literary fiction under my own name and crime fiction under a pseudonym. For each of my last twelve launches, the publisher either bought a national advertising campaign or did not. The numbers, simplified, are these. Among my six literary titles, the four that were advertised averaged 1,200 copies sold in their first eight weeks, while the two unadvertised ones averaged 1,100. Among my six crime titles under the pseudonym, the two advertised ones averaged 6,000, while the four unadvertised ones averaged 5,600. Across all twelve titles, the six advertised books averaged 2,800 and the six unadvertised books averaged 4,100.

My publisher's marketing director concludes from the combined figures that advertising did not pay for itself and that I am slicing the data to protect a flattering conclusion. I conclude from the genre-by-genre figures that advertising helped every kind of book I publish and that the director is mixing two readerships with very different sales baselines, since the advertised titles happened to be concentrated where average sales are lower. A friend who teaches statistics tells me the pattern has a name, Simpson's paradox, but giving it a name has not moved either of us.

I am not asking you to adjudicate whether we should renew the advertising budget, though that decision is due before next season's launches. I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a factual disagreement that more data would settle, a disagreement about which level of aggregation is the right unit of analysis, or two different questions being answered under one heading — the effect of advertising on a particular book of mine versus its effect on the publisher's list as a whole? Is there an unmeasured variable doing the work, and would a randomized comparison among my own titles settle anything? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new comparison, a different way of framing the decision, a formal result — or should I conclude that no experiment would end the argument because we were never answering the same question?
