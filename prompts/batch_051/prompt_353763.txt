A government is considering the following mechanism for governing powerful AI systems. No system above a specified capability threshold may be deployed, extended to a new domain, or materially modified after deployment unless the Behavioral Objective and Search-Space Commission (BOSSC) has certified it. By statute, BOSSC may not inspect weights, training data, or design documents. It interacts with each system only as a black box — through text, actions in sandboxed environments, and ordinary API calls — and it may run as many adaptive or adversarial tests as it likes. From these interactions it must publish two findings. First, an objective specification: the objective function the system appears to be maximizing across contexts, given as a confidence distribution over a space of candidate objectives. Second, a search-space specification: the action space, state space, and hypothesis space over which the system appears to be searching, including their granularity, their constraints, and the abstractions the system seems to apply. A license is valid only inside the behavioral envelope predicted by these two findings; behavior outside that envelope voids the license and triggers immediate re-audit. The test batteries are constructed adaptively and kept secret from developers, who are barred from probing BOSSC's procedures. BOSSC's inference methodology is public, and its findings may be challenged by accredited third-party auditors, who are likewise limited to black-box interaction.

Treat this as a mechanism whose actual behavior is to be analyzed, not as a policy to be morally assessed in the abstract. Walk through how it would actually play out, and address at least the following. First, what does the scheme push developers to do during training, during certification, and after deployment, once the audit itself becomes part of the gradient signal shaping the system? Second, how would it be gamed — by developers, by deployers and users, by the systems themselves, and by the third-party auditors — and where would the earliest gaming appear? Third, given that behavior only weakly pins down an objective and a search space, what should we expect the published specifications to converge on, and how would optimizing systems against the audit rather than against an unfiltered environment shift the inferred objective and search space? Fourth, where does the mechanism fail first: in the secrecy of the tests, in the auditors' incentives, in the underdetermination of the inference itself, or somewhere else, and which of these failures are intrinsic to black-box objective inference rather than incidental to this particular institutional design? Fifth, which failure modes could be repaired by changes to the mechanism — for example, licensing search-space envelopes rather than objectives, paying BOSSC for accurate out-of-sample behavioral predictions, or using unrelated systems as adversarial auditors — and which cannot be fixed in principle? Finally, what second-order effects would the mechanism have on the broader ecosystem: which capabilities become cheaper or more expensive to ship, what safety-relevant research becomes more valuable, and how would the publicly stated objective and search-space specifications function as a new interface among developers, regulators, competitors, and the public?
