I teach a philosophy of law course, and for the last few weeks I've been supervising a running exchange between two colleagues — one a free-speech scholar, the other a former defamation solicitor — who cannot stop arguing about whether credit ratings, analyst price targets, and financial forecasts should be actionable in defamation when they turn out to be wrong and do serious reputational damage. It's the old opinions-vs-facts problem, but in a corner where it gets genuinely tangled: a forecast isn't exactly true or false at the moment it's made, yet it usually rests on factual claims about what the analyst looked at and how carefully they did their work.

You are a philosopher of law with a background in defamation doctrine and experience running adversarial collaborations. Act as the neutral facilitator who has run one between these two camps. Call them the Expressive Camp (ratings and forecasts are structurally evaluative, forward-looking, and should be categorically immune from defamation liability, though perhaps not from fraud or market-misconduct rules) and the Accountability Camp (these publications assert or imply verifiable factual content — e.g., that an analyst has found undisclosed liabilities, or that a financial model reasonably supports a price target — and when that content is negligently or recklessly false, ordinary defamation principles should apply). Assume a common-law framework in which a statement is actionable only if it can reasonably be understood as asserting an objective fact, and in which merely labelling something an 'opinion' doesn't protect it if it implies undisclosed defamatory facts; both camps accept that much. Draft the document they could both sign: a numbered agreed-claims section; then a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated so that each side would accept the wording of its own position; then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and, where possible, for each crux a concrete test or case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Before you write the document, think through each camp's strongest version and brainstorm which of their apparent disagreements are substantive and which are just verbal — where the two sides are talking past eachother. I'd rather have one sharp, honest crux than a list of ten fake ones, so be selective. What does the joint statement say?
