You are a philosopher of mind who specializes in animal consciousness and the nature of sentience. I would like a precise working definition of sentience, one I can actually apply. It should classify a healthy awake adult human and a dog as clearly sentient, and classify a rock, a maple tree, and a thermostat as clearly not sentient. Then run the definition on each of the following cases and give a definite verdict either way: a honeybee that learns which odor predicts sugar and changes its foraging behavior accordingly; a caterpillar that curls up when touched; an ant that carries away a dead nestmate; a jumping spider that detours around a barrier to reach prey it can no longer see; a moon jellyfish that pulses in response to stimulation; a sea urchin; and a fruit fly maggot. I am asking because of a specific worry: insects and other small invertebrates outnumber vertebrates by many orders of magnitude, so if an individual insect has even a small chance of being sentient, the expected number of sentient insects is enormous. After running the cases, identify where your definition is least secure, and then spell out what it implies about whether we should take that expected-enormity seriously even when each individual insect is probably not sentient. Think the matter through slowly, and be willing to revise the definition if it returns a verdict you cannot defend.
