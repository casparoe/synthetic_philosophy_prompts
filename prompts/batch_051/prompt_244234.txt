I'm putting together a small workshop for a mixed group — a few project managers, a couple jazz musicians, an ER nurse, some software developers — on whether planning is overrated. I want to open with a "which position on planning do you actually hold?" decision tree, and I need help building it. I don't want it to be one of those magazine quizzes where each answer maps transparently to a label ("are you a planner or a free spirit?"). I want the tree to sort people by how they actually answer a set of concrete cases, not by which label they like.

Here are the constraints. The tree should have around eight leaves, and it should be able to seperate at least these positions: (1) planning as constitutive of rational agency — you can't act over time at all without something plan-like; (2) planning as a useful but dispensable tool — plans are for coordination and memory, nothing more; (3) the "planning is essential, plans are disposable" view — the process matters, the artifact doesn't; (4) improvisation as a distinct skill that planning can actively undermine; (5) a satisficing view where planning is about setting stopping rules rather than optimizing; plus whatever other positions fall out of the logical space. I'd also like the tree to flag any combinations of answers that no sensible person occupies, and say whether that's because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. If two leaves can't be separated by any diagnostic case, say so instead of inventing a fake question.

For the questions themselves, use concrete cases like these (you can modify or add a few, but keep the flavor): a ten-day vacation with no reservations; a wedding toast you have to give in twenty minutes; a software project where the spec keeps changing; a jazz solo over changes you've never heard; a medical team running a code; and a friend who makes a detailed five-year career plan. Each question should offer two or three answers that force a choice about what planning is for, when it's worth doing, and what makes a plan good — without mentioning the labels above. The audience includes people who answer "it depends on the domain" to everything, so build that in: the tree should diagnose which pattern of domain-dependence lands a person in which leaf, and at what point "it depends" stops being a position and becomes a refusal to take the question seriously.
