For the past few years I have worked in public health policy, and I keep running into an asymmetry that I cannot justify in either direction. On one side, medical practice is strict about consent. If a doctor wants to prescribe a drug that carries, say, a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a serious stroke, the patient must be told what the risk is and may refuse for any reason or none; giving the drug without that discussion would be treated as a serious violation. On the other side, the same legal system routinely approves things like a highway widening, an expanded sewage-treatment plant, a diesel bus depot, or an industrial facility next to a residential area, after an environmental review that openly estimates the added air pollution will cause some number of additional asthma cases, heart attacks, or premature deaths among the people who live nearby. No individual resident is asked to consent. The affected people usually have no right to veto the project, and what they get instead is a public comment period, a regulatory limit, or perhaps mitigation money, not anyone's actual agreement to accept the risk.

I sometimes explain the asymmetry to myself by saying that a doctor inserts something into a specific person's body, while a highway merely moves people around and leaves pollution as a by-product. But that does not feel like a principled line: the people near the highway breathe the pollution, so something is entering their bodies too, just less directly and after being dispersed among many. What I want to know is what view of risk and consent this pattern presupposes, if it presupposes one at all. Does it presuppose that individual consent is owed only for deliberate, identifiable impositions on a specific body, and that dispersed, statistical, many-to-many risks are properly governed by collective decision instead? Or does it presuppose that some risks count as background features of social life and may be imposed so long as they fall below a regulatory threshold? Or that consent is required only when a risk is traded for a direct personal benefit, and not when the harm falls on some people while the benefit goes to others? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say whether the two halves of the pattern are coherent under that theory, and identify the differences between the two cases that do the real work. If the practice is philosophically underdetermined, explain the range of theories that could license it and what would have to change in either medical-consent law or environmental permitting if we insisted on a single theory across both. Please write the answer in American English.
