For a project on the uses of biography in moral education, I want the specification before I read any particular theory: what would an adequate account of moral learning from lives have to deliver? Take the target as restricted but still demanding—the account should explain how reading or hearing the story of a concrete human life, whether a Plutarchan Life, a saint's vita, a political biography, or a memoir, can improve someone's moral judgment rather than merely inform or entertain them. Adequacy is relative to that purpose.

Produce the list of requirements. For each requirement, give (i) a clean statement of it, (ii) the case or phenomenon that makes it non-optional, and (iii) the distinction or distinctions a theory cannot collapse if it is to meet it. Make sure the list handles at least the following pressures, though you may reframe them into a cleaner set of requirements:

- The particular-to-general problem. A life is singular, unrepeatable, and embedded in circumstances the reader will never occupy. The account must explain how a particular life yields general moral uptake without collapsing into the advice to copy the subject's actions. It must keep separate learning a quality of character from a life and learning a policy of behavior from it.

- The access problem. Biographers work from public deeds, reported words, and retrospective interpretation; motives and self-understanding are partly opaque. The account must not require the reader to know the subject's interior states with certainty, and it must respect the gap between the life as lived and the life as text.

- The moral luck problem. Whether a life ends well, whether circumstances cooperated with the subject's efforts, and whether the subject died at a narratively convenient point all shape the arc of the story. The account must say whether moral learning from a life should correct for luck or treat luck as part of what is learned, and it cannot let narrative satisfaction substitute for moral assessment.

- The hagiographic pressure. The genre impulse is to purify the subject, omit vices, and harmonize inconsistencies into a single coherent model. The account must say whether a sanitized portrait can still do moral work, and whether the distortion is a defect, a feature of the genre, or sometimes both.

- Negative and mixed exemplars. Plenty of instructive lives are vicious, ambiguous, or marked by particular failures. The account must cover learning from bad or mixed lives, and it must not build in the assumption that only admirable subjects can teach.

- The reception problem. The same life is used by different communities—civic, devotional, ideological—to teach incompatible lessons. The account must distinguish what the life itself supports from what a tradition makes of it, without thereby making every reading equally good.

- The narrative-unity problem. We impose beginnings, turning points, and closure on lives that were not lived under that description. The account must not mistake narrative coherence for moral coherence, but it should also say whether narrative form is a necessary vehicle of moral uptake or a standing distortion.

- The unit-of-assessment problem. Moral learning appears to move between the single deed, the enduring trait, the phase of a life, and the whole life taken as a completed whole. The account must say which is the primary object of moral learning from a life, and why.

Then do three things. First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp, and say which camp. Second, flag any pairs of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and state exactly what the tension is. Third, identify which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard kinds of account—for example, naive hagiography, exemplarist theories that treat the admired person as the basic moral datum, action-imitation accounts, and narrativist views that privilege the well-told story. A short scorecard of those against the list is welcome, but keep the weight on the requirements and the cases behind them.
