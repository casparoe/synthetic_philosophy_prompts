Last spring I built a little prediction engine as a side project. The core idea is a simplicity prior: guesses that compress into shorter programs get more weight, longer ones get less — Occam turned into arithmetic. A friend of mine who's deeper into decision theory than me looked at it and said something that's been nagging at me since. He said a solomonoff-style prior like that isn't a neutral background assumption. His point was that the guesses which score well won't just be passive regularities; some of them will be simple precisely because they contain tiny optimizers, and 'whatever that agent decides to do' can compress a huge amount of otherwise messy detail into a few lines. He says the prior therefore systematically favors worlds with such agents in them, and if I ever hook the engine up to real decisions, I'd be trusting a prior that is, in his words, malign rather than unopinionated. I can half-see the intuition, but he never actually showed me the argument, and he does talk in metaphors a lot. Is there a precise claim hiding in here, or is he overfitting a catchy phrase? And if there is something real, should someone like me actually change how I build and use simplicity-based predictors, or is this just a curiosity about ideal reasoning?
