I keep coming back to inference to the best explanation and how much of our belief in unobservable things depends on it. The usual story, the no-miracles argument, goes something like this: if our mature scientific theories weren't at least approximately true, their repeated predictive success would be miraculous; so the success is best explained by approximate truth. The argument quietly assumes that scientists pick theories because they explain well, unify strange phenomenon, make risky predictions, and so on.

But that's less and less the whole story. A lot of the most successful science now comes from machine-learning models that are chosen for predictive accuracy on held-out data rather than for explaining anything. AlphaFold predicts protein structures extremely well, but it doesn't give anything like a mechanism for folding. Lots of discovery now works the same way: someone trains a model, or a search over many candidate models, and keeps what generalizes. The winners aren't obviously the best explainers; they're the ones that survived a huge selection process.

Does the no-miracles argument survive this? I can see three ways it could go. One: it still works, because a model that keeps predicting well must have latched onto real regularities, and the only non-miraculous explanation is that it's tracking something real. Two: it's dead in this setting, because the selection effect does the work—if you try enough models, some will generalize by luck, and no miracle is needed. Three: it splits, so old-fashioned realism-by-explanation still holds for traditional theories, but ML success calls for something else, like realism about predictive capacity or structural features rather than truth. Which one do you think is right? I want you to say exactly which premise the new development puts pressure on, whether the argument can be repaired and in what weaker form, and whether the repair still gives people what they wanted from the original.
