For our purposes the case is this: a hitchhiker can only get a ride from a keenly perceptive driver if the driver is convinced that the hitchhiker genuinely intends to pay once he is safe, but the hitchhiker knows he has no such intention and that, left to his own devices, he never will. The defender's position is that the hitchhiker can and rationally should bring it about that he acquires that intention — by a drug, a precommitment, or whatever intervention makes the intention genuine — because a disposition that will later act against his immediate interests can still be the rational thing to install now, and this counts as rational self-management rather than self-deception, a mistake, or a change of identity.

Here is my opening move. Your telling of the story seems to require the hitchhiker to be two people: the cool planner who installs an intention because it will be read as sincere, and the later passenger who acts from that intention as his own. What connects them? If it is one continuing self, then the roadside change is just an ordinary change of mind, and you have lost the special category of rational irrationality. If it is not the same self in the relevant sense, then the planner has merely used a future person as a means to a ride — and I do not make myself irrational when I trick a stranger. One agent or two? And either way, where is the rational irrationality?
