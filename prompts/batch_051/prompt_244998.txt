Upper-level undergraduate philosophy of language — final exam, 90 minutes. Answer one essay question, about 700 words. Total: 20 marks.

Question. A search committee asks a referee, known to be candid, whether a candidate is a strong researcher. The referee writes:

(1) "She keeps every office hour, returns exams within a week, and has never missed a department meeting."

For a second candidate, another referee writes:

(2) "Her research is unorthodox, but it is rigorous."

For each of (1) and (2), state what the referee communicates without literally saying it, and explain how a Gricean hearer would recover it, naming any maxims that are exploited. Classify each case as a conversational implicature, a conventional implicature, or neither, and defend the classification using cancellability and detachability (and, where relevant, calculability). Finally, address this worry about (1): the committee's inference seems to be prompted by what the referee omitted rather than by the literal content of what she wrote. Explain whether Grice's distinction between what is said and what is implicated is by itself enough to make sense of this, and if not, what must be added.

Model answer. In (1) the referee says only that the candidate is conscientious and collegial. Because the committee needs an evaluation of the research, and the referee is cooperative, the committee reasons that the referee cannot truthfully say anything positive about the research and intends the committee to recognize this. The implicature is roughly that the candidate's research is weak, or at least that there is nothing positive the referee is willing to say about it; its exact content is indeterminate. The referee flouts the maxim requiring one to be as informative as the exchange requires, and the maxim requiring relevance, so this is a particularized conversational implicature. It is cancellable (the referee could add "though I mean no comment on her research") and non-detachable (any wording with the same literal content in the same context would carry the same implicature). It is calculable from the Cooperative Principle.

In (2) "but" has the same truth conditions as "and" — both conjuncts are asserted — yet it conventionally signals a contrast or unexpectedness: unorthodox work is not expected to be rigorous. This contrast is not derived from any maxim; it is part of the encoded meaning of "but." It is non-cancellable (adding "though I mean no contrast" yields oddity) and detachable (substituting "and" removes the contrast while preserving what is said). It is not calculable from the Cooperative Principle. So (1) is a conversational implicature and (2) is a conventional implicature.

The said/implicated distinction is not by itself enough for (1). What makes the omission communicative is the maxims together with the assumption that the referee is cooperative and with mutual knowledge about the practice of reference letters — what the committee asked and what the referee could have said. Moreover, the committee's inference counts as speaker meaning only if the referee intended the committee to draw it and intended that intention to be recognized. So the distinction must be supplemented by reflexive communicative intention and by background mutual knowledge.

Marking scheme (20 marks).

- (4) Identifies the implicature in (1) and traces it to the flouting of the Quantity and Relation maxims via the Cooperative Principle, rather than treating it as an entailment or as a lie. Error: treating (1) as part of what is said, or claiming the referee merely violates the maxims.
- (4) Identifies the contrast in (2) as a conventional implicature and notes that "but" shares truth conditions with "and." Error: classifying (2) as a conversational implicature or as part of what is said.
- (4) Correctly applies the tests: (1) cancellable and non-detachable; (2) non-cancellable and detachable; and draws the right classification. Error: reversing the tests or asserting that all implicatures are cancellable.
- (4) Explains that the inference in (1) is speaker meaning only if the referee has the right reflexive intention. Error: identifying speaker meaning with whatever the committee happens to infer.
- (4) Argues that the omission in (1) is made communicative by the maxims and by mutual background knowledge about reference letters, not by the said/implicated distinction alone. Error: saying the distinction is sufficient and ignoring context.
