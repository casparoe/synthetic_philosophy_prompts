I am a novelist, not a trained philosopher, and I am reading around metaethics because a character in my novel is writing a dissertation defending an expressivist account of moral language. A book for general readers gave me the following summary of the view and its central problem:

“Expressivism is the view that moral language does not describe how the world is. To say that lying is wrong is not to attribute a property of wrongness to lying; it is to express a practical attitude of approval or disapproval toward lying, rather as booing expresses dislike without reporting on one’s own psychology. The Frege–Geach problem is the famous difficulty this creates for moral language when it appears inside larger sentences. Consider the argument: if lying is wrong, then getting one’s brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore getting one’s brother to lie is wrong. This argument is plainly valid. But the expressivist seems unable to explain why it is valid. The sentence ‘lying is wrong’ occurs once inside the antecedent of the conditional, where it is not asserted, and once as an asserted premise. In the premise, the speaker expresses disapproval of lying; in the conditional, no such attitude is expressed, because the speaker is only entertaining the thought that lying is wrong. Since the two occurrences carry no common descriptive content, the rules of inference have nothing to operate on, and the argument collapses into a sequence of attitude-expressions with no logical connection between them. Expressivists have proposed various repairs, but the standard lesson is that the Frege–Geach problem exposes a fatal flaw: moral language must after all have a descriptive, belief-like core.”

I cannot tell whether this is a fair account, an overly tidy condensation, or a subtle straw man, and I would rather not build my character’s intellectual world on a misunderstanding. Could you go through it and separate what a careful expressivist would accept from what they would reject or restate? In particular, I would like to know whether the Frege–Geach problem is better understood as a refutation of expressivism or as a standing challenge that subsequent expressivists have tried to answer without abandoning the view.
