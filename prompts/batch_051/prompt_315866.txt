Me and my friend got into it last night after he said the marsh behind his house getting drained for a new housing development might actually be a good thing. We went back and forth for a couple hours, and he eventually laid out this argument, numbered and everything:

1. Most wild animals live lives that are, on balance, painful — they starve, freeze, get eaten alive, die of disease, and the good parts don't make up for any of that.
2. Destroying a habitat doesn't harm the animals there, because mostly it just means those animals never get born in the first place. You can't harm someone by preventing them from existing.
3. If a life would have been, on balance, painful, then it's better that the life never starts.
4. So paving over a marsh prevents a lot of painful lives from starting, which lowers the total amount of suffering in the world.
5. But the same logic applies to every marsh, forest, and grassland, so we should think a world with much less habitat — and maybe no wild animals at all — is a better world.

He was weirdly calm about step 5. He said I only resist it because I like the idea of wild nature, not because the argument fails.

And the thing is, I cant find the bad step. Step 3 sounds obviously right to me. I don't actually know whether step 1 is true for voles and frogs, but it seems plausible enough, and he says theres plenty of evidence for it. But the conclusion that the best state of the earth is a big parking lot can't be right. Or if it is right, I want to know what else I'd have to accept.

So where exactly does this break? Which premise or step should I reject, and what does it cost me? Please don't just tell me the conclusion is ugly — I already know that. I want the diagnosis.
