I'm retired now after a career in organic chemistry, and I've gotten into philosophy late in life, mostly through a small reading group that meets at the library. Lately the golden rule keeps getting pulled out to end arguments, and I have a nagging sense it's doing less work than people think. Last week we were arguing about a case where a patient has told his doctor in advance that he would rather not be told if he has a terminal diagnosis, and the doctor then discovers exactly that. One of the group said the golden rule settles it: treat others as you would want to be treated, and you would want to be told. That shut the discussion down, but I dont think it settled anything. In the patient's place I would want to be told, sure, but I can just as easily imagine someone using the same rule to say you must not tell, because they would rather not be told if they were the patient.

What I'd love is for someone to take that sentence apart the way I used to take apart a reaction mechanism. What distinct precise claims could "do unto others as you would have them do unto you" be making? Maybe it's a rule about consulting your own preferences, or about universalizing your maxim, or about respecting others' preferences, or just a rough heuristic with no sharp edge at all. For each reading: is it trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false? Is it a decision procedure or more of a consistency check on self-serving reasoning? Which readings would actually support my friend's conclusion in the doctor case, and which would block it? And how do the standard problem cases—the masochist, the person with unusual tastes, the parent and the child, people in very different circumstances—attach to specific readings rather than to the rule as a vague whole? Finally, if the rule is broken, is there a fixed version actually worth keeping, or does something like "treat others as they want to be treated" just inherit all the same problems? I'd rather have the readings laid out as a menu than one tidy answer.
