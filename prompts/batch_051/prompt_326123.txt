Friends of mine have been arguing about what they actually owe each other in small matters, and I've started to suspect that ordinary social practice has a more worked-out position on this than any of them. Consider the relevant practices: "Dinner at seven" is treated as a promise even when nothing was explicitly undertaken, and a one-off lateness is forgiven almost automatically if the person has texted ahead or arrives apologetic. Chronic lateness, by contrast, is penalized in a way that looks less like punishment for discrete broken promises and more like a downgrade in the person's general standing as someone you can count on. Early arrival is not praised the way lateness is blamed. "I'll try to make it" is understood to create less obligation than "I'll be there," and "I'll be there" creates less obligation than a written RSVP to a wedding, even though all three are just words. And the person who fails to show because of something trivial is judged harshly, while the person who fails for a reason entirely outside their control is judged barely at all.

My question is what theory of promising—or of what small promises are for—this whole pattern presupposes. Does it presuppose that a promise's binding force comes from the expectations it plants, from a social convention, from an act of self-imposed obligation, from the relationship it expresses and maintains, or from something else? Does the practice require one of these to be true, or is it compatible with several of them but more naturally read one way? In particular, can a view on which a promise is a discrete, self-contained obligation explain why the practice cares so much more about a person's overall reliability than about any individual breach, or does the practice presuppose something more like a standing trait of character? And is the practice internally consistent? For example, is treating the rushed apology-text as significantly less blameworthy than silence consistent with the view that the obligation was already fully binding from the moment the promise was made?

Please take a definite stand rather than just laying out the options. Reconstruct what the practice strictly presupposes, distinguish that from what it merely fits well with, and say which theory of promissory obligation best accommodates the whole pattern and why. Then tell me what would actually change in how we hold each other to small promises if that theory were made explicit and followed through consistently.
