The topic pressed itself on me recently, when I found myself on a patient-safety committee that had to decide how to describe a rare complication of a new procedure — no base rate, no settled mechanism, only a handful of case reports. I wanted to say it was a possibility without committing to a probability, and I could not articulate what I meant. Reflection has since pushed me toward a reductive view. I now hold that possibility talk is nothing over and above probability talk: to say something could happen is simply to say it carries positive probability under some relevant probability — objective chance, rational credence, or a frequency in an appropriate reference class — and any distinction between possibilities is ultimately a distinction in how much weight they receive.

I want to know what would count against this view, not merely what complicates it. In particular, what should I make of the fact that a perfectly ordinary uniform distribution over a continuum assigns probability zero to each exact outcome even though the outcome appears possible, as when a spinner can land on this very point? Is that a decisive counterexample, or merely an artifact of idealization that a defender can absorb by requiring regular probabilities — probabilities that assign zero only to the impossible — and if the price of that move is infinitesimal-valued probabilities, is the move explanatory or ad hoc? Equally important is the converse: can someone assign positive probability to something impossible, such as a mathematical falsehood they do not recognize as false, and would that show that probability and possibility come apart in the opposite direction? I would also like an accounting of the ways both terms split — chance versus credence versus frequency, and logical versus nomological versus epistemic possibility — and whether the view survives only by pairing senses opportunistically. And I want to know whether probability in fact presupposes an antecedent space of possibilities, in which case the reduction runs the wrong way, and whether there are cases of possibility with no coherent probability over them at all, such as uncertainty about the outcome space itself.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish the ones a committed reductionist would concede as genuinely counting against the view from those they would dismiss as category mistakes, and say whether the view, once fortified, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether, for a view of this kind, that would be a defect. I would personally find it most persuasive to be shown a single case in which possibility and probability point in different directions within one sense of each term, rather than a case that shifts senses mid-argument.
