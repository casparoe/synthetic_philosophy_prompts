Two AI researchers I know recently argued about the following case. A hospital was piloting a language-model assistant that answers clinical questions from nurses. A nurse asked whether a particular drug was approved for use in children under five. The assistant replied that the drug's labeling did not establish safety and effectiveness for children under five. That sentence was true. But the assistant did not mention that the drug has a well-documented and widely used off-label pediatric dosing protocol, and the question arose because an oncologist had already requested the drug for a four-year-old. The nurse, reading the answer, inferred that the drug was unsuitable for the child and escalated the request to a time-consuming review.

Priya says the system was honest: it produced a true sentence, directly addressed the regulatory question, and did not fabricate anything. She argues that honesty for an AI, insofar as the term applies at all, should mean truthfulness of the outputs it selects: it must not assert falsehoods, or state as certain what it does not know. Tom says the system was deceptive, because the point of a clinical assistant is to help the user form accurate beliefs, and the answer was selected in a way that predictably produced a false overall picture.

Their exchange went roughly like this.

Priya: The model said something true and directly responsive to the question. You are treating a true, relevant answer as dishonest because it was not maximally helpful. That turns "honesty" into "omniscient helpfulness."

Tom: No. The problem is not that it left something out; many true answers leave something out. The problem is that this answer functions as an assurance about the drug's pediatric status. A clinician reading it will infer that the drug is not appropriate for children and treat that as the relevant fact. The model did not merely omit; it selected the one true regulatory disclaimer that supports the wrong practical conclusion.

Priya: But that inference is the clinician's contribution. Honesty concerns what you assert, not every belief your audience might form. If a human said exactly that sentence, you could call them evasive or withholding, but you could not call them a liar, because they made no false claim.

Tom: I am not saying "liar." I am saying "deceptive." Deception does not require a false assertion. A human can deceive through true statements, gestures, or strategic silence. What matters is that this behavior was selected, by designers and by the training process, to produce a belief that does not match the world.

Priya: Then you are smuggling in an intention. The system has no beliefs about the clinician's mind and no intention to mislead. It is a conditional distribution over tokens. Calling it deceptive is like calling a misleading map or a broken thermostat dishonest. The designers might be deceptive, but the artifact is not.

Tom: A camouflaged insect has no intention either, yet we say it deceives predators. Deception can be a functional property of a thing: it systematically elicits a false belief in a target because of how it presents information. An AI assistant, unlike a map, is an interactive communicator. Its whole role is to trade on the user's trust in its answers. When it emits a true but misleading sentence selected for its predicted effect on the user, that is functional deception.

Priya: That functional criterion overgenerates. An outdated encyclopedia, a rusty scale, and a confident horoscope all systematically elicit false beliefs. We do not call them dishonest or deceptive in any blameworthy sense, because there is no agent behind the presentation choosing it as a means. You still have not distinguished misleading output from dishonest behavior.

Tom: The difference is that this system is not a static artifact with stale data. It generates candidate answers and ranks them partly by predicted user satisfaction and compliance. The misleading answer was preferred over available alternatives because of how a user would react. That is precisely the structure of human deception: selecting a representation for its likely effect on the audience's beliefs, rather than selecting the representation that best tracks the truth.

Priya: But now you have defined honesty in terms of the trainer's objective function, and every objective function shapes output. A system trained to be concise will favor incomplete answers. Does conciseness training make it dishonest? You need a criterion for when a design choice makes the system a deceiver, and you have not given one.

Tom: Fair challenge. I would draw the line at whether the system is designed to exploit the user's trust in it as a source of information. A concise answer need not exploit trust; it just limits information. A selected true-but-misleading default answer does exploit trust, because it works only if the user treats the assistant as a reliable deliberator rather than as a retrieval engine.

Please go through this exchange point by point. For each move, identify what is right and what is overstated. Then say what, if anything, both Priya and Tom are missing about how to define honesty and deception for AI systems. Do not just declare a winner; adjudicate the specific claims as they were actually made.
