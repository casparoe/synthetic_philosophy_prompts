In a lecture on infinite regress arguments, my philosophy professor summarized the distinction between vicious and benign regresses roughly like this: "A regress is vicious when each step passes the explanatory buck to the next step, so that the original item never gets explained or justified. That is exactly what happens in the epistemic regress: if every justified belief needs another justified belief as its reason, then the chain either stops or goes on forever, and if it goes on forever then no belief is ever actually justified, because at every point the justifying reason is still deferred to something later. By contrast, a regress is benign when the later items do not have to be in place before the earlier ones obtain, as with truth: if p is true then it is true that p is true, and so on, but p's truth does not wait on all the rest. So infinitism, the view that an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons can justify a belief, is just a confusion: it mistakes a benign regress for a way of satisfying the original demand for a reason."

I found this intuitive, but I later learned that infinitism is held by contemporary epistemologists, and I now suspect the professor's final step moves too quickly from "the chain does not end" to "no belief is justified." Is this a fair summary of the vicious/benign distinction and of the case against infinitism? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what an infinitist would reject, and say whether the finitist argument survives those corrections.
