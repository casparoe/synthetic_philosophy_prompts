You are an expert on the ethics and philosophy of language preservation; think carefully and do not settle on a verdict too quickly. I sit on the grants committee of a foundation whose mandate prioritizes documenting languages at immediate risk of disappearing, but whose rules require that fieldwork proceed with genuine community consent. Before next week's meeting I must circulate a short written opinion on a proposal to build a complete audio, video, and grammatical archive of a dying language isolate—a language with no known living relatives—now spoken fluently by only seven people. The five elderly fluent speakers support the project and want their knowledge recorded before they die. The two youngest fluent speakers, a middle-aged brother and sister, have written asking us not to fund it: they argue that the community has long accepted the language's decline, that the language belongs to their family's intimate life, and that outside researchers archiving it would turn their grief into a specimen. The applicants reply that the archive is urgent, that the language encodes ecological and conceptual distinctions found nowhere else, and that access to the archived materials can be sealed and later governed by the community. Before settling on a recommendation, briefly set out the two or three strongest considerations on each side. Then draft my written opinion: recommend concretely whether to fund, reject, or fund with conditions (specify the conditions and any time frame), engage honestly with the strongest argument on the losing side, and close by identifying which consideration, if it turned out otherwise, would most likely change the recommendation and where your reasoning is most fragile.
