Consider the boundary problem — the question of who belongs to the demos, meaning who is entitled to participate in a polity's binding collective decisions. Four criteria recur in debates about this. The all-affected principle says standing goes to everyone whose interests are substantially affected by the decision. The all-subjected principle says standing goes to everyone who is subject to the polity's coercive enforcement of the decision. The residency principle says standing goes to those whose lives are organized around the polity's territory and institutions over time, whether or not the immediate decision touches them. The social-membership principle says standing goes to those whom the other members tacitly treat as one of them: people with whom they share the daily, informal burdens and expectations of membership.

Now take a hypothetical city-state scheduled to hold a binding referendum on secession from a larger federation, where the outcome would redraw borders and alter the legal status of many people outside the city. Its charter enfranchises only current legal residents who have lived in the city for at least five years. Four groups claim standing nevertheless. First, former residents who moved abroad two years ago but retain property in the city and remain liable for city property taxes. Second, cross-border workers who commute in daily, earn most of their income in the city, and use its roads, transit, and emergency services, but live in the neighboring state. Third, long-settled irregular migrants who have lived and worked in the city for a decade, send their children to its schools, and are subject to its laws, but lack legal residence status. Fourth, a diaspora descended from the city's founders, most of whom have never lived there, who hold no property and pay no tax, but whose cultural and family identities are tied to the city.

Go through each group against each criterion, and say where the criteria underdetermine the verdict or pull in opposite directions. Then address the larger question: is the five-year residency rule a defensible operationalization of one of these principles, and would this case show that at least one of the four criteria must be revised or abandoned? In particular, ask whether any single criterion can handle all four groups without becoming either absurdly overinclusive or question-begging.
