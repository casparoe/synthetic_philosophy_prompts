You are an expert in early Chinese political philosophy, especially Han Feizi's Legalism and the Confucian appeal to virtue it opposed, and in modern thinking about incentives and intrinsic motivation. Please think slowly; begin by reconstructing the strongest version of each side's position, then adjudicate the actual claims one by one rather than splitting the difference.

Two members of my school's philosophy club, Mina and Jonah, recently got stuck in an argument about a proposal to replace our honour code with a demerit-and-incentive system: severe penalties for cheating, plus modest privileges for reporting it. Mina supported the proposal; Jonah opposed it. I was present the whole time and never managed to work out who was right. I will report the exchange as accurately as I can.

Mina began by saying that the school should stop trying to make everyone virtuous and instead design rules that work on people as they actually are. She cited Han Feizi: on her reading, he did not claim that people are all selfish, only that a ruler cannot tell which subjects or officials are genuinely good and should not have to rely on there being enough of them. Clear laws with reliable rewards and punishments make behaviour predictable even from mediocre or bad people, whereas a virtue-based order works only if you happen to have virtuous people in the right places, which cannot be guaranteed.

Jonah answered that this treats a school as though it were a state of strangers. A school is precisely the kind of community that can cultivate shared norms, so installing a system of surveillance and punishment teaches students that the only reason not to cheat is fear of being caught. He claimed the system would fail the moment monitoring lapsed, and that it would crowd out the very honesty it says it does not need.

Mina said he was ignoring the point. Han Feizi's argument, she insisted, is not that people are incapable of goodness but that good people are too scarce and too hard to identify for an institution to run on them. An honour code that depends on each teacher's moral discretion is rule by guesswork. A fixed schedule of offences and penalties protects students from arbitrary judgement, which is an independent reason to prefer it.

Jonah then raised the familiar problem of watching the watchers. If incentives do all the work, the people who draft and enforce the rules must themselves be kept honest by incentives, and then another layer must watch them, and so on. At some point, he argued, someone has to care about the rules for their own sake, or the structure has no bottom. Han Feizi's scheme therefore smuggles in the virtue it claims to replace.

Mina replied that this confuses the competence of the designer with the fuel of the system. An engine needs a skilled engineer to build it, but it does not run on the engineer's personal goodness; it runs on the regularities of the machinery. A Legalist-style school policy is meant to keep the institution working on its worst day, when the admirable people are absent.

Jonah answered that a school is not merely an engine, because its rules shape the students who pass through it. Even if Mina is right about Han Feizi's statecraft, the analogy with a school fails exactly where she needs it: a school is partly in the business of forming motivation, not only producing compliance. A policy that turns honesty into a transaction may leave students less honest in the long run, which is a failure even by the school's own purposes.

Neither moved. I do not want a generic response that both sides have a point. Please adjudicate their actual claims point by point: whether Mina has Han Feizi right or is softening him; whether Jonah's watching-the-watchers objection lands or overreaches; whether and where the state-to-school analogy breaks; and what, if anything, both are overlooking. Please also tell me which step in your reasoning is least secure and which consideration would, if it were borne out, change your verdict. Answer in British English.
