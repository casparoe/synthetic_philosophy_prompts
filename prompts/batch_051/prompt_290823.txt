Write a dialogue of exactly five exchanges between two equally sophisticated decision theorists, Aditi and Ben, about how to size bets in a repeated favorable gamble. The gamble works as follows. A starting bankroll is wagered over discrete rounds; in each round an even-money bet is offered on an event with probability p = 0.6. The agent stakes a fraction f of the current bankroll, with 0 ≤ f ≤ 1, reinvesting all winnings and withdrawing nothing; after the round the bankroll is multiplied by (1 + f) on a win and by (1 − f) on a loss, and a bankroll of zero is absorbing. Aditi defends geometric rationality: stake f = 2p − 1 = 0.2 each round, because this maximizes the expected logarithm of the bankroll, which is the same as maximizing the long-run growth rate, and it avoids ruin. Ben defends expected-utility orthodoxy: stake whatever fraction maximizes the expectation of one’s utility function over final wealth; the logarithm has no objective privilege, so a risk-neutral agent should maximize expected bankroll and therefore stake the largest permitted fraction, while smaller bets express a particular risk taste rather than a law of compounding.

Make the dialogue a real argument rather than a recitation of positions. Each speaker must respond to the point the other just made. At minimum, Ben must press Aditi on (i) whether choosing the logarithm is not just adopting one arbitrary utility function in disguise, and (ii) why a single realized trajectory should matter more to a real agent than the ensemble of possible trajectories that an expectation averages over. Aditi must press Ben on (i) what it means to follow a policy whose ruinous outcomes have probability approaching one even as its arithmetic expected wealth grows, and (ii) why the arithmetic mean is the relevant summary when returns compound multiplicatively across rounds. Have one of them concede at least one point and let the disagreement narrow, so that by the final exchange they identify the precise residual question that would settle the rest. Do not resolve the argument by fiat.

Format: a numbered list of exactly five exchanges, with no introduction or conclusion. In odd-numbered exchanges Aditi speaks first and Ben replies; in even-numbered exchanges Ben speaks first and Aditi replies. Label each speech with the speaker’s name. Each speech is one or two sentences.
