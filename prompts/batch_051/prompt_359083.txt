A city council will soon vote on two proposals concerning the same historical figure: a 1920s public-health reformer who founded the city's children's hospital and who also led the eugenics campaign that sterilized thousands of its residents. The first proposal is to erect a new bronze statue of him in a public square; the second is to remove his name from the hospital. Many of the same people who oppose erecting the statue also oppose renaming the hospital. Their stated reason is that a new statue would honor the man, while the old name merely records a good deed and the origin of a good institution. But the proposed statue's plaque was to mention only the hospital and his free clinics, not the sterilizations, so both the statue and the name can equally be described as commemorating the deed rather than the man. Is there a principle that actually generates both votes — the distinction between honoring a person and honoring an action, the difference between creating a new honor and leaving an old one undisturbed, the relative prominence of a figure cast in bronze over lettering on a sign — or only a preference for keeping whatever is familiar? Give the strongest candidate principle you can, test it against a case in which the honored deed and the wrongdoing are the same act (a fortune, an institution, or a discovery that is itself the source of the harm), and then say whether the asymmetry between the proposed statue and the existing name should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
