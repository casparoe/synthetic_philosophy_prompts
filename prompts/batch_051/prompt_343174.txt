I keep coming back to the old shallow pond case: you walk past a pond, a kid is drowning, and you could wade in and save him, but it would ruin your shoes and make you late for work. Everyone has the intuition you'd be awful to just walk on. Now change one thing. Suppose the pond is on your commute and the reason kids keep ending up in there is a broken fence, so every couple months another kid falls in. This morning there's a kid in the water, and there's also a pile of fence-mending supplies sitting by the path that would let you fix the fence for good, or at least for a year or two. You can't do both: if you stop to fix the fence, the kid in the water drowns; if you wade in and save the kid, the fence stays broke. Going by how often kids fall in, fixing the fence would probably prevent two or three more drownings before next summer. What does the standard pond argument commit you to here — saving the kid in front of you, fixing the fence, or does the original case just not decide it? If it doesn't decide it, what extra assumption is doing the work, and does it matter that the future kids are nameless and the one in front of you isn't?
