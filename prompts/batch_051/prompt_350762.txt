You serve on the Academic Regulations Committee of a university whose undergraduate curriculum has for nearly a century required every student to pass a course in oral disputation: a term-length course built around formal public debates on set theses, conducted in a distinctive format before faculty examiners. The requirement predates the modern majors system, consumes scarce teaching resources, and is the most common reason students cite when transferring or appealing their progress. The archives contain almost nothing about why the founders imposed it; the committee's best historians can recover only that it was adopted after an acrimonious governance split, and no one can now state its rationale with confidence.

The Committee must recommend to the Senate whether to retain, reform, or abolish the requirement. One faction argues that abolishing an unexplained long-standing requirement would violate Chesterton's fence: before pulling down a fence one must understand why it was put there. Another faction replies that when a diligent search turns up no rationale, that absence is itself a reason to remove the requirement, and that refusing to do so is status quo bias dressed up as epistemic humility.

Draft the memo. It should give a clear recommendation and the reasoning for it; explain precisely what Chesterton's fence does and does not require when the original rationale is unrecoverable; distinguish the fence from a broader Burkean reverence for inherited institutions; say whether and how reversibility should affect the decision; and give an honest, non-caricatured treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. End with a general test the Senate could apply in future cases to decide when an old requirement whose rationale has been lost may be removed.
