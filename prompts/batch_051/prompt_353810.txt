I'm the communications director at a mid-size environmental nonprofit, and I'm trying to build a standard my writers can actually use before anything goes out. We send maybe six or seven fundraising and advocacy emails a month, plus donation landing pages and occasional direct mail, and the board keeps flagging individual pieces as manipulative. I honestly can't always tell where the line is, and I dont think the writers can either. The stakes are real for us: one badly worded appeal can end up in a local news story or get a donor complaining to the board, and I have to sign off on everything. The writers are not philosophers, and I'm not either, and they have maybe 15 to 20 minutes per piece to run whatever I give them, so it has to be a checklist or decision procedure, not a seminar.

What I want is a reusable procedure for deciding whether a persuasive message (an email, a landing page, a phone script) is still legitimate persuasion or has crossed into manipulation, grounded in the actual philosophical work on manipulation vs. legitimate persuasion, as best you can simplify it for this setting. Give me the questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because no rule settles it. Then run it on two or three cases: (1) a donation landing page that opens with a picture of a specific suffering animal and says "Donate in the next 24 hours to have your gift doubled," with the recurring-donation box already ticked; (2) an advocacy email that says "97% of our members already support this bill — add your name to show the statehouse"; (3) a direct mail piece that includes free address labels and a note saying "we sent you these; can you send us $12?" Include at least one case where the procedure tells me I can't run something I'd otherwise have sent, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do. Also, separate what is actually settled in the philosophy of manipulation from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — dont present a contested view as the consensus, because I'll have to defend this to my board and I need to know which parts are genuinely up for judgment.
