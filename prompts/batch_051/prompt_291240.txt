I cover science and ideas for a living, so I keep running into the same puzzle: when two smart people look at the same evidence and still disagree, is at least one of them being irrational, or is that just what it costs to have different starting points? I've been working with a rough rule: disagreement is rational only if it traces back to different evidence or different information somewhere in their histories; once everything is on the table and everyone's reasoning is out in the open, leftover disagreement means somebody made a mistake. But my gut verdicts only partly line up with that rule.

Here are the cases I keep score on. Two climate modelers run the same data and end up at 30% vs 70% chance of a severe heat event, and they've swapped code and reasoning. I think one of them is wrong, though I cant say which. A pharmacologist and a grad student read the same vaccine-safety study; the grad student's sister had a bad reaction to a different drug, so she stays more skeptical. That doesn't strike me as unreasonable. Two macroeconomists with the same reports spend a day arguing about recession odds, 70% vs 30%, and by the end each can predict the other's objections and neither has moved. That bugs me, but I can't say exactly why one of them is being irrational. Two cosmologists agree on every relevant observation about the fine-tuning of the universe and have swapped arguments for years; one says design, one says selection effects. I want to call that a real difference of judgment, not an error.

What principle, if any, gets all four — something like the common prior assumption or a patched-up version — and which of my verdicts has to go? And what does the best version say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: two reporters in my newsroom have the same leaked documents, both covered this beat for a decade, one thinks they prove a corruption scandal and the other thinks they're inconclusive, and after a week of arguing neither has moved. Is one of them making a mistake?
