Suppose a country adopts a scheme in which any parent over 68 whose assessed income falls below a subsistence threshold may file with a local Family Maintenance Board naming one or more adult children. Before any hearing, the Board must attempt mandatory facilitated conciliation; if that fails, the Board may issue a support order, but only after weighing documentary and testimonial evidence from both sides. Contribution amounts are computed as a graduated fraction of each child's disposable income above a floor, reduced by credits for documented unpaid care given to the parent in the preceding decade, for support already provided to younger or disabled siblings, or for a formal finding that the parent substantially abandoned, abused, or neglected the child. Orders last up to five years and may be renewed. Enforcement is deliberately soft: non-payment is not a criminal offense, but it may be reported to credit agencies and it lowers the child's priority for certain public benefits. The scheme's official rationale is that filial support need not be left to private affection, but should be specified as a concrete obligation that the state can administer without intruding into family feeling.

Analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a moral proposal. What incentives does it create for parents and for adult children, both before a claim is filed and after one is resolved? Who would be most likely to use it, and which of its features would most shape that selection? Pay particular attention to the credits for care and for parental failure: do they function as neutral fact-finding, or as leverage in family negotiations, and how might they be gamed? Then evaluate the design against the friendship model of filial duties, which holds that adult children owe their parents not a quasi-contractual debt, but whatever an ongoing affectionate relationship would require. Where does the institution fit that model, where does it strain against it, and could its failure modes be fixed without abandoning the scheme?
