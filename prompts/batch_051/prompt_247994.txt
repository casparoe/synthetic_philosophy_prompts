Suppose the main AI labs got together and set up a scheme to neutralize the competitiveness constraint — the thing where a lab that pays the alignment tax (shipping a slower, dumber, or more expensive system in exchange for safety) loses market share to one that doesn't. Call it the Alignment Offset Club. Each quarter, every member lab has to describe, under audit, the most capable model it could deploy if it ignored safety, and the auditor scores that model on a published benchmark suite and estimates the revenue per capability point it would earn. The lab also submits what it actually shipped. The auditor measures the capability gap between the two, converts the gap to dollars with a fixed public formula, and the lab pays that amount into a shared pot. The pot is then paid back out to members in proportion to the capability they gave up, so a lab that ships a much weaker but safer system than it could have gets back more than it paid, while a lab that ships close to it's unsafe frontier ends up a net contributor. The auditors see everything but only publish aggregate numbers.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. Give me your five-year prediction for how it actually behaves. Start with how the definitions get gamed: who understates their unsafe frontier, who overstates what they shipped, and how the benchmark suite gets gamed until it stops meaning what it used to. Then tell me whether the scheme actually changes the real safety-versus-capability decision, or just moves the pressure into the auditing and accounting layer. Then do the edges: new labs outside the club, open-weight releases that don't earn revenue per point, members threatening to leave, and what happens the first time the pot gets big enough to matter. And be explicit about which links in your chain are robust and which are guesses.
