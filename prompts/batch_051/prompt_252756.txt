After forty years in physical chemistry I've started reading philsophy of mathematics, and there's one thing I can't get past. The constructivists and the classical mathematicians have been at it for a century, but as far as I can tell they agree on all the formal facts: classical logic is consistent, intuitionistic logic is a proper part of it, and there are translations that have embeded classical arithmetic into intuitionistic arithmetic by sticking a double negation in the right places. Yet nobody moves. The constructivist says a proof of not-not-P isn't a proof of P — a proof that there must be a number with some property because assuming there isn't one leads to contradiction still doesn't hand you the number. The classicist says that's just a psychological scruple about an existence claim whose meaning is perfectly clear. In chemistry, when two groups agreed on the data and still disagreed, it usually meant they were measuring different quantities, and I keep wondering whether that's what's going on here.

My working theory is that this isn't a mathematical disagreement at all but a disagreement about what a proof is for — a certificate you could in principle run, versus a guarantee that no counterexample can exist. Is that right, or is there a real formal or empirical question hiding underneath? I don't want a survey of the history; I want a diagnosis of why the argument is stuck and what kind of disagreement it really is. And given your diagnosis, is there anything — a theorem, a distinction, a change of question — that could actually end it, or should I expect the standoff to be permanent?
