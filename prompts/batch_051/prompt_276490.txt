Every few years I try to get clear on the old units of selection fight and every time I come away feeling like the two sides arent actually contradicting each other—they're just insisting on different things while using the same labels. The gene's-eye people (Dawkins, Williams, the selfish gene crowd) say selection really acts on genes; organisms and groups are just vehicles that genes build. The multilevel selection people say that's a perverse way to talk: selection acts on whatever interacts with the environment as a unit, and sometimes that unit is a group, with group-level fitness differences doing real causal work. What gets me is that both sides mostly agree on the actual math—the covariance/Price-equation-style bookkeeping comes out the same either way—and on the concrete cases (a trait spreads or it doesnt, at some frequency). Yet the argument has run since the 60s and doesn't look any closer to ending. My working theory is that it isn't an empirical disagreement at all, and isn't even one disagreement: one side is answering the question of what replicates, while the other is answering the question of what gets selected, and underneath there's a buried argument about what an adaptation explanation has to point at. I want a diagnosis, not a history lesson. Is this disagreement verbal, is there a real empirical question about how often group selection actually happens in nature hiding inside it, or is it about standards of explanation? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, is there anything—a distinction, a formal result, a change of question—that would actually move the debate, or should people just accept it's not going to end?
