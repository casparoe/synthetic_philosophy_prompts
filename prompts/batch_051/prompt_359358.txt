Before I settle whether information about a person belongs to that person in a property-like sense that the law should recognize, I want the specification of what any adequate theory of personal-data ownership must get right. Write the desiderata: the cases it must deliver correct verdicts on (a fact about me that a stranger observes and remembers; a biometric or genetic record I never consented to; an inference a firm draws from my purchase history, such as a prediction that I am pregnant; a sentence I post publicly; an aggregate or de-identified dataset in which my contribution is essential but tiny), the distinctions it must not collapse (above all, between a fact and a particular record of that fact, between a right to control others' access and a fully alienable property right, and between sensitive and trivial data), and the established results it cannot contradict, such as the legal principle that facts as such are not ownable, the economic fact that information is non-rivalrous and so any ownership claim must specify just what is excluded from whom, and the persistence of some privacy interests even after disclosure. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are merely one camp's preference. Then say which single requirement excludes the most popular theories—the Lockean labor-mixing view, privacy-as-control, and the position that personal data is not property at all—and whether any pair of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, since that last part is what I most want to see.
