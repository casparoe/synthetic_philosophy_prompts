I run a small online discussion group, mostly philosophy with a lot of current-events arguments, and I'm sick of people smuggling in contested assumptions as if everyone already agreed to them. So I've been thinking about a mechanism to make the common ground explicit. Here's the proposal.

The forum software keeps, for each thread, a public ledger of background claims: the propositions everyone is being treated as accepting. Whenever a post presupposes something — a definite description, a 'since we all agree' clause, a loaded 'after X, obviously Y' construction, whatever — any reader can nominate that presupposition for the ledger. The nomination costs the nominator one token. The speaker then has forty-eight hours to either retract the claim, in which case the speaker loses two tokens and the nominator gains two, or defend it, in which case the nominated claim goes to a vote of the other members. If the vote upholds it, the claim is added to the ledger, the speaker gains two tokens, and the nominator loses the token they paid. If the vote rejects it, the speaker loses three tokens and the nominator gains two. Everyone starts with forty tokens a month, and tokens don't roll over.

My hope is that this prices presuppositions and makes accommodation visible: a claim nobody challenges becomes common ground by default, but a claim someone is willing to pay to challenge has to be earned. I'm also unsure whether unchallenged presuppositions should just lapse into the ledger automatically after forty-eight hours, or whether they should need a quorum of readers passively accepting them; I'd like your view on that too if it matters.

I want you to walk me through how this actually plays out, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. What does it incentivise people to do with their tokens, and how do people game it — do they farm tokens by presupposing things that are easy to accommodate, or by nominating trivial presuppositions they know will pass? What sort of claims get presupposed, and by whom? Does the vote become a popularity contest or a way to punish people? What happens to the difference between what's on the ledger and what people actually take for granted — can the real common ground end up smaller than, or different from, the ledger? And most importantly: once accommodation is something you have to pay for and something a speaker can profit from, does it still do the normal conversational job, or does it turn into a different activity altogether? Where does the whole thing fall apart first?

Don't worry about whether the moderators would approve it or weather it's fair to newcomers — treat it as a mechanism. And please tell me how confident you are in your predictions overall, and call out the single point you're least sure about. Write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold, like a normal email.
