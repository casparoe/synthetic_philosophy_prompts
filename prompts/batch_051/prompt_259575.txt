I volunteer at a county historical society and for the last year I've been stuck between two of our board members who can't stop arguing about what an archive is even for. Luisa runs the "keep what comes" camp: she says our job is to preserve whatever people hand us and make it findable; the second we start choosing, we're rewriting history on purpose and later generations will think the stuff we didn't collect never happened. Marlon runs the "chase the silences" camp: he says that's a fantasy, because we already choose all the time by waiting for things to show up. He says the stuff that arrives on our doorstep is the paper trail of the already-recorded, and if we don't go out and collect oral histories from the farmworkers, the historically Black neighborhoods, and the women who worked in the cannery, our "neutral" shelves are just a monument to people who never had to be chased.

Theyve finally agreed to do an adversarial-collaboration writeup, and I got assigned to make it happen. Please draft the document the two of them could actually both sign. It needs:

1. A numbered list of claims they both accept about how records get made, who tends to leave a paper trail, what an archive's budget and staff can and cant do, and how an archive becomes lopsided. Make these specific enough to mean something, not just truisms.

2. A table of the exact remaining disagreements. Each side's position has to be phrased so that Luisa or Marlon would read their column and say "yes, that's mine, put it that way."

3. The cruxes: the handful of sub-questions where if one side were proven right, the other would actually change their mind. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, and state in advance what each predicts.

Things they'd want worked into the table: whether "we only keep what's offered" is actually a neutral policy; whether digitizing a lopsided collection fixes the bias or just makes it easier to search; whether we should judge records by what the current community needs or by what a historian in 200 years might need; and what to do when a donor wants to keep a record accessible but the people it's about say it's hurtful (our ongoing fight over the old county asylum admission ledgers). Also, Marlon keeps saying an absence in the archive is itself a kind of record, and Luisa keeps saying that's just a fancy way of saying we have nothing — sort out whether that's a real disagreement or just words.

Write it like two smart, suspicious, good-faith people could stand up at our meeting and read it out loud together without either of them cringing.
