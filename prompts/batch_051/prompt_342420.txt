I serve on the diagnostic policy committee of a regional children's behavioral health network. At Thursday's meeting we vote on a proposal to introduce, in our electronic records and referral criteria, a new category for adolescents whose psychotic-like experiences are transient and subthreshold: hearing one's name in neutral noise, fleeting odd beliefs, mild perceptual distortions. The case for the category is that flagging these adolescents for early specialty follow-up may reduce the chance of later psychosis. The most serious objection on the committee is not about predictive validity or false positives. It is that once we give the state a name, the name becomes available to the patient, the family, and the school, and it may change the trajectory it was meant only to flag. I spent last evening arguing about this with a friend, who pressed the point in explicitly philosophical terms: a classification can loop back and alter the people it classifies, and those altered people then alter the meaning of the classification — what Ian Hacking called the looping effect. She argued that in adolescents especially, a fluid and still-forming self can consolidate around a risk label, so that the category would help create the very population it purports to describe. I found myself defending the proposal despite this, because refusing to name and monitor these adolescents carries its own looping risk: the absence of a category can mean absence of care, and silence is not neutral. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should engage the strongest version of the objector's position rather than a caricature of it.
