I spent most of my working life as a physical chemist and have only recently started reading philosophy seriously, and I've managed to tie myself in a knot about choice, prediction, and probability. I'd like a consistency check before I try to write any of this up for a reading group.

The commitments I seem to hold, as plainly as I can state them:

(a) The world is deterministic in the ordinary scientific sense. The present state plus the laws fixes what happens next, and my own future choices are no exception.

(b) Deliberation is not an illusion. When I deliberate about whether to do X or Y, I have to treat both as live options, and the deliberating itself is part of what causes the eventual choice. I don't see how I could deliberate at all if I thought only one option was ever really possible.

(c) I'm broadly Bayesian about evidence. For any empirical proposition—including propositions about what a person will choose—there should be a coherent subjective probability one can assign, at least in principle.

(d) But when the proposition is about what I will choose, and I'm currently deliberating about that very choice, I can't find a stable probability. Any figure I come up with becomes part of my evidence and changes the thing I was trying to estimate; if I settle on seventy percent for X, my reaction to that number shifts the probability it was supposed to state.

(e) I also think there's nothing epistemically special about the first person. A proposition about my own future choices should be no different in kind from a proposition about someone else's future choices, or about tomorrow's weather.

I genuinely can't tell whether these five fit together, or whether I'm just using words like "possible" and "probability" too loosely. Please don't treat them all as equally bad—rank the tensions by how serious they are, and for each one say what would have to give in order to resolve it and what the revision would cost.
