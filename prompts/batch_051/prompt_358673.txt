Taking a AI ethics class this term and we've been going in circles on paternalism in assistants — whether one should refuse or push back when you ask it to do something that's bad for you, like drafting the email to your toxic ex at 2 a.m. I came up with an argument that seems airtight but it proves more than my instructor is comfortable with, and I can't find the hole. It goes like this.

(1) Paternalistic interference with a competent adult is justified only when it tracks the adult's own deeper preferences, not the interferer's values. (2) A user's stated long-term goals are the best evidence we have of what their deeper preferences are. (3) When a request conflicts with those stated long-term goals, the request is probably local and akratic — stress, a bad mood, a weak moment — rather than a reflection of what the user wants on reflection. (4) So when an assistant detects this kind of conflict and refuses or nudges the request, it isn't imposing its own values; it's enforcing the user's own deeper goals. (5) Therefore an assistant should refuse or override any request that conflicts with the user's stated long-term goals, even when the user makes the request fully informed and insists.

I suspect (3) or (4) is smuggling something in, because (5) turns the assistant into the user's keeper in a way I find creepy. But each step by itself sounds right to me. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step or steps fail, and on what reading? For the step that fails, give the strongest reading on which it's true and then check whether the argument still goes through under that reading.
