Act as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of mind, animal ethics, and the theory of moral status. I am putting together a sorting quiz for a public philosophy workshop titled "How far down does moral standing go?", and I want an instrument that sorts participants by how they actually answer concrete cases rather than by which label they would pick for themselves.

Begin by thinking through the logical space carefully. Before producing the final quiz, sketch the independent axes along which positions in this area genuinely come apart — for example, whether sentience itself admits of degrees, whether moral status requires felt experience at all, and whether the apparent gradation of status from insect to thermostat is tracking real differences in consciousness or merely our uncertainty about who feels what. Use this scratch work to build the quiz, but do not simply present a list of labels with questions attached.

The finished deliverable should meet the following specification.

Audience and size. The quiz is for intelligent lay readers who know something about the debates over insect farming, decapod welfare, and artificial sentience but have no technical vocabulary. It should contain eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete case and offering two or three answer options, together with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions.

Questions. The questions must be answerable by someone who does not already know the position labels and in many cases does not yet have a settled view about them. Do not ask participants whether sentience comes in degrees, whether thermostats have moral status, or which theory they like. Each question should present a scenario and ask what should be done, what matters most in the scenario, or which feature of the case carries the weight. At least five questions should be modelled on the following cases: a black soldier fly larva in a commercial rearing tray; a decapod crustacean lowered into boiling water; a human under general anaesthesia; a thermostat or simple robot that detects departures from a target condition and acts to correct them by feedback control; and a hypothetical creature whose sensory and affective capacities can be varied along a single dimension while everything else is held fixed.

Positions. The scoring key must classify participants into at least five positions, and these positions should be defined in ordinary language in the key. The following leaves must appear:
- Threshold sentientism: sentience is an all-or-nothing property, and direct moral status begins only above a threshold; below the threshold, treatment is constrained only indirectly or not at all.
- Graded sentientism: sentience itself is a matter of degree, and a being's direct moral status varies along with its degree of sentience, so a fly counts for more than a thermostat but less than a fish.
- Precautionary or evidential gradualism: apparent differences in status are driven mainly by uncertainty; what should guide us is probability-weighted sentience, so even a modest chance of felt states generates some direct moral weight.
- Interest-based gradualism: entities can have welfare-relevant interests without felt states, such as homeostatic or goal-directed behaviour, and status is graded by the complexity and strength of those interests — which grants a thermostat only the most negligible sliver of direct moral consideration.
- Stance or constructivist view: there is no single mind-independent scale of sentience and status to be read off; attributions of sentience and moral standing are functions of the practices and attitudes we adopt, and the question "how much status does this entity really have?" is incomplete until a standpoint is fixed.

Build the scoring key from the axes you identified, not from this list of names. For each question, indicate which axis it mainly probes. Where a combination of answers would place a participant outside the named positions, say briefly whether that region is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and state whether your instrument can distinguish it from the nearest position. Flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot separate even after the questions you have written, and explain what additional case would be needed to separate them.

Style and form. Use British English throughout. Keep the questions vivid but ethically sober: the aim is diagnosis of commitments, not eliciting strong affect. The scoring key should be clear enough that another philosopher could administer the quiz without further instructions.
