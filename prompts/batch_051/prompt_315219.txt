I am a philosophy professor preparing a paper on the structure of legal asymmetries, and I keep returning to a case that resists easy resolution. Under the Nordic model of prostitution law — in force in Sweden, Norway, Iceland, France, Ireland, Canada, and elsewhere — it is a criminal offence to buy sexual services, but it is not a criminal offence to sell them. The two parties to a single consensual transaction thus stand in opposite legal positions: the buyer commits a crime and faces penalties, while the seller commits no crime and, in many formulations, is expressly treated as a victim and offered exit services or support rather than punishment. On its face, this looks like a striking asymmetry in legal practice: the very same act is prohibited for one party and permitted for the other. My question is not whether the Nordic model is better overall than full legalization or full decriminalization. It is narrower: is this internal asymmetry — punishing only one side of a two-party exchange — defensible on its own terms, or is it an unstable compromise that should be resolved in one direction or the other?

Take the role of a careful philosopher of law and applied ethics thinking through this with me. Begin by brainstorming the strongest candidate justifications for penalizing only the buyer. Consider, at minimum: differences in bargaining power and vulnerability; the claim that the buyer creates the market while the seller merely responds to underlying conditions; the expressive message a legal system might be trying to send; the deterrence logic of targeting demand; and the practical case for shielding sellers from prosecution. But do not simply list these. For each candidate, test it against nearby cases before relying on it — for instance, ask whether the same reasoning would justify criminalizing only the buyer of stolen goods while leaving the seller of stolen goods untouched, or only the employer but not the worker in a black-market labour arrangement, or only the purchaser but not the vendor of an illegally harvested organ. Where a justification would prove too much, or collapses under those comparisons, say so explicitly.

Then commit to a position. Is the Nordic asymmetry justified, or should it be given up? If you defend it, identify precisely which feature of this transaction does the justificatory work, and explain why that feature does not force the same asymmetry in the nearby cases. If you reject it, say whether the asymmetry should be resolved toward criminalizing both sides, decriminalizing both sides, or some third arrangement, and why. In either case, state and seriously answer the strongest objection to the view you take rather than sweeping it aside. I want one carefully reasoned verdict, not a survey, and not a reading list.
