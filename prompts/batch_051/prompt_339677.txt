I'm a PhD student working on the ethics of death, and I've been going back and forth on the old Epicurean mirror argument for the view that death isn't bad for the person who dies. The passage I keep returning to is from Lucretius, On the Nature of Things, Book III (trans. W. E. Leonard):

"Look back:
Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld
Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth.
And Nature holds this like a mirror up
Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone.
And what is there so horrible appears?
Now what is there so sad about it all?
Is't not serener far than any sleep?"

I want a short essay that uses this passage as its hinge. Lucretius is treating our prenatal nonexistence as a mirror of our posthumous nonexistence: if the one doesn't trouble us, why should the other? I know the standard responses roughly -- the deprivation account, the denial that past and future are symmetrical, the point that an earlier birth could have contained goods too, the thought that what we fear is dying rather than being dead. Weigh them against this passage and against each other, and actually land somewhere. Whatever your verdict -- wether the mirror works, fails, or only works against a few specific attitudes -- say it plainly, don't end on 'the debate is complicated'. And write in British English, please.
