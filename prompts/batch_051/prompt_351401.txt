People in AI governance keep reaching for Fearon's rationalist explanations for war whenever they talk about AI races. The thought is: even rational actors end up in wars and arms races because bargaining fails under private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or issue indivisibility. And an AI race looks like all three at once: labs hide their progress, they can't credibly commit to stopping at a safety threshold once they're ahead, and "being first to AGI" looks like it might be an indivisible prize.

But I can't shake the feeling the analogy is doing alot of the work. Fearon was writing about states fighting over largely rival goods like territory and influence, where the losers of a war still exist afterward and where war is costly enough that there's usually some bargain both sides would prefer to fighting. In an AI race the racers are often private labs, not states. The thing being raced for is non-rival-ish: the knowledge, the trained weights, the ability to hire the right people. And a lot of the costs of racing hard—the safety corners cut, the externalities—fall on everyone else rather than the racers themselves, so it's not obvious the racers even prefer some negotiated slowdown to just going flat out.

So here's what I want to know. Does the Fearon framework survive the move from war between states to AI races, or does it only survive as a loose metaphor? Which specific premise is under pressure—the unitary state actor, the costs-of-war assumption that creates a bargaining range, the nature of the contested good, something else—and can any of the three mechanisms be repaired so they still do real explanatory work in the AI setting? Or does the rationalist story just collapse into "the incentives are broken," which isn't a diagnosis at all. And if a repaired version does exist, is it still saying something interesting, or just relabeling the obvious?

One more thing: please keep clear which parts of this are settled in the IR/game-theory literature and which parts are genuinely contested, so you don't present a contested reading as the consensus. And don't give me a bibliography; I just want the reasoning.
