I am trying to discover what principle, if any, captures my own judgments about acts whose individual causal contribution to a large aggregate is negligible. I have fairly firm verdicts on the following cases, and I want to know whether those verdicts are consistent and what principle they point to.

1. I buy a factory-farmed chicken every week for dinner. I regard this as wrong. I know the purchase does not cause any identifiable chicken to be raised or slaughtered; the supplier's orders are set by thousands of purchases, and one weekly buyer will not shift them. Still, I do not think the smallness of my causal contribution excuses it.

2. I fly across the Atlantic once a year to visit a close friend. I regard this as permissible, or at most a very minor fault. The flight contributes a real but minuscule amount to climate change. I find this much easier to accept than the chicken, though I cannot point to a difference I trust.

3. I vote in national elections and think that I should. I do not believe my vote will decide anything, and I would not advise anyone that their vote is the pivotal one. Even so, I think of not voting as a civic failure.

4. I leave my air conditioning running when I leave the house on a hot day. I treat this as trivial and feel no real blame. The extra energy is negligible next to industrial use, and I would say it harms no one in particular.

5. I once boycotted a brand linked to forced labor. I did not believe my refusal to buy from the company would affect its sales, but I thought the boycott was the right thing to do.

6. When asked to sign a petition for a policy I support, I sign even though I think my signature adds effectively nothing to the chance of passage. I think slightly worse of someone who refuses on the ground that their signature will not matter.

These verdicts do not clearly hang together. In the chicken, voting, boycott, and petition cases, I refuse to accept that negligible individual contribution is an excuse. In the flight and air-conditioning cases, I accept something very like that excuse. But if the character of an act is enough to make the chicken wrong, it should make the flight wrong too; and if negligible contribution is an excuse for the flight, it should be an excuse for the vote.

Is there a principle that captures most or all of these verdicts? State it precisely, and say which case motivates each clause. Then apply the principle to cases I have not listed: buying a carbon offset for the flight; eating meat from a small farm that slaughters a fixed number of animals each year; declining a work trip by air even though the seat will be filled regardless; buying an imported product whose production caused a small amount of pollution; and attending a protest whose outcome would be exactly the same whether or not I attend. Be honest about which of my original verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and note whether some simpler principle would be preferable even if it overturns one or two of them.
