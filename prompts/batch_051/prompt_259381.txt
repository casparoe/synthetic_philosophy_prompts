You are a professional philosopher with expertise in Schopenhauer, Kantian transcendental idealism, and contemporary work on the metaphysics of consciousness and value. Think carefully, and begin with some unstructured brainstorming before presenting your final answer.

I've come to accept, provisionally, a version of Schopenhauer's position in The World as Will and Idea, Volume 1. The view I hold is roughly this. The world has two sides. As representation, it is ordered by space, time, and causality, and exists only for a knowing subject. As thing in itself, it is will — a blind, restless, unceasing striving, present in gravity, in plant growth, in animal instinct, and in human desire. Because the will can never achieve final satisfaction, suffering is not an accident of life but its positive, fundamental character; pleasure is merely the temporary cessation of a striving, the felt absence of pain. I am particularly moved by the argument in Book 2, around section 18, where Schopenhauer moves from the double knowledge we have of our own body — as representation and as will — to the claim that will is the inner essence of every phenomenon, and that its blind striving is the key to understanding nature from the inside.

I want to know what would count against this view. I don't mean the standard objections that it is gloomy or that it cannot be empirically tested in a straightforward way. I mean the serious philosophical defeaters. Specifically:

1. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or results in other areas — philosophy of mind, neuroscience, evolutionary biology, formal decision theory, the metaphysics of causation — would have to be true for this position to be false, incoherent, or to collapse into something much weaker?

2. For each candidate defeater, is it decisive, merely awkward, or only a problem for a specific reading of the claim?

3. Which of these would a committed Schopenhauerian themselves concede counts against the view — and which would they dismiss as a confusion, category-mistake, or superficial objection?

4. Is the view, as I've stated it, sufficiently exposed to evidence and argument? Or has it been qualified so thoroughly that it risks nothing at all? If the latter, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it acceptable for a metaphysical picture of this scope?

I would personally find most persuasive a defeater that comes from inside the view itself — for example, a tension between the claim that the will is blind and the claim that it objectifies itself in the organized, apparently end-directed structure of organisms. Please give me a ranked map of the view's exposure, from what would change my mind immediately to what I can reasonably set aside. Be honest about the live escape routes rather than merely listing logical possibilities.
