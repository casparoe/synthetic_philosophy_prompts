I read a chapter in a popular science book that explained the gene's-eye view of evolution, and I'm trying to figure out how much of it to trust before I cite it in an essay. The chapter's version went something like this. Natural selection doesn't really act on organisms, because organisms are temporary and their traits get scrambled by sex, whereas genes persist down the generations, so the gene is the true unit of selection. Organisms—and groups too—are just disposable vehicles that genes build to carry copies of themselves into the next generation. The upshot, the author said, is that you should stop asking what a trait is for from the organism's point of view and ask instead what that stretch of DNA is doing to get itself copied; lions don't hunt for their own sake, they're survival machines for the hunting-behavior genes. From there the chapter said genuine altruism should be rare and mostly confined to close kin or to tit-for-tat deals, where apparent kindness is really delayed self-interest. It ended by saying this is why group selection is a dead idea: if adaptations are always for the good of the gene, then no trait can evolve because it benefits the group or species, and any appearance of group-level benefit has to be explained away as a byproduct of gene-level competition. This feels mostly right but a little too neat, and I can't tell where the slide happens. Is it a fair summary of the gene's-eye view? I'd like you to go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful defender—Dawkins or Williams, say—would accept, which parts are subtly off, and wether the final conclusion about group selection actually follows from the accurate bits.
