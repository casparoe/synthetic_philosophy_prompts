Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about Mill and free speech, and I'm still annoyed about it. He kept saying that once you buy the harm principle you've basically conceded that speech can be restricted whenever it hurts people: emotional harm, reputational damage, a hostile environment — all of it counts as harm to others. I kept insisting Mill is way more protective of expression than that, but I couldnt quite say why without sounding like I was moving the goalposts.

So here's what I want. Take two passages from On Liberty that seem to pull in opposite directions. In Chapter I, Mill states the one simple principle, roughly: the only purpose for which power can rightly be exercised over someone against their will is to prevent harm to others, and a person's own good, physical or moral, is never enough to justify it. In Chapter II, defending liberty of thought and discussion, he says something much stronger, roughly: if all of mankind except one person held an opinion, and only that one held the opposite, silencing the one would be just as unjustified as the one silencing everyone else. No mention of harm there — it sounds almost absolutist.

Those two sure look like they conflict. Reconcile them for me, or tell me they can't be, and say what the reconciliation costs. Give me the candidate readings: maybe harm in Chapter I is narrower than my friend thinks; maybe the Chapter II argument is about truth and doesnt actually run through the harm principle; maybe Mill changed his mind between chapters; maybe the absolutist line only covers the expression of opinion and still leaves room to punish incitement, defamation, or harassment. For each candidate, say whether it's honest to the text or special pleading. And then stick the landing: on your preferred reading, what does Mill actually say about speech that predictably causes psychological harm or reputational damage — can it be regulated or not?
