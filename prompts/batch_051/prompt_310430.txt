I've been trying to build a version of the special-claims-to-compatriots view that doesn't just lean on culture, language, or blood, and I wound up with something I call the "mesh criterion." The claim is: a compatriot has a special claim on me only to the extent that our lives are actually entwined through shared liability-and-risk institutions—same legal system, same tax base, same public debt, same unemployment and pension pools, same exposure to central bank decisions, same police and courts. Not identity, not shared history, not even formal citizenship by itself. So a long-term resident non-citizen in my city has, on this view, a thicker claim on me than a co-citizen who's lived abroad for twenty years and pays no taxes and shares none of my risk pool. That's a feature of the view, not a bug, because I want partiality to track real mutual exposure, not sentiment. I know there are positions that make special duties turn on shared coercive schemes, but I'm not saying coercion alone—I'm saying shared vulnerability to the same institutional failures. That's the bit I think is new. Where does this go wrong? Is it just statism in different words, or does it collapse into ordinary cosmopolitanism once you push on it? Reconstruct it in the strongest form you can, name the nearest existing view, and then give me your honest committed assessment of where it fails or stops being distinctive. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then reason, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
