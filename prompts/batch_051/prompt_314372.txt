I've been going back and forth with a friend about Fitch's paradox, and I've realized that before either of us commits to a fix we should first write down what a fix is even supposed to do. The setup, as we've been using it: the anti-realist wants the principle that if a proposition is true, then it's possible for someone to know it. Fitch's derivation then seems to show this collapse into the much stronger claim that if p is true, p is actually known. Compressed, the proof goes: take an unknown truth p — that is, p and no one knows p. By the anti-realist principle, it must be possible to know that whole conjunction. But knowing a conjunction means knowing each conjunct, and knowing the conjunct 'no one knows p' would, since knowledge is factive, imply that no one knows p — which contradicts the other conjunct. So an unknown truth can't exist, and every truth is already known.

I keep seeing people pull on one specific step of that derivation and leave the rest standing: restrict the principle to atomic or "basic" truths, move to an intuitionistic background logic so the last classical step doesn't land, wrap the proposition in an 'actually' operator so the thing known isn't quite the original Fitch-laden proposition, or deny that knowing a conjunction requires knowing each conjunct. Maybe one of these is right, but the choice is being made without a spec. So give me the desiderata that an adequate theory of knowability has to meet, assuming the goal is to save the anti-realist intuition that truth can't outrun possible knowledge without making us all actually omniscient. For each requirement, say what case or consideration forces it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are expensive-but-optional. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the standard responses, and which one the most popular response quietly violates. I don't need a reading list or who defended what; a table for the requirements is fine.
