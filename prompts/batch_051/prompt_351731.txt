Last week in my seminar on the economics and governance of advanced AI, two students got into an argument during office hours that continued over lunch, and I was the only person present for the whole thing. I told them I would write up a referee's note for our study group, but I honestly don't trust my own command of the material enough to adjudicate it alone. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

For background, the lecture gave us two terms. The alignment tax is the cost in capability, speed, or profit that a developer pays for making a system more aligned or safer than an unconstrained baseline would be. The competitiveness constraint is the claim that a lab cannot unilaterally pay more of that tax than its rivals without losing the race, so that individual labs face pressure to keep safety spending low absent coordination or regulation.

Here is the argument:

(1) Maya began: the alignment tax is a real cost of doing the right thing, and under the winner-takes-all logic of AI races, the lab that pays the smallest tax wins. If lab A takes extra time and compute to make its model safer while lab B skips that work, B ships at the same capability earlier or cheaper. So the competitiveness constraint pushes every lab toward the legal floor, and the only escape is regulation or some coordination mechanism that makes all labs pay the same tax.

(2) Julian replied: this assumes there is some fixed frontier where a model has a given level of capability and the safety work is bolted on at a price. But safer systems can be strictly more capable systems. A model that misgeneralizes less, that doesn't produce the failures its users actually notice, and that isn't sycophantic is a better product, not a worse product with a virtue attached. So the tax is an artifact of comparing a real safe model to a hypothetical unsafe model that gets credited with equal capability it may not in fact have.

(3) Maya answered: fine, call those free lunches. The competitiveness constraint is about the margin, not the average. Whatever the architecture, there will be decisions that improve safety at a small cost to benchmark performance or to deploy date, even if only because they require additional testing and iteration. In a close race, the lab that happens to skip exactly those margin-level decisions wins. The existence of some free lunches doesn't make the marginal dollar free.

(4) Julian replied: that marginal argument only works if the market cannot tell the difference at the point of deployment. If the corners you cut reliably show up as refusals, errors, or edge-case failures that buyers can observe, you don't win the race — you lose reputation, and you absorb the costs in returns, lawsuits, or regulatory scrutiny. So even many marginal tradeoffs are illusory: they are tradeoffs between a better product and a worse product, not between safety and capability.

(5) Maya said: now you've narrowed the case to harms the buyer can observe immediately. But the canonical alignment problems are precisely the ones where the harm falls on third parties or appears later. The unsafe model may be indistinguishable from the safe one at the point of sale, and the lab doesn't internalize the diffuse costs it imposes. In those cases the unsafe model really is cheaper to the lab, which is exactly why the competitiveness constraint bites: the benefits of safety are dispersed, while its costs are private.

(6) Julian answered: if that's your point, it's just the ordinary argument that safety is a public good and markets underprovide public goods. That is true of every industry with externalities, and it's not a special iron law about AI. We already have the familiar remedies — liability, insurance, standards, and regulation — and calling it an "alignment tax" makes it sound like a novel fact about machine learning when it's really a textbook externality problem.

(7) Maya finished: calling it ordinary doesn't make it tractable. Here the harms can be catastrophic and hard to trace to any particular design decision, so liability and standards may arrive only after the damage is done. "It's an externality" names the problem; it doesn't dissolve the race. If anything, the difficulty of assigning responsibility makes the ordinary remedies weaker than usual, which sharpens the competitiveness constraint rather than domesticating it.

Please go through this exchange move by move and adjudicate it. For each of the seven moves, say what is sound, what is overstated, and what is being conflated or assumed. Flag any premise both of them seem to share that a careful treatment would question. Then say who is more right overall about the original question — whether the competitiveness constraint makes unsafe development inevitable absent coordination — and explain your verdict without collapsing it into "both have a point."
