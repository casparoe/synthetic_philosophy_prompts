The chapter's summary goes basically like this:

"Grice's core distinction is between what a sentence means and what a speaker means by uttering it. His analysis of speaker meaning is that S means that p by an utterance iff S intends the audience to believe that p, intends the audience to recognize this intention, and intends that recognition to be at least part of the audience's reason for believing p. On top of this, Grice distinguishes what is said — the truth-conditional content you get from the conventional meanings of the words together with fixing reference and ambiguity — from what is implicated. The most important kind of implicature is conversational implicature, which arises because participants in a conversation take each other to be following the Cooperative Principle and its maxims of Quality, Quantity, Relation, and Manner. The stock example is the recommendation letter that says only that a candidate has excellent handwriting: the writer is violating the maxim of Quantity, and the reader infers from this that the writer means the candidate is not good at philosophy. Grice argued that conversational implicatures have three hallmark properties: they are calculable from what is said together with the maxims, they are cancellable, and they are non-detachable, meaning you can't get rid of them just by rephrasing the sentence, since they attach to what is said rather than to the particular words used. The chapter then concludes that Grice gives us a clean division of labor: semantics handles the literal, truth-conditional meaning of sentences, and pragmatics handles everything beyond that."

I'm mostly on board with the intention analysis and the maxims, but the non-detachability part nags at me — couldn't you rephrase what's said and lose the implicature, or am I confusing rephrasing with changing the content? And the final division of labor (semantics = literal truth conditions, pragmatics = everything else) sounds too clean given some lecture asides about how contested that boundary is.
