I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've been assigned a piece on how people decide whether a pattern in an ancient text is really there or read into it. At a workshop I was covering, two participants spent dinner arguing about the 153 fish in John 21, and they both made enough sense that I couldnt referee it, so I'm passing it off.

Leah said the symbolic reading is stronger than people allow. John bothers to record exactly 153 large fish rather than roughly a hundred and fifty; 153 is the 17th triangular number; and John is the most number-saturated of the gospels. Early readers worked like that all the time. Letters did double duty as numbers, Revelation flags the beast's number, 666, as something to calculate, and an early Christian writer even decoded Abraham's 318 servants as tau (the cross-shaped letter, 300) plus iota-eta (18, the first two letters of Jesus). So treating the count as just fishing data is reading modern habits backward.

Marcus said this is how you fool yourself. 153 is also an Armstrong number (1^3 + 5^3 + 3^3 = 153) and a Harshad number. If you run a number through enough tests—triangular, square, cube-sum, digit-sum, gematria—something's going to look special. He called it the look-elsewhere effect from particle physics: scan enough channels and you get a spurious signal. Fishermen count catches for practical reasons, and the burden should be on the person claiming a code.

Leah replied that falsifiability is the wrong demand for a text like this. The test should be whether the reading coheres with the author's wider patterns and the conventions of his early readers. And 153 is the triangle of 17, with 17 = 10 + 7; John works seven into every structural seam of the book, so the count lands exactly where a numerate ancient writer would want it. The literal reading, by contrast, has to explain why a resurrection appearance pauses to do inventory.

Marcus came back that coherence is no test at all, since people find coherence in noise all the time and have used this same number to support opposite theologies. Without a cue in the text or an early source handing over the code, it's story-building after the fact.

I'm stuck, and I need the what's-actually-going-on version of this. Adjudicate it point by point: who's right about which specific claim, who's more right overall, and what are they both missing?
