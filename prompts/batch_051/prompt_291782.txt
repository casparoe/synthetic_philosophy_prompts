A friend walked me through this and I can't find the exit. He swears it shows the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion is just vibes.

(1) If manipulation is wrong, it's wrong because it bypasses or subverts the target's capacity to govern their own response.

(2) But even the most straight-down-the-line rational persuasion works only through the target's actual psychology: what they find salient, who they take to be credible, which examples feel apt, what tone gets them to update.

(3) The target didn't choose those features. You no more choose what you find salient than you choose what you find insulting.

(4) If an influence gets its grip through features of the target's psychology that the target didn't choose, then it bypasses their self-governance in just the way manipulation does.

(5) He says the obvious escape won't work. It's tempting to say self-governance just requires the capacity to reflect on first impressions, but reflection is itself steered by unchosen salience, trust, and temperament, so that moves the problem back a step rather than solving it.

(6) So respecting the target's rationality can't distinguish legitimate persuasion from manipulation.

(7) But that can't be the end of it: if testimony, advice, and evidence-giving are manipulative in the same sense as gaslighting, the concept has lost the very work we need it to do.

He says the only stable options are to reject (1) and say manipulation isn't wrong as such, or to accept (7) and treat ordinary persuasion as a standing moral hazard. I'd most like to keep (1) and (7). Which step should I reject, and what does saving both force me to say about how influence and self-governance actually relate?
