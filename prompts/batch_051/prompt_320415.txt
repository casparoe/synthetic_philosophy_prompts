i'm doing a PhD on legal personhood and I keep running into the line that giving AI legal personhood is just like giving corporations legal personhood. the argument goes: we already treat an incorporeal thing as a rights-bearing unit because it organises activity, enters contracts, and can be sued, so why not do the same for an AI. people say it with such confidence that the analogy is doing all the work, but I suspect it's dodgy.

I've been reading Hobbes's Leviathan, chapter XVI, Of Persons, Authors, and Things Personated. He goes through the idea that some things cannot be authors themselves because they cannot give authority, and he explicitly discusses inanimate things — churches, hospitals, bridges. He says they are personated by a rector, master, or overseer: the representative acts, and the thing is benefited or bound by that action. I think he says they cannot be persons because they cannot be authors. what strikes me is that this is already close to why we say a corporation is an artificial person, but the inanimate things Hobbes lists are not exactly legal persons in the modern corporate sense — they are more like institutions or holdings that a prelate or governor acts for.

so here's what I want. audit the corporate-person analogy for AI personhood properly. give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the shareholders, the board, the capacity to authorise, the artificial person, the overseer — and then the places it breaks. I don't want the hand-wavy version where 'AI is like a corporation' and that settles it. I want the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion versus the ones that don't. in particular, does Hobbes's treatment of inanimate things actually support treating AI as something that can be personated, or does it pull the other way? and once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that AI should be a legal person — or at least that there's no conceptual problem with trying — survive, or was the corporate analogy carrying it the whole time.

please write in British English. and I'd rather you argue with the analogy than just catalogue it.
