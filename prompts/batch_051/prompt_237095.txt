In my high school history class, we keep returning to the question of who started the Cold War, and I have adopted a personal rule for these debates because they otherwise turn into contests over which event counts as the first hostile move. My rule is this: I do not accept an attribution of blame unless the person can do two things. First, they have to identify a specific decision by the side they blame and state what that side should have done instead. Second, they have to give me some reason to think that, had that decision been different, the rivalry would not simply have emerged later or in a different form — in other words, that the alternative action would have plausibly prevented or substantially reduced the conflict, rather than merely rearranged the timeline. I use the same rule when we discuss other prolonged conflicts.

I want to know whether this rule is defensible as a way of forming beliefs about historical blame. It seems to protect me from vague accusations that a country was aggressive or provocative over a long stretch of time. But I suspect it may be biased in two ways. It might favor whichever side made the last clear decision before open hostility, while overlooking the slow accumulation of smaller choices that made a breakdown highly likely. It might also treat overdetermination as exculpation: if either of two sides could have set off the conflict, my rule might let each side point to the other and escape blame, whereas ordinary moral judgment might say both are at fault. Is my rule a good epistemic policy, or does it quietly build in a controversial answer to the question of what historical blame requires? If it is too blunt, is there a revised version that keeps its advantages without these distortions, and should I apply different standards to different kinds of conflicts?
