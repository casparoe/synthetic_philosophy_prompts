Exam question: Explain Nietzsche's distinction between master morality and slave morality in the first essay of On the Genealogy of Morality. Specifically: how does ressentiment generate the slave revolt in morality, and how does the resulting good/evil pair differ from the noble good/bad pair?

Student answer:

The first essay distinguishes two ways of creating values. Master morality begins with a yes to oneself. The noble, strong, beautiful person calls what he is good, and the bad is only the leftover negation of that: the common, the weak, the low. There's no moralizing in it; it's a statement of rank and distance.

Slave morality runs in reverse. Because the weak cannot strike back, ressentiment builds up — the frustration of people denied real action, who get their revenge imaginatively instead. From that, they call the powerful person evil first, and only afterward do they call themselves good, meaning the harmless, patient, humble ones. So the good/evil distinction is born from saying no to an outside, while good/bad is born from saying yes to oneself. The lambs and the birds of prey illustrate this: the lambs make the eagle evil for eating them, and then turn their own weakness into merit.

The one place I'd push is whether Nietzsche means this as history. He writes as if an actual enslaved class, somewhere in the ancient world, rose up against its masters and replaced the old noble vocabulary with the new priestly one that Christianity later spreads. Read that literally, and the bad of the masters and the evil of the slaves start to look like the same operation seen from opposite sides: each is just the evaluator's name for the group it opposes, and the only difference is who is on top when they say it. So the switch from good/bad to good/evil is less a change in content than a reversal of direction.
