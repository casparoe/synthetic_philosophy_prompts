I'm currently taking a course on the philosophy of discrimination law, and our discussion of protected characteristics keeps returning to one criterion above the others: immutability. On this view, what makes a ground of discrimination suspect is that it is a trait a person neither chose nor can change—race and sex are the standard examples—so that penalizing someone on its basis seems to hold them accountable for a morally arbitrary fact about themselves. I want to see how far that argument extends under a specific new development. Suppose polygenic embryo screening became accurate, inexpensive, and routine enough that a substantial share of children had some traits selected by their parents—not only disease risks, but height, probable cognitive style, and broad personality dispositions. The person who later has those traits still did not choose them, but they are no longer simply accidents of nature; someone chose them, on the child's behalf, before the child existed. Does the immutability argument for treating a ground as suspect survive this development? Which premise is really under pressure—the premise that unchosen traits are morally arbitrary, the premise that immutability is what tracks that arbitrariness, or the premise that the distinction between chosen and unchosen does the relevant work? If the argument needs repair rather than abandonment, what would a repaired version say about which traits count as protected? I am after the fate of the philosophical argument itself, not a policy recommendation.
